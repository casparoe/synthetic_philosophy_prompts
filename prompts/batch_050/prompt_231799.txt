I chair my philosophy department's curriculum committee and we've been deadlocked for two months between three proposals for restructuring the major (roughly: minor tweaks, a moderate overhaul, and burn-it-down interdisciplinarity). At the last meeting a colleague who'd read something about voting theory proposed we settle it by ranked ballot with instant runoff, assuring the room that this makes strategic voting pointless so everyone can just rank honestly. I'm fairly sure that's wrong in principle -- the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem seems to say any non-dictatorial rule over three or more options is manipulable sometimes -- but I'm genuinely unsure how much a worst-case theorem should weigh when picking a procedure for a committe of fourteen people who all know each other and will keep meeting for years. Draft the memo I'll circulate before the next meeting: recommend a specific procedure (or a procedure plus safeguards), explain in plain terms what the theorem does and doesn't establish, and give the strongest honest version of my colleague's instant-runoff pitch before saying whether it survives. One wrinkle I'd like addressed head-on: the three proposals plausibly fall on a single spectrum from least to most change, and I've seen it claimed that when options line up like that, plain majority rule over pairs has nice strategy-proofness properties (median voter type results). Should that change what we do, or is it a red herring given that not everyone may agree on the ordering?
