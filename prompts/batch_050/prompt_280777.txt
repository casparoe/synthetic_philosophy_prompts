Next month I am presenting a paper at a two-day workshop on the metaphysics of time, co-hosted by my philosophy department and our university's gravitation research group. The audience will be roughly two-thirds philosophers of physics — people whose working assumption is that special and general relativity have settled the ontology of time in favor of a four-dimensional block universe — plus several practicing relativists and only one or two metaphysicians sympathetic to me. Almost none of them work on the A-series/B-series literature itself; they know it mostly as a historical preamble in textbooks before the real physics begins, and they are professionally allergic to talk of temporal "flow."

My thesis: eternalists who take the block universe seriously cannot recover genuine change, only spatial-like variation along a temporal dimension, and a theory of time that cannot accommodate change fails to describe time at all. The central exhibit of the paper is this passage from J. M. E. McTaggart's 1908 paper in Mind, "The Unreality of Time":

"It would, I suppose, be universally admitted that time involves change. A particular thing, indeed, may exist unchanged through any amount of time. But when we ask what we mean by saying that there were different moments of time, or a certain duration of time, through which the thing was the same, we find that we mean that it remained the same while other things were changing. A universe in which nothing whatever changed (including the thoughts of the conscious beings in it) would be a timeless universe."

I read McTaggart here as issuing a challenge rather than merely reporting an intuition: either locate change within the B-series alone, or concede that the block is timeless. My argument is that the standard eternalist moves — change as a thing's bearing different properties at different times, change as variation in proper time along a worldline — either smuggle in tense under another name or reduce change to a pattern no more temporal than the color gradient along a barber pole.

Prepare me for the question period. Specifically, I want:

1. The eight to ten objections I am actually likely to get from this audience — philosophers of physics and relativists, not A-theorists — ordered by how damaging each would be if I handle it badly. Phrase each the way they would phrase it, in their vocabulary (relativity of simultaneity, foliation, worldline, tenseless predication, "you're reifying the now," "that's a fact about perception, not ontology"), not as a straw man.
2. For each objection, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, followed by a candid note on where that reply is weakest or what it quietly concedes.
3. What I should concede up front in the talk itself so the Q&A does not waste time there — for instance, that I am not disputing the mathematics of relativity and that I accept the frame-dependence of simultaneity.
4. The single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the session, and how to phrase it for people who think in spacetime diagrams rather than in the language of tensed properties.
5. The question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that most directly threatens my inference from "no change in the block" to "the block is timeless" — and my best honest answer to it, even if that answer is a strategic retreat.

One complication to build into your preparation: at least one person in the room will know McTaggart well enough to point out that the passage my paper rests on comes from an argument whose conclusion — the unreality of time — I reject. Prepare me for that as well: how do I defend taking over McTaggart's conditional premise that change is essential to time while refusing the second half of his argument (the alleged contradiction in the A-series), without appearing to cherry-pick the parts of his reasoning that suit me?
