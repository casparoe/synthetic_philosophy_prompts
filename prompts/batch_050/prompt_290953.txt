I am a novelist in the early stages of a book about a minor functionary -- someone whose hands are clean in every causal sense but whose labor keeps something ugly running. To get my own character right I want to understand a predecessor, so I'd like a committed reading of Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day through the philosophical problem of complicity: participating in wrongdoing one does not cause, order, or even fully endorse. Stevens never harms anyone himself. He dismisses the two Jewish refugee maids only because Darlington instructs it, and he says as much to Miss Kenton, while also telling her the matter is not theirs to judge. He polishes the silver and serves the wine at the 1936 conference where Darlington's pro-German diplomacy is worked out over dinner. His father's death coincides with that conference, and Stevens stays at his post. The novel's defense of him -- and his defense of himself -- is that he merely served well, and that dignity in service is a virtue independent of what is being served. I want you to take a definite position, not survey the options. Answer these, in each case with your reasons rather than a menu of views: First, is Stevens actually complicit, or does the standard thought -- that complicity requires some intentional participation in the wrong, not just causal contribution to an institution -- let him off, given that he neither chose Darlington's politics nor understood them? Second, does it matter to the verdict that he foresaw the wrong (he admits to Miss Kenton, years later, that the maids' dismissal troubled him at the time) but did not intend it? Third, does his later self-deception -- the way he edits his own memories of the dismissal and of Darlington -- mitigate or aggravate the complicity, and does his final, partial recognition on the pier change anything? Fourth, where does the novel itself stand: does Ishiguro dramatize service as a moral alibi, or does the book quietly suggest that Stevens's professionalism is itself a character flaw rather than a neutral skill? Where the novel resists the complicity frame, say so honestly rather than forcing it. Lead with your bottom line on whether Stevens is complicit in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my questions. I will borrow your answer's structure, not its sentences, for my own book.
