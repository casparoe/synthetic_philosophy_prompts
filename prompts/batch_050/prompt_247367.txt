I'm a stats/ML person (forecasting models, mostly) trying to make sense of the philosophy-of-science literature on verisimilitude, a.k.a. truthlikeness -- the idea that one false theory can still be closer to the truth than another. Here's what I've pieced together, correct me if I've garbled any of it:

Popper wanted to say science progresses by replacing theories with ones that have greater verisimilitude, and he gave a definition in terms of consequence classes: theory A beats theory B if A's true consequences include B's, B's false consequences include A's, and at least one of those inclusions is strict. Apparently this collapsed in the mid-70s when Miller and Tichý (independently, I think?) showed the definition implies no false theory can be closer to the truth than any other false theory. So later people switched to distance-based approaches: a theory picks out some region of the space of possible states of the world, the truth picks out a point, and verisimilitude is some function of how close the region is to the point. But Miller also has this nasty argument that any such measure depends on which language you describe the world in -- he has some trick with weather predicates where you can re-describe the same situation so that rankings of closeness flip.

I have decent fluency with all of that. Concretely: what's the statistical counterpart of a "theory" -- a point distribution? a model class? of "the truth" -- the data-generating process? of Popper's consequence classes -- maybe something about stochastic dominance or nesting of distributions? Does the distance-based approach just become "pick a divergence and measure distance to the true distribution," and if so, does Miller's language-dependence argument reduce to the mundane fact that there's no canonical divergence and KL is asymmetric / reparameterization behaves weirdly? Or does something in his argument survive that my framework can't express?
