For our school's spring film festival I'm writing a short screenplay about a real historical figure -- a nineteenth-century astronomer -- and, as every historical drama does, I have invented private conversations, domestic scenes, and inner conflicts that nobody ever recorded. When I described the project to my friend Mira, who takes ethics very seriously, she argued that what I am doing is not merely artistically risky but straightforwardly immoral, and her reasoning disturbs me because I cannot locate the step where it fails, even though it seems to prove far too much: if she is right, then essentially every historical novel, biopic, and stage play about a real person is an ongoing wrong, and the entire genre should be abandoned rather than refined. Her argument runs as follows. (1) It is wrong to state things about a real person as fact when you know perfectly well that you invented them and that person has no opportunity to correct you; that, she says, is precisely what separates a lie from a story. (2) The dead have no opportunity to correct anyone. (3) Any dramatization of a real dead person requires the writer to place invented words, motives, and episodes into the public record of that person's life, and audiences in fact come away believing they have learned something about the actual human being, which is why people watch such films in the first place. (4) Therefore any dramatization of a real dead person amounts to stating invented things as fact about someone who cannot answer back. (5) Therefore dramatizing the real dead is wrong in every case, and the respect we show for celebrated historical films is respect for a respectable form of defamation.

Please tell me where this argument goes wrong, if it does. If I had to choose, I would much rather surrender (4) or (5) than (1), since (1) strikes me as almost obviously correct, but I would like to know what it costs me to keep (1) -- and I notice, uneasily, that (1) itself contains the phrase about lies and stories, which may already give me more than Mira realizes. A good answer should name the specific step or premise to reject, explain why that step seemed innocent, and price the alternatives honestly. I would also like to know whether the obvious defense of my screenplay -- that fiction is not assertion and that no reasonable viewer mistakes a dramatization for a documentary -- actually survives (3)'s empirical claim that audiences do treat biopics as history lessons, or whether the honest conclusion is that those of us who dramatize the dead owe them something the genre has never bothered to specify.
