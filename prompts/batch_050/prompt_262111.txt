I have no formal training in history or philosophy, but over the past year I have been reading on my own about path dependence -- the QWERTY keyboard, why some regions stayed poor after colonization, the idea that small accidents early on get locked in. Along the way I built an argument that now worries me, because it seems to prove that historical contingency is an illusion, and I cannot accept that. I want to know where it breaks.

(1) To say a historical outcome was contingent is to say that some small change early in the process would have produced a genuinely different outcome.
(2) A small early change to any ongoing process can do only one of two things: either it gets absorbed -- the process compensates and settles back to where it was heading -- or it propagates and cascades.
(3) If the change is absorbed, the outcome was going to happen anyway. There is no contingency there, only the appearance of a close call.
(4) If the change cascades, then the resulting world is different, but it is different in a way no one chose, aimed at, or could have foreseen -- the difference is the output of a lottery, not an alternative anyone actually had.
(5) A state of affairs only counts as a genuine alternative to what happened if it was connected to what people were doing, choosing, and striving for at the time. A difference that floats free of anyone's agency is like saying I "could have been" a different person if a cosmic ray had struck a different neuron: technically true, empty as history.
(6) So for any outcome, either it was locked in (by 3) or the only "alternatives" are lottery noise (by 4 and 5).
(7) Therefore "history could have gone differently" is never true in any sense that matters.

The conclusion proves too much. It implies that the outcome of every war, every revolution, every election was either inevitable or a coin flip -- and, worse, it seems to imply the same about my own decisions, since the argument nowhere depends on the scale of the process. I would rather give up almost anything else than accept that human choices are never more than noise, and I would also like to keep (1), since it seems like a harmless definition. If the argument is valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to abandon and what I lose by abandoning it. If it contains a sleight of hand, name the exact step where it happens and explain why it looked innocent. And if the whole thing rests on a confusion -- for instance, if my way of setting up the options in (2) already rigs the conclusion -- I would rather have that said plainly than receive a polite repair job on an argument that does not deserve one. Please be as self-critical about your own diagnosis as you are about mine: if there are two defensible places to cut and you can only rank them on contested grounds, say which grounds those are rather than presenting your preferred cut as the answer.
