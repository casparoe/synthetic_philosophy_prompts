I teach an undergraduate philosophy of language seminar (18 students, one 75-minute session) and want an activity that makes students feel the force of Hofstadter's distinction, in Gödel, Escher, Bach, between the frame message, the outer message, and the inner message: his claim that understanding requires first recognizing that something is a message at all, then bootstrapping the decoding mechanism, and only then reading the content — and his further suggestion that meaning is not located in the symbols themselves but in the interplay between message and decoder. Design a hands-on activity in which small groups receive a string of symbols in an unknown code and must pass through all three stages themselves: the frame should be something they have to notice (a pattern that announces "I am a message" without saying in what), the outer message should let them construct the key from within the string itself, and the inner message should be trivially easy to read once the key exists. Give me the actual string(s) or a precise recipe for generating them, plus rules, a timeline, and a debrief that maps each stage of what the groups did onto the three layers. Then add one wrinkle that forces the location-of-meaning question into the open — for instance, a second string engineered to look richly structured but constructed so that it has no inner message — and tell me how to run the comparison without tipping it off in advance. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and which feature of Hofstadter's picture (the jukebox analogy, DNA as a message, the role of a sender's intentions) the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply by lecture.
