I've been reading about Daniel Dennett's proposal that the self is a "center of narrative gravity" — a fiction the brain generates, much as a novelist generates a protagonist, rather than a thing that sits inside the head receiving experience. If I take this seriously, though, it seems to follow that nobody is literally home: there is only a character in a story the brain tells itself, and characters in stories don't really suffer, decide, or act. Two consequences trouble me, and I'd like to know whether Dennett's view can absorb them or whether they constitute a reductio. First, on what basis could I be responsible for what "my" character did last year? An author isn't blameworthy for her villain's crimes, so why should a brain be blameworthy for the deeds it narrates — and if the answer is that the brain causes them, hasn't the fiction been doing none of the explanatory work all along? Second, if my future suffering is something that will befall only a fictional character, do I have any reason to care about it now, beyond the reason a reader has to care what happens on the next page? Please explain how the view is supposed to handle these problems, or, if you think it cannot, where exactly the account collapses.
