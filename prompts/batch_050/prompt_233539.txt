I'm in a reading group that's been working through the free will literature, and last week I tried to convince a hard-determinist friend that Frankfurt-style cases basically settle the debate in favor of compatibilism. It didn't go well -- she came back with a version of my own argument that seems to prove way more than I wanted, and I can't find the bad step. Here it is. (1) Take the standard setup: Jones decides on his own to shoot Smith and does it, while Black sits in the wings with a device that would have forced the decision had Jones wavered. The device never activates. Jones is clearly responsible. (2) So responsibility doesn't require alternative possibilities; it depends entirely on the actual sequence -- Jones's own reasons producing his own decision producing his act. (3) A device that never does anything plays no causal role in the sequence, and something with no causal role can't be what responsibility turns on. (4) Now vary the case: instead of a dormant device, suppose a neuroscientist actually implanted Jones's reasons months ago, programming exactly the deliberation and decision that occurred -- but from the inside the actual sequence is identical: same reasons, same decision, same shot, no felt compulsion. By (2) and (3), the only thing that distinguishes this case from the original is causal history, which by hypothesis isn't part of the actual sequence, so manipulated Jones is responsible too. (5) Generalizing: any excuse that works by pointing at the source of the actual sequence (manipulation, indoctrination, determinism itself) is ruled out by the actual-sequence principle. (6) But that's absurd -- the implantation case is the paradigm of someone who isn't responsible; if anything excuses, that does.

So: where does this go wrong, if it does? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) -- the ordinary Frankfurt intuition feels close to unshakeable -- and I'd be most willing to sacrifice (4), but I want to know what rejecting each step actually costs me, especially whether denying (4) forces me to say that causal history matters to responsibility in a way that smuggles alternative possibilities back in through the side door. And please be upfront about the weak points in your own diagnosis: which premise you think is doing the hidden work, what argument or consideration would change your mind and push you toward a different step, and where the literature-ish consensus (such as it is) would say you've gone wrong.
