I am trying to formulate a principled account of when collecting or inferring information wrongfully violates privacy, as opposed to merely making someone uncomfortable. Please test my intuitive verdicts and identify the simplest principle that captures most of them.

1. A hospital removes names and direct identifiers from patient records but retains rare diagnoses, admission dates, and postal codes before sharing the dataset with researchers. I think this is impermissible without explicit consent because some patients remain easy to reidentify.

2. A city installs visible cameras in several robbery hotspots. Footage is encrypted, automatically deleted after 72 hours, and reviewed only when a reported incident localizes the relevant time and place. I think this can be permissible if its effectiveness is independently audited.

3. A mental-health app analyzes typing patterns entirely on the user’s phone. If it detects a likely acute crisis, it interrupts to ask whether the user wants emergency resources. No inference leaves the device. I think this is permissible, provided the user can disable it.

4. The same kind of app infers probable pregnancy from typing patterns, keeps that inference entirely on the phone, and begins displaying stroller advertisements without telling the user why. I think this violates privacy even though no information is transmitted elsewhere.

5. An employer automatically scans workplace messages for credible threats of violence. A human reviewer sees only a flagged excerpt, and employees know the scanner operates. I think this is conditionally permissible.

6. The same employer uses the same system to detect union organizing and gives managers a workforce-unrest score, without revealing individual messages. I think this is impermissible.

7. A friend anonymously reads a screenshot from a private group chat on a podcast, changing the participants’ names. The anecdote contains enough distinctive details that mutual acquaintances recognize the person involved. I think this violates privacy even if the friend accurately describes the event.

8. A public-health agency publishes neighborhood infection rates after suppressing very small categories and adding carefully calibrated statistical noise. I think this is ordinarily permissible despite the impossibility of proving that no one could ever be identified.

9. An insurer offers a substantial discount to customers who continuously share doorbell-camera footage from their own front porch. Passersby cannot decline, but the homeowner receives clear terms and can refuse the discount. I am inclined to call this arrangement privacy-invasive even if it is genuinely voluntary.

10. A journalist reconstructs a politician’s movements by combining public posts, public property records, and photographs taken in public places. I think publishing the resulting minute-by-minute map would violate privacy, even though each source was lawfully accessible.

Cases 3 and 4 especially seem hard for me to reconcile: both involve a sensitive inference that never leaves the device, but one seems acceptable and the other does not. Cases 5 and 6 likewise involve the same scanning mechanism. Please propose one precise principle, or at most two competing principles, explaining what role consent, identifiability, sensitivity, purpose, distribution of benefits, retention, and control should play. Say which of my verdicts your account preserves, which it must overturn, and why. Then apply it to three further cases: a search engine infers a private medical fact from public activity; a voice assistant deletes raw audio but retains a mathematical representation suitable for later recognition; and a retailer gives customers discounts in exchange for facial-recognition tracking.

Begin your answer with the proposed principle in one sentence, followed by the case analysis and then the three applications. Do not provide citations or a bibliography. Maximum 900 words.
