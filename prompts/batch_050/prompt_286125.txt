I am the organizer of a two-day interdisciplinary workshop titled "Do Language Models Play the Game of Giving and Asking for Reasons?", and I will deliver the opening remarks — roughly ten minutes, so about 1,200 spoken words. The room is split evenly down the middle. One half consists of philosophers of language who work in or around Brandom's inferentialist program; they know the vocabulary of commitments, entitlements, and scorekeeping cold, and they will wince audibly at any sloppy use of words like "knows," "believes," or "means." The other half consists of machine-learning researchers who build and evaluate large language models; several of them regard the entire inferentialist framework as an elaborate way of refusing to say what it would take to settle the question, and at least two are waiting to object that a system's internal activations are exactly where meaning lives, not in any social practice. I am personally sympathetic to inferentialism, but as chair I must not be seen to have decided the workshop's question in advance.

Write the remarks. The philosophical framework has to do real structural work, not decorative work, and in particular the speech must:

- Build the entire address around one running example that both audiences can follow without specialist background: a customer-service chatbot that says "I apologize for the inconvenience; I've issued your refund, which will arrive in three to five business days" — where the refund genuinely does arrive, every time, processed automatically. The inferentialist half of the room will immediately see why this case is loaded (an apology and a promise are moves that normally undertake commitments and make the speaker liable to sanction; here something is reliably delivered downstream, but is anything undertaken?). The remarks should use this case to pose the workshop's central question in inferentialist terms — is the system a scorekeeper, a score-keeper's instrument, or merely a very reliable vending machine for sentences? — without answering it.
- Translate, on the fly, the vocabulary each side will naturally use into terms the other can accept as non-question-begging. When the remarks mention what the engineers would call the model "representing" a refund policy, that must be recast in the language of inferential roles and reliably differential responsiveness, and vice versa — the audience should watch the translation happen and understand that the workshop succeeds or fails on whether such translations preserve or lose what matters.
- State, fairly and without endorsing it, the most natural Brandomian verdict — that meaning requires undertaking commitments and being held to them within a practice of mutual scorekeeping, and that no current system does this — and then state, equally fairly, the strongest available inferentialist-friendly response on the models' behalf, so that the philosophers hear their view taken seriously and the engineers hear that it does not simply settle things by definitional fiat.
- Close by giving the workshop a single question it must answer before it disperses, phrased in inferentialist vocabulary but answerable, in principle, by either side's methods.

One firm constraint: do not quote Brandom, Sellars, Wittgenstein, or anyone else verbatim, and do not attribute specific claims to specific texts; the remarks should speak the vocabulary fluently without name-dropping scaffolding. No citations, no reading list — this is a speech. The tone should be that of a host who is allowed one moment of dry wit early on to relax the room, and who is thereafter precise, neutral, and visibly in command of the question rather than of an answer.
