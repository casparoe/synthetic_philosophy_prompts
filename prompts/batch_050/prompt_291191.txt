I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm preparing a feature on why the debate over AI alignment so often degenerates into experts talking past one another. My working hypothesis is that part of the confusion is taxonomic: some researchers speak of "the alignment problem" as if it were a single, crisply stateable technical difficulty, while others insist it is an umbrella term covering several loosely related problems that differ in their threat models, their solution concepts, and even the disciplines needed to address them. Before I write the piece, I want to stress-test both readings by seeing them argued at full strength against each other.

Please write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors: one who defends the view that the alignment problem is fundamentally one problem — roughly, that sufficiently capable AI systems will not reliably pursue what their designers or users intend, and that this difficulty has a unified core — and one who defends the view that it is several distinct problems (for instance, specifying the right objectives, ensuring learned behavior actually tracks those objectives under distributional shift, and whatever human-side questions about whose intentions should count) that have been misleadingly bundled under one label. Both characters should be genuinely sharp; neither should be a strawman, and each must respond to the other's actual arguments rather than recite talking points. Have them engage at least these cruxes: whether the distinction between outer and inner alignment (including the possibility of deceptively aligned mesa-optimizers) shows that "one problem" talk hides a real split or instead merely subdivides a single underlying difficulty; whether the superintelligence-style argument for a unified problem depends on contestable assumptions about future AI architectures; and whether treating alignment as one problem versus many has practical consequences — for research funding, for governance, and for how seriously different failure modes get taken.

Two constraints on how the dialogue handles the underlying facts. First, keep the epistemic status of claims straight: where something is genuinely contested among informed researchers — say, whether deceptive alignment is a serious default risk or a speculative scenario — the characters should mark it as contested rather than assert it as settled, and where something is broadly agreed, they may treat it as shared ground. Neither character's overall position should be presented as the consensus view. Second, keep it tight: the dialogue itself must be no more than 800 words, followed by a brief neutral note of at most 100 words identifying where, in your judgment, the genuine unresolved disagreement between the two positions lies — so I can see the crux without you adjudicating it.
