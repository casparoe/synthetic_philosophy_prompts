I share a house with three other recent graduates, and our kitchen table has become a running seminar on distributive justice since two of us started working and one did not. Last week we were arguing about whether our city is justified in levying a surcharge on long-vacant investment properties to fund social housing, and my housemate ended the discussion by saying that the whole debate was settled by Nozick: from each as they choose, to each as they are chosen. When I pressed him, he conceded he had never actually read Anarchy, State, and Utopia and knew the line only as a slogan. I have since read the relevant chapter, including the Wilt Chamberlain example, and I found the passage where Nozick states his core principle far more carefully than the shorthand suggests: "a distribution is just if everyone is entitled to the holdings they possess under the distribution." That sentence seems to me to do much less work than the slogan does in casual use, since it is silent about how entitlements arise and merely shifts the question to the principles of acquisition and transfer, and I suspect the catchy version smuggles in a great deal that the careful version leaves open.

What I would like is a disambiguation I can bring back to the kitchen table. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that the one-liner could be expressing: for instance, a merely descriptive claim about what distributions emerge when people transact freely; a normative claim that any distribution reached by voluntary steps from a just starting point is thereby just; a conceptual claim that entitlement rather than conformity to a pattern is the right currency of justice; an empirical claim that liberty inevitably upsets patterns, so that maintaining any preferred pattern requires continuous interference; a polemical claim that redistributive taxation is morally on a par with theft; or perhaps no propositional claim at all but a piece of rhetoric. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or something actually false, and be explicit about what "choose" and "chosen" would have to mean on that reading, since the passive construction seems to hide precisely who is doing the choosing and under what constraints.

Most importantly, tell me which readings would actually support the use my housemate made of the slogan, namely ending an argument about a specific tax policy, and which would not. In particular, does any plausible reading follow from the quoted passage alone, or does the move from the passage to the policy conclusion require additional premises, such as a theory of just initial acquisition or a view about the rectification of past injustice, that the slogan lets its user skip defending? I would also welcome your verdict on whether the shorthand is a fair compression of Nozick's own position or a distortion of it, and if the latter, whether the distortion is doing argumentative work that the more careful statement could not survive.
