I'm a novelist — quiet domestic stuff, mostly — and at a writing retreat last month another writer told me my manuscript was too cozy, and then dropped the line that good art should comfort the disturbed and disturb the comfortable. It was obviously meant as a knockout argument for why I should write something bolder and more political, and honestly it kind of worked, which is why I'm annoyed. What is that slogan actually claiming, though? Is it a definition of art, a rule about who art is for, a prediction about what great art tends to do, a moral duty on the artist, or just a vibe dressed up as a principle? Lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, from the trivial (some good art comforts some people, sure) to the genuinely radical (a work that disturbs nobody has failed as art, or comfort and disturbance are the only axes of artistic value that matter), and say which of those readings are defensible and which are obviously false. Most importantly: which readings, if any, would actually justify her telling me to scrap the quiet novel? I'd rather have a menu I can argue with than a lecture.
