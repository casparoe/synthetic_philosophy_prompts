I teach a mixed "Biology and Society" course at a community college -- about 24 students, mostly non-majors, one 75-minute session. The topic has stopped being abstract for us: at a recent school board meeting in our district, a speaker argued against expanding the free breakfast program on the grounds that traits like height and cognitive test scores are "highly heritable," which he took to mean they're fixed by genes and therefore beyond the reach of anything a school can do. Several of my students were in the audience and came to class genuinely unsure whether he had a point -- they'd heard "heritability" used the same way in online debates about intelligence and didn't have the tools to say where the inference goes wrong, or whether it even does. I want them to feel the answer rather than be told it.

Design an in-class activity in which the students themselves generate a small population of "organisms" (plants, lab mice, imaginary creatures -- whatever works) from a simple set of rules you specify -- something like drawing from prepared decks of genotype cards and environment cards -- and then compute, in groups, how much of the variation in their population traces to which source. The crucial design problem: the activity must be staged so that the students first construct a population in which the trait comes out as highly heritable by the correct variance-partitioning definition, and are then confronted -- by a rule change or a second population you introduce -- with two facts that flatly contradict the school board speaker's conclusion: first, that a trait with near-total heritability in their population can still be shifted dramatically by a uniform environmental change; second, that two populations can each show high internal heritability while the difference *between* them is entirely environmental in origin. I want the students to reach these results from their own numbers before I ever put the terms on the board, so please structure the timing accordingly: no lecture up front, and the phrase "heritability" should not appear until the debrief.

Include: the exact card decks or generation rules and why they're calibrated the way they are; a minute-by-minute timeline; the worksheets or recording scheme students use; the debrief script that maps specific moments of the session onto the correct interpretation of heritability (population-relative, environment-indexed, silent about causes of group differences, and compatible with large treatment effects); what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this -- for instance, students smuggling the everyday sense of "inherited and unchangeable" into the statistical term, or groups miscounting shared environments -- and how to handle each; and an honest closing note on which features of real heritability estimation (say, the assumptions of twin studies, or genotype-environment correlation) a card-game simulation cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply by other means.
