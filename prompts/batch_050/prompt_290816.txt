I'm building a personal taxonomy of responses to the lottery paradox, mostly so I stop re-deriving the same three moves every time it comes up in some thread. Here's what's already on my list:

1. Lockeanism + dropping agglomeration (Foley-style): rational belief just is credence above some threshold, and the paradox teaches us that rational beliefs needn't be closed under conjunction. Bullet bitten, paradox dissolved.
2. The degres-of-belief dissolution: the binary category of 'outright belief' is the actual problem; in lottery-type situations rationality only demands credences, so once you give up the binary there's nothing left to be paradoxical.
3. Accuracy-first arguments: even granting that outright belief is a legitimate category, expected-accuracy considerations say you should believe nothing (or almost nothing) about the individual tickets, since each such belief is way more likely to be false than your high credence suggests — so epistemic utility itself tells you to stay silent.
4. The 'it's about knowledge, not belief' move: everyone agrees you don't *know* ticket 7 will lose, and safety- or margin-for-error-type accounts explain exactly why; once you keep knowledge and rational belief cleanly apart, the paradox deflates into a familiar point about knowledge.

Give me one more response that isn't on the list and isn't a rewording, special case, or mashup of 1-4. Ideally it should also say something about the preface paradox — whether the same move works there, or if not, why the two paradoxes genuinely come apart (my gut says the preface case is doing different work but I can't articulate why). Then be honest about whether your candidate is actually any good or just novel. And since people on my feed talk about this stuff with way more confidence than seems warranted: flag what's basically settled here versus what's still live disagreement among epistemologists, and don't dress up one camp's position as the consensus.
