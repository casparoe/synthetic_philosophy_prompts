Next month I'm presenting a paper to a formal epistemology seminar defending this thesis: an agent whose evidence is structurally incomplete — thin, of unknown reliability, or bearing on the hypothesis only indirectly — is rationally required, not merely permitted, to hold an imprecise credence representable as a set of probability functions, and the dilation phenomenon, where acquiring new evidence widens the set rather than narrowing it, is the correct response to evidence of unknown quality rather than a reductio of the framework. The audience is about ten people: two committed subjective Bayesians who treat precision as constitutive of rationality, three formal epistemologists working on Bayesian confirmation who are open-minded, one statistician fluent in the robust-Bayes tradition, and several graduate students. Prepare my Q&A. Give me the objections in roughly the order I'll actually get them, phrased the way these particular listeners would phrase them — I expect the inertia worry (once imprecise, no amount of evidence re-sharpens you), the decision-theoretic challenge (a single precise credence tells you how to act; a set does not, so which choice rule am I smuggling in — maximin, Γ-maximin, admissibility — and can it be motivated independently?), and the charge that I've built the verdict in by hand by calling the evidence "of unknown reliability." For each objection: what I should concede up front, what I should hold, a two-minute reply, and where the reply is weakest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Throughout, mark clearly which premises of mine rest on results that are genuinely settled in this literature and which rest on claims still contested among people who work on imprecise probability, so that I don't present a minority position as the consensus.
