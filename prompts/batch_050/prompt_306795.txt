Alright, some background first: I retired three years ago after a long career as a research chemist (mostly spectroscopy, if that matters) and I've been falling down the philosophy of mind rabbit hole ever since. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher of mind who knows the consciousness literature cold. Take your time with this, think carefully, and maybe brainstorm the candidate weak points in my argument before you commit to a verdict — I want a diagnosis, not a summary of the debate.

I recently read a piece by Daniel Dennett attacking the idea of qualia, built around two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn. Both used to love the company's coffee and now neither can stand it, but they explain it differently: one says the coffee tastes exactly the same to him as it always did, he's just lost his appreciation for that flavor; the other says his standards and appreciation haven't budged — it's the flavor itself, how the coffee tastes to him, that has changed. Dennett's point, as I understood it, is that nothing could settle which of them is right — not their own introspection, not brain scans, not any future science — and from this he concludes the traditional notion of qualia (private, ineffable, intrinsic "feels") is somehow broken.

Fine. But I tried to write his argument out properly, in the numbered-premises style you people like, and when I do it seems to prove way too much. Here is my reconstruction:

1. If two hypotheses cannot be told apart by any possible evidence — not by the person involved, not by any outsider, not by any future science — then there is no real difference between them; they're the same hypothesis wearing different hats.
2. In the coffee case, "his qualia changed" and "his qualia stayed fixed while his reactions and preferences changed" cannot be told apart by any possible evidence, not even by the taster himself.
3. So there is no fact of the matter about whether his qualia changed — and if there are no facts about qualia beyond what's accessible, then qualia as traditionally conceived don't exist.
4. But premise 1 doesn't just apply to coffee. Consider "the universe came into existence five minutes ago, complete with fossils, light already in transit, and all my memories" versus "the universe is billions of years old." No possible evidence distinguishes these.
5. Likewise "my colleagues are conscious" versus "my colleagues are flawless zombies." No possible evidence distinguishes these either.
6. So by premise 1, there is no fact of the matter about the age of the universe, and no fact of the matter about whether anyone but me is conscious.
7. But 6 is absurd. I spent four decades measuring things like the age of rocks, and I'm not throwing that out because of a thought experiment about coffe.
8. So the argument must go wrong somewhere — the same machinery can't be allowed to dissolve qualia and geology in one go.

A few things about where I stand. I'm quite attached to premise 1 in some form — as a working scientist, "no possible evidence, no real difference" feels less like a premise and more like the job description. Premises 4 and 5 also seem obviously right to me; that's just what underdetermination is. So I suspect the trouble is in 2, or in the move from 2 to 3, but I can't put my finger on it.

Third, give me an actual verdict at the end — which premise should I give up, or which bullet should I bite — rather than a menu of options. And if you think my reconstruction misrepresents the qualia-skeptic's argument from the start, say so plainly and show me the better version before answering.
