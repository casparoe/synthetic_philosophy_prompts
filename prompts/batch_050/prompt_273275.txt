I am grading an undergraduate philosophy of physics assignment and would like a careful, point-by-point evaluation of the following student response.

Assignment question, worth 20 points:

Consider a simplified Everettian model of measurement. A spin-1/2 particle is prepared so that a z-spin measurement yields the amplitude √3/2 for up and 1/2 for down. During measurement, the particle, detector, and observer become entangled, and decoherence produces effectively noninteracting up and down branches.

Define weight-sensitive rationality as the following decision rule: after decoherence, an agent should evaluate acts by expected value, using the squared moduli of the branch amplitudes as the weights, rather than by counting branches, observers, or records.

Before the measurement, the agent is offered a ticket that pays $12 if the outcome is up and nothing if it is down. Answer all four parts:

1. State the Born-rule problem as it arises in this Everettian setting. In particular, distinguish the claim that both outcomes occur from the question of what should govern pre-measurement uncertainty or rational action.
2. Assuming risk neutrality and no transaction costs, calculate the maximum price the agent should pay under the stipulated rule. What further premise, if any, would be needed to move from this rule for rational action to the claim that the weights also represent the agent’s degrees of belief?
3. Suppose that, if the down outcome occurs, the apparatus automatically produces 1,000 classically correlated copies of the down record. These are additional records within the down branch, not 1,000 independently weighted branches, and the process leaves the original amplitudes unchanged. Should this affect the rational price? Distinguish record multiplicity, branch multiplicity, and branch weight.
4. Identify one respect in which this toy case understates the difficulty of justifying the Born rule in full quantum theory.

Student response:

In an Everettian measurement, the spin, detector, and observer evolve unitarily into an entangled state. Decoherence makes the up and down branches effectively noninteracting, so neither outcome is eliminated by collapse. The Born-rule problem is therefore not the problem of explaining why the unobserved outcome disappears, since it does not disappear. It is the problem of explaining why the squared amplitudes should govern an agent’s expectations and choices, rather than some alternative such as assigning equal weight to every branch.

The expected payoff is 3/4 × $12 + 1/4 × $0 = $9. Thus a risk-neutral agent should pay at most $9. Because weight-sensitive rationality has been stipulated in the question, no further premise is needed to say that the agent’s uncertainty is 3/4 for up and 1/4 for down: the same numbers that guide rational action are thereby also the agent’s degrees of belief.

The 1,000 copies of the down record should not change the price. Under the stipulated assumptions, they are correlated records associated with one down outcome, not 1,000 additional branches with independent weights. A branch-counting rule might treat the multiplication of records as changing the odds, but a weight-sensitive rule continues to assign weights 3/4 and 1/4. This illustrates the difference between the number of records or observers and the weights supplied by the quantum state.

The toy case assumes a clean two-outcome measurement, exact decoherence, and a risk-neutral agent, so it stipulates matters that would otherwise require argument. In full quantum theory, however, decoherence itself supplies an exact preferred basis and divides the state into wholly noninteracting branches. The remaining Born-rule problem is therefore only the decision-theoretic one of explaining why agents should care about branch weights.
