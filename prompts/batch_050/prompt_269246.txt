For a high-school philosophy club project, I am trying to determine what distinguishes phenomenal consciousness from mere access to information. My case judgments are:

1. During an absorbing conversation, a driver brakes at red lights and changes lanes safely but later remembers nothing and denies having seen the road. I judge the visual states unconscious.
2. While talking, I do not notice a refrigerator hum, yet when it stops I immediately recognize that it had been sounding and feel that I was hearing it all along. I judge it phenomenally conscious but inaccessible at the time.
3. While attending to the center of a screen, I cannot report peripheral letters, but my visual field seems filled with vague shapes and colors rather than blank. I judge the general peripheral character conscious even if its details are inaccessible.
4. A blindsight patient reliably guesses a stimulus’s location while sincerely denying any visual experience. I judge the information unconscious.
5. A standing belief can guide my reasoning without being consciously entertained. I judge it access-conscious but not phenomenally conscious.
6. In a split-brain case, an image presented to the non-speaking hemisphere guides a hand response while the speaking hemisphere denies seeing anything. I am inclined to posit an experience available to one subsystem rather than no experience at all.

My tentative principle is that task-specific processing is unconscious; sustained integration into a sensory field is sufficient for phenomenal consciousness; and broadcast to reasoning, memory, reporting, and flexible action produces access-consciousness. Does some precise version of this principle preserve all six verdicts, especially the difference between cases 1 and 2? If not, which verdict should be abandoned? Please also explain how the resulting account modifies global workspace theory and what it predicts about a conscious person temporarily unable to speak and a nonhuman animal with no language but flexible planning. State your bottom-line principle in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble or restatement of the question.
