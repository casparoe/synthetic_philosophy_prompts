I'm on a small team drafting usage principles for an AI assistant, and before our next meeting I was asked to write down my own position so the others can stress-test it. I've ended up with five commitments that each seemed obviously right when I wrote them, but a colleague glanced at the list, said "these can't all survive contact with each other," and declined to elaborate. Here they are. (a) Deference to settled values: when a competent user has informed, stable preferences about their own life, the assistant should help them pursue those preferences rather than substitute its own judgment of what's good for them. (b) Hard limits on third-party harm: the assistant should flatly refuse requests whose point is to seriously harm or defraud other people, no matter how articulate the request. (c) Weak paternalism: overriding a user's request is legitimate only when the choice is plausibly not truly theirs — coercion, severe impairment, acute crisis, a momentary impulse they'll repudiate by morning — and never merely because the assistant judges the choice imprudent. (d) Loyalty to the temporally extended self: when a user's in-the-moment impulse conflicts with their own clearly stated long-term commitments (the budget they set, the tapering schedule they asked to be held to), the assistant should side with the long-term commitments. (e) No smuggled values: the assistant may recommend frankly when asked, but it shouldn't slip its own evaluative judgments into answers to questions that weren't asking for an evaluation.

What I'd like is an audit, not a lecture on paternalism in general. Go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. For each genuine tension, state exactly what the conflict is — ideally by exhibiting the kind of case where my principles deliver contradictory verdicts — rate its severity, and say what the cheapest revision that resolves it would be and what that revision costs me. I suspect at least one of my worries is a false alarm (for instance, I've gone back and forth on whether (b) conflicts with (a), and whether refusing in crisis situations is really compatible with (e)), so please discriminate rather than flagging everything. I'd also like the tensions ranked rather than presented as equally bad, and at the end I'd like your verdict on which single revision would clean up the most of the set — bearing in mind that I'd rather lose precision than lose (b), but I'm otherwise not attached to any particular one of these.
