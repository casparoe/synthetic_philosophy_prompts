So I'm giving the retirement toast for my boss of eleven years next month, and I've talked myself into a corner and need help getting out. Background: he founded our (mid-size, quant-ish) trading firm, he's stepping down at 67, and the man is a genuine, card-carrying Machiavelli obsessive — multiple annotated copies of the Prince and the Discourses in his office, the two conference rooms named after Cesare Borgia and, more worryingly, Piero Soderini. His favorite topic, the thing he'll go on about for forty minutes after two drinks, is fortuna and virtù. His official self-mythology is that the firm is a monument to virtù: discipline, boldness at the right moments, building your dikes and embankments before the river floods rather than after.

Here's the corner. Last year, over dinner, slightly drunk, he told me that the honest Machiavellian reading of his own career is that he got lucky: his particular temperment (patient, paranoid, allergic to leverage) just happened to fit the decades he was dealt, the way Fabius's caution fit Hannibal's invasion and Scipio's boldness fit the situation after, and the Machiavellian tragedy is that nobody can actually change their nature when the times change, so the best you can do is quit before the wheel turns. Which, he said, is exactly why he's retiring at what everyone thinks is the top. So the man is publicly commited to (a) his success being earned and (b) his success being luck, and he's sophisticated enough to hold both at once and to immediately notice if my toast only honors one of them. I've read rationalist blogs long enough that my instinct is to carefully steelman both horns of the dilemma, but that makes for an absolutely terrible toast. The room is maybe 120 people: half traders and PMs who've absorbed the office Machiavelli stuff secondhand and will enjoy any reference they catch, a quarter industry people, and the rest his wife, kids, and old friends who know him as a sweet workaholic. I get about 6-8 minutes, so call it 900-1100 words.

Act as a professional speechwriter who also has a philosophy PhD specializing in Renaissance political thought. First, brainstorm in bullet points 4-5 possible structural approaches for threading this needle and tell me which you'd pick and why. Then write the speech. Hard requirements: it must land as genuine affection and admiration, not a lecture and definately not a hit piece; the fortuna/virtù problem has to be the actual spine of the speech, not a decorative name-drop; and the resolution has to be one he'd respect — don't just declare it was all skill (he'd register that as flattery, i.e. false) or all luck (insulting, and he'd also consider it false). Paraphrase Machiavelli rather than quoting him. Get the Fabius/Scipio point right, because it's his favorite and he will be listening for whether I understood it. One joke about the conference room names would be welcome.
