I'm taking a course on practical rationality and we've just covered the sunk cost fallacy, but I keep finding my own case judgments don't line up with the textbook treatment, and I want to know whether they add up to a coherent principle. Here are my verdicts:

1. Staying in a cinema to finish a film you're not enjoying, purely because the ticket cost fifteen pounds, seems clearly irrational to me.

2. An investor who refuses to sell a declining stock because selling would "realize the loss," when she'd never buy the stock at its current price, also seems clearly irrational.

3. But a novelist who has written two hundred pages of a draft and declines to abandon it for a genuinely better idea seems to me to be reasoning perfectly well, even if the better idea, judged on its prospects alone, would win.

4. Similarly, someone who sticks with a difficult PhD project in year four partly on the grounds that quitting would waste the previous years strikes me as at least potentially rational, not obviously confused.

5. Yet when someone says we must continue a war so that those who already died "shall not have died in vain," I find the reasoning hollow and dangerous.

6. Finally, a person who deliberately buys a non-refundable annual gym membership because she knows the money is already spent and the membership will thereby get her to exercise seems to me to be *exploiting* sunk cost reasoning rather than falling for it.

Is there a single principle about when past investment may legitimately enter deliberation that delivers all six verdicts? My own guess is that cases 3 and 4 are fine because the past investment has changed what completing the project is worth (the finished object now partially exists, or quitting carries a distinctive cost), whereas cases 1, 2, and 5 involve only the felt pressure of the expenditure itself — but I'm not sure that distinction survives case 6, where the expenditure itself is doing the work, just deliberately. If my principle needs repair, repair it; if no principle saves all six, tell me which verdict you'd overturn and why. And say what the best candidate principle predicts for a case I haven't listed: a government deciding whether to complete a half-built infrastructure project whose projected benefits no longer justify its remaining costs.
