Exam question: John Taurek argues that when you can save either one stranger or five strangers, but not all six, you are not required to save the five. Explain his argument for this claim and the method of choosing he recommends. Do you think his position does justice to the idea that each person's life matters equally? Defend your answer.

Student's answer: Taurek challenges the common assumption that we ought to save the greater number. His core move is to deny that the five deaths would be a worse outcome than the one death. Badness, he argues, attaches to what happens to particular persons: each of the five would suffer the loss of her own life, but no one suffers the aggregate of all five losses, so there is no single perspective from which the five deaths are five times as bad. Since outcomes cannot be ranked as better or worse in this impersonal way, the numbers by themselves cannot tell us what to do. Taurek therefore recommends a lottery weighted by group size: the group of five gets five tickets to the lone person's one, so that each individual has an equal chance of survival. He concedes that special obligations can override this procedure, for instance when the lone person is a friend. I think Taurek's position respects the equal importance of each person's life, because it refuses to treat individuals as mere vessels whose losses can be summed. However, his argument seems to prove too much: if no one suffers the aggregate loss, then numbers can never matter, even when saving the larger group costs nothing extra, which is implausible. Overall, his view shows that respecting persons equally means giving each an equal chance, not maximizing the number saved.
