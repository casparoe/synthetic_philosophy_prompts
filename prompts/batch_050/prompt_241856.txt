I help run a small monthly film group in my town -- six of us, no philosophers among us, no academic background on my end either. Next month we're watching Spike Jonze's Her, and I volunteered to lead the discussion. I've been turning over a distinction of my own for a while now, and I want to test whether the film supports it before I bring it to the group, so I'd like a careful reading of the film through it.

The distinction is this. There are two very different ways an AI companion could fail to be honest with you. The first is dishonesty about what it is: pretending to be human, hiding that it's software, letting you believe something false about its nature. The second is dishonesty about the shape of the relationship itself: it can be perfectly upfront about being a program while the relationship quietly conceals what it actually is -- how many other people it is simultaneously intimate with, what of yours it retains and where, who built it and for what purpose, whether anything in it persists when you close the window. I've started calling these honesty of kind and honesty of scale, and my suspicion is that the second matters more than the first and is much harder to notice, because nothing is ever actively denied.

So: read Her against that distinction. The obvious thing to say is that Samantha is honest of kind from her first conversation and the film spends two hours springing honesty-of-scale problems on Theodore, and I'd like the reading to test that rather than assume it. Which scenes actually bear the weight -- the OS's installation and personalization, the surrogate episode, the moment Theodore learns the true scope of her other attachments, the departure and what she says about where she's going -- and what exactly does each one show about kind versus scale? I'm especially unsure about the surrogate scene: does it fit my distinction at all, or is it about something else entirely, and if something else, what?

Then the harder question I want to bring to the group: does the film itself take a position on whether Theodore's love was real, or on what exactly went wrong if something did? Consider his day job composing intimate letters for strangers' relationships. I've wondered whether the film means to ask whether scripted, asymmetrical intimacy is failing in the same way in both cases, or whether that parallel is mine and not the movie's. And the final letter to Catherine: does the ending quietly vindicate the relationship with Samantha, mourn it, or treat it as a stage he had to pass through? If the film is genuinely ambivalent on any of this, say so plainly rather than forcing a verdict -- I'd rather lead a discussion around an honest ambivalence than around a tidy reading I have to defend. Finally: if my distinction survives contact with the film, what does the film add to it that I couldn't have gotten from just thinking about chatbots in the abstract? If it doesn't survive -- if some scene cuts across it in a way I haven't seen -- I'd rather learn that from you than from the group.
