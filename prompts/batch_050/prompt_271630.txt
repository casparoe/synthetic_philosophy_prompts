Back in 2018, a Chinese scientist named He Jiankui announced that he'd used CRISPR to edit the CCR5 gene in human embryos, and that twin girls (Lulu and Nana) had been born carrying the edits. The near-universal reaction from geneticists and bioethicists was horror: the edit was medically unnecessary, the technique was sloppy, the consent process was dubious, and virtually nobody in the field thought germline editing was ready. He was later sentenced to three years in prison in China. The striking thing, though, is that nothing had to go wrong institutionally for this to happen -- the materials were standard lab equipment, and any one of probably hundreds of researchers worldwide could have done the same thing whenever they felt like it.

I want you to analyze this episode through the lens of the unilateralist's curse. For anyone rusty on the term: it's the idea that when an initiative can be undertaken by any single member of a group acting alone, and members differ in how favorably they judge it, the initiative tends to get undertaken by the most optimistic or most risk-tolerant member -- even if the median or overwhelming majority judgment is against it. The more independent decision-makers there are, the more likely the most permissive judgment gets expressed in action, so the group as a whole is biased toward acting even when "the group" in some sense opposes acting. The standard remedy suggested is for would-be unilateralists to deliberate and defer to the shared judgment, i.e. to voluntarily behave as if a collective decision procedure existed even though none does.

Here's what I want specifically. First, do the mapping carefully: who exactly is the population of potential unilateral actors here, what was the initiative, what did the distribution of judgments plausibly look like (was He a lone outlier or just the far end of a real spectrum -- some serious people were calling for cautious germline work even then), and what made the action possible without anyone else's cooperation. Second, tell me where the theory fits awkwardly. The curse is usually modeled as sincere epistemic disagreement -- everyone aiming at the same good, differing only in their estimates. Does that describe He, or does it matter that he kept his work quiet, that he announced it at a summit for maximum splash, that fame and national prestige were plausibly in the mix? If the motive was partly self-interest rather than honest optimism, is this still the curse, or something the theory doesn't cover? Also: he did apparently talk to some senior figures beforehand, a few of whom reportedly cautioned him -- does that mean the "deliberation" remedy was tried and failed, and if so does that damage the proposed solution?

Third, run it in the other direction: what does the episode show about the theory? On one hand, germline editing had been technically feasible for years before anyone did it -- moratoria, stigma, and professional norms held it off, wich looks like evidence the curse is weaker than advertised because informal norms can substitute for a veto structure. On the other hand, those norms stopped hundreds of people and failed to stop one, and one is all it takes -- which looks like the curse exactly as predicted. Which reading is right, and does it depend on how irreversible and self-propagating the act is (a baby is not like a leaked document or a software release)? Finally, if you were designing institutions for emerging dual-use tech (gain-of-function research, open-source bio, frontier AI models) and took the curse seriously, what would actually lift it -- licensing, pre-registration, mandatory review -- and which of those just relocates the unilateralism to whoever controls the loopholes?
