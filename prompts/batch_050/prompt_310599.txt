I'm a high school senior and one of the editors of our school's satire magazine. For the next issue, I'd like a piece that takes aim at the modern descendant of the Protestant work ethic: the belief that work is a moral calling, that idleness is a character flaw, and that leisure is only legitimate when it makes you better at working.

Write an internal FAQ document (roughly 600-700 words) issued by the Human Resources department of a fictional corporation, "Meridian Dynamics," that has taken this ethic to its logical conclusion. The FAQ should answer employee questions in the relentlessly warm, upbeat voice of corporate wellness-speak, covering at least the following:

- whether employees are permitted to have hobbies that develop no "transferable skills";
- how to report a coworker for "unproductive rest";
- the purpose of the company's mandatory sleep-optimization program (maximizing output, though HR must never say so plainly);
- how the company regards its French subsidiary, which keeps insisting on a 35-hour week and long lunches;
- and how employees should understand the annual memorial for a colleague who died at his desk from overwork (what Japanese workplace culture calls karoshi), which the company celebrates as its highest honor.

The humor has to come from exaggerating features that genuinely exist, not from inventing absurdities: the secular survival of the idea of work as a calling, the moral anxiety around "time theft," the instrumentalization of rest (naps, vacations, and mindfulness all justified as productivity tools), and the real phenomenon of deaths from overwork being reframed as dedication. The irony must stay completely deadpan — HR sincerely believes every word — while remaining legible: a reader should be able to tell exactly which attitudes are being satirized without any winking at the audience. The standard objections to the work ethic (that leisure has value in itself, that a culture treating overwork as virtue will grind people down) should land through what the document itself says, never by being stated outright.

Please don't break character with an author's note or any framing commentary; the piece should read as a genuine corporate document that someone has leaked to our magazine.
