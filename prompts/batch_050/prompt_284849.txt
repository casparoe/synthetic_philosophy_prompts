I have been keeping a list of cases where fiction asks me to imagine something and I find that I either can or cannot, and I want to know whether any principle unifies my reactions. Here are the cases, with my honest verdicts:

1. A fantasy novel in which the laws of physics differ wholesale — magic works, the dead return, time runs backward in certain regions. No resistance at all. I go along effortlessly.

2. A novel set in a world whose inhabitants lie constantly because, in their circumstances, lying genuinely does maximize flourishing, and the narration presents their lying as morally right, and in that world it seems to be. Some mild strain, but I can go along: the moral facts differ because the circumstances differ, and that I can accept.

3. A novel that simply asserts, in the narrator's own authoritative voice, that cruelty to children for amusement is good — not that the inhabitants believe it is good, but that it is good. Hard block. I do not feel I have failed to imagine a world; I feel the author has said something false, and my attention shifts from the story to the author.

4. A novel in which the narrator describes a character's cowardly betrayal, in full detail, and then calls it brave. Same kind of block as case 3, though slightly weaker — I keep wondering whether the narration is unreliable or ironic.

5. A work of satire in which monstrous things are praised in a deadpan voice. No resistance — I take the praise as inverted and enjoy it. Yet the literal narrative voice is doing exactly what case 4's narrator does, and there I balked.

6. A story in which, within the fiction, two plus two equals five, not as a notation change but as arithmetic fact. Total block, and here I do think the thing is strictly unimaginable, not merely refused.

7. A richly textured historical novel told from inside a slaveholding society, in which the slaveholders' worldview is rendered sympathetically and the narration never condemns them. I can inhabit this for long stretches, even though the moral deviation from my own convictions is at least as deep as in case 3. The depth of imaginative development seems to buy the author latitude that a flat assertion does not.

8. A short story consisting of a single paragraph asserting that in some far-off land, a certain real historical atrocity was in fact morally right. Strong resistance, close to case 3 — worse, in fact, because it concerns actual victims.

Two pairs here trouble me. Cases 4 and 5 seem structurally identical — an authoritative narrative voice mislabels conduct — yet one blocks me and the other does not, and the difference appears to be only that I suspect irony in one and not the other. Cases 3 and 7 deviate from my morality to the same degree, yet the well-built world gets through where the flat assertion fails. Any principle that explains one of each pair seems to misclassify the other.

What I want: a candidate principle — or a small set of candidates — stated precisely enough to deliver verdicts, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. In particular, I want to know whether my data force a distinction between what I cannot imagine and what I refuse to imagine, between what an author can make true in a fiction by stipulation and what lies beyond authorial authority, or between descriptive and evaluative content — and which of these distinctions, if any, does the real work. Please also give the principle's verdicts on cases I did not list: first-person unreliable narration, fiction that asks the reader to suspend a conviction they hold about the actual world rather than about morality in general, and whether moral beliefs formed inside a fiction can legitimately be exported out of it. Tell me honestly which of my eight verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate, and what it would have me revise. Finally, say what would change your mind: which further case, or which distinction I may have missed, would lead you to a different principle, and where in your analysis the reasoning is most likely to fail.
