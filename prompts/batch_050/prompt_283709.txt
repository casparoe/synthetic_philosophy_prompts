Bit of a weird one. I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, and I've got an argument I half believe and half want destroyed. Context: I work on a customer-support chatbot. Legal told us the bot must never lie, full stop. So naturally the team got very good at writing answers that are technically true. This actually happened: a user asks "do you keep my chats?", and the bot replies "your messages are protected with AES-256 encryption". Every word of that is true. The user reads it as "no, we don't keep them", and, well, that's the entire reason it's phrased that way.

Now the argument, which a mate on the team laid out and which I can't find the hole in:

(1) Lying is wrong because it abuses trust: you assert p, p is false, and the listener relies on your say-so.
(2) Trust attaches to what you actually assert, not to whatever the listener goes on to infer. Otherwise speakers would be responsible for every daft misreading anyone walks away with, which is clearly untenable — responsibility for an inference lies with the person drawing it.
(3) So a speaker whose every asserted sentence is literally true has honoured whatever duty truthfulness imposes; if the listener forms a false belief, that's the listener's own doing.
(4) Deliberately crafting true sentences so the listener predictably draws a false conclusion — like the encryption answer — is therefore morally fine, no matter how predictable the misimpression.
(5) But that can't be right. If a human support agent gave that answer they'd be sacked and we'd call them a liar to their face, and it honestly doesn't feel one bit better than lying.
(6) So apparently I'm committed to "misleading with strictly true statements is just as bad as lying" — and that seems to prove too much in the other direction. Is a witness in court who answers a slightly different question than the one asked really in the same moral bucket as a perjurer? Surely not.

So where does the argument go wrong, if it does? If I have to choose between keeping (2) and keeping (5), I'd rather keep (5) — I can't bring myself to defend the bot's answer — but (2) feels load-bearing for how language even works, and I'd like to know what rejecting it costs. Tell me which step you'd reject, why it looked fine in the first place, and what you're giving up by rejecting it. Bonus points if the diagnosis has any practical implications for how we should write the bot's system prompt, since right now our instructions basically encode (4).
