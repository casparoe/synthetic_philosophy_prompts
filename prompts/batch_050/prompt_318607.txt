In the spring of 2003, within weeks of the invasion of Iraq, the Coalition Provisional Authority under Paul Bremer issued two decrees that reshaped the country: one barring senior Baath Party members from public employment, and one dissolving the Iraqi army along with the security institutions of the old regime. Party membership had often been nominal — a condition of teaching school or holding a civil service post — yet the purge reached deep into the state. Hundreds of thousands of soldiers, most of them conscripts, were sent home, many initially unpaid. Within a year an organized insurgency was drawing on precisely the pool of armed, humiliated, and idle men the decrees had created, and both orders are now widely cited as catastrophic errors, though whether they were the decisive ones remains contested.

Analyze this episode through the lens of Burkean conservatism and Chesterton's fence, running the analysis carefully in both directions.

First, the mapping. State what the Burkean position actually claims — that inherited institutions embody tacit knowledge their reformers rarely possess, that standing arrangements should be presumed to serve functions invisible from outside — and identify concretely what the fences were here: what latent functions a conscript army and a mass party may have served beyond repression (employment, integration across sectarian lines, a ladder of status for young men, administrative continuity), and what the fence heuristic would have counseled instead, distinguishing the purging of an institution's leadership from the abolition of the institution itself.

Second, where the lens strains. The Baathist state was barely a generation old, imposed and maintained by terror rather than organically grown, and Burke was no enemy of every revolution — he sided with the American colonists. Does his conservatism govern the preservation of a tyranny's instruments at all, and if it does, has the theory lost its discriminating edge? What does understanding why the fence is there demand of a foreign occupier with little local knowledge and less time — and is that demand satisfiable, or does the principle in practice license paralysis in exactly the situations where it is invoked?

Third, the reverse direction. Does 2003 vindicate the fence heuristic, or is the evidence confounded by everything else the occupation did and failed to do? And can a heuristic that only ever says not so fast be confirmed or disconfirmed by any case — is Chesterton's fence a theory, or a temperament wearing one?
