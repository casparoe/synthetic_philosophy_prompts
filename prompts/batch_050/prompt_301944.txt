So my wife and I rewatched 12 Angry Men last week (the 1957 one) and I keep turning it over in my head against the Condorcet jury theorem, which I half-remember from a poli sci course years ago. The setup seems almost designed as a counterexample: eleven guilty votes, one holdout, and if each juror is even slightly more likely than a coin flip to be right and they vote independently, the majority is overwhelmingly likely to be correct -- so Fonda's character should be the noise, not the hero. But of course the whole film is him slowly flipping the room through deliberation, which is exactly the thing that breaks the independence assumption the theorem needs, since jurors who talk to each other stop being independent sensors and their errors get correlated (the baseball tickets guy, the old man wanting attention, the bigot whose reasons everyone else is embarrassed to share). Read the film through the theorem seriously: which of Condorcet's actual assumptions does the film dramatize failing -- competence, independence, sincerity -- and which scenes map onto which failure? Also, does the film cheat by making the defendant actually innocent (as far as we can tell), since the theorem says nothing about any single case, only about frequencies, so one holdout being right proves nothing? And there's the wrinkle that real juries need unanimity, not a majority, which the theorem as I learned it doesn't cover -- does that make the film a better or worse illustration? Where I'm genuinely unsure, and where I'd like you to be careful: I gather it's settled what the theorem mathematically says and what its assumptions are, but contested whether deliberation helps or hurts real-world group accuracy (some people argue sharing information improves competence even as it wrecks independence, others that cascades and conformity make groups dumber).
