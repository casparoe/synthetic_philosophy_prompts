I've been thinking about something I guess you could call "reasons without reasoners" -- when it's okay to say a system did something *for a reason* even though nobody sat down and reasoned about it. I keep applying one rule in some cases and another rule in others, and I want to know what principle, if any, I'm actually using. Here are my gut verdicts:

A chess engine sacs a knight and I say "it gave up the piece in order to open the f-file" -- totally fine, that's a real explanation even though nothing in the code contains that sentence. The peacock's tail is large *in order to* attract mates -- also fine, even though no peacock and no designer ever aimed at that. A company raises prices "because it wants to protect its margins" -- fine, even if no single employee, including the CEO, personally had that as their reason (the pricing team was following a formula, the execs were reacting to the board, etc.). But: a guy in a psych study gets primed with elderly-related words and walks slower down the hall, and afterwards I say the priming explains his behavior but it was NOT his reason for walking slowly -- that one I feel strongly about. Similarly, a guy betrays his friend out of jealousy he won't admit to, and later gives this beautifully principled justification that *happens* to be what a perfectly rational person in his shoes would have done -- I'd say the justification is still not his reason, even though it's a good reason and even though he believes it is. And a thermostat "thinking" the room is too cold -- that's just a manner of speaking, not a real rationale, even though it's useful shorthand.

So the ones that bug me: the company and the thermostat both lack any individual reasoner, yet I count one and not the other. And the peacock and the primed walker both involve no deliberation at all, yet I say one has a rationale "in order to" and the other only has a cause. Is there one principle that delivers all of these verdicts, or do I have to give one up? If it helps, I suspect the answer has something to do with whether the system would *correct* itself -- like whether there's some feedback loop that treats the outcome as a goal -- but I can't get that to cleanly separate the company from the thermostat either, so maybe my suspicion is wrong. Also tell me what your best principle says about the case I keep flip-flopping on: a big AI model that produces outputs you can predict really well by attributing it the goal of seeming helpful and harmless, even though no one ever wrote that goal down anywhere and (let's suppose) nothing inside it explicitly represents it. Does it have that reason, or are we just in thermostat territory with better PR?

One more thing: when you answer, please be clear about what's basically settled in this area (if anything) versus what's genuinely contested among philosophers who work on this stuff -- I don't want a contested position presented to me as the obvious consensus view.
