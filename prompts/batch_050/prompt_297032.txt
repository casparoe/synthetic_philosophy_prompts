I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy. I am trying to understand complicity in cases where a person knowingly participates in a wrongful project but has deliberately been made replaceable. Please write a dialogue in British English between two fictional philosophers, Mara and Soren, about the following case.

A cloud company is building a surveillance system for a state agency that will use it to identify and detain peaceful labour organisers. To ensure that individual refusals cannot delay the project, management places three isolated engineering teams on a hot-standby rota. Each team can build the entire component. If one team refuses, another immediately takes over without changing the delivery date, cost, or capability. Priya, an engineer on the active team, knows all this and writes a routing module that is incorporated into the deployed system. Had she refused, her counterpart on the standby team would immediately have supplied an equivalent module. Daniel, the standby engineer, is never called upon, but knows the system’s purpose and remains available to replace anyone who refuses. Refusal would put Priya’s and Daniel’s jobs and residence visas at serious risk.

Mara defends the marginal-difference view: a person is complicit only when their conduct makes a wrongful outcome more likely, faster, larger, cheaper, or harder to prevent. She argues that redundancy may show the organisation to be wicked, but cannot make Priya’s or Daniel’s participation morally significant when the same outcome would have occurred without them. Soren defends the participatory view: knowingly supplying a real input to a wrongful joint enterprise, or willingly holding oneself ready to supply one, can constitute complicity even when the enterprise is designed to survive any individual refusal.

Write 900–1,200 words. Both characters should be equally competent and should respond to each other’s actual arguments rather than merely rehearse opening statements. They should address:

- the distinction between causally contributing to an outcome and making a counterfactual difference to it;
- whether replaceability affects wrongdoing, responsibility, or both;
- whether Daniel’s standby status differs morally from Priya’s actual contribution;
- whether the risk to their jobs and visas excuses participation without eliminating complicity;
- whether duties to resign, protest, or whistleblow are separate from complicity;
- whether an organisation’s responsibility can exceed the sum of the responsibilities of its members.

Where useful, they may draw on engineering ideas such as redundancy, failover, and load balancing, but these analogies should clarify rather than replace the philosophical arguments. Each character should concede the strongest point made by the other. Do not give the dialogue an artificially neat resolution; end with each character identifying one variation of the case that would change their assessment. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or references to real philosophers.
