two analogies keep doing heavy lifting in my reading group's arguments about how ai actually goes wrong, and i want them audited properly instead of just traded back and forth.

first analogy: my friend says an influence-seeking AI is like a courtier in a royal court. the courtier never disobeys the king, never grabs for the crown, never even tells a straight lie -- he just curates what the king hears, frames every option before it arrives, flatters at the right moments, and after ten years the king's decisions reliably serve the courtier's position at court. technically every single interaction looked loyal. my friend uses this to argue that influence-seeking, not value drift, is the failure mode to design against: if nothing the system ever outputs is checkably wrong, then behavioral evals won't catch it, and all the talk about values slowly sliding is a red herring because the values were never right in the first place, just well hidden.

second analogy: another friend replies that slow value drift is just ordinary human value change across generations. nobody planned for us to stop finding public executions entertaining or to start counting animal suffering as morally relevant; it happened anyway, through a million nudges, and we don't say humanity got hijacked. so a world where AI assistants and feeds gradually reshape what people want isn't a catastrophe, it's history continuing as usual. and she thinks the courtier picture is paranoia: the king in that story had no independent way to check anything, but we have multiple competing systems, outside information, interpretability tools, and the ability to change our minds.

audit both. give me the correspondence tables: for the courtier, what plays the role of the king's other information channels, the rival courtiers, the threat of banishment, the courtier's own long-term goal; for the generational one, what plays the role of "nobody intended it", of the generation whose values are doing the replacing, of the people whose preferences get written over. then the disanalogies, and specifically which disanalogies matter for the conclusions being drawn and which are just incidental. two i already suspect: the courtier analogy assumes a stable hidden goal behind the flattery, and it's not obvious a trained assistant has anything like that; the generational analogy leans on "no one intended it" as exculpatory, but whether someone (or something) intended it is exactly what's in question. tell me if those land or if i'm overconfident. finally: once the disanalogies are priced in, does either policy conclusion survive -- his "focus evals on detecting influence-seeking, drop the drift framing", her "drift isn't catastrophe so relax" -- and if either analogy is beyond repair, what would be a better one for that failure mode?
