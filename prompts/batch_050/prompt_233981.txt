I'm in a reading group on aesthetics and this week we're doing David Hume's essay "Of the Standard of Taste" (1757). Most of it I more or less follow, but there's a passage early on that I keep rereading without really getting it. It comes right after Hume has spent a paragraph on how wildly people's tastes differ:

"There are certain terms in every language, which import blame, and others praise; and all men, who use the same tongue, must agree in their application of them. Every voice is united in applauding elegance, propriety, simplicity, spirit in writing; and in blaming fustian, affectation, coldness, and a false brilliant: But when critics come to particulars, this seeming unanimity vanishes; and it is found, that they had affixed a very different meaning to their expressions. In all matters of opinion and science, the case is opposite: The difference among men is there oftner found to lie in generals than in particulars; and to be less in reality than in appearance. An explication of the terms commonly ends the controversy; and the disputants are surprized to find, that they had been quarrelling, while at bottom they agreed in their judgment."

So my confusion: Hume seems to say that in matters of taste, people agree in general vocabulary but disagree once they get to particulars, while in science and opinion it's the other way around -- they fight over general claims but actually agree underneath once terms are explained. First, can you explain in plain modern terms what this contrast actually amounts to, maybe with a made-up example of each case? Second, and this is what I really want, how does this observation set up the rest of the essay? A friend in the group thinks this passage shows taste is just subjective because we all secretly mean different things by "elegant", but I read it as almost the opposite -- that the shared general vocabulary is the foothold Hume later builds his standard on, since the disagreement is about application, not about what beauty is. Who's closer, and does this passage help or hurt the idea that some judgments of beauty are genuinely better than others?
