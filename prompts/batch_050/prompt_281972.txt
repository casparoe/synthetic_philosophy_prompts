Before I compare any formal models of decision-making under ambiguity, I want to know what I should be asking of one. Write the desiderata: what an adequate theory of rational choice when probabilities are unknown or imprecise has to deliver. Include the phenomena it must accommodate (the Ellsberg choices, the difference between ambiguity aversion and ordinary risk aversion, whether it should treat ambiguity about self-generated events differently from imposed ambiguity), the results it must reduce to in the limiting case where probabilities are known, and the distinctions it must respect (between a person's betting odds and their confidence in those odds, between a preference over ambiguous prospects and a belief about them), with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Mark which desiderata you regard as non-negotiable for any theory and which look like commitments of particular modeling camps, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — in particular, whether full dynamic consistency of choice over time can coexist with strict ambiguity aversion, since that is the part I most want to see reasoned out. Close briefly with how the two main modeling strategies — representing ambiguity as a set of priors versus as a second-order probability distribution over priors — fare against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves and the cases that motivate them; the scoring is secondary.
