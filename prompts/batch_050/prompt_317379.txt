So I'm re-reading Albert Schweitzer's The Quest of the Historical Jesus (1906; the English translation came out in 1910), the book that supposedly buried the whole nineteenth-century industry of liberal Lives of Jesus. In the final chapter he drops this:

"The Jesus of Nazareth who came forward publicly as the Messiah, who preached the ethic of the Kingdom of God, who founded the Kingdom of Heaven upon earth, and died to give His work its final consecration, never had any existence."

Here's the game. Schweitzer died in 1965, just before New Testament scholarship took the turn I want to ask about: the codification of the so-called criteria of authenticity (double dissimilarity, embarrassment, multiple attestation, coherence, and friends) and then, from the 1980s on, the Third Quest, with people like E.P. Sanders claiming that even if a full biography is off the table we can at least nail down a bedrock of secure facts about Jesus' aims and fate.

Say Schweitzer had lived passed all that, actually read it, and been asked for a verdict. What would he say? Some options to triangulate between (feel free to reject all of them):

- The criteria are a genuinely new instrument, a sieve rather than a mirror, so his verdict was right about the liberal Lives but simply doesn't touch the newer work, which wants far less than a Life.
- The criteria are just rationalism and liberalism in lab coats: each scholar tweaks the dials and out pops a different Jesus (apocalyptic prophet, cynic-style sage, social revolutionary, charismatic healer), and the sheer spread of Third Quest results is itself the proof that the projection-machine is still running.
- Something subtler: the most trusted criteria arguably deliver exactly what he predicted. Double dissimilarity prizes whatever fits neither Judaism nor the early church, and embarrassment prizes what the church would rather forget, so the method, if it works at all, manufactures a Jesus who is precisely "a stranger and an enigma" to his own time and to ours. Would he read that as vindication rather than refutation?

Ground your answer in the passage and in the overall argument of the Quest, not in a generic book-report summary of what scholars think. Then, at the end, steelman the reply a modern criteria-scholar would give to a resurrected Schweitzer -- presumably something about the difference between constructing a usable Jesus for the present and merely establishing a modest factual minimum -- and tell me honestly whether Schweitzer has a comeback, or whether the passage overreaches and the criteria really do sit outside its blast radius.

British English please, roughly 600-900 words, not counting the quoted passage.
