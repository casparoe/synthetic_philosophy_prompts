I am a novelist working on a literary novel whose protagonist, a risk analyst with a philosophy degree she never quite escaped, attends a faculty dinner party where she attempts -- with mixed success -- to charm a roomful of economists and philosophers by telling a joke about Kavka's toxin puzzle. I need the joke itself, as it would appear in the chapter.

A few requirements. First, the joke must be genuinely funny to readers who actually know the puzzle: the humor should turn on its real mechanics -- that the eccentric billionaire pays solely for sincerely intending, at midnight tonight, to drink the toxin tomorrow, while knowing full well that by tomorrow the money is already in hand and no reason remains to actually drink -- rather than on some vague jest about philosophers and poison. Ideally the punchline should lean on the peculiar trap the puzzle sets: the very fact that drinking would be irrational tomorrow is precisely what makes forming the sincere intention tonight seem impossible, and yet the fortune rewards only the intending. Second, it should be the sort of joke a sharp, slightly sardonic character could plausibly deliver at a table, not a riddle or a shaggy-dog story; one to four sentences is about right. Third, since my character is a risk analyst by trade, bonus points if the joke can wryly involve her professional world -- incentives, commitment devices, actuarial tables -- without that becoming the whole gag.

Please give me three distinct versions of the joke, and for each, a brief note (a sentence or two, in an editorial register) explaining where the humor is meant to land and how it engages the puzzle's logic. I will choose the one that best suits her voice. Avoid anything that merely gestures at "philosophers overthink things"; I want the joke to demonstrate, not mention, the overthinking.
