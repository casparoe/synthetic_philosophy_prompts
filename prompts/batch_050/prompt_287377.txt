I'm trying to work out what I actually think privacy is, going by my reactions to cases, and I'd like to know what account they add up to. My verdicts:

1. A diary left open on a park bench: reading it is a violation, even though the owner took no steps to conceal it.
2. A conversation audible through thin apartment walls: overhearing it accidentally is not a violation; pressing a glass to the wall to hear better is.
3. A stranger noticing my prescription bottle on the café table: not a violation. My pharmacist mentioning the same prescription to a friend: a violation — even though in both cases the same fact changes hands.
4. A data broker assembling a detailed dossier from entirely public records — property filings, voter rolls, court dockets: a violation, even though no single record was private.
5. A retailer inferring my pregnancy from my purchase history before I've told anyone: a violation, though I voluntarily handed over every transaction it relied on.
6. A photographer selling telephoto shots of a celebrity sunbathing in their walled garden: a violation; the same shots taken on a crowded public beach: not, or at least much less clearly — and I can't fully justify the asymmetry with case 4.
7. My employer reading messages on the account it provides, under a policy I signed: not a violation, though it involves deliberate access to content I treat as intimate.

Is there a single account of privacy — cashed out in terms of control over information, restricted access, contextual norms, or something else — that delivers all of these, and if not, which verdict should I give up? Cases 4 and 6 pull me in opposite directions, and I suspect cases 1 and 7 are in tension as well. Please state the best candidate principle (or two) precisely, indicate which clause of it each case motivates, say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save, and tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: police flying a drone over my fenced backyard without a warrant.
