The question was: "Hedonism about welfare holds that pleasure is the one thing that's intrinsically good for us. Critics object that pleasures are too heterogeneous to share a common nature. State the objection carefully, explain two ways hedonists have responded, and assess whether either response succeeds."

Here's the student's answer:

The heterogeneity objection says that pleasures don't have anything in common. The pleasure of eating a great meal, the pleasure of a hot shower after a long run, and the pleasure of finally understanding a difficult proof all feel completely different. So if hedonism says pleasure is one thing that is good, and pleasure turns out not to be one thing, hedonism is false.

The first response is the felt-quality view: all pleasures share a single distinctive feeling, even if we can't easily put it into words. Sidgwick held a view in this neighborhood. The second response is attitudinal hedonism, as developed by Feldman: pleasure is not a sensation at all but an attitude -- taking pleasure in something, or enjoying it. You can take pleasure in anything (meals, showers, proofs), so heterogeneity is no problem: the objects differ, but the attitude is the same.

The felt-quality view is implausible, because introspection simply reveals no common feeling across these cases. Attitudinal hedonism succeeds. The objection assumed that pleasure is a kind of sensation, and once we see that pleasure is an attitude instead, the objection dissolves. Therefore the heterogeneity objection fails and hedonism stands.
