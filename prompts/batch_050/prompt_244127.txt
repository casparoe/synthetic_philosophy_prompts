I spent forty years as a fisheries biologist and retired three years ago, and since then I've been reading philosophy the way I used to read tide tables, which is to say obsessively and slightly over my head. I've been working through Aristotle's On Interpretation (in the Edghill translation), and early on, in chapter 4, he says: "Yet every sentence is not a proposition; only such are propositions as have in them either truth or falsity. Thus a prayer is a sentence, but is neither true nor false." Fine, I thought, clear enough. Then I got to the famous chapter 9, the one about tomorrow's sea battle, and I tried to reconstruct what I take to be the worry there in my own words, as an argument. The problem is that my reconstruction seems to prove something I refuse to believe, and I can't find the leak. Here it is:

(1) Every proposition is either true or false. There's no third status; that's just what it is to be a proposition rather than a prayer or a command, per the Aristotle line above.
(2) "There will be a sea battle tommorow" is a proposition. It's a plain declarative sentence, nobody's praying or ordering anything.
(3) So that sentence is true right now, or else false right now.
(4) If a proposition about tomorrow is true today, then it's no longer genuinely open today how tomorrow goes. A truth has to be true in virtue of something, and if both outcomes were still really on the table, there'd be nothing yet in the world for the sentence to be true of, so it wouldn't be true, contradicting (3).
(5) So whichever way tomorrow goes, it is already settled today.
(6) The reasoning doesn't care about the subject matter. Swap "sea battle" for "I will wear the green shirt" or "my granddaughter's wedding gets rained on" and the same conclusion follows: it's all settled already.
(7) So deliberation is always a waste of time. What we deliberate about is fixed one way or the other before we start weighing reasons.
(8) But deliberation is obviously not a waste of time. I deliberated about whether to replant the veg patch last spring and the deliberating made an actual difference to what happened.

Now, I'm not even sure (7) is where the absurdity stops. Nobody blinks at the thought that truths about the past are fixed — of course they are, the past is over. So maybe the honest conclusion is that (6) shouldn't bother me either and I've just got a sentimental prejudice in favour of the future. That's what I mean by the argument proving too much: I can't tell whether it exposes a bad premise or a bad intuition in me.

If I had to say what I most want to protect, it's (8), and (2) seems harmless, so I suppose the fight is among (1), (3), and (4) — but I'm not confident about that ranking.

Here's what I'd like from you, and please take this seriously: don't hand me a menu. I can look up three-valued logic and branching futures and supervaluations on my own, and a survey of options leaves me exactly where I started. Pick the one step you think the argument actually breaks at, commit to it, and defend it — including telling me what it costs you, because every exit from this thing seems to cost something. And if you think the argument is sound and deliberation really is the waste of time it says, have the nerve to say that instead, and explain to a man who counted fish for four decades why his spring replanting was as pointless as praying.
