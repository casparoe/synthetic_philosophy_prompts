A few months ago I stumbled onto Lewis Carroll's tortoise dialogue (the 1895 one where Achilles tries to get the tortoise to accept that the conclusion follows from two premises, and the tortoise keeps going "ok granted, but now write down as a new premise that the conclusion follows from those," forever) and I kind of fell down a rabbit hole. What got me hooked is that it maps almost perfectly onto stuff from my day job as a software engineer. A compiler just applies its rules. Nobody asks the type checker to justify its own inference rules, and if you tried, you'd need a meta-type-checker, and so on. When people bootstrap a compiler (compiling the compiler with itself) everyone in the room understands this isn't a proof that the compiler is correct, it's just how you get the thing off the ground. So from the engineering side the whole thing looks less like a deep problem and more like a category error: rules aren't theorems, inference rules are the things you do, not the things you derive.

But then I started reading the philosophy and apparently this is still a live fight, 130 years later. There's the rule-circularity debate (can a justification of modus ponens itself use modus ponens, and is that vicious or fine), there's the Kripke/Wittgenstein rule-following stuff about how no rule determines its own application, there's people arguing you can be entitled to basic inference without any justification at all, and meanwhile there are logicians who happily reject classical principles and just build different systems. Everyone seems to agree on the core point -- you cannot give a non-circular, from-scratch justification of deduction -- and yet nobody seems to move anywhere. The fight is entirely about what that fact means.

My working hypothesis, which you should feel free to demolish: this isn't one debate, it's at least three wearing a trenchcoat. (1) A psychological question -- what would actually get a stubborn reasoner (the tortoise) to accept a conclusion. (2) An epistemic question -- can I know modus ponens is valid without circularity. And (3) a metaphysical/semantic question -- what makes an inference valid in the first place. The tortoise is obviously about (1), but everyone treats it as a problem about (2), and the people who say "no non-circular justification is needed" are really answering (3). If that's right the debate is stalled because it's mostly verbal.

So here's my actual ask: diagnose this. Is the impasse verbal, or is there a substantive disagreement hiding in there (maybe about what "justification" is even supposed to deliver, or about whether entitlement and justification are the same thing)? Is the engineering intuition -- that demanding a justification for the rules themselves is just ill-posed -- a genuine solution, or does it miss something real that the philosophers are onto? And given your diagnosis, what would progress even look like here: a clean separation of the questions, some formal result, everyone just agreeing to stop? Or is this the kind of problem that can't be solved and can only be dissolved, and if so, why hasn't it dissolved yet?
