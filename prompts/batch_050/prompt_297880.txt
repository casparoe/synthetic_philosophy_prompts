For the draft I'm currently working from, I've adopted roughly Gregg Caruso's position: no one deserves punishment in the basic-desert sense, retributivism is unjustified even if compatibilism about free will is true, and practices of blame and incarceration should be rebuilt on something like a public-health or quarantine model, where restraint is justified the way we justify isolating carriers of disease. What I want is a map of this view's exposure, written for me before I send the piece to fact-checkers. What would count against it? I'd like the strongest candidate defeaters, including Daniel Dennett's line that something in the neighborhood of desert is needed to make sense of proportionality and of the wrongness of punishing the innocent, the empirical question of whether cooperative human societies can actually function without retributive sentiment, and the objection that the quarantine model licenses indefinitely detaining harmless people who are statistically dangerous. For each defeater, tell me whether a committed skeptic about desert would concede it as genuine counterevidence or absorb it as a design problem for the replacement institutions, and rank the defeaters from potentially fatal to merely embarrassing. Finally, tell me honestly whether the position has been qualified into something that no empirical finding or thought experiment could touch, and if so whether that matters for a normative view of this kind. Please write the answer in American English, in continuous prose only: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, just the way you would write a long, careful letter.
