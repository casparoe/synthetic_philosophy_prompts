I'm in a graduate seminar on ethical methodology, and last week we spent two hours picking apart the way a well-known applied ethics paper argued for its conclusion. The pattern was completely standard, and once we noticed it we started seeing it everywhere: the author states a general moral principle, tests it against a handful of hypothetical cases, discards or "revises" whichever intuitions resist the principle, then adjusts the principle in light of the intuitions she keeps, iterating until the whole set fits together. Nobody in the seminar balked at the method itself — this back-and-forth toward reflective equilibrium is just how analytic moral philosophy is done, in journals, in teaching, in ordinary conversation about hard cases. But afterward a friend asked me a question I couldn't answer: what would you have to believe about moral knowledge for this procedure to be a reasonable way of finding anything out, rather than just a way of tidying up your opinions?

So here is what I'd like worked out. Reconstruct the implicit epistemology of this practice. Does it presuppose that pre-theoretical moral intuitions have evidential weight — that their contents are, however imperfectly, responsive to moral facts or truths — rather than being mere psychological residue of upbringing and evolution that happens to feel compelling? If so, does the common move of privileging "considered judgments" made under calm, informed conditions amount to a tacit theory about which intuitions are reliable and why, and can that theory be stated without circularity? Second, does the method presuppose that coherence between intuitions, principles, and background theories is evidence of truth rather than merely evidence of consistency? It seems the realist and the constructivist will answer this differently, so tell me whether the practice as actually conducted is neutral between them, or whether something in it — say, the willingness to let a stubborn intuition overturn a principle, or the expectation that other people's equilibria should converge with mine — quietly commits its practitioners to one side.

Finally, tell me where the practice is underdetermined: which features of it several rival metaethical theories would license equally well, and which features, if any, no coherent theory could license. And then run the replacement test: suppose we become convinced by the debunking picture, on which our case intuitions track fitness-relevant heuristics and cultural parochialism rather than anything normatively significant. What, concretely, would have to change in the way applied ethics papers argue, in how we teach the trolley-style cases, and in what equilibrium with our own judgments is even supposed to be for? If the answer is that almost nothing would change — that philosophers would keep doing exactly this anyway — what does that reveal about what the practice was really presupposing all along?
