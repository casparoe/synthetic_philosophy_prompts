Each fall, in the final meeting of my undergraduate seminar on epistemology, I set aside the last half hour for something other than argument, and this year I would like to close our unit on radical skepticism with a poem. We have spent three weeks on the brain-in-a-vat hypothesis and the standard repertoire of responses — Moore's proof by ostension, Putnam's contention that an envatted brain cannot so much as refer to vats and so cannot truly think the thought that it is one, the disjunctivist's insistence that veridical perception and perfect illusion are not the same mental state, and the contextualist's suggestion that the skeptic wins only by quietly raising the standards for knowledge past anything we ordinarily demand — and my students have noticed, with some exasperation, that the hypothesis survives each of these in slightly altered dress. I want the poem to take that resilience as its subject rather than to pretend it away.

Write a sestina whose six recurring end-words are drawn from the skeptic's small stock of vocabulary — I suggest hand, vat, know, dream, proof, and world, though you may adjust the set if you can justify the substitutions — so that the form itself makes the point the seminar kept arriving at: each stanza can stage one of the responses we studied, and yet the same six words return at the line-ends in their fixed rotation, untouched by the argument unfolding inside the lines. The envoi should gather all six at once and land somewhere honest — not a triumph for the skeptic, and not a refutation either, but the recognition that the hypothesis may be less a threat to be defeated than a permanent structural feature of the concept of knowledge. A reader who knows this literature should be able to tell which stanza belongs to Moore, which to Putnam, and so on, and should find each position rendered accurately rather than caricatured; a reader who knows none of it should still find the thing worth reading as a poem.
