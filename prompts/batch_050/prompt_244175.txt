I’m a novelist revising a thriller, and a late-night argument with a friend who studied philosophy has made me worry that my instincts about knowledge are a mess. My protagonist, Mara, gets a reliable-looking message that the north bridge is open. In one scene, acting on it merely saves her twenty minutes; in another, she is driving an ambulance and being wrong would be catastrophic. I want the same evidence to justify different reactions, but I also want her decisions to feel objectively rational rather than dependent on how some narrator happens to describe them.

Here’s roughly what I currently believe: (1) the strong knowledge norm of practical reasoning is right—Mara may rationally treat a proposition as her reason for acting only if she knows it; (2) pragmatic encroachment is true in a subject-sensitive form, so her practical stakes can change whether her evidence is sufficient for knowledge even when the evidence itself stays fixed; (3) contextualism about knows is also true, meaning the truth of a knowledge claim depends on the epistemic standards operative in the speaker’s conversational context; (4) whether Mara acted rationally is an objective fact and cannot vary simply because one character describes her in a casual context while another uses skeptical standards; (5) when gathering more evidence would be prohibitively dangerous or costly, Mara can be rationally permitted to act on excellent evidence that she herself recognizes falls short of knowledge; and (6) skeptical possibilities can shift the conversational standards without making ordinary low-stakes knowledge claims false. Could you audit this package for consistancy? Separate genuine tensions from merely apparent ones, rank the tensions by severity, and explain exactly what would have to be revised in each case. Please don’t just give me a survey of contextualism and pragmatic encroachment—I want to know which of my commitments pull against each other and what the cheapest repairs would cost. Please answer in American English.
