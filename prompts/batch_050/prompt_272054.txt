A popular science book I've been reading explains Dennett's idea of "strange inversions of reasoning" more or less like this: "Before Darwin, everyone assumed that competence requires comprehension — that anything clever in the world must have been made by something cleverer. Darwin turned this on its head: natural selection produces brilliant design from a mindless, mechanical process, so the appearance of design in nature proves there was never a designer at all. Turing then did the same trick for thinking. Since a machine can pass for a thinker just by following rules it doesn't understand, human reasoning is really just mechanical symbol-shuffling too, and our feeling that we understand what we mean is a kind of user illusion." Something about this bugs me — mainly the leap from "design without a designer" to "no designer", and the claim that understanding is an illusion — but the book is well reviewed, so maybe the problem is me. How fair is this as a summary of what Darwin and Turing actually showed, and of what Dennett takes from them? Please separate what's accurate, what's a defensible simplification for a popular audience, and what a careful reader would push back on, and be clear about which parts are broadly settled and which are contested among philosophers rather than established — don't present Dennett's stronger conclusions as if they were the consensus. Also, write it as continuous prose, like an email: no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables. And in British English please.
