My younger brother had a psychotic episode last year and for a while he was convinced that our parents had been replaced by near-perfect impostors who'd undergone surgery to copy them. He's a lot better now, on meds, and he can talk about it pretty openly, which is how I ended up down this rabbit hole: during the episode he would say, completely deadpan, that these strangers were poisoning him -- and then he'd still eat the dinner our mom cooked, because he said the impostors had to "keep up appearances" and wouldn't poison him where anyone could see. When I started reading the philosophy literature on delusions I assumed the big question would be something like what goes wrong in the brain, but instead there's this whole fight about whether delusions are even *beliefs* at all. One side says obviously they are: patients assert them, defend them, feel distress in proportion to them, the two-factor picture (weird experience plus some reasoning failure) treats the delusion as the patient's best explanation of the experience, which is just what belief-formation is. The other side points at cases exactly like my brother's: he "believed" the woman in front of him was an impostor but hugged her goodnight anyway, never called the police, never asked for DNA tests. If your so-called belief doesn't guide your actions, in what sense do you believe it? And this argument has apparently been going for decades with both sides just recycling the same points.

Here's my hypothesis, and tell me if it's naive: I think this debate has stalled because the two sides aren't actually disagreeing about patients at all. They're disagreeing about what "belief" means -- the belief side is using a functional, explain-the-evidence notion, and the other side is using a thick, action-guiding, integrated-with-your-life notion, and neither notion is wrong, they're just different concepts that ordinary language happens to cover with one word. But my girlfriend (who's a linguist, so possibly biased) says I'm too quick to call everything a verbal dispute, and that there's a real empirical question buried in there somewhere -- maybe about whether the inconsistency in patients' behavior is itself a symptom that needs explaining rather than evidence about belief. So: what kind of disagreement is this really? Verbal, factual, a dispute about which phenomena count as data, or something about bedrock intuitions concerning what minds owe us? I'd like a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of positions, and given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually move the debate -- a distinction nobody's made, an experiment, a case study, a change of question -- or argue honestly that nothing will and explain why that's not a scandal.
