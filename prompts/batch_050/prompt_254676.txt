Two famous thought experiments are often treated as making the same point, but arguably they pull in opposite directions. In Daniel Dennett's "Where Am I?" scenario, a person's brain is removed and placed in a vat while remaining in radio control of the body, and the story is engineered so that every natural candidate answer to the question of where the person is located -- with the brain, with the body, or wherever the person's point of view seems to be -- is destabilized in turn, culminating in a switch of control after a brain transplant that leaves the subject unsure which embodied person he now is. In Derek Parfit's teletransportation cases, a person's body is scanned, destroyed, and perfectly reconstructed elsewhere from new matter, and the puzzle is whether the reconstructed person is numerically identical to the original or merely a perfect successor.

Compare these two scenarios with respect to the following three dimensions, devoting a section to each:

1. What kind of question each thought experiment is designed to attack. Dennett's puzzle targets a question about location (where the subject is), while Parfit's targets a question about survival (whether the subject persists). Are these really different questions, or does each scenario covertly presuppose an answer to the other? For example, does deciding where Dennett's subject is just amount to deciding which body he is identical to, and does deciding whether Parfit's traveler survives just amount to deciding where he ends up?

2. The role of continuity of control and experience. Dennett's setup hinges on the brain remaining causally connected to the body throughout, so that experience and agency are never interrupted; Parfit's standard teletransportation involves complete destruction and reconstitution, with no ongoing causal link. Now consider a hybrid case that neither author discusses: the brain is scanned and copied, and control of the body is handed over, back and forth, between the original brain and the copy, switching every few minutes, with the subject reporting a seamless stream of consciousness throughout. Does this hybrid case behave more like Dennett's scenario or Parfit's? Which features -- continuity of experience, continuity of causal control, material continuity -- does each author's framework implicitly treat as decisive, and which does the hybrid case show to be doing the real intuitive work?

3. What each author concludes about the intelligibility of the original question. Dennett's moral is roughly that the question where the subject is has no determinate answer once the intuitions it relies on are pulled apart; Parfit's moral is roughly that numerical identity can fail to obtain while everything that matters in survival is preserved. Are these the same conclusion stated in different vocabularies -- both denying that a certain fact exists -- or is there a substantive disagreement between dissolving the question (Dennett) and answering it in the negative while downgrading its importance (Parfit)? If a judge had to issue a ruling in the hybrid case from section 2 -- say, which brain's operator inherits the subject's bank account -- which author's framework would give the judge better guidance, and what does your answer reveal about the practical difference between the two morals?
