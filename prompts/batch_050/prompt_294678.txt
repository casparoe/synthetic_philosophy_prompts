I'm preparing for a graduate seminar on population ethics, and for the final session I've tried to build what I thought would be a straightforward argument from classical utilitarian premises to a pronatalist conclusion — but the conclusion I've ended up with is one neither I nor my classmates can accept, and I can't locate the error. The starting point is the passage in Book IV of Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics where he considers whether the utilitarian ideal requires maximising the average happiness of a population or the total: Sidgwick argues there that, so long as additional lives would on balance contain more pleasure than pain, the utilitarian end is best served by increasing numbers, so that the optimum population is not the one where average happiness is highest but the one where the total sum of happiness is largest, even if average happiness falls as numbers grow. Taking that doctrine seriously, here is the argument. (1) If bringing about an outcome would increase the total sum of happiness, then, other things equal, we have a moral reason to bring it about, and this reason is proportionate to the amount of happiness added. (2) A couple who expect to raise a child whose life will contain, on balance, substantially more happiness than suffering can thereby increase the total sum of happiness. (3) So such a couple has a moral reason to have the child, and indeed to have further children so long as each additional child's expected happiness is positive, even if each addition somewhat reduces the wellbeing of the parents and the existing children, provided the losses are smaller than the gains. (4) A moral reason of this kind cannot be set aside merely because acting on it would be burdensome to the agent: we don't accept that a wealthy person may ignore famine relief just because giving is inconvenient, and parenthood, for most people in rich countries, is no more burdensome than the sacrifices we routinely describe as obligatory. (5) Therefore most couples in affluent societies are morally required to have as many children as they can raise to a level of wellbeing that is merely decent, and a couple that chooses to remain childless in order to travel, pursue careers, or enjoy leisure is doing something seriously wrong — comparable to declining to rescue someone at moderate cost to oneself. (6) But that conclusion is absurd: remaining childless, or stopping at one child, is at worst morally neutral. So something in the argument has to go.

My instinct is that I want to keep (1) and (6) if I possibly can, since (1) seems like the least controversial thing a consequentialist could say and (6) seems like bedrock common sense. But when I press the argument, I find the squeeze tightens: if I deny that the reasons generated by possible people are real reasons, I seem to undermine the parallel claim that we have reasons not to create children whose lives would be miserable, which I don't want to give up; if I say the burdens of parenthood are different in kind from the burdens of giving, I need an account of why, and the obvious candidates (that parenthood is a project constitutive of one's identity, or that nobody owes the world their reproductive labour) seem equally available to the person who declines to give to charity on the ground that their money is bound up with their life plans. So: tell me where the argument goes wrong, if it does. Name the premise or step you would reject, explain why it looked plausible in the first place, and be candid about what rejecting it costs — in particular, what it commits me to about the wrongness of bringing a miserable child into existence, about the comparability of population ethics with ordinary duties of aid, and about whether Sidgwick's own totalist principle can survive the diagnosis or has to be abandoned with the argument.

Please write your answer in British English. A diagnosis of roughly 500–800 words would suit my purposes, and I'd rather you priced the alternatives honestly than rescued the argument cheaply.
