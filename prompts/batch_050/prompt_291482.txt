So I'm doing an independent study this semester on the ethics of organ transplantation, and before my advisor will let me take a position on anything she wants me to get clear on what we'd even want out of a definition of death in the first place. Her complaint is that people in this literature keep talking past each other because they're secretly asking the concept to do different jobs -- one side wants a biological fact of the matter, the other wants a criterion you can legally and practically use at 3am in an ICU, and they argue as if those must coincide. So here's what I want from you: write the desiderata. What does any adequate account of when a human being is dead have to deliver? Which cases must it get right, which distinctions must it respect, which practical and moral jobs does it need to be able to do? Give the case or consideration motivating each requirement, not just the requirement itself, and say for each whether you think it's genuinely non-negotiable or whether it's really just a prejudice of one camp.

To seed this a bit, here are the things already confusing me, which you should feel free to incorporate, refine, or dismiss. First, there's apparently a live fight over whether death has to be *irreversible* cessation of circulation/brain function or merely *permanent* (won't come back on its own and won't be reversed), because donation after circulatory death only waits a few minutes before procurement, and hearts that have stopped for five minutes can sometimes be restarted -- so is the patient dead or just not-going-to-be-resuscitated? Second, the newer normothermic regional perfusion protocols, where surgeons restart circulation in the body after declaring circulatory death but clamp off the vessels to the brain: does that procedure un-kill the patient, and should an adequate definition even care what we do after the declaration? Third, the standard hard cases: anencephalic infants, patients in a persistent vegetative state, brain-dead pregnant women kept on somatic support for weeks, people under deep hypothermia whose hearts have stopped but who can be revived hours later, and the cryonics enthusiast who insists current criteria just mark the limits of our technology. Fourth, should the definition track the death of the organism or the death of the person, and can one definition do both? Fifth, there's a worry that any criterion adopted in a transplant context is gerrymandered to maximize organs -- is it a desideratum that the definition be fixed independently of procurement interests, or is that naive?

Once you have the list: flag any pair (or triple) of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, since that would itself be an interesting result, and identify which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates. Then, briefly -- this part matters less to me than the criteria themselves -- score the three main contenders against your list: the whole-brain criterion, a higher-brain criterion, and the traditional circulatory-respiratory criterion. Assume my end goal is a definition good enough to anchor a hospital's procurement policy, so grade for that purpose, not for metaphysical purity.
