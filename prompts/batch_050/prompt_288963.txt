So I've been going down this rabbit hole lately on the whole fight between virtue ethicists and the psychologists who say character traits basically don't do the work we think they do. I read about the Milgram obedience experiments, the one where seminary students walked right past a man slumped in a doorway because they'd been told they were running late, and the one where finding a dime in a payphone made people way more likely to help a stranger. It honestly messed me up a little, because I do think of myself as "an honest person" and apparently the evidence says that's mostly situational confidence.

Anyway here's what I want: a satirical advice column, Dear Abby style, maybe 600-700 words, written by a columnist who is a fully committed situationist and refuses on principle to ever explain anything by anyone's character. Readers write in with totally normal problems -- a husband who cheated, a coworker stealing lunches from the break room fridge, a kid who keeps lying -- and the columnist deadpan-insists there is no such thing as a dishonest person, only people placed in honesty-undermining circumstances, and then gives advice that is entirely about redesigning the situations. Move the fridge. Restructure the marriage's honesty-promoting environment. He never breaks character, never acknowledges how deranged this sounds, and the jokes should come from him taking the actual research completely seriously -- the fundamental attribution error, the weak cross-situational consistency in those old studies of kids lying and cheating -- rather than from him just being a cartoon weirdo. A psychologist who knows this literature should laugh because it's accurate, not despite it.

Two more things. First, at least one letter should get situationist advice that is... actually kind of good? Like the environmental redesign genuinely seems wiser than the "find a better class of person" alternative, and the columnist doesn't notice he just won a point. Second, the virtue ethicist's comeback should be lurking in the piece somewhere -- the idea that lab experiments on strangers' one-off behavior tell you nothing about habituated virtue, or that virtue was always supposed to be rare so of course most people fail under pressure -- but the columnist should brush it off in a way a specialist would recognize as specifically unfair, like he's dodging the strongest version on purpose. Keep it deadpan the whole way through, no winking, no "and that's the joke" at the end. The column should definately end on a letter that makes you slightly unsure who won.
