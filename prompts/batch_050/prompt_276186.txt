I work in quantitative social science, and my working vocabulary is that of modern causal inference: directed acyclic graphs, confounding and collider bias, mediation analysis, instrumental variables, selection effects, partial identification, and the distinction between estimating an effect and merely bounding it. I have only a secondhand sense of the debate within Marxist theory about historical materialism, and I want it restated in my vocabulary.

Here is what I would like translated, one thesis at a time. First, the claim that the economic base (the forces and relations of production) determines the legal, political, and ideological superstructure. Second, the weaker-sounding claim that the base merely constrains or conditions the superstructure, with room for what is called the relative autonomy of the latter. Third, G. A. Cohen's proposal that base-superstructure explanations are functional explanations: legal and political arrangements persist because of what they do for the productive relations, not because anyone intended them to. Fourth, the idea that the causal arrows also run upward, from law and ideology back down to production, without this refuting the primacy of the base.

For each thesis, give me the most faithful rendering you can manage in causal-inference terms: say what the base and superstructure would be as variables or sets of variables, what "determines" amounts to (a total effect? a set of direct effects? an identification claim?), how "constraint" differs from "influence" in graph terms, whether functional explanation maps onto selection dynamics rather than onto anything a DAG can draw, and what "relative autonomy" would have to mean statistically.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which claims translate cleanly, which survive only with distortion (and say exactly where the distortion enters), and which seem to have no expression in this vocabulary at all. I suspect candidates for the last category: the talk of dialectical relations rather than one-way causal arrows, the notion of a social totality in which the base is itself partly constituted by what it supposedly causes, and the role of class agency and struggle, which does not look like a variable. For each piece of residue, give me your judgment about whether it marks a genuine limitation of the causal-inference framework for thinking about long-run social change, or whether it instead suggests the original thesis was less precise than it appeared and the translation has done us a service by exposing that.

Throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested: both among Marxist theorists themselves (where I gather almost every one of these theses is disputed, including whether "determination" was ever the right word) and among causal-inference practitioners about what their tools can represent. Do not present a contested reading of historical materialism as the consensus one, and flag wherever my hoped-for translation turns out to presuppose a choice among rival readings.
