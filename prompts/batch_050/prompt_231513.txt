I host a mid-sized podcast about AI governance and next week I'm interviewing a researcher from a frontier lab who argues that the alignment tax is basically a myth -- her line is that alignment and capability are convergent, so the techniques that make a system aligned (better instruction-following, honesty, doing what the operator wants) are the same ones that make it useful, and therefore no rational developer will ever face a real tradeoff between safety and competetiveness. She publishes on this and she's debated it a bunch, so she's heard the obvious objections and has smooth answers ready. I don't want to spend the hour letting her repeat her talk.

Draft a sequence of about eight interview questions that actually pin the view down. I want the sequence to do some work: start by forcing her to distinguish between alignment costs paid during development (extra training runs, red-teaming time, slower iteration) and costs paid at deployment (refusals, latency, guardrails that degrade the product), because I suspect her convergence story sounds much more plausible for one of these than the other. Include a question about selection pressure: even if the tax is only, say, a few percent in compute or capability, markets and racing labs presumably select on margins way smaller than that, so does a tiny tax still decide who ships first? Have a follow-up ready for when she responds that alignment is also marketable -- customers want models that don't lie or go off the rails -- because I want to press on whether customers can actually *detect* misalignment the way they can detect a slow model, or whether the market only rewards the visible parts of safety.

Also include one question pushing on her favorite historical analogy -- she likes comparing alignment to quality assurance rather than to security, but in software history security was famously a tax nobody paid until breaches or regulators forced the issue, so ask her what makes AI more like the QA case, and be ready with a follow-up when she says AI is different because failures are visible to users immediately. End the sequence with a question about what evidence or empirical result would convince her the tax is real and large after all -- she's good at framing everything as on-the-one-hand-on-the-other, so I want a question that makes her commit to something falsifiable in advance. For each question, give me a short note on the dodge she's most likely to use and how the follow-up should close it off.
