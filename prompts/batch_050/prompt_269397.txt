I am taking a second-year course in the philosophy of cognitive science, and last week we spent the seminar on machine translation and understanding, centring on an essay by Douglas Hofstadter in which he argues that statistical translation systems, however fluent their output, do not understand anything they translate — they shuttle between surface patterns without the words ever connecting to a world. Afterwards, two coursemates of mine, Daniel and Ruth, got into a long disagreement in the corridor, and it has been bothering me because I genuinely cannot tell who came out on top. (1) Daniel's opening move was that the fluency of these systems is evidentially worthless: a machine can produce a perfect translation of a sentence about a marathon runner's heart giving out without knowing that hearts pump blood, just as a parrot can produce a perfect sentence without grasping it, so Hofstadter is right that there is nothing behind the curtain. (2) Ruth replied that this proves too much, since human translators rely on surface cues all the time, and that the systems plainly do exploit something like world knowledge — they resolve pronouns correctly in sentences where only a grasp of the physical situation could settle the reference, and if that kind of behavioural success does not count as evidence of understanding, she cannot see what evidence for anyone's understanding, human or machine, ever could. (3) Daniel conceded that ordinary cases get mimicked well, but proposed a sharper test: give the system a passage containing a deliberate factual error — say, a travel piece asserting that the Danube empties into the North Sea. A translator who understood the content would flag it, footnote it, or at least hesitate; the machine serenely translates the mistake, which shows the words mean nothing to it. (4) Ruth pushed back hard: flagging errors is simply not the translator's job, professional human translators faithfully translate errors every day, and an author who had not asked for corrections would be irritated by the interference — so by Daniel's own test, a competent but unpedantic human translator would fail to count as understanding, which reduces the test to absurdity. (5) Daniel then shifted ground and said that even if the behavioural evidence were exactly equivalent, the system's symbols still would not be about anything, because nothing in its training connects words to perception or action, whereas Ruth's words are anchored in a lived world; Ruth answered that co-occurrence structure is a shadow cast by the world and carries real information about it, and that much of her own understanding is second-hand in just this way — she talks perfectly competently about quarks, the Treaty of Westphalia, and the inside of the Sun without any perceptual contact with any of them.

Please adjudicate this properly, move by move. For each of the five numbered moves, say whether it succeeds, fails, or only partly succeeds, and briefly why. I would particularly like to know: whether Ruth's demand for a behavioural criterion is fair or whether it begs the question against Hofstadter's position; whether Daniel's error-flagging test actually distinguishes understanding from mimicry, given Ruth's reply; and whether Daniel's final retreat to grounding is a genuine argument or a change of subject. Then give me an overall verdict — who is more right, on what specific point each of them is simply correct, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
