I am designing a 75-minute undergraduate ethics session for mixed majors who have already met act utilitarianism but mostly understand it as a slogan about doing the most good. The session’s target is the demandingness objection to consequentialism, taught as a serious structural problem rather than as a complaint that morality is inconvenient. I want to anchor the session in this passage from John Stuart Mill’s Utilitarianism, Chapter I:

"All action is for the sake of some end, and rules of action, it seems natural to suppose, must take their whole character and colour from the end to which they are subservient. When we engage in a pursuit, a clear and precise conception of what we are pursuing would seem to be the first thing we need, instead of the last we are to look forward to. A test of right and wrong must be the means, one would think, of ascertaining what is right or wrong, and not a consequence of having already ascertained it."

Do not explain the objection to me; plan how I should teach it. Treat the Mill passage as the hinge: students should first see why a consequentialist might want morality to have a test, then feel how the same test, once applied impartially to ordinary time, money, attention, and affection, seems to colonize the whole of life. The plan should specify the order in which the components must be introduced and what each depends on: impartial welfare as the relevant end; rightness as a standard rather than a personality trait; the distinction between a criterion of right and a decision procedure; the category of supererogation; the difference between doing more good and being required to do more; and the point at which demandingness becomes an objection to the theory rather than a report of weak motivation.

Give me one carrying example for the whole session. It should be mundane enough that no student can dismiss it as a philosopher’s fantasy, but clean enough that uncertainty about outcomes does not swallow the point. I am imagining something like one free Saturday, £80, a sick relative nearby, a flood-cleanup crew across town, paid overtime whose earnings could be donated, and the option of resting before exams; improve or replace this if needed. Say what the example must contain and must not contain: comparable stakes without fake precision, identifiable beneficiaries without melodrama, a genuine choice among permissible goods, no secret knowledge, no trolley-style deaths unless they earn their place, and enough repetition in small choices that students see the problem is cumulative rather than heroic.

Map the misconceptions in the order students will voice them and say exactly where in the sequence I should head each off. Include at least: demandingness means the theory is stressful rather than wrong-making; the objection proves people are selfish; satisficing obviously solves it; rule-consequentialism obviously solves it; if everyone did a little more the problem disappears; integrity is just a fancy name for not wanting to help; and the objection confuses what is wrong with what is blameworthy. For each, state whether I should answer immediately, defer until a later distinction exists, or let it stand temporarily because correcting it too early would prevent the objection from being felt.

Include the interpretive work on the passage: whether Mill’s talk of a test makes consequentialism more vulnerable to the charge that every moment is on moral trial, or whether separating test from deliberative guidance gives a principled reply; whether the reply preserves impartiality or quietly reintroduces permissions for special relationships; and what students should conclude if a theory gives the right verdicts in extreme cases but makes ordinary leisure presumptively suspect. Do not turn the session into a survey of named positions and do not give readings.

End the plan with two checks for understanding: a two-minute written diagnostic in which students classify a new case as too costly, wrongly intrusive, epistemically unmanageable, or correctly demanding; and one oral question that distinguishes those who can state the objection charitably from those who can only repeat that utilitarianism asks a lot. Say what I should explicitly refuse to settle at this level. The last line of your answer should be a one-sentence summary beginning with Summary:.
