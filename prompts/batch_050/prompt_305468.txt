At a philosophy of physics workshop last month I asked what I thought was an innocent question: why is it that mathematics developed for purely internal reasons — group representation theory, say, or the spectral theory of self-adjoint operators — turns out, decades after the mathematics was settled, to be exactly what quantum mechanics needs? A senior participant waved the question off with the old line about the "unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences" (the phrase titles a much-cited essay by the physicist Eugene Wigner, though most people who use it seem to know it as a free-floating slogan). He spoke as though naming the phenomenon dissolved any obligation to explain it. What struck me more, over drinks afterward, was hearing two other attendees deploy the same phrase to opposite ends: one, a theist, took it as evidence of a rational order in nature that naturalism cannot accommodate; the other, a naturalist, took it as proof that mathematics is simply whatever formalism we have found fits the world, so its "effectiveness" is a selection artifact and there is nothing to wonder at.

I would like the slogan taken apart properly. First, lay out the distinct precise theses the phrase could be expressing — distinguishing at least (a) the bare claim that mathematics applies to nature, (b) the stronger claim that it applies more accurately, or in more places, than we had any antecedent reason to expect (and please say "unreasonable" relative to whose expectations, and set by what baseline), (c) the claim that mathematics developed without any eye to application turns out applicable, as opposed to mathematics invented in response to physical problems, and (d) the claim that the applicability is in principle inexplicable rather than merely unexplained. Second, for each thesis, say whether it is a truism, a substantive but defensible claim, or probably false, and whether it stands in need of explanation or instead purports to be one. Third, say which readings, if any, actually license the three uses I witnessed: closing the explanatory question, the theist's inference, and the naturalist's inference — I suspect at least two of the three equivocate between readings, and I would like the equivocation located precisely. Fourth, if no defensible reading supports my colleague's dismissal, sketch what an intellectually honest response to my original question would have to look like — even if the honest answer is that nobody knows. Finally, if you know the essay behind the slogan at all, say which of your readings its author seems to have intended, since I gather his own attitude was closer to wonder than to dismissal.
