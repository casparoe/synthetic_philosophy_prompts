You are a professional philosopher specializing in normative ethics. Think carefully, and begin with a brief brainstorm of possible diagnoses before settling on your answer.

My department's ethics seminar has been stuck for two semesters on the demandingness objection to consequentialism. The consequentialists keep repeating the standard rebuttals: the demands fall on everyone, commonsense morality is itself more demanding than it admits, and no principled cutoff exists between reasonable and unreasonable sacrifice. The objectors keep replying that none of this touches their intuition that a morality which routinely required you to abandon your career and relocate wherever you could do the most good would not be a morality you could live inside. Nobody has moved an inch.

Here is my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the disagreement is not really about how much morality may ask but about what a moral theory is for. The consequentialists treat a theory as a ranking of outcomes that agents then approximate in practice, while the objectors treat it as a code meant to be internalized, lived by, and used to hold one another accountable — and demandingness is only an objection to the second. On this reading, the two sides are answering different questions under one name.

Diagnose the stalled debate rather than adjudicating it. Is the disagreement substantive or verbal? If substantive, is the bedrock a dispute about the function of moral theory, about the structure of moral reasons, or about intuitions neither side can support with further argument? Assess my hypothesis against what each side actually says, including whether the familiar distinction between a criterion of rightness and a decision procedure already dissolves it or merely restates it. Then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a sharper distinction, a formal result, an empirical finding about moral psychology — or argue that no progress should be expected, and why.
