I'm a third-year philosophy student taking a seminar on the epistemology of assertion, and I also sit on the editorial board of our department's undergraduate journal. This semester the board has to draft a "standard of warranted publication": a rule stating what relation an author must bear to a claim before the journal will print it as asserted rather than flagged as conjecture. Two of my fellow editors have been deadlocked for weeks. Editor A holds that the only fundamental rule is a knowledge norm: one is properly positioned to assert that p only if one knows that p. Editor B holds that a rational-belief norm suffices: assertion is proper whenever the asserter justifiably believes p, and unlucky assertions (justified but false, or justified and true but Gettiered) are blameless, not defective. Both have agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and I've volunteered to chair it.

Please draft the joint statement they should produce. I'd like it structured as follows:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign. Please include at least: what an assertion is (as opposed to guessing, hedging, or presupposing); which everyday linguistic data both sides accept as data (e.g., the naturalness of challenging an assertion with a demand for how the speaker is positioned to say that, the felt impropriety of asserting lottery propositions one believes only on statistical grounds, and Moore-paradoxical conjunctions); and what each side concedes the other explains well.

2. A disagreements table with the exact residual points of dispute, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording of its position. At minimum, cover: whether the unlucky but justified asserter violates a norm or merely fails to conform to one; whether "excuse" can be cleanly separated from "warrant"; and whether the norm of assertion is constitutive of the practice or derivable from general epistemic and conversational principles.

3. A cruxes section. For each crux, give a case or argument both editors accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. I suspect the genuine cruxes are: (a) how each view handles selfless assertions, where the speaker justifiably believes a claim their own evidence does not fully support; (b) Gettiered assertions; and (c) whether correction-and-retraction practices track falsity or track lack of justification. If you think one of these is merely verbal, say so and explain why.

4. A final section applying the statement to our actual decision: what each norm would have us write into the journal's publication standard for three concrete cases — a student asserting a historical claim on the strength of a single reputable secondary source; a student asserting survey results they analyzed correctly but on a flawed dataset they had no reason to suspect; and a student passing on, as fact, something a trusted professor told them that turns out to be wrong. Where the two norms diverge in their verdicts, note whether the disagreement from section 2 explains the divergence or whether new issues arise in the practical setting.

The document should be something both editors could actually sign, not a verdict on who's right.
