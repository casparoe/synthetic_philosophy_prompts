I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I’m writing a sidebar after a cosmology-and-philosophy panel. One panelist ended an exchange about Gödel’s rotating-universe solution by saying, “Gödel showed time is ideal.” Nobody challenged her because everyone was tired, but my editor wants to know what that slogan could actually mean.

Please unpack the different precise claims hiding in it. I’m especially interested in separating at least these possibilities: Gödel found mathematically consistent universes containing closed timelike curves; those universes have no single global ordering of all events as earlier and later; local time and causal structure can still be perfectly objective even without that global ordering; the mere possibility of such a world is supposed to tell us something about time in the actual world; and time is “ideal” in the stronger philosophical sense of depending on minds rather than being part of mind-independent reality. Add any important readings I’ve missed.

For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantial but defensible claim, a dubious inference, or just false; explain what work rotation is doing in Gödel’s construction; and distinguish the mathematical existence of a solution from physical possibility and from evidence about our universe. Please also say which readings would actually support the panelist’s apparent conclusion that time is not real, and which would only support something much weaker, like there being no universal now.

Write it for smart readers who can handle a little geometry but not tensor notation, and don’t turn it into a bibliography. If my request is built on a confused premise about Gödel’s arguement, say so plainly rather than smoothing it over.
