I am a novelist, and I am stuck on a scene. One of my characters, a philosopher, is meant to unsettle a dinner guest by arguing that the meanings of our words — and even the contents of our thoughts — are fixed partly by things outside our heads rather than by anything within them. I want her to make the case with a thought experiment of her own devising, which means I first need one that does not already exist in the philosophical literature she and I both know.

Here are the Twin-Earth-style cases already on my list; please do not offer any of them or a re-skin of one:

1. Putnam's original: a near-duplicate planet where the colorless, tasteless stuff in the rivers and lakes is a different compound (XYZ, not H2O), so that Oscar and his molecule-for-molecule twin mean different things by "water" despite being internally identical.
2. The deference case: I cannot tell an elm from a beech, yet my uses of "elm" and "beech" pick out different trees because I defer to experts — content fixed by the division of linguistic labor, no duplicate planet required. (Swapping in some other pair I cannot distinguish — two metals, two diseases, two hardwoods — would not count as new.)
3. Burge's social case: a patient sincerely reports arthritis in his thigh; in a nearby linguistic community where the word covers such ailments, his belief would have had a different content — so thought content depends on communal usage, not merely on the physical environment. (Running the same move on brisket or sofas would not count as new.)
4. Slow switching: a person unknowingly transported to Twin Earth gradually comes to mean XYZ by "water," used to probe how and when content migrates with one's history of being embedded in an environment.

These four exhaust, so far as I can tell, the standard mechanisms on offer: microstructure plus ostension, expert deference, communal usage, and embedding history. What I want is one further case in the same family — a scenario that supports the conclusion that meaning or thought content is partly externally fixed — whose mechanism is none of these four, and which is dramatizable: something I could stage between characters, built around a discovery a person could plausibly make in a scene.

Because I will be comparing candidates as I revise, please give me your answer as a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "case_name": a short name I can use in my notes.
- "scenario": the setup, at most 150 words, in a form I could nearly transcribe into my character's dialogue.
- "externalizing_mechanism": the factor outside the individual's head that fixes content in your case, and an explanation of why it is not microstructure, expert deference, communal usage, or embedding history as deployed above.
- "difference_from_listed": an object with the four keys "water_xyz", "elm_beech", "arthritis", and "slow_switching", each value one sentence stating why your case is not a variant of that one.
- "best_objection": the strongest reply a clever reader — or my skeptical dinner guest — could give to the claim that the scenario shows anything about meaning.
- "dramatic_scene": one or two sentences on how the discovery might actually unfold between characters.

Honesty above flattery: if what you produce is, on reflection, a disguised instance of one of the four listed cases, say so plainly in "best_objection" rather than papering over it.
