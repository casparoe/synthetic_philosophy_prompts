I am presenting a paper at a two-day workshop on explanation in history and the philosophy of causation, and I need to prepare for the question period. My thesis is as follows: historians routinely describe events as overdetermined -- the outbreak of a war, the collapse of a regime, the success of a revolution -- citing several independently sufficient causes, yet I argue that most putative cases of historical overdetermination dissolve on inspection. Either the alleged causes are not individually sufficient (each one needed the others' background cooperation, so what we have is conjunctive causation misdescribed), or they are not genuinely independent (they share upstream common causes, so they are one causal process wearing several descriptions). I conclude that true overdetermination in history is about as rare as it is in the much-discussed firing-squad cases, and that historians should replace the vocabulary of overdetermination with a distinction between causes that are robust (the event would still have occurred had that cause been removed) and causes that are load-bearing (removing it changes the outcome, its timing, or its manner).

The audience: roughly half are metaphysicians of causation, fluent in the counterfactual literature, preemption, trumping, and the standard diagnostics for overdetermination, who suspect historians of sloppy causal talk; the other half are working historians, including two senior scholars of the First World War who regard causal overdetermination as a commonplace of good historiography and are allergic to philosophers telling them their explanations are confused. A few graduate students from both camps will be present.

Prepare my question-period briefing. Give me the eight or so objections this specific audience will actually raise -- not the objections a general philosophy crowd would raise -- ordered by how much damage each would do to the paper if it landed. Phrase each objection the way its likely raiser would phrase it, in that group's vocabulary, and say who in the room is most likely to raise it. For each, give me: what I should concede up front, a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, and a note on where the reply is weakest and could be pressed further. Also tell me the single question I am hoping nobody asks, why it is dangerous, and the best available holding response if it comes anyway. Please write the whole briefing in British English.
