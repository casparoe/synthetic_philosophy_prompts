Act as a careful philosopher of law and ethics with a taste for precise structural analogies. I want you to compare the paradox of blackmail with a puzzle from the philosophy of language, treating the comparison as a live hypothesis rather than a loose metaphor.

Background: the paradox of blackmail is that a blackmail demand combines two acts, each permissible on its own — telling a discreditable truth, and requesting money — into a transaction that is criminal. In semantics, by contrast, the principle of compositionality holds that the meaning of a complex expression is exhausted by the meanings of its parts and their mode of combination; idioms like "kick the bucket" are the standard counterexamples, since something shows up at the level of the whole that was not in the parts.

First, brainstorm briefly in bullet points: at least three ways blackmail might be relevantly like an idiom, and at least three ways it might not be. Then write a short essay addressing the following. (1) If moral impermissibility can emerge from combining permissible acts, is that more or less puzzling than semantic non-compositionality, and why? (2) Take two standard responses to the paradox — that what is wrong is the leverage structure of the threat itself, and that what is wrong is the incentive blackmail creates to seek out and traffic in others' secrets — and restate each as a claim about the mode of combination rather than the parts; does this reframing strengthen or weaken them? (3) Does the analogy give us any reason to think legal norms behave more like idioms than like compositional phrases, or does it collapse under scrutiny?

Keep the essay under 600 words, avoid hedging, and end with a clear verdict on whether the analogy survives.
