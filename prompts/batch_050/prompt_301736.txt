Question
(a) State the independence axiom of expected utility theory precisely, in terms of lotteries and mixtures. [5]
(b) Present the two choice problems from Allais's classic example and show that the commonly observed preference pattern is incompatible with the independence axiom. [7]
(c) Suppose both lotteries in the first problem are each mixed with an independent 50% chance of a new prize, £2 million. Does the independence axiom still constrain the ranking of the two resulting compound lotteries? Explain, and say what, if anything, this tells us about whether the psychological force of Allais's example turns on certainty itself. [8]

Student's answer
(a) Independence: for any lotteries p, q, r and any α in (0, 1], p is weakly preferred to q if and only if αp + (1−α)r is weakly preferred to αq + (1−α)r, where αp + (1−α)r is the compound lottery yielding p with probability α and r otherwise. Mixing both options with the same third lottery cannot reverse the ranking.

(b) Problem 1: A gives £1m for certain; B gives £5m with probability 0.10, £1m with probability 0.89, and nothing with probability 0.01. Problem 2: C gives £1m with probability 0.11 and nothing otherwise; D gives £5m with probability 0.10 and nothing otherwise. Most subjects choose A and D. Normalise u(£0) = 0. Then A ≻ B iff u(£1m) > 0.10u(£5m) + 0.89u(£1m), i.e. 0.11u(£1m) > 0.10u(£5m), whereas D ≻ C iff 0.10u(£5m) > 0.11u(£1m). These contradict each other, so no utility function rationalises the pattern. Equivalently, each option in Problem 1 is a mixture of the corresponding option in Problem 2 with a common 0.89 component, so independence forces the rankings to match. Kahneman and Tversky explain the pattern by loss aversion: the 1% chance of leaving empty-handed in B is coded as a loss relative to the certain £1m, and losses loom larger than equivalent gains. Savage himself reportedly chose A and D when first shown the example, and concluded on reflection that his choices had been mistaken.

(c) The axiom is silent about the modified pair. Independence governs mixtures of lotteries with a common lottery, but £2 million is a single certain outcome, not a lottery, so the axiom does not directly apply to mixtures involving it; any constraint would depend on how one chooses to represent the compound. Behaviourally, though, we should expect the preference for the safe option to weaken in the modified pair, since neither option now offers certainty. This suggests that the psychological work in the original example is done by certainty itself rather than by the algebraic structure of the lotteries.
