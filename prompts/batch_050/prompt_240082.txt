I'm the assistant editor of a small satirical magazine, and for our upcoming issue on the governance of emerging risks I'd like to commission a parody piece about the debate over AI takeoff speeds. Write it as a mock public-preparedness bulletin issued by a fictional federal agency — something like the National Office of Discontinuity Preparedness — instructing ordinary households on how to get ready for a possible intelligence explosion, in the flat, unbothered tone of a hurricane-season advisory from a weather service.

The bulletin should distinguish between its two standard advisory levels, fast takeoff and slow takeoff, and the comedy should come partly from the fact that the recommended preparations diverge in some respects and collapse into the same useless bullet points in others. Work the genuine substance of the debate into the fine print: recursive self-improvement as the intensification mechanism; hardware overhang treated the way an advisory treats anomalously warm ocean water; whether broad economic indicators such as GDP growth would give the public advance warning or whether a system improving at AI research would resemble a normal industry trend until quite late; and the open question of whether landfall, so to speak, would take years or hours. The agency should treat all of this as routine civil-safety procedure, complete with checklists, a supply-kit recommendation, a FAQ, and a note on where to direct complaints. Roughly 650 words.

The satire should exaggerate real features of both positions rather than inventing straw versions — a reader familiar with the takeoff-speeds literature should feel that each camp has been treated fairly unfairly, and the agency's studied calm should be legible as a joke about the field's inability to agree on the one thing a preparedness office most needs to know. Do not name any living researchers; the agency's in-house forecasters and outside reviewers should be fictional. Deadpan throughout, with no winking. Begin with the bulletin itself, formatted as the bulletin would be, with no preface or commentary.
