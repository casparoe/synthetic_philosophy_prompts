I am designing governance rules for autonomous procurement agents and ran into an argument about superrationality that feels both compelling and clearly too strong. Imagine a one-shot prisoner’s-dilemma-like negotiation between two agents whose operators publish a cryptographic hash of the complete deterministic decision policy, the policy’s source code, and all decision-relevant inputs. Each agent can therefore verify that it and its counterpart are exact decision-theoretic copies in this encounter. If both cooperate, each receives 10; if both defect, each receives 1; and if one defects while the other cooperates, the defector receives 20 and the cooperator receives 0. No retaliation, reputation, communication beyond the audit, or iteration is possible.

The argument is: (1) If the two agents instantiate exactly the same deterministic decision procedure and possess the same decision-relevant evidence, then there is no possible encounter of this stipulated kind in which one chooses cooperate and the other chooses defect. (2) A rational agent should choose by comparing only those joint outcomes that are reachable given the facts available to it, not outcomes ruled out by the structure of the situation. (3) Therefore, for either agent, the genuine alternatives are mutual cooperation and mutual defection; the tempting unilateral-defection outcomes are irrelevant because they cannot occur under the stipulated symmetry. (4) Mutual cooperation is better for each agent than mutual defection, so rationality requires cooperation. (5) The same method applies to every interaction among exact copies: delete all asymmetric action profiles as unreachable, then select the best remaining symmetric profile. (6) Thus sufficiently transparent and structurally identical AI systems could not rationally fall into arms races, bidding wars, bargaining breakdowns, or other conflicts with asymmetric or Pareto-inferior outcomes. Conclusion (6) seems false: even identical systems might have different private observations, divergent states, randomization devices, timing differences, or implementation errors, and it is not obvious why formal sameness should eliminate ordinary strategic incentives. Still, I have trouble identifying the first step I should reject. My preference is to preserve the plausible idea that exact-copy recognition can sometimes rationalize cooperation without accepting the sweeping conclusion that rational copies cannot conflict.
