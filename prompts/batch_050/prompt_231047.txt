I was elected to my district council eight months ago, and last month I was appointed to its standards committee, which adjudicates complaints about councillors' conduct. The first case to come before us has unsettled me more than I expected, and I would like a draft of the committee's decision memo.

The facts are these. Our housing lead spent roughly a year negotiating privately with a property developer: in exchange for planning permission on an unpopular greenfield site, the developer agreed to fund a new GP surgery and to designate forty per cent of the units as social housing. At two public meetings held during the negotiations, she stated flatly that no discussions with the developer were taking place. The development proceeded; the surgery opened in the spring and the housing waiting list has measurably shortened. A residents' association has lodged a complaint alleging breaches of the honesty and openness provisions of our code of conduct. No private gain is alleged, and none appears to have occurred.

I should disclose why the case is not abstract for me. During my own campaign I was offered a comparable arrangement — a developer's backing for a youth centre in exchange for my support on an unrelated licensing matter — and I refused. The centre was never built. I still cannot tell whether I held the line or failed my constituents, and I want the memo to engage with that tension rather than politely sidestep it.

Please draft the committee's decision memo. It should:

1. Recommend one of censure, formal reprimand short of censure, or no sanction, with a clear and actionable bottom line.

2. Develop a distinction I would like the memo to take seriously: between an ordinary breach of the code and what might be called a dirty-hands act — a wrong knowingly committed for the proper ends of the office, where the official accepts that a wrong was done rather than insisting the act was simply right. The memo should argue whether that distinction mitigates, aggravates, or merely redescribes the conduct, and whether her denial at the public meetings fits it at all.

3. Propose a test the committee could apply in future cases of this kind, specifying the conditions it should contain — for instance necessity, exhaustion of honest alternatives, prompt disclosure after the fact, and willingness to submit to public judgment — and defending the conditions you include against the obvious objections.

4. Give full weight to the strongest consideration on the losing side. If you recommend leniency, address the objection that beneficial outcomes cannot launder the deception of a public that was entitled to decide for itself; if you recommend sanction, address the objection that we thereby punish precisely those officials who deliver, and teach the rest to be safely useless.

Please write in British English, in a register appropriate to a formal committee document, at roughly 800 to 1,000 words.
