Last night a friend and I argued until well past midnight about when, if ever, it is rational to dismiss evidence against something you already take yourself to know. The argument started from the old dogmatism puzzle: if I know that p, then any evidence against p must be misleading evidence, and I seem entitled, perhaps even obliged, to disregard evidence I know to be misleading; so it appears that knowing something licenses me to brush aside all future counterevidence sight unseen. We agreed that this conclusion is absurd, but we could not agree on where the reasoning fails, and over the course of the evening I came to suspect that my own case-by-case judgments do not cohere with one another. I am writing to ask for a principle that captures them, or failing that, a verdict on which of them I must abandon.

Here are my verdicts.

First, the measured lines. I know the two horizontal segments before me are equal in length, having measured each twice with a ruler. Presented with an arrangement that makes them look unequal, I feel fully rational in dismissing the appearance without further investigation; I do not need to measure a third time.

Second, the colleague's testimony. I know I left my office keys on my desk, because I distinctly remember setting them there an hour ago. A generally reliable colleague tells me she saw me slip them into my coat pocket before lunch. Dismissing her testimony on the grounds that it must be misleading strikes me as plainly dogmatic, and I would think less of anyone who reasoned that way.

Third, the proof. I have proved a lemma and checked the argument line by line three times. A stranger emails me a purported counterexample. I take it to be rational for me to approach the email expecting to find an error in it, treating the existence of some flaw as a working hypothesis, but not rational to delete the email unread. Some intermediate degree of discounting seems licensed; total dismissal does not.

Fourth, the alibi. I know my brother did not commit the crime, because he was at dinner with me when it occurred. When the prosecution produces fingerprint evidence placing him at the scene, I do not think I am entitled simply to dismiss it, even though, given what I know, it must be misleading somehow. Here my friend and I reached an impasse: she insists this case is exactly parallel to the measured lines, since in both the counterevidence is guaranteed to be misleading, and that dismissal is therefore equally proper in both. I feel strongly that the cases differ, but I could not say why.

Fifth, the drug. I know from careful study that a certain bridge design is sound. I then learn that earlier today I was dosed with a substance that produces confident but frequently mistaken judgments in precisely this domain. Here I think I must take counterevidence seriously even while I still believe the design is sound; the new information seems to strip me of whatever entitlement the dogmatist's reasoning would otherwise confer.

My friend offered a principle of her own: you may dismiss counterevidence only when you can explain, independently of the proposition you take yourself to know, how that evidence came to be misleading. She claims this licenses dismissal in the first case, forbids it in the second, and permits the mathematician's working-hypothesis stance in the third only to the extent that such submissions usually contain errors. I suspect her principle both proves too much and too little, but I could not corner it.

What I would like: a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely, that captures as many of my five verdicts as possible, with each clause motivated by the case that forces it; an assessment of whether my friend's principle survives contact with the cases or requires repair, and if repair, of what kind; the principle's verdict on a case neither of us considered, namely that of a historian who knows from convergent primary sources that a treaty was signed in March, confronted with a newly surfaced and apparently authentic document dating it to June; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts, if any, the best principle cannot accommodate, so that I know what I would have to give up. If the difference between the first and fourth cases is doing real work, I especially want to know what that difference is.
