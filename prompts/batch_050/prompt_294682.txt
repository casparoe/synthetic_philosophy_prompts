I need a bit of help drafting something for work. I'm secretary to the board of a big housing co-operative (about 3,000 households, own our blocks, decisions by member ballot at the AGM). We're about to vote on a huge roof-and-cladding renewal programme, £40m-ish, funded through a levy on members, plus some refinancing. Turnout at these ballots is miserable (under 10%) and honestly most people voting have no idea what's in the scheme.

One of our board members, an economist, has tabled a motion for what she calls knowledge-weighted voting: any member can sit a short multiple-choice test on the co-op's finances and on the specific motion, and if you pass, your vote counts double for that ballot. Her motion paper opens with this from John Stuart Mill's Considerations on Representative Government (1861), chapter 1:

"Carry the analogy of mechanical contrivances as far as we will, a man does not choose even an instrument of timber and iron on the sole ground that it is in itself the best. He considers whether he possesses the other requisites which must be combined with it to render its employment advantageous, and in particular whether those by whom it will have to be worked possess the knowledge and skill necessary for its management."

She reads this as Mill's blessing: institutions only function when the people operating them have the knowledge and skill for it, so weighting votes by demonstrated knowledge is just good maintenance. I'm not convinced that's what the passage says -- it reads to me more like a warning that you have to fit the institution to the capacities of the people you've actually got, which could cut against her scheme as easily as for it. The board is split down the middle and I have to write the decision memo before Thursday.

Could you draft it for me, around 800-1,000 words (the Mill quote doesn't count towards that)? It needs to:

- Reccommend a concrete course of action -- adopt, reject, or amend (e.g. pilot it, make the weighting advisory, or replace it with something else entirely) -- with a draft resolution the board can actually vote on.
- Settle who's reading the Mill passage correctly, and say what follows for the proposal either way. Don't just bin it if she's wrong, and don't wave it through if she's right.
- Take the strongest argument on the losing side seriously, whichever way you come down.
- Grapple with the obvious practical-philosophical snags without name-dropping: who writes and marks the test, what an appeal looks like, which members predictably won't pass (shift workers, people whose first language isn't English), whether passing a quiz about a motion is the same thing as judging it well, and what happens to the co-op's legitimacy when down-weighted members still have to pay the levy. Also whether a decision like this even has a 'right answer' a test could track, given it trades off things like affordability against building quality.

Keep it in memo form, headed, and written so a busy board member could skim the recommendation and reasoning in five minutes. Thanks!
