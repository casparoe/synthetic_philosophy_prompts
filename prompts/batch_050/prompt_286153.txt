I’m a retired condensed-matter experimentalist who has lately fallen down the philosphy-of-physics rabbit hole. At our little reading group I tried to explain why swapping two electrons is not like switching two ball bearings when nobody is looking, and my explanation got poetic but probably sloppy. Write a 19-line villanelle about identity and indiscernibility in quantum mechanics. Use these two refrains verbatim: We swapped the names, not the things and The minus sign makes no new fact. Let their meanings shift each time they return: the first should begin as the naïve picture of secretly labeled particles and end by questioning whether there were ever bearers for those labels; the second should begin with the antisymmetry of a two-fermion state and end with the puzzle of a formal change that leaves every measurement untouched.

Make the philosophy do the structural work rather than decorating the poem with quantum vocabulary. Keep separate the inability to tell particles apart, the permutation invariance of the theory, and the stronger metaphysical claim that electrons lack individual identity; don’t pretend the last follows automatically from the first two. Also avoid saying that indistinguishable particles literally merge, occupy exactly the same place, or are merely too small for our instruments. The closing quatrain should leave a real tension: if no possible observation can settle which electron is which, is the question physically empty, or is there a fact of the matter that physics cannot reach? No explanatory prose afterward—I’d rather the poem be a little severe than greeting-card mystical.
