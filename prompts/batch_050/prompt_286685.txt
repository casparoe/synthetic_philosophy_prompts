You are an expert in formal decision theory who has spent years coaching graduate students and job candidates through hostile seminar Q&As. You know the orthodox expected utility tradition from the inside -- von Neumann--Morgenstern, Savage, the representation-theorem literature, the standard replies to Allais -- and you also know its heterodox critics well enough to anticipate where a defense of heterodoxy will be pressed hardest. Think carefully before you write; begin by brainstorming, for your own use, the full space of objections an orthodox audience could raise, and only then select and order the ones that matter.

Here is my situation. Next month I am giving a job talk defending risk-weighted expected utility theory -- the view associated with Lara Buchak -- according to which rational agents maximize not expected utility simpliciter but a rank-weighted sum: outcomes are ordered from worst to best, and a risk function re-weights the probabilities attached to outcomes depending on where they fall in that ordering, so that an agent can be globally risk-averse or risk-seeking in a way that the curvature of the utility function alone cannot capture. My talk makes three claims: first, that this framework vindicates the rationality of common Allais-type preferences that orthodox theory must either condemn as inconsistent or rescue by implausibly re-describing the outcomes; second, that risk sensitivity is an attitude toward the structure of a gamble, not a fact about how much the agent values money, and so cannot be folded into a concave utility function without losing the phenomenon; third, that the view earns its keep normatively by modeling the intuition that the worst-off position in a choice has special deliberative significance.

The audience is a decision theory seminar composed almost entirely of orthodox expected utility theorists. Specifically: two economists trained on the von Neumann--Morgenstern axioms who regard the independence axiom as close to non-negotiable and who know the rank-dependent utility literature in economics; one formal epistemologist whose affection for a theory tracks the elegance of its representation theorem; one philosopher who thinks the standard orthodox responses to Allais (re-describe the outcomes, regret is a consequence too) have never been adequately answered by the heterodox; and several graduate students who have learned the Dutch book and money-pump arguments as refutations of anything that abandons independence.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience -- not the generic objections I would get from a popular audience -- ordered by how much damage each would do to my candidacy if I handle it badly. Phrase each objection the way this audience would phrase it, in their vocabulary (independence, separability, dynamic consistency, resolute choice, betweenness, representation theorems), and identify which audience member is most likely to raise it. For each objection, tell me: what I should concede up front, a reply I can deliver in two to three minutes at the whiteboard, and the point at which that reply is softest and liable to be pressed. I particularly want preparation on the dynamic choice problem -- what happens to my view at sequential decision nodes -- and on the economist's likely reaction that everything I have said was already available in the rank-dependent expected utility literature and therefore is either old news or refuted by the same experiments. Finally, tell me which question I should be hoping nobody asks, and what my best available answer to it is.
