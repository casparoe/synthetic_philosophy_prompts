So I help design the character-education curriculum at a private middle school (grades 6–8), and at our last staff meeting I got pushback that my whole approach contradicts itself. I wrote down my actual positions to bring to a consultant we might hire, and before I do that I want to know if theyre right that the thing doesn't hang together.

A bit of background on where I'm coming from. I'm mostly a Mencius guy with a soft spot for Xunzi on methods. My proof text is a line from the Mencius (Jin Xin I, 7A:5, in Legge's old translation), which I take to be a dig at purely habitual, unreflective goodness:

"To act without understanding, and to do so habitually without examination, pursuing the proper path all the life without knowing its nature - this is the way of multitudes."

OK so here's where I've landed:

1. That passage is basically right: someone who behaves correctly out of drilled-in habit but could never say what they're doing or why is morally second-rate. The end goal of moral education is people who act well *and* understand their own conduct, not well-behaved multitudes.
2. In practice though, our main tool should be Xunzi-style: early, constant, repetitive drill in etiquette and routine — lining up, forms of address, classroom rituals, rehearsed responses to conflicts — started years before the kids can grasp any reasons for it.
3. Human nature is good in Mencius's sense. The moral feelings (compassion, shame, deference, approval/disapproval) are already in every kid as sprouts. Education only ever extends and nourishes what's already there; it can never install a motivation that isn't present.
4. The small number of kids who persistently show no empathy at all — we've had two or three in my time — can't be morally educated, full stop. They should be identified early and put on a behavioral-management track (clear rules, incentives, supervision), not the moral-formation track, because you can't develop what isn't there.
5. Explicit teaching of moral principles or ethical argument is pointless, maybe worse than pointless. Reasons can't move anyone whose feelings aren't already engaged, so lecturing a kid on fairness accomplishes nothing.

A few things I'd like to know, in order of how much I care:

- Which of these are actually in tension with each other, and which just *look* like they are? I suspect some pairings that seem contradictory dissolve if you're careful about what Mencius means by "understanding" and "examination" versus just reciting doctrine — please sort the real problems from the fake ones.
- Rank the real tensions by severity. For each one, tell me exactly what commits me to what, i.e. which premise of mine does the damage.
- For each real tension, what's the cheapest revision that fixes it, and what does it cost me? (E.g. if I have to downgrade drill from "the main tool" to something else, say so and say what I lose.)
- If you had to pick ONE single change that makes the whole set maximally coherent, what is it? Note I don't have a strong attachment to any particular one of these, so don't assume I want to protect #3 or whatever.
- Also, does the passage I'm leaning on actually say what I need it to say? One of my colleagues claims I've misread it — that Mencius is complaining about people who *never* reflect, not ranking habit-based goodness below reflective goodness as such, and that nothing in it is inconsistent with drill-first-understand-later. If she's right that helps me, if I'm right it seems to hurt me, so I need a verdict.

Please write your answer in American English, and be specific — this goes in front of a consultant who's going to charge me by the hour, so I'd rather arrive with the mess already mapped.
