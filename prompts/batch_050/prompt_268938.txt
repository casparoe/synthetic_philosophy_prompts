So I'm on the grants committee of a small animal welfare fund (fictionally, for a piece I'm writing) and I keep getting stuck on a question I want to see hashed out properly. We have one pot of money and two proposals: a stunning-requirement campaign for farmed shrimp (trillions of animals, tiny brains, decent but contested evidence of sentience) and a cage-conversion campaign for pigs (millions of animals, big complex brains, rock-solid evidence of sentience). Where the money should go seems to hinge on whether brain size (or neuron count, or whatever) is morally relevant *at all*, and if so how.

Write a dialogue between two people on the committee who disagree about exactly this:

- Mara defends some version of the scaling view: roughly, that an individual's capacity for welfare (the range and intensity of what it can feel) tracks something about its nervous system, so that, all else equal, a pig's suffering matters more per pig than a shrimp's suffering matters per shrimp, and this can justify funding the pig campaign even though far fewer animals are helped.
- Theo defends the flat view: once a being clears the bar of phenomenal consciousness, one unit of suffering is one unit of suffering regardless of the brain producing it, so given even a modest probability that shrimp feel anything, the sheer numbers swamp everything and the shrimp campaign wins easily.

Requirements, because I've read a million mushy versions of this debate:

- They must actually fight over a principled way to *compare* welfare across species, not just assert their conclusions. I want them to grapple with what a "unit of suffering" even means if brains differ, and whether it makes sense to say a shrimp's worst hour is equal to a pig's worst hour.
- Mara has to deal with Theo's best counterexamples: elephants and whales have bigger brains and more neurons than humans, so does her view say a human life matters less than an elephant's? And octopus neurons are mostly in their arms -- does neuron count even pick out the right thing?
- Theo has to deal with Mara's best pressure points: his view seems to imply that a trillion barely-sentient insects outweigh all mammal suffering combined, and that there's some sharp (or suspiciously vague) threshold where moral status switches fully on. What does he say to someone who finds both absurd?
- Neither is allowed to strawman. Mara is not a cartoon speciesist who only cares about smart animals, and Theo is not a naive utilitarian bot who has never heard of welfare *intensity*. Each should conceed at least one real point to the other.
- End the dialogue with them identifying the one empirical or conceptual question where, if it got answered, they'd actually change their mind -- not with them hugging it out.

About 1200-1500 words. Casual register is fine, they're colleagues grabbing coffee not giving keynote speeches.
