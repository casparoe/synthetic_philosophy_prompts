I'm a novelist -- literary fiction, mostly close third person, lots of dialogue, lots of subtext. A friend tried to explain Brandom's inferentialism to me over beers: the idea that what a sentence means isn't about what it points at in the world but about what it commits the speaker to, what would commit someone to saying it, and what follows from it. And honestly my first reaction was, wait, that's just how dialogue works in a novel. A line means something because of what the character has set up before it, what they're now on the hook for, what the other characters can do with it. So here's what I want: restate inferentialism as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually think in -- character voice, subtext, what a line of dialogue "does" in a scene, the way an offhand remark in chapter two constrains what someone can plausibly say in chapter twenty, Chekhov's gun, the difference between what a character says and what the narration lets you infer. Don't dumb it down, just translate it. Then give me the residue report: which parts of Brandom's picture map cleanly onto fiction-craft, which parts you had to fudge, and which parts just have no counterpart at all. My guess is the stuff about truth and reference breaks first, since my characters aren't answerable to a real world, only to me and to each other -- but maybe I'm wrong about that. And the thing I actually care about: is the residue a gap in the novelist's toolkit, a sign the philosophy is tracking something fiction can't express, or is it possibly the reverse -- something fiction handles natively that the theory is clunky about? If the answer changes how I should think about writing dialogue, definately tell me.
