My sister's favorite sentence is "you can't help who you love." She used it last year to defend dating someone all her friends hated ("it just happened, what was I supposed to do"), and then used it again this month to explain why she's leaving him ("the feeling's just gone, you can't force it"). I pointed out she was using the same line to justify opposite moves and she said "exactly, that's the point," which annoyed me enough that I'm here.

Take the slogan and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making, roughly from most trivial to most radical. For each one I want: (a) the claim stated as an actual thesis, not a vibe, (b) what would have to be true about love, the will, and reasons for it to hold, and (c) a verdict — trivially true, substantive and defensible, probably false, or incoherent.

Some things I specifically want sorted out, since I suspect the slogan hides a bunch of different claims under one hood:

- Is it a claim about who you fall for, about whether you fall at all, about whether you can keep it going once it's started, or about whether you can kill it off? Those seem independent — you could "not help" the start but totally be able to end it, or vice versa. Treat them separately.
- One reading seems to just be "everything you do, you can't help, because determinism," which would make the slogan true of flossing and therefore useless. Flag that one and quarantine it, but also say whether there's a nearby version that isn't trivial — like love being specifically the kind of state that doesn't answer to reasons the way action does.
- Is there a reading where the point isn't about control at all but about justification — i.e., loving someone isn't the sort of thing that needs a defense, so asking her to justify it is a category error? That's different from "I couldn't do otherwise" and I think she might actually mean this one.
- Does the slogan survive the observation that people do seem to talk themselves into and out of love all the time — staying in a relationship, doing the work, noticing the feeling deepen? What's the strongest reading that accommodates that?

Then the payoff: tell me which readings, if any, actually support her two uses of it, and whether any single reading supports both at once or whether the two uses secretly rely on different readings (my suspicion). And give me your best guess at which reading people who say this usually have in mind, even if it's a mushy one. Plain language please, I'm not a philosopher, I just want ammo for Sunday dinner.
