Six months ago I was seconded from our Osaka office to Rotterdam to help integrate a Dutch engineering firm we had acquired, and the experience has turned a philosophical question into a pressing practical one. My Dutch colleagues are direct to a degree that still startles me: a design is "simply wrong", a deadline "unacceptable", a manager's proposal dissected in front of his subordinates. My Japanese colleagues communicate the same criticisms through hedging, silence, and carefully ambiguous formulations that the Dutch side either misses entirely or reads as evasive. I find myself mediating daily, and each camp has privately told me the other has a moral problem, not merely a stylistic one.

In search of a principled way through this, I have been reading Kant's 1797 essay against Benjamin Constant, usually translated as "On a Supposed Right to Tell Lies from Benevolent Motives". Kant argues there that truthfulness is a formal duty owed to everyone without exception, and that even a lie told to a would-be murderer, from the best of motives, leaves the liar answerable for whatever consequences follow, whereas the person who keeps strictly to the truth cannot be faulted for the outcome. Taking this seriously seems to me to settle my workplace dilemma in favour of the Dutch. If truthfulness in one's declarations is an unconditional duty limited by no consideration of expediency, then the softened reassurances I was raised on — telling a colleague his presentation went well when it did not, assuring a client we will "give the proposal every consideration" when we intend to decline it — are simply lies with better manners, and the face-saving indirectness of Japanese business culture is a systematised form of the very thing Kant forbids. On this reading, Dutch bluntness is not rudeness but the one morally defensible policy, and my task as a manager is to retrain the Japanese side rather than translate between the two.

Before I act on this, I want to know whether my reasoning is sound. Does Kant's argument in fact establish what I need it to establish: that indirectness and the benevolent white lie fall under the prohibition just as the lie to the murderer does? And is it correct, as I have assumed, that a culture of directness is thereby a culture of honesty, so that the Dutch and Japanese norms differ in moral worth rather than merely in register? I would rather be told I have misread the essay, or misdescribed the situation, than build an integration policy on either error.
