So I'm a senior and my ethics bowl team made finals, and I volunteered to give our opening case. Basically I argue that advance directives (living wills, DNRs, that kind of thing) deserve less moral authority when someone develops dementia than when they end up in a coma. My argument is: in a coma the current self is just gone, so the earlier self's wishes are the only ones left and they should rule. But with dementia the person still exists and has genuine present preferences — they seem happy, they don't want the feeding tube pulled, whatever — and the old directive should only get veto power if the later self is irrational in the way Ulysses was when he had himself tied to the mast. A content dementia patient isn't irrational though, they're just a different valuer with different values, so the directive shouldn't trump what they want now.

The judges panel at finals is a bioethics prof from the state med school, a hospice nurse, and an elder law attorney.
