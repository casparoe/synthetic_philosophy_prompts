I am a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on personal identity, and for my final essay I want to compare two accounts of the self that strike me as oddly close yet possibly answering different questions: Hume's bundle theory and Dennett's idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity.

My starting point is the Appendix to Hume's A Treatise of Human Nature, where Hume revisits his Book I account of personal identity and confesses that it lands him in a labyrinth he cannot escape. In my own words (please check this paraphrase against the text and correct it if I have misread him): Hume finds himself committed to two principles he can neither render consistent nor bring himself to renounce -- first, that all our distinct perceptions are distinct existences, and second, that the mind never perceives any real connection among distinct existences. He notes that the difficulty would vanish if perceptions inhered in something simple and individual, or if the mind perceived some real connection among them, but his own empiricism forecloses both options. He ends in professed sceptical despair about the section rather than with a repair.

Dennett, by contrast, seems cheerful where Hume is despairing. On his view, the self is not a discovered unifier but a useful fiction: the brain spins narratives, and the self is the abstract point of view -- the center of narrative gravity -- that those narratives posit, much as a physical object's center of gravity is a theorist's posit rather than a further part of the object.

Here is my question, which I would like answered in roughly 800 words (any discussion of the Hume passage does not count toward that length). Does Dennett's move actually solve the problem Hume flags in the Appendix, or does it merely change the subject? One might argue that Dennett dissolves the tension by renouncing Hume's second principle's implicit demand -- we were never going to perceive a real connection, but a constructed, narrative one does the same explanatory work, so nothing is missing. Alternatively, one might argue that Hume's two principles concern metaphysics (what perceptions are and what binds them) while Dennett's narrative self concerns only how the brain represents itself, so the narrative fiction presupposes precisely the bundle of unconnected, distinctly existing processes that generated Hume's despair -- in which case Dennett inherits the labyrinth rather than escaping it. Please take a position on which of these readings is right, and in doing so address who comes closer to a consistent account of the self: is Hume's honest inconsistency a defect that Dennett repairs, or a symptom of a problem Dennett's fictionalism avoids stating rather than resolves?

Finally, please end with a candid note on the verdict's fragility: state which argument or consideration would most plausibly change your mind (for example, a particular reading of the Appendix, or an objection to treating centers of gravity as the right model for fictional posits), and identify the step in your own reasoning where you think error is most likely to have crept in.
