I convene a small political theory workshop, and for its final session two regular participants have agreed to stop arguing past each other and attempt a formal adversarial collaboration. The first is a dimensional realist about left and right. She holds that the distinction names a genuine, psychologically grounded axis of political orientation — anchored, on her telling, in durable dispositions concerning equality versus hierarchy and openness versus order — and she takes as her central evidence the fact that positions on seemingly unrelated questions (taxation, criminal punishment, immigration, religious authority) cluster together far more than chance would allow, that the labels recur across countries and centuries, and that voters can often place a wholly novel issue on the spectrum without being told where their side stands. The second is a coalitional skeptic. He holds that left and right are retrospective labels for contingent alliances stitched together by historical accident — the postwar American fusion of market liberalism with social traditionalism, the European fusion of secular socialism with organized labor — and that each side has no unity beyond oppositional positioning relative to the other. His central evidence is the mutability of content: positions that defined one side within living memory (on free trade, on foreign intervention, on the defense of established institutions) have migrated to the other while the labels survived intact.

Draft the joint statement the two of them should produce. Specifically: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims both can sign, covering at minimum the observed constraint among political attitudes, the documented reversals in each side's content, and whatever both accept about the cross-cultural recurrence of the terminology; (2) a disagreements table in which each residual dispute is stated in wording that the side whose view it describes would itself accept; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party — and, for each crux, a concrete case or body of evidence both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance (candidate material includes psychometric correlations between temperament and ideology, elite-led reversals in which voters followed their leaders across a position, voters' capacity to sort novel issues, and coalition partners who openly disdain one another yet vote together); and (4) an explicit verdict on which portions of their dispute, if any, are verbal — for instance, disagreement over what would count as a 'real' distinction rather than over any shared expectation about the world. Where either position cannot, on its own terms, explain the other side's central evidence, the statement should say so rather than paper over it.
