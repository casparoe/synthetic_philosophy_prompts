Write a dialogue between two philosophers of mind arguing about this case. A lab trains a big recurrent neural network, and let's grant for the sake of argument that it's plausibly having some kind of experience while it runs. Then they "distill" it: for every input sequence it could ever receive, they precompute the network's exact response and store all of it in an astronomically large lookup table. From then on, they run the system purely by looking entries up. One character argues that nothing morally relevant happens during a lookup — all the real computation, and so any suffering or joy, happened once at compile time when the table was built, and querying it is just playback, like replaying a recording of a scream. The other argues that if the original network was sentient, each lookup re-instantiates the very same functional state, so the table version matters just as much — and that the "it all happened at compile time" view has absurd consequences, since it makes sentience depend on the history of how a state was produced rather than the state itself. Have them actually respond to each other's points, and let them dig into where the disagreement really lives: is it the internal causal structure of a computation that matters, the counterfactuals it would support, or its causal history? As a wrinkle, they should also grapple with the version where the table gets built and then never queried — did the experiences happen once, at build time, or not at all? Neither side should be a strawman.
