Got into a long argument with my brother over thanksgiving that I'm still annoyed about, and I want to know if philosophy has already sorted this out or if we were just flailing. Here's the case. Our uncle is constantly lecturing everyone about how disgusting smoking is, how smokers are weak-willed, poison their kids' lungs, burden the health system, the whole speech. Thing is, he's never touched a cigarette in his life and has never wanted to -- cigarettes repulse him, always have. So far, fine, no hypocrisy there. But here's what set my brother off: our uncle is a heavy drinker who's tried to quit maybe five times and failed every time, and he gets very defensive if anyone brings it up. My brother's claim was that the uncle has no business moralizing at smokers, because the only reason he's not one is luck of the draw -- he happened to get hooked on a different vice. Swap the circumstances and he'd be the guy outside the restaurant in the cold. My view was that this is unfair: you can't convict someone of hypocrisy for things they would have done in some alternate life, hypocrisy requires actual inconsistency, actual preaching what you don't practice.

So the configuration I'm trying to get at is: someone condemns a failing, they don't have that failing, but there's good reason to think that if their circumstances, temptations, or luck had been different, they'd have it too -- and everyone involved kind of knows this. It's clearly not classic hypocrisy, since there's no gap between what they say and what they do. It's also not quite the standard "who are you to talk" case, because that usually targets people who've actually done the thing. Is there an established term for this -- some notion of counterfactual or dispositional hypocrisy, or a specific way this shows up in the literature on who's entitled to blame whom? And more importanty than the label: how should we carve it? Does the uncle lose standing to condemn smokers, or does standing only track your actual record, or is there a third option where his blame is permitted but weakened somehow? A related case that might help triangulate: a reformed alcoholic who now condemns drinkers -- he'd ALSO be a drinker under slightly different circumstances (namely, the circumstances he was actually in two years ago), but most people feel his blame is fine, maybe even especially credible. What's the relevant difference between him and my uncle, if there is one? If there's no name for the uncle case, give me your best attempt at one, with a definition sharp enough that it doesn't just swallow ordinary moralizing by anyone with human weaknesses.
