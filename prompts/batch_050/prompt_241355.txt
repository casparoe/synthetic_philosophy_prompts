So in my civics class we ran this mock trial and it kinda broke my brain. A ferry company knows that of the 500 passengers on a crossing, only about 400 paid, but they have no record of who. They pick one passenger at random and sue her for the fare, and they're entire case is: there's an 80% chance she didn't pay. Apparently real courts usually refuse to rule for the plaintiff on nothing but a number like that, even when the math clears the usual civil standard. But then someone in class pointed out that DNA matches are also just giant probability numbers, and courts eat those up.

Write a dialogue between two classmates afterwards who take opposite sides. One thinks pure statistical evidence is a perfectly legit basis for a verdict whenever the probability is high enough -- eyewitnesses misidentify people constantly and nobody can put a clean number on that, so at least the statistics are honest. The other thinks something crucial is missing in the ferry case that no number, not even 99.9%, could supply -- but she has to explain why that missing ingredient doesn't also rule out DNA evidence, or bite the bullet and say courts should be way more suspicious of DNA than they are. Have them actually engage with each other's arguments instead of talking past each other, have them try a couple variations on the case to stress-test their views (like what changes if the passenger wasn't picked at random, or if this were a criminal trial), and by the end figure out whether they still disagree and exactly where.
