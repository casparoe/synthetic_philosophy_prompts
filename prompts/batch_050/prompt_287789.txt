I've been going back and forth on the 2006 Pluto business and I'd like someone to run it through Wittgenstein's family resemblance and language-games stuff, properly, in both directions.

Quick recap of the facts as I have them, correct me where I'm off: the discovery of Eris in 2005, which at the time looked bigger than Pluto, forced the question, and at the IAU's Prague meeting in August 2006 the astronomers voted in a formal definition of planet — three conditions: orbits the Sun, massive enough to be round, and has cleared it's neighbourhood. Pluto fails the third and got moved into a new category, dwarf planet, which the resolution explicitly said is not a kind of planet. Only a fraction of the attendees were apparently still around for the vote. Then came a big public backlash — petitions, t-shirts, indignant letters from schoolkids, at least one US state legislature passing a daft resolution declaring Pluto a planet anyway. And among the astronomers' own grumbles: Earth co-orbits with piles of asteroids, so "cleared its neighbourhood" needs a charitable reading if you don't want to demote half the solar system.

Now the analysis. Forward direction first: map the episode onto the theory. Which different language games were being played — professional planetary science, schoolroom astronomy, pop culture — and how did the same word function in each? Was the vote a move within one game, or an attempt by the players of one game to legislate for all the others? And here's the bit I find genuinely interesting: the IAU tried to convert planet from a family-resemblance concept (learned off exemplars, the nine balls on the poster) into a classical concept with necessary and sufficient conditions. As I read Wittgenstein, a chunk of his point is that plenty of concepts just are the family-resemblance kind, and demanding sharp necessary-and-sufficient conditions for them is a confusion born of craving generality. So is Prague 2006 a natural experiment that tested that claim?

Then the reverse direction, which matters more to me. Does the episode confirm the theory, embarrass it, or hit a case it never anticipated? The confirmation story: the public carried on using planet the old way, use shrugged off the stipulation, meaning-is-use wins. The embarrassment story: technical terms really do get stipulated by professional bodies and nobody blinks — chemists and physicists redefine their constants and categories all the time without petitions — so maybe for some words use just is whatever the relevant institute says, and Wittgenstein undersells institutional control of meaning. And there's a case he never faced: his own central example, game, is a word nobody governs. Planet turned out to be a word with a standards body. Does the theory have the resources for that distinction, or does it just fall silent? Where it's silent, say so rather than forcing it to pronounce.

Three knots I want addressed, not skipped: (i) a dwarf star is a star, but a dwarf planet is officially not a planet — what does that oddity do to the claim that the definition captured anyone's use, including the astronomers' own? (ii) the third criterion is itself vague and arguably family-resemblance-flavoured, so did the IAU actually escape Wittgenstein's picture or just shove the fuzziness down one level? (iii) was the backlash really use resisting stipulation, or sentimental attachment to a ninth poster ball, which the theory might class as irrelevant to meaning?

And be honest about the epistemics: flag the step in your reading where you're least confident, and tell me what would flip your verdict — what would the facts have had to be, or what argument would have to go through, for the episode to count against Wittgenstein rather than for him, or the other way round depending where you land.
