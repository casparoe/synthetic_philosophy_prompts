I'm TAing a first-year jurisprudence course and grading short essays. The question was: "Hart and Fuller disagreed about whether there is a necessary connection between law and morality. Using the Nazi 'grudge informer' case, explain each thinker's position and identify exactly where they part company." Here's one student's answer:

"Hart defended the separability thesis: whether a rule counts as law is a matter of social fact, not moral merit. On his view the 1934 Nazi statute the informer invoked was valid law, however wicked, so the court had no honest choice but to acquit her, since punishing her would mean denying the statute was law retroactively. Fuller countered that law has an 'inner morality' captured by his eight desiderata of legality -- generality, promulgation, clarity, non-retroactivity, consistency, possibility of compliance, stability, and congruence between rules and official action. Because a legal system serving evil purposes necessarily violates these principles, the Nazi regime's enactments failed to count as law at all, and the informer could be punished for what was always murder-adjacent conduct. So Hart and Fuller disagree over whether moral badness can rob a rule of legal validity, and the case shows Fuller's approach avoids Hart's uncomfortable conclusion."
