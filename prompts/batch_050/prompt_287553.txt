So I've been slowly working through the Hart-Fuller debate and I keep hitting the same wall: each side wins on its own scoreboard, and then declares victory. Before I read any more jurisprudance I want the scoreboard itself. Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of what law is has to deliver to count as a theory of law at all. Which cases it has to get right (the grudge informer, orderly-but-wicked regimes, customary law, maybe international law), which distinctions it has to respect (law vs. orders backed by force, law vs. morality, validity vs. goodness, what officials do vs. what they say), which puzzles it has to dissolve or at least not trip over -- with the case or consideration motivating each requirement.

Then the parts I actually care about:

1. Which of the standard demands are genuine desiderata and which are camp prejudices dressed up as neutral criteria? Like, is "the theory must allow that an evil statute can still be law" a real requirement or a positivist axiom? Is "the theory must explain why law claims moral authority over us" real or a natural-law axiom? I want each one adjudicated, not just listed.

2. Are any subsets of the desiderata jointly unsatisfiable? I have a hunch you can't simultaneously hold (a) that identifying the law never requires moral argument, (b) that the grudge-informer verdict was legally right rather than retroactive legislation in disguise, and (c) that something like Fuller's eight principles of legality are merely instrumental for whatever goals a system happens to have. Tell me whether that hunch survives scrutiny, and if not, construct the trilemma (or worse) that does.

3. Which single desideratum does the most work in excluding candidates? I.e. if I could only keep one filter, which one kills the most theories?

Two test cases I made up that I want the list to be usable on, so build the desiderata with these in mind:

- A charter city where the law is whatever a trained model outputs when you feed it the statute corpus plus case history. It's applied consistently, everyone can query it, but no human can state the rules in advance and the model's "reasons" are post-hoc rationalizations. Positivists and Fuller both seem to choke on this, but for different reasons, and I want the desiderata to show exactly where.

- A community running entirely on prediction markets and conditional self-executing contracts -- no legislature, no courts, no officials, just norms enforced by escrow and reputation. Is this law, proto-law, or not law, and which desiderata settle the question (or reveal that the question is confused)?

Then, briefly -- a paragraph each, don't let this eat the answer -- score Hart-style positivism, Fuller's procedural natural law, and Dworkin's interpretivism against the list. What I'm really after is whether the list itself already forecloses one of them, i.e. whether the whole debate is secretly about which desiderata are real.

Where I'm coming from: I basically buy the standard rationalist line that words don't have intrinsic meanings and definitions are about carving reality at its joints, so I'm allergic to both sides trading on raw intuition about the word "law". If the honest conclusion is that "is X law" is the wrong question and there's a better one to ask instead, the desiderata should reflect that -- but I want that conclusion earned, not assumed.
