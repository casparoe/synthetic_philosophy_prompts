Next month I'm presenting at a cross-cultural epistemology workshop, defending a (somewhat updated) version of the Mīmāṃsā position on testimony: roughly, that the validity of a piece of testimony is intrinsic and doesn't need to be established by some independent check, because demanding positive justification for every belief launches a regress, so the default has to be "valid unless defeated" rather than "suspect until proven." The twist that makes people sit up is the authorlessness part: on the classical view the Vedas are apauruṣeya, unauthored, and I want to argue this is actually an epistemic advantage, not a weird theological quirk, because authored testimony inherits the author's possible ignorance, bias, and deceit, so removing the author removes the main source of defeaters. I'll also rely on the companion doctrine that invalidity is extrinsic -- a cognition gets shown false by a later defeating cognition, not by failing some upfront certification procedure.

Here's the audience problem. The room is mostly anayltic epistemologists who work on testimony and know the reductionism/anti-reductionism debate (Hume vs. Reid, Coady and Burge style views) but have never read a word of Kumārila, plus two philosophers of religion from a Christian background who I suspect are waiting to ask why my argument doesn't equally validate the Bible or the Quran, and one person with Buddhist studies training who will definitely know the Dharmakīrti-style critiques of treating scripture as a pramāṇa. So I need objections from people who are sharp but coming at this cold, not from Indologists.
