I am a high school student in a philosophy club, and we recently debated whether “canceling” someone is a form of punishment or merely other people exercising their own freedoms. During the debate, one student repeatedly said that “freedom of speech is not freedom from consequences.” The example we were discussing was a student who makes an offensive but nonviolent post online and is then removed from a club, blocked by classmates, criticized publicly, and disinvited from a school event. No government action is involved, but the consequences are clearly intended to make an example of him and discourage similar speech. I felt that the slogan might be equivocating between several different claims, but I could not articulate exactly what they were.

Please use the following passage from Chapter III of John Stuart Mill’s On Liberty as the anchor for your explanation: “That mankind are not infallible; that their truths, for the most part, are only half-truths; that unity of opinion, unless resulting from the fullest and freest comparison of opposite opinions, is not desirable, and diversity not an evil, but a good, until mankind are much more capable than at present of recognising all sides of the truth, are principles applicable to men’s modes of action, not less than to their opinions.” Does Mill’s argument imply that social criticism, exclusion, boycotts, and deplatforming are generally legitimate ways for people to act on their own judgments? Or does his emphasis on fallibility, half-truths, and the value of diversity give us reasons to worry when informal social pressure becomes organized and punitive?

Please separate the slogan into several precise possible claims—for example, claims about legal rights, moral immunity, other people’s freedom of association, the proportionality of responses, and the difference between criticism and punishment. For each interpretation, say whether it is trivial, plausible, controversial, or likely false, and explain whether it would actually justify the treatment of the student in my example. Please also distinguish between consequences that merely follow from others exercising their liberty and consequences deliberately designed to deter, stigmatize, or incapacitate someone. End by explaining how Mill’s passage complicates the slogan rather than simply settling the issue. Please write your answer in American English, and do not provide a bibliography or outside citations.
