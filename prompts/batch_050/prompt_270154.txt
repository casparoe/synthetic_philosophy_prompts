Ok so here's the deal. A few months ago I spent three agonizing weeks deciding whether to move across the country for a job, finally pulled the trigger, felt great about it, and then my brother-in-law (who's a neuroscientist and honestly kind of a menace at family dinners) told me about the Libet experiments and now I can't stop thinking about it. I've been keeping a list of where my gut lands on different versions of this stuff and I want to know if there's an actual principle underneath, or if my gut is just a mess.

My verdicts, for what they're worth:

1. The original Libet setup -- urge to flex your wrist, brain shows a ramp-up a few hundred milliseconds before you feel the urge. Verdict: totally fine, free will intact. The urge isn't the decision, and who cares what my neurons were warming up to before I noticed anything.

2. The later decoding studies (Soon et al. I think?) where they could predict left vs right button from brain activity like 7-10 seconds before the person felt they'd decided, at like 60% accuracy. Verdict: this one bothers me more than it probably should, but I still say free. 60% is barely better than a coin flip.

3. Hypothetical: the decoder gets to 100%, predicts your "choice" ten seconds before you feel anything. Verdict: that's genuinely free-will-undermining. At that point my "decision" feels like it's just the last thing to hear the news.

4. But -- and here's where my brother-in-law pounced -- my wife can predict with basically 100% accuracy whether I'll order dessert at a restaurant before I've consciously "decided," and I feel zero threat from that. Verdict: benign. Doesn't touch my freedom at all. I can't currently explain why 3 and 4 feel different, and he knows it, and it's annoying.

5. Brain stimulation case: surgeon stimulates cortex, patient's arm rises, patient sincerely says they wanted to move it. Verdict: not free, that's just stimulation wearing a decision costume. The confabulation makes it worse, not better.

6. The veto thing: urge arises, you can still cancel it at the last second. Verdict: this rescues freedom even in the Libet setup, which is part of why case 1 doesn't scare me. Free won't, if that's a real phrase.

7. And honestly I think the whole literature generalizing from wrist-flicks and arbitrary button presses to actual decisions -- like my job move, which involved weeks of deliberation and pros/cons lists -- is overreach. Verdict: even a perfect decoder for meaningless choices wouldn't tell us much about deliberated ones. Though I admit I can't say exactly why deliberation would break the prediction, since it's also just brain stuff happening in time. Which kind of undermines this verdict, I know.

So: is there one principle that explains why 3 scares me and 4 doesn't, that also handles the stimulation case and the veto, and that says something sensible about the arbitrary-choice vs real-deliberation thing? My best guess is something like "prediction only threatens freedom when it bypasses your own deliberative machinery," but my wife's dessert prediction doesn't bypass anything either and I'm not sure the stimulation case fits. If the principled answer is that I have to give up one of my verdicts, tell me which one and don't be nice about it. Also -- and I want this stated explicitly, not implied -- what finding or argument would change YOUR mind about whatever principle you land on? Like where is your reasoning most likely to fall apart, definately include that part.
