I help organize a reading group on the foundations of sentience, and two of our members have spent most of the year talking past each other. One defends a strong form of computational sufficiency: any system that implements the right functional organization -- the right causal-functional organization of information processing -- is sentient, regardless of what it is made of, how fast it runs, or how large or small it is, and a sufficiently faithful simulation of a brain would have the experiences that brain has. The other defends a biological or causal-naturalist skepticism: what matters morally is not an abstract computational description, which she argues is either under-determined (nearly any physical system can, under some mapping, be read as implementing nearly any computation) or satisfied by things that obviously lack experience (lookup tables, recordings, systems whose states merely match a description rather than causing one another), so genuine sentience requires the kind of real, self-sustaining causal dynamics that living brains have and simulations merely describe.

Please draft the joint statement the two of them could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims, including what both accept about the mapping problem for computation, about why intuitions about consciousness are unreliable evidence here, and about the moral stakes of misattributing or failing to attribute sentience; (2) a table of their residual disagreements, each formulated in wording that the side it describes would endorse -- in particular, formulate the naturalist's view without caricaturing it as mere carbon chauvinism, and the functionalist's view without caricaturing it as indifference to causation; (3) a list of the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side -- I suspect the central ones are whether a computation's identity is fixed by its causal role or by its mathematical description, whether there is a principled line between systems that implement a computation and systems that merely encode it, and whether the gradual-replacement intuition pump (neurons swapped one by one for functional equivalents) survives careful scrutiny or begs the question; and (4) for each crux, an argument, thought experiment, or possible future finding that both parties agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction or expected update recorded in advance.

End the document with a short section identifying any disagreement they now regard as verbal rather than substantive -- for instance, whether part of the dispute reduces to different uses of "implements" or "simulation" -- and explain why it is verbal. The deliverable is the constructive text itself, not a summary of the debate, and both parties should be able to put their names to it.
