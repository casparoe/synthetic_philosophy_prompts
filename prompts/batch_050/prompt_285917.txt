I direct a small war memorial that commemorates the dead of a series of twentieth-century conflicts, and my board has asked me to propose a standing policy for one recurring question: whose names belong on our memorial wall? We decide this several times a year, and each decision is effectively permanent, so I want a written decision procedure that any future director or committee can run, rather than a string of ad hoc judgments. The hard cases are not soldiers who died fighting in plainly just defensive wars -- those go on the wall -- but everyone else: volunteers who fought knowingly in an unjust war of aggression; conscripts who fought in that same war under threat of prison; career soldiers of an unjust regime who, in the particular battle where they died, committed no crime and fought discriminately; soldiers of a just cause who committed individual atrocities; and child soldiers.

The procedure I want should be grounded in the actual philosophical dispute over the moral status of combatants, which I understand roughly as follows. On the traditional view associated with Michael Walzer, combatants on both sides of a war are moral equals: having surrendered their right not to be attacked by taking up arms, they wrong no one by fighting, so long as they obey the rules of combat, and it is therefore permissible to honor enemy soldiers as soldiers regardless of their cause. On the revisionist view associated with Jeff McMahan, there is no such moral equality: a combatant fighting for an unjust cause is objectively in the wrong when he kills, however scrupulously he fights, and the familiar excuses -- duress, ignorance, conscription -- mitigate blame without erasing the wrong. I am not asking you to adjudicate between these views. I am asking you to translate the dispute into a sequence of questions my committee can actually ask about a candidate for the wall: which facts about the candidate must be established at each step (voluntariness, knowledge, conduct in battle, availability of refusal), what evidentiary standard suffices when records are seventy years old and archives are incomplete, where the two theories converge so that a decision can be defended to anyone, where they diverge so that the committee must make an institutional value choice, and what form that choice should take -- a presumption with exceptions, a majority vote, a separate category of commemoration, or something else.

A few constraints on the deliverable. It must be runnable by educated laypeople in a single afternoon session per candidate; it must specify what the committee does when a step cannot be answered from the record (default to inclusion, default to exclusion, or defer, and why); it must include a provision for descendants' objections, since we hear from families on both sides; and it should be honest about which steps smuggle the theory-choice back in under the guise of procedure. Apply the finished procedure to three worked cases: a conscript in an unjust war with an unremarkable service record; a decorated volunteer in an unjust war whose diaries show he understood the cause and believed in it; and a soldier of a just cause documented to have executed prisoners once, under orders, late in the war. If any of these applications produces an outcome a memorial committee would find repugnant, say so rather than smoothing it over, and say whether the fault lies in the procedure or in the underlying theory. Finally, if you think the entire request is misconceived -- for instance, if no procedure grounded in this dispute can settle who deserves commemoration, because commemoration is not really about desert -- tell me that directly instead of delivering a procedure you believe is hollow.
