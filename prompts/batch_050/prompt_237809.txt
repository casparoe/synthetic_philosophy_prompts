I'm a second-year grad student and my value theory seminar spent basically four weeks this term on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism -- the question of what all pleasures have in common that makes them pleasures -- and by the end I was genuinely convinced the debate is structurally broken in some way I can't quite articulate. The set-up as I understand it: hedonists want to say that pleasure is the only intrinsic good, so they owe us an account of what unifies things like eating a good meal, finishing a marathon, hearing a symphony, being praised, solving a proof, and the glow of post-workout tiredness. The "felt quality" people say there's some distinctive phenomenal tone present in all of these, varying only in intensity. The "attitudinal" people (my advisor keeps pointing us at Fred Feldman here) say no: the experiences have nothing phenomenally in common, and what unifies them is that the subject takes some positive attitude toward them -- welcoming them, being glad they're happening, wanting them to continue. A couple of holdouts in class went for a desire-based or functionalist gloss instead.

Here's what I mean by structurally broken. Nobody in four weeks actually moved positions. The felt-quality people just kept reporting, with total confidence, that when they introspect across their pleasures there IS a common positive tinge, and they accused the attitudinal side of having coarse introspection or of confusing the object of pleasure with the pleasure itself. The attitudinal side kept reporting, with equal confidence, that introspection reveals no such common tone -- the pleasures of a massage and of a philosophical insight feel nothing alike, and what they share is only the stance we take. Then the debate would restart at square one. It felt less like an argument than like two groups insisting on opposite reports of the same datum, each treating their own report as obviously the data the theory has to save. My tentative hypothesis, and I stress it's just a hypothesis: the disagreement isn't really about phenomenology at all, it's a fight over what counts as data -- first-person introspective reports versus the theoretical need for a unified good -- dressed up as a fight about what experience is like. A classmate disagrees with me and says it's purely verbal, that "pleasure" is being used in a thin technical sense by one side and a thick folk sense by the other, and the whole thing dissolves once you flag that.

So my actual question: what kind of disagreement is this? Verbal, substantive-phenomenological, a clash over what counts as evidence, a case of each side answering a different question (what does pleasure feel like vs. what makes states valuable) under one heading, or bedrock introspective intuitions that can't be argued past? Please diagnose it using what each side actually says rather than just picking a winner, and given your diagnosis, tell me what would even count as progress -- a sharper distinction, an empirical finding from psych or neuroscience, a new thought experiment that could move an attitudinalist, or an argument that this is just what bedrock looks like and nobody should expect resolution. Also say whether you think my hypothesis or my classmate's is closer to the truth, or neither. At the very end, give me your one-sentence diagnosis as the last line, wrapped as \answer{...}, where the ... is a short label for the type of disagreement plus one clause on what progress would require.
