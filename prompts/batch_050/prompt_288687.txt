Ok so in my phil of mind seminar we've been doing the problem of other minds, and I wrote up an argument for our discussion board that I'm now worried proves way too much. Here it is:

(1) The only mind I have direct access to is my own. Anything I know about other minds has to be inferred from outward behavior and physical stuff.
(2) The strength of that inference depends only on how similar the other thing's behavior and body are to mine, since my own case is the only data point where I can actually check behavior against experience.
(3) Other humans are massively similar to me in both respects, so my belief that they have minds is strongly justified.
(4) But similarity comes in degrees, and in the respects that seem relevant to producing mind-like behavior -- complex information processing, reporting internal states, reacting to damage, learning, that kind of thing -- a sophisticated enough AI could match us.
(5) So once such a system exists, I'd be just as justified in believing it has a mind as I am in believing my flatmate does.
(6) That's absurd. Being conversationally indistinguishable from a person shouldn't be enough to settle that something is conscious.
(7) So one of (1)-(4) has to go, and the scary candidate is (3): maybe my belief that other humans have minds is itself only as strong as the analogy, i.e. not that strong.

Which step do I actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If I have to pick what to keep, I'd rather keep (1) and (3) -- I don't want to end up a practical solipsist about humans just to avoid being generous to chatbots. Also our prof keeps dropping hints about Wittgenstein and "criteria" like that dissolves the whole setup, so if the right move is to reject (1), I want to know why that's not just trading one mystery for another.
