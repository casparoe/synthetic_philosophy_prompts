I'm designing a handout for an undergraduate applied ethics unit on what I've been calling "the dropout problem": cases where a collective harm is real and large, but any one person's contribution to it seems to make no detectable difference -- one vote in a national election, one round-trip flight, one chicken dinner, one long shower in a drought. My students reliably split into two camps within the first ten minutes: those who think the math of tiny expected effects settles everything, and those who think the math is somehow missing the point of the question. What neither camp can do is say what exactly they're disagreeing about, so I want a decision tree that forces them to locate their disagreement before they're allowed to defend it.

Please build me that tree. It should start from the shared premise -- a case where my individual act's effect is at most imperceptible -- and end at named positions, with each branch node phrased as a concrete question or case a student could answer without knowing which position it sorts them into (so not "do you accept expected-utility reasoning?" but something like a case where the threshold-lumpiness of the supplier's response is or isn't stipulated). The leaves must include at least: the view that small expected effects fully settle the matter (the act is wrong whenever its expected harm is positive); the threshold/complicity view on which an act can be wrong despite making no difference because of what it participates in; the universalization or generalization view; the expressive or virtue-based view; the view that the obligation is really a collective one that devolves onto members; the deflationary view that the individual act is simply permissible and the felt pressure is a mistake; and the political-turn view that the whole framing is wrong because the operative duty is to push for structural change rather than to clean up one's own consumption. Use these cases at the nodes: the single flight, the single vote, the chicken purchase where the industry demonstrably responds only in discrete production batches, and water use during a declared shortage. Where two of these leaves cannot actually be separated by any question you're willing to put on the tree, flag the pair rather than inventing a spurious node. And where a coherent combination of answers has no named occupant, mark the empty region and say in a sentence whether it's empty because the position is unstable, merely unpopular, or collapses into a neighboring leaf.

Return the whole thing as a JSON object with three fields. First, "nodes": a list where each entry has "id", "question" (the exact wording as it would appear to a student), "distinction" (one sentence on what philosophical fault line the question exploits), and "branches" (mapping each possible answer to the id of the node or leaf it leads to). Second, "leaves": a list where each entry has "position", "route" (the answer pattern that lands a student there), "core_claim" (one sentence), and "price" (the most awkward commitment a student who lands here has to swallow). Third, "diagnostics": an object with "inseparable_pairs" (each with the pair and the reason no clean question divides them) and "empty_regions" (each with the answer combination and the one-sentence diagnosis of why nobody sits there).
