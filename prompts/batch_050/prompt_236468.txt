I am working through the first edition of Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859), and I have gotten stuck on a passage in Chapter IV, a few pages after Darwin first defines natural selection. Here it is:

"As man can produce and certainly has produced a great result by his methodical and unconscious means of selection, what may not nature effect? Man can act only on external and visible characters: nature cares nothing for appearances, except in so far as they may be useful to any being. She can act on every internal organ, on every shade of constitutional difference, on the whole machinery of life. Man selects only for his own good; Nature only for that of the being which she tends."

My difficulty is the following. I have recently come across Daniel Dennett's expression "free-floating rationale": a genuine reason for something's being the way it is — the reason the cuckoo's egg resembles its host's eggs, the reason hearts have valves — that exists without anyone ever having reasoned it or represented it. Dennett presents Darwin's theory as the discovery that the world is full of such rationales: there is a reason for every adaptation, yet no reasoner behind any of them. Now look again at how Darwin actually writes in this passage. Nature is introduced precisely as a someone: she cares about some things and not others, she acts on the whole machinery of life, she tends beings, and she selects for their good rather than her own. Nor is this merely agentive shorthand used to name a mindless process — Darwin is arguing here, on the basis of Nature's imagined dispositions, that her selection is superior to man's. Elsewhere he answered readers who accused him of turning selection into a kind of active power or deity by pointing out that physicists speak of gravity as governing the motions of the planets without being charged with theology — a defense of metaphor as harmless brevity. But brevity does not seem to explain this paragraph, where the personification carries the argument: Nature's greater power and impartiality are stated entirely in terms of what she notices, wants, and cares about.

What I would like help with is this. First, try rewriting the passage without attributing anything to Nature — no caring, no tending, no selecting for anyone's good — while preserving everything Darwin needs it to establish. Can it be done, and if the result loses something, whether persuasive force or explanatory content or the standard by which Nature's selection counts as better, tell me exactly what is lost. I want to know whether the personification is eliminable ornament or load-bearing machinery. Second, the hinge of the passage seems to be the relocation of the good: man selects for his own good, Nature for the good of the being she tends. Is that relocation, from the selector's interests to the selected thing's interests, precisely the move that converts a reasoner's reason into a free-floating rationale? Or does talk of a being's good itself covertly reintroduce a valuer, since good, unlike mere survival, seems to require someone to whom it is good? Third, consider the strange construction "nature cares nothing for appearances" — a negative personification, an ascribed indifference. Is denying Nature a care less metaphorical than granting her one, or is it if anything worse, in that it implies appearances are the sort of thing a selector could care about and so installs the fiction of a possible carer at the very moment Darwin means to dispense with one? And does the drift within these few lines from lower-case "nature" to capitalized "Nature" track anything in the argument, or is it a printer's accident I should ignore? Fourth, if this paragraph really is a description of a free-floating rationale couched in the vocabulary of a reasoner, which specific words would have to change for it to say outright what it allegedly means — and would the corrected version have persuaded anyone in 1859?

One request about the form of your answer. I already know that Victorian scientific prose personifies nature and that biologists still use teleological shorthand and generally get away with it; please do not answer at that level of generality. Work from the actual words of this passage — "cares," "tends," "for his own good," "for that of the being which she tends" — and from the question of whether reasons can exist without reasoners.
