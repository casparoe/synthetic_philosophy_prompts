So I got into this massive argument with my friend Tomas last weekend — he's a historian — and it's still bugging me. We were arguing about when the Anthropocene started. His line is basically that dating it to the mid-20th century is a geologist's power grab that lets colonialism and industrialization off the hook, and that if you take the concept seriously it starts with colonization, or the Columbian exchange, or at least the Industrial Revolution. My position (which I'm now committed to defending, unfortunately) is that the Great Acceleration date is the right one: the post-1950 spike in everything — radionuclides from the bomb tests, plastics, concrete, fertilizer nitrogen, CO2 — leaves a globally synchronous marker in sediments and ice that earlier candidates don't, and that a periodization should be anchored to something the Earth system itself records, not to whenever historians find it morally convenient. As far as I understand, the geologists' own proposal to formalize it as an epoch got voted down recently anyway, so the whole thing is in flux.

Here's the thing: in three weeks I'm giving a talk defending this at our university's environmental studies colloquium, and the audience is a nightmare mix. There will be a couple of actual geologists/stratigraphers who care about golden spikes and formalization protocols, a cluster of environmental historians who I suspect mostly agree with Tomas, and some humanities people who treat "the Anthropocene" as a political-cultural concept and think the dating question is really about who gets blamed. Plus a handful of ecologists who think the whole debate is a distraction.
