My roommate and I TA'd a value theory seminar together and he's been pushing this argument about hedonism that he says is airtight. I think it has to go wrong somewhere, because the conclusion is nuts, but I cant find the weak joint. Here's his argument:

(1) If all pleasures share one phenomenal property -- a common "pleasure-feeling" -- then you should be able to find it by introspection: put a warm bath, a good joke, solving a proof, and the relief when a headache stops side by side and locate the shared ingredient.

(2) Do that and you find nothing shared. These experiences feel completely different from each other. There is no common glow, tingle, or tone.

(3) So pleasure is not a feeling at all. What makes something a pleasure is an attitude toward it: you want the experience to continue, you're pleased that it's happening.

(4) But an attitude can exist with no feeling attached -- I can want my headache-free state to keep going while noticing nothing at all.

(5) So pleasure can occur with zero phenomenal character. Nothing it's like.

(6) So a being could live a maximally pleasurable life while experiencing nothing whatsoever. Pleasure doesn't even require consciousness.

(7) That's absurd, so either we bite it or one of these steps is wrong -- and he swears every step is just a definition or an obvious observation.

Which step do I reject? For what it's worth I'd most like to keep (2), since it matches my own introspection pretty well, and I'd like to keep the assumption that a pleasure you couldn't in principle experience isn't a pleasure. But I want to know what each rescue costs.

Please answer as a professional philosopher who works on well-being and knows the hedonism literature cold. Think carefully: before diagnosing, briefly steelman each step so I can see why it looked fine, then name the step you'd reject and price out the alternatives. Also tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis overall, and single out the one claim in your answer you're least sure about.
