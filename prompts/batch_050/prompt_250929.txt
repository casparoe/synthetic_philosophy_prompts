A bestselling book on habit formation I've been reading devotes a chapter to Benjamin Franklin and summarizes the moral perfection project from his Autobiography like this:

"In his twenties, Franklin drew up a list of thirteen virtues — Temperance, Silence, Order, Resolution, Frugality, Industry, Sincerity, Justice, Moderation, Cleanliness, Tranquility, Chastity, and Humility — and resolved to attain moral perfection by mastering them. Working through one virtue per week in thirteen-week cycles, he tracked his lapses in a pocket notebook, marking each fault with a black spot. The project was essentially religious: Franklin wanted to purge himself of sin in the eyes of God, and his list was his personal distillation of the Christian virtues. In the end he judged the experiment a failure — he never came close to perfection, and he gave the scheme up, concluding that a person's character is fixed and that self-improvement regimens of this kind only produce discouragement. His own later life, with its well-known moral lapses, proves he was right to abandon it."

The author then uses the episode to argue that quantified self-tracking can change behavior but not character, so modern habit apps are fine for flossing but deluded about self-transformation.
