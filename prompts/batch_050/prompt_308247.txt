My city is debating switching from first-past-the-post to ranked-choice voting for municipal elections, and at the last public meeting a guy from the "no" campaign handed out a one-pager he'd written based on an article he'd read about Arrow's impossibility theorem. His summary went roughly like this: in the 1950s the economist Kenneth Arrow proved mathematically that once you have three or more candidates, there is no such thing as a fair voting system -- every method for combining voters' individual rankings into a group ranking must violate at least one basic fairness condition, like respecting unanimous preferences, or like making the outcome between two candidates depend only on how voters rank those two and not on their opinions about some third candidate. The only "methods" that satisfy all the conditions do so by making one person a dictator. Therefore ranked-choice can't actually be fairer than what we have now, it's all equally arbitrary, and anyone claiming otherwise just hasn't understood the math. He also threw in that Arrow's theorem is basically a fancy generalization of the old observation that majority rule can cycle (A beats B, B beats C, C beats A), so really it's been known for centuries that elections are incoherent.

My instinct is that he's right that there's a real and important theorem here but wrong about what it licenses him to conclude, and I'd like to know exactly where before the next meeting, since I want to argue for the switch. Some specific things I can't adjudicate myself: does the theorem actually cover *every* voting method, or only methods that aggregate rankings the way his description assumes? I've heard that systems where you score candidates (say 0 to 5) fall outside it, and if that's true then "every method is unfair" is already an overreach. Does "dictator" mean what it sounds like it means, or is it a technical term doing quieter work? And even granting the whole theorem, does "every rank-aggregating method violates at least one of several plausible conditions" really support "all methods are equally arbitrary," or is that like saying that since every diet has some downside, donuts and vegetables are equally good for you? Tell me which parts of his summary a careful social choice theorist would accept, which parts are misrepresentations or conflations, and whether his conclusion about the ballot measure follows from the parts that survive.
