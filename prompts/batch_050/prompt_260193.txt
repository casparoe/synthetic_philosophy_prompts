So here's the situation. I spent thirty-odd years as an experimental condensed matter physicist, retired in 2019, and then -- mostly out of boredom during lockdown -- fell into a philosophy reading group that meets at the public library every other Thursday. We're a mix of retirees and two grad students, and lately we've been chewing on decision theory. Last month we did the St. Petersburg paradox, which I found cute but not that troubling, since everyone at the table immediately said "well nobody has infinite money, and utility of money flattens out anyway." Fair enough. But then one of the grad students sprung the Pasadena paradox on us -- the one where the payoffs alternate in sign so the expectation isn't even infinite, it's just undefined, since the positive and negative parts both diverge and the sum depends on the order you add things up in. That one genuinely bothered me. The rest of the group shrugged and said it's a mathematician's parlor trick, no real decision looks like that.

I want to push back on that shrug at our next meeting, so here's what I'm asking you to do: construct a thought experiment to my specifications. I want a decision problem where the expected value is undefined in the Pasadena way -- not plus infinity, not minus infinity, undefined because of the conditional convergence -- but where the setup is at least physically realizable in principle: payoffs generated by something like coin flips, a die, a Geiger counter clicking, whatever, with an actual person (a casino owner, an insurance buyer, an investor, I don't care) deciding whether to pay some entry fee. Constraints: no demons, no perfect predictors, no appeal to a being with infinite resources handing over infinite cash -- if the payoffs grow without bound, the story needs some plausible mechanism for that growth (doubling pots, escalating contracts, whatever you like), and you should flag where the idealization sneaks in, because my reading group will pounce on it. Also, and this is the part I care most about: the undefinedness has to come from the payoff structure itself, not from the agent lacking probabilistic information. I don't want a case where the fix is just "get more data."

After the scenario, do the math honestly: show that the positive and negative parts of the expectation each diverge, and show that rearranging or relabeling the same outcomes at the same probabilities changes what answer a naive calculation seems to give -- that's the bit I want to dramatize for the group, since physicists are trained to trust that rearranging a sum is harmless. Then tell me: should a rational agent pay, say, fifty dollars to play this game? Is expected utility theory simply silent here, or does it give a verdict? Say which structural feature of your case is doing all the work, what happens to the case if you remove that feature (does it collapse back into plain St. Petersburg?), and how a defender of expected utility maximization would try to wriggle out -- bounding the utility function, truncating the game, whatever move they'd make -- and whether that move actually rescues the theory or just concedes my point. Keep the scenario itself to a paragraph if you can. I'll be reading it aloud, so make the story vivid enough that a room full of engineers doesn't check thier phones.
