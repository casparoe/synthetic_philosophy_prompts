At a rationality meetup last night someone brushed off Pascal’s mugging with the line: a tiny probability times a huge payoff is still just a payoff. It was being used to say you should hand over $20 to a guy claiming he can remotely torture a astronomical number of simulated people unless you pay, and also that funding some weird x-risk spreadsheet project definately beats bednets if the expected-value column is bigger. This sounds both like decision-theory 101 and like a great way to become a mark.

Can you turn that one-liner into a menu of precise theses it might mean? For each reading: state it formally enough that I can tell what would count against it, say whether it is trivial, plausible-but-substantive, or just false, and say whether it actually supports handing over the $20 or preferring the x-risk project. Please include at least these possible readings, and add any I missed: literal expected-value maximization when probabilities are known; expected-value maximization with subjective credences; a claim that utility must be unbounded; a dominance argument; a risk-aversion-is-irrational claim; a credibility or leverage-penalty rule where absurd claims get discounted for how easily they can be made up; a bounded-utility fix; an anti-fanaticism constraint; and a two-tier view where normal longshots are fine but mugging-shaped offers are not.

Also distinguish the mugging from ordinary low-probability high-stakes choices and from Pascal’s wager without turning this into a bibliography. Tell me which reading the meetup guy probably needs for his conclusion, what that reading costs, and what reply lets me keep expected-value reasoning in normal life without having to pay every confident lunatic. Be explicit about what is settled versus genuinely contested among decision theorists and philosophers, don’t present a contested fix as the consensus, and answer in American English.
