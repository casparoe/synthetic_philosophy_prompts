I teach an upper-level undergrad seminar on explaining mass violence (cross-listed history/philosophy, 18 students, one 75-minute session), and we're in the middle of the Browning–Goldhagen fight. Quick version of where the class is at: Browning's Ordinary Men argues the members of Reserve Police Battalion 101 were mostly unremarkable middle-aged guys, not fanatics, who killed because of conformity, gradual escalation, and not wanting to look weak or shirking in front of their peers — and the chilling detail for Browning is that their commander openly gave them the option to step out before the first massacre and almost nobody took it. Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners replies that the situational stuff is window dressing and the real engine was a culturally entrenched eliminationist antisemitism that made the killing feel like the sensible, even obvious thing to do. My students get the arguments on paper but I don't think they feel how either mechanism is supposed to work from the inside.

Design me an in-class activity that makes them feel the tug of both explanations. What I want specifically:

1. A staged, escalating, low-stakes complicity structure -- some game or task with points or tokens (no real money) where participating in something mildly ugly starts trivial and ramps up by small steps, so nobody ever faces one big dramatic refusal moment, just a series of tiny ones.

2. A built-in analog of Trapp's offer: an opt-out that is genuinely costless in terms of points but publicly visible and socially slightly awkward, so we can see whether anyone takes it and, more interestingly, when.

3. Some way to give Goldhagen's side a fair shot inside the room -- e.g. a between-subjects wrinkle where part of the class has been fed a week of background framing casting the target group (which can be entirely fictional) as dangerous or contaminating. Tell me how to set this up without it being obvious what the manipulation is, and how to read the results: does the primed half escalate faster, opt out less?

4. Rules, materials, timeline for the 75 minutes, and a payoff scheme that creates real incentive without real stakes.

5. A debrief plan that maps specific moments in the session onto Browning's mechanisms (conformity, incrementalism, diffusion of responibility) and Goldhagen's (pre-existing cultural frames doing the work), and that turns the opt-out data into a live argument between the two books rather than me lecturing.

6. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- I'm guessing at minimum: students who immediately smell a Milgram rerun and either refuse to play or ham it up, and the risk that someone gets genuinely upset or feels branded as an executioner in front of their friends. Tell me how to handle each.

7. And be honest about what the activity structurally cannot reproduce: real stakes, ideology marinated in for decades rather than a week, the fact that the actual perpetrators didn't know they were in anyone's experiment. If a classroom game in principle can't distinguish situation from character -- because the students' awareness of being studied swamps both -- say so and tell me what the debrief has to supply by hand instead.
