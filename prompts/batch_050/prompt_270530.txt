I'm a second-year graduate student in a medieval philosophy program, and in two weeks I'm presenting a forty-five-minute seminar paper on Book V of the Consolation of Philosophy. My thesis is that Boethius's solution to the foreknowledge problem does not actually depend on his famous definition of eternity as the simultaneous and complete possession of unending life. The standard reading, which most of my cohort absorbed from survey courses, is that God escapes the dilemma by standing outside time and seeing all events at once. My argument is that this picture is rhetorically foregrounded but philosophically inert: the real work is done by Boethius's distinction between simple and conditional necessity, together with his claim that the mode of knowledge follows the nature of the knower rather than the nature of the known. On my reading, you could grant God an existence inside time and the argument would still go through, because contingency is preserved at the level of the event even when the event is infallibly cognized. I developed this view after noticing, while writing a term paper last semester, that the necessity distinction gets far less attention in the secondary discussion I could access than the eternity doctrine does, and I've become convinced the emphasis is misplaced.

The audience will be my seminar: our professor, a specialist in late antique philosophy who is sympathetic to the standard reading and has told me in office hours that she thinks the eternity doctrine is load-bearing; two classicists who work on the Consolation's literary structure and tend to resist any reading that treats the text as a vehicle for extractable arguments; a medievalist working on Aquinas who will want to know whether I'm attributing my reading back onto Boethius from the thirteenth century; and four other graduate students at various levels of preparation. I expect the professor and the Aquinas student to be the serious threats, and the classicists to be unpredictably dangerous in a different register.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do to the paper if I handle it badly, each phrased the way the person raising it would plausibly phrase it in the room. For each objection, tell me what I should concede without fight, what I should hold, and give me a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, flagging where the reply is weakest. In particular, I want to be ready for the charge that I am reading the necessity distinction with anachronistic precision, for the objection that my reading makes the eternity passages ornamental in a way no charitable interpreter should accept, and for the textual point that Boethius himself seems to present the eternal present as the solution's centerpiece rather than as scaffolding. Finally, include the one question I am privately hoping nobody asks, so I can think about it in advance rather than improvise.
