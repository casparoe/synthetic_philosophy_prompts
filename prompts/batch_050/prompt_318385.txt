I sit on the grants committee of the Carrow Trust, a foundation with £8 million left to disburse before it winds up, and we can fund only one of our two remaining applications. Application A would scale up a proven malaria-prevention programme and is confidently expected to avert around 2,000 deaths. Application B would fund a speculative survey-and-deflection programme aimed at a hypothesised class of hard-to-detect comets; the applicants' own model puts the chance that the programme prevents a human extinction event at about 1 in 10 million, but because the stakes include not only eight billion living people but (they argue) trillions of possible future lives, the expected value exceeds A's by many orders of magnitude. A junior analyst has proposed that the committee adopt a standing "credence floor": any probability estimated below one in a million is to be treated as zero for decision purposes, on the ground that estimates that small reflect the noise in our own reasoning more than facts about the world. The chair calls this an evasion -- a refusal to follow expected value where it leads -- and notes in passing that a floor would let applicants launder tiny probabilities by bundling many small risks into one proposal. Please draft the committee's decision memo: recommend which application to fund, say whether the credence floor should be adopted as standing policy, and engage seriously with the strongest argument on whichever side loses.
