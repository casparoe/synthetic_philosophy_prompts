so my coworker sent me this explainer post about AI safety stuff and there's a paragraph on "low-impact AI" that I keep going back and forth on. it goes like this:

"Researchers worried about AI side effects have found a way around the hardest problem. You'd think telling an AI 'don't cause side effects' requires spelling out everything humans care about, which is hopeless. Instead, the trick is to penalize the AI for changing the world at all. One approach measures how much the agent shifts the state of its environment away from what would have happened if it had just done nothing, and another penalizes it for gaining or losing power -- roughly, for changes in what it *could* do, captured by how its attainable rewards shift. Since neither of these mentions human values, we get side-effect avoidance for free: the agent stays conservative, leaves things alone, and we never had to write down a utility function for the world. The main caveat is that these agents can be a bit passive, but that's the price of safety."

I buy the basic pitch but something bugs me and i cant put my finger on it. a few specific things i want checked:

1. is it actually true that these methods penalize "changing the world"? i thought at least one of them measured changes in the agent's *options* rather than physical changes, which seems like it matters a lot -- a robot that locks all the doors hasn't physically changed much but has changed alot about what anyone can do.

2. the "for free, no human values needed" part smells too good. doesn't the choice of what counts as the baseline (what "doing nothing" means, compared against *when*) smuggle in a judgment? and if you penalize via "attainable rewards," someone chose which rewards count.

3. the caveat at the end -- "a bit passive" -- is that the real worry? i vaguely remember reading that the actual failure mode is agents actively preventing changes they didn't cause, like stopping someone from eating a sandwich because it changes the world. that sounds like the opposite of passive.

So: which parts of the paragraph would a researcher in this area sign off on, which are conflations or overstatements, and once you've corrected it -- does the core claim survive, that "avoid side effects" can be defined without a full specification of value? or does correcting the summary bring value-specification back in through the side door?
