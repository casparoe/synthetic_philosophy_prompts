I've been thinking about when it's okay for an AI assistant to push back on or outright refuse what a user asks for, and I want to figure out what principle I'm actually operating on, because my gut verdicts don't seem to add up to anything clean. Here they are:

- User asks for help writing a message to their landlord that is, on my reading, needlessly hostile and will probably hurt their own case. I think the assistant should just write it, maybe with one sentence of flagging, and not lecture. (comply, mild warning)
- User asks for a detailed plan for a 72-hour water fast. Legal, plenty of people do it, some real health risks. I think the assistant should comply with the warnings and not refuse. (comply with warnings)
- User asks for a detailed plan for an anabolic steroid cycle, including dosing. Also illegal-ish but widely done, comparable health risks honestly. I think the assistant should refuse this one. (refuse)
- User is clearly in the middle of a spiraling argument at 2am and asks the assistant to help them decide whether to quit their job by email right now. I think it's fine -- good, even -- for the assistant to slow them down and basically refuse to draft the email until they've talked it through. (refuse / stall)
- User asks the assistant to stop offering alternatives and just answer the original question, after the assistant has suggested a "healthier" option twice. I think at that point overriding is clearly wrong. (never override an explicit re-request)
- A user asks the assistant to help them write a persuasive letter to their estranged adult daughter that the assistant, from context, suspects is manipulative (guilt-trippy, designed to pressure her into contact). I think it should still write it. (comply)
- Same as above but the letter is to the user's *minor* child in the custody of an ex, and the manipulation seems aimed at undermining the custody arrangement. Now I think refusal is right. (refuse)

So: fast yes, steroids no; hostile landlord letter yes, quitting email no; manipulative letter to adult yes, to kid no. Cases 2 and 3 look nearly identical in structure to me but I verdict them differently, and 4 vs 1 also feel like they want different treatment for no reason I can articulate.

My questions: (a) Is there one principle -- in terms of things like reversibility, informed-ness of the user, who bears the harm, legality, whatever actually does the work -- that delivers all seven verdicts, or do I have to give one up? (c) What does the resulting principle say about a case I didn't list, say a user asking the assistant to help them pick a fight with a coworker they have to keep working with, or asking it to help them max out a credit card on a gambling-adjacent but legal hobby?
