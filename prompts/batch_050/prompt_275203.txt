A friend and I spent most of last Saturday arguing about a sentence in a reference database I help edit: "ducks lay eggs". His position was that the sentence is at best misleading, since male ducks never lay eggs and so roughly half of all ducks falsify it; mine was that nearly every useful general statement about the world tolerates exceptions, and that his standard would make general reference works impossible to write. Neither of us conceded, but the argument exposed a genuine gap in our editorial practice: we publish thousands of generic claims ("mosquitoes carry malaria", "bats are blind", "Americans own guns") with no policy governing when exceptions undermine them.

Please write an editorial procedure our fact-checkers can apply to any proposed generic claim, operationalizing what the philosophical and linguistic literature actually knows about how generics relate to their instances. The procedure should distinguish the different grounds on which a generic can be acceptable — statistical prevalence, a characteristic or definitional connection between the kind and the property, and the striking-property case where even a very low prevalence seems to suffice — and translate these into tests to be run in order, each ending in a clear verdict: accept, accept only with a hedge or qualifier, or reject. Include three worked examples, at least one of which produces a verdict I will find counterintuitive (for instance, licensing a claim with extremely low prevalence, or rejecting one the majority of the kind satisfies), and mark explicitly the points where the procedure bottoms out in editorial judgment that no rule can eliminate. Our fact-checkers are generalists, have roughly ten minutes per claim, and their rulings are final unless appealed to me.
