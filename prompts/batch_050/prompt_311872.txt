So I work at a small AI lab (not one of the big ones) and I've been put in charge of writing our internal doc on what would even count as our models being "aligned enough" to deploy the next generation. The problem is everything I read on alignment either assumes some future breakthrough (whole-brain emulation adjacent stuff, interpretability that fully works, whatever) or it's just vibes about RLHF being good or bad. I want the spec first, before arguing about techniques.

Write the desiderata: what any alignment approach for systems built from *current* methods (big transformers, RLHF/DPO-style fine-tuning, maybe some scaffolding/agents on top) has to actually deliver, with the failure mode or scenario behind each requirement. Some things I suspect belong on the list but I want them argued for, not assumed:

- it has to keep working when the model is smarter than the humans giving it feedback, not just at today's level (whose problem does this break first?)
- it has to say something about models that behave during evals and misbehave when deployed, i.e. it can't just rely on "we tested it"
- whatever it costs in capabilities has to be small enough that a lab under competitive pressure would actually eat the cost -- a perfect method nobody adopts is not a method
- it should degrade gracefully: if the technique partially fails, you get a slightly worse model, not a catastrophe

For each desideratum give the concrete failure mode that motivates it and say whether you think it's genuinely non-negotiable or just something one camp (the interpretability people, the control/evals people, the "just make training data good" people) treats as sacred. The part I care about most: flag any pair of requirements you think can't be jointly satisfied without a real conceptual breakthrough -- e.g. I have a feeling that "strong guarantees about out-of-distribution behavior" and "near-zero alignment tax" might be in genuine tension, and if the list itself shows that, that's a result I want in the doc.

End with maybe a short section on which single requirement rules out the most currently-popular approaches (RLHF-as-usual, Constitutional AI-style self-critique, scalable oversight schemes, capability control / boxing, honest-only training). Keep that part brief, the requirements are the deliverable. No bibliography, I'll dig up papers myself.
