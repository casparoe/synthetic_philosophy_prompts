Last week a friend and I argued into the small hours about a pattern I think I keep seeing in how testimony gets received, and we parted without agreeing even on whether there is anything there to be named. Her position was that I was describing ordinary fallible skepticism; mine was that I was describing something with a distinctive structure, and I'd like to know whether the philosophical literature agrees with one of us.

Two instances of the pattern. First, asylum adjudication: an applicant whose account of her persecution is word-for-word consistent across three interviews months apart is disbelieved on the grounds that genuine traumatic memory is fragmentary and her consistency shows rehearsal; an applicant whose account drifts between interviews is disbelieved on the grounds that inconsistent testimony is unreliable. Consistency discredits, and so does inconsistency. Second, workplace harassment complaints: a complainant who submits a calm, dated, itemized account is told it reads as prepared and strategic, since a genuinely distraught person would not be so organized; a complainant who presents her account tearfully is told she is too emotional to assess events objectively. Composure discredits, and so does distress.

What strikes me is that the disbelief seems fixed in advance, with the justification selected afterward to fit whatever register the testimony happens to arrive in. Detail becomes evidence of coaching and vagueness becomes evidence of poor memory; fluency becomes evidence of calculation and haltingness becomes evidence of confusion. When every possible register can be recruited as grounds for discredit, it starts to look as though the testimony was never really being evaluated at all -- as though the verdict preceded the hearing and the stated reason is decorative. My friend counters that each of these heuristics is individually defensible, that adjudicators genuinely do need to watch for coached witnesses, and that the conjunction of individually reasonable heuristics across different cases does not add up to anything sinister.

Is there an established name for this configuration, in the literature on epistemic injustice or anywhere else? I know it sits near Fricker's notion of testimonial injustice, but the standard version of that idea runs through identity prejudice against the speaker, and I am not sure the moving-target phenomenon requires any prejudice about who the speaker is. It seems able to operate on the manner of testimony alone, and perhaps even to be sincerely deployed by a hearer with no prejudice at all, who simply finds the testimony suspicious and then reaches for whichever register-based warrant is available. If there is no established term for this exact thing, I would like you to coin one and define it precisely, because a bare label will not settle my argument with my friend. In particular, I want the edges marked: how the phenomenon differs from testimonial injustice proper, how it differs from hermeneutical injustice, and how it differs from ordinary and sometimes plainly justified wariness about coached or calculated testimony -- since border guards and trial lawyers would be negligent if they never entertained such doubts. Finally: could a hearer exhibit this pattern without prejudice, and if so, does it still deserve to be called an injustice rather than merely a fallacy, or does the wrong in it lie elsewhere?
