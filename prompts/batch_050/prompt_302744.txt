I have no academic background, but I have been trying to understand functionalism and the idea of multiple realizability on my own. In On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem, Alan Turing writes:

> However, by altering its m-configuration the machine can effectively remember some of the symbols which it has “seen” (scanned) previously. The possible behaviour of the machine at any moment is determined by the m-configuration qn and the scanned symbol S(r). This pair qn, S(r) will be called the “configuration”: thus the configuration determines the possible behaviour of the machine.

My amateur reading is that Turing is not defining memory by the material a machine is made of. A machine’s next behavior depends on its present configuration and input, so two physically different machines could be equivalent if their relevant states and transitions matched. I also realize that this passage is only about computation, not about consciousness, and that it would be a mistake to treat it as proving functionalism about the mind.

Write a deadpan satirical certification manual, about 850 words, titled Manual for the Certification of Mentally Equivalent Systems, issued by an imaginary government agency called the Office of Equivalent Organized Systems. The agency grants “Mind Equivalence Certificates” to systems made from neurons, silicon chips, hydraulic valves, relay racks, or trained pigeons. Format the piece as numbered regulations, including an eligibility section, an inspection procedure, a table of fees, and an appeals process.

The target should be the glib slogan that “organization matters, material does not,” not serious functionalism itself. The humor should arise from the agency’s attempt to turn Turing’s comparatively precise notion of configuration into bureaucratic rules for certifying minds. Without directly lecturing the reader, the regulations should expose the following problems:

1. The same physical system can be described at many levels of organization, so the agency must decide which level makes two systems genuinely equivalent.
2. Perfect behavioral equivalence may still leave open whether anything is felt from the inside.
3. “Material does not matter” cannot mean that literally any collection of objects counts as any system one chooses to describe it as.
4. Insisting that only familiar biological tissue can support a mind would be substrate chauvinism, but approving every imaginable substrate would make certification meaningless.
5. Turing’s account of a machine configuration does not by itself settle whether mental states are computational states.

End with an application that the agency cannot process: a room-sized network of relays whose output exactly matches a certified mind, but whose relays are rearranged every night without changing the relevant transitions. The final regulation should make the unresolved philosophical question clear while remaining funny.
