I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about automated negotiation — software agents haggling over prices, ad placement, cloud contracts, that kind of thing — but the question is older than the tech. A colleague walked me through an idea that apparently exists in the technical decision-theory literature and it broke my brain a little. It goes like this. Sometimes you can put an offer on the table that leaves every single party strictly better off than the status quo. Nobody loses anything, everyone would sign, an outside observer would call it a win-win. And yet, the idea goes, a sensible institution should still refuse to allow deals of that shape. Not for fairness reasons, and not because someone is hiding losses in the fine print. The problem is what accepting deals of that shape does to future incentives: if such offers reliably get accepted, it becomes profitable for people to deliberately engineer the situations that force these offers onto the table, and everyone's barganing position erodes in the long run even though each individual deal really does make every party better off. The crude illustration is ransom: paying up can leave both victim and kidnapper better off than the kidnapper carrying out the threat, which is exactly why you don't want a world where paying is automatic.

Here's what I want for the piece. Construct a thought experiment, set somewhere ordinary — an office, a hospital, a rental market, a school district — where a proposed outcome leaves literally everyone involved better off than doing nothing, and yet a well-designed institution should refuse to permit it, purely for the incentive reason above and not because of any hidden harm. Constraints: no science fiction, no mind-reading, no violence or threats of violence (I want the clean economic version, not the crime version), the deal must be genuinely voluntary for everyone, and the party who profits most from the deal must be the one who engineered the situation that makes the deal attractive in the first place. Then show the work: state the payoffs before and after for each party, walk through why each party would rationally accept, explain why the institution should still block it, and identify which single feature of the setup is doing the work — i.e., what happens to your verdict if you remove that feature, and does the case then collapse into an ordinary win-win transaction that should obviously be allowed? Finish by steelmanning the opposition: how would someone argue that the institution's refusal is just paternalism dressed up in game theory, and what's the strongest reply?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the one feature that makes the whole case work — then give the scenario and the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
