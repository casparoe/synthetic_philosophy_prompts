I was recently promoted to run a department of about forty people, and in the last few months I have noticed a pattern I cannot name, and I would like to know whether anyone has. I will give two instances, because I suspect the abstract version will undersell what is strange about it.

First: one of our projects was failing, expensively, and had been for some time. In one-on-one meetings, every senior engineer told me privately that the project was doomed and added that everyone else obviously knew this too. As far as I can tell they were right on both counts. The project's state came up constantly in corridor conversations, in emails, in small working sessions. Everyone knew, and everyone knew that everyone knew. Yet at the monthly review meeting, month after month, the project sailed through with pro forma updates. Then, at one review, I stated the situation plainly and in so many words. Within a week the project was cancelled, with broad agreement and no drama, as if a switch had been flipped. Nothing about the consequences of speaking had changed. The people in the room were the same people who already knew.

Second: a deadline everyone privately understood to be impossible. A junior analyst mentioned it at a meeting, but hedged -- something like "we might be a bit tight on the timeline" -- and nothing happened. Two weeks later a project manager stated, flatly and unambiguously, that the deadline would not be met, in a meeting with the same attendees. Plans changed that day. Again, the information was not new to anyone in the room. What was new was the way it was said.

I have looked at the obvious candidates and none of them quite fit. Pluralistic ignorance is about people wrongly believing others do not share their view; here everyone correctly believed everyone else knew. Groupthink is about harmony pressure suppressing dissent; but there was no dissensus to suppress, and fear of speaking first cannot explain why behavior changed afterward, since the costs of acting were identical before and after. The Abilene paradox involves the group misreading each other's preferences; here nobody was misreading anyone. So: does this exact configuration -- a fact universally and mutually known, which nonetheless cannot be acted on until it is stated publicly and unambiguously -- have an established name? What precisely does the public statement add that the prior situation lacked? What has to be true of a statement for the switch to flip: does it matter who says it, how hedged it is, whether it could later be plausibly denied? If there is no single established concept, please coin a term and define it, with boundary cases that separate it from the candidates above.

One request about the epistemics of the answer: please be explicit about which parts of the answer rest on settled results (I gather there are formal results in logic and game theory nearby) and which parts are genuinely contested among people who study this -- for instance, any empirical claims about how this plays out in real organizations. Do not present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus.
