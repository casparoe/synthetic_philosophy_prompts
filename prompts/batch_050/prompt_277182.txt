I'm in a graduate reading group on social epistemology, and last week we worked through the reductionism–anti-reductionism debate about testimony, with Hume on one side and Reid on the other. In the Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man, in the part of Essay VI where Reid draws an extended analogy between perception and the credit we give to human testimony, he argues in effect that our inclination to believe what others tell us is an original principle of the mind rather than a conclusion from experience: nature tilts judgment toward belief, and testimony is accepted on its own footing unless something specific weighs against it, just as a perception is. That struck me as attractive — it explains how children learn anything at all, and it avoids the obvious problem that a fully Humean reduction of testimonial justification to personal observation would leave nearly all of my beliefs (my birthday, the capital of Mongolia, the contents of my own medical records) ungrounded. But I tried to turn Reid's picture into a precise argument, and it seems to prove something absurd, and I can't find the misstep. Here it is. (1) Testimony is a basic source of justification, on a par with perception and memory: a hearer is justified in accepting what a speaker says without first assembling positive, non-testimonial evidence that the speaker is reliable. (2) If a source is basic, then accepting its deliverances is the rational default; what acceptance requires is only the absence of undefeated reasons against it. (3) A reason can defeat the default only if it is itself justified — an unjustified suspicion can't cancel a justification. (4) My grounds for distrusting any particular testifier are themselves testimonial somewhere down the chain: I avoid the tabloid because critics I trust panned it; I discount my colleague's rumor because a third party told me he's a fabulist; even my sense that a stranger is shifty leans on things I've been told about people like him. (5) But then those grounds cannot rationally be deployed against testimony as such, since invoking testimony to defeat testimony simply re-instantiates the source whose credit is in question. (6) Therefore I can never be justified in refusing to believe what anyone tells me, absent non-testimonial evidence of unreliability — which, in the ordinary cases, I never have. So I'm rationally required to believe the stranger on the train platform who tells me the line is closed, the email assuring me I've inherited money, and my colleague's rumor alike, until perception hands me a reason not to. That conclusion is gullibility dressed up as principle, and I refuse it — but I want to keep (1) if I possibly can, because it seems to me the only alternative to testimonial skepticism.
