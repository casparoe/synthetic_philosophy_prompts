You are a professional philosopher with specialist expertise in normative ethics, especially consequentialism, the metaphysics of personal identity, and the literature on aggregation. I want you to think carefully and work in two stages: first, privately brainstorm the genuine pressure points in the position described below — the places where the standard replies are weakest or most expensive — and only then write the deliverable.

Here is the situation. I host a philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is the author of a new book defending utilitarian aggregation against the separateness of persons objection. Her book's central move is a dissolution rather than a rebuttal: she argues that the objection covertly presupposes a robust, morally load-bearing metaphysics of the boundaries between persons, and that once one accepts a reductionist view of personal identity — on which what matters in survival is psychological connectedness and continuity, admitting of degrees — intrapersonal aggregation across the stages of a single life and interpersonal aggregation across lives stand or fall together. So, on her view, the objector faces a dilemma: accept aggregation everywhere, or abandon ordinary prudence (she argues no one will pay the second price). She also claims, more provocatively, that the felt force of the objection is entirely explained by the fact that interpersonal harms often go uncompensated — so the objection is really about compensation and distribution, not about separateness at all, and a sufficiently redistributive utilitarianism is immune to it.

Draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions, in the order I should ask them. The questions should do real work on the content of her position, not invite her to restate it. In particular, I want the sequence to accomplish the following:

- Open with a question that gets past the book's framing immediately — no warm-up questions about her intellectual background.
- Include at least one question pressing the prudence analogy at its weakest joint: whether the moral permissibility of trade-offs within a life really depends on the metaphysics of survival rather than on the unity of agency, planning, and the fact that the later self can be compensated by the earlier sacrifice — and whether her view therefore implies that trade-offs become more permissible as psychological continuity degrades (dementia, radical personality change, long time horizons).
- Include at least one question forcing her to say precisely which version of the separateness objection her dissolution targets — the complaint that utilitarianism fails to take seriously the distinction between persons as such, or the complaint that it permits imposing uncompensated losses on some for the benefit of others — and to concede that her redistribution point answers at most one of these.
- Include at least one question about whether her strategy extends to cases involving merely possible persons, where there is no one whose identity-bounds could do the objector's work — and whether that shows the objection was never really about the metaphysics of persons in the first place.
- For every question, give me the reply she is most likely to make (she is quick and has thought about the obvious objections), and then a follow-up keyed to that reply that pushes past it.
- End the sequence with one question about what would count against her view: what result — in the metaphysics of persons, in moral psychology, or in our reflective judgments about cases — would force her to admit the dissolution fails.

Keep the questions themselves short enough to be asked aloud in one breath. Do not include citations, references to specific passages, or a reading list; the questions should turn on the arguments themselves.
