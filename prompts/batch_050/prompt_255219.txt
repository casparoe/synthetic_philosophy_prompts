I am a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar in epistemic logic, and I am currently drafting the evaluative framework for an essay on Fitch's paradox of knowability. What I need from you is the list of requirements that any response to the paradox must satisfy in order to count as an adequate solution — the specification, not a survey of proposed solutions, although a brief closing word on how the main families of response fare against the list would be welcome.

For context, the paradox as it was presented to us: from the knowability principle that every truth is possibly known, instantiate the principle with a conjunctive truth of the form p-and-nobody-knows-that-p; distribute knowledge over the conjunction, apply the factivity of knowledge to the second conjunct, and a contradiction appears inside the possibility operator. The principle then collapses into the claim that every truth is actually known, and the existence of unknown truths — common ground between the parties to the debate — is lost.

The purpose of my essay is to adjudicate between two verdicts: that the paradox refutes the knowability principle outright, or that it merely forces a principled restriction of it. Please choose and frame the requirements with that purpose in view. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or instead a preference of a particular camp. The list should address at least the following, though I expect you to add what I have missed:

- the moving parts of the derivation: which of factivity, distribution over conjunction, the classical step at the end of the proof, and the instantiation of the principle with the conjunctive proposition a solution may legitimately touch, and at what cost in each case;
- the retained kernel: whatever survives must still capture why the principle attracted anyone in the first place — the thought that there is something objectionably mysterious about truths that could not even in principle be known;
- the demarcation problem: a solution that restricts the principle must say which propositions are excluded and why, whether 'those whose being known is inconsistent' marks a principled boundary or a patch, and whether the restriction invites a revenge version of the argument run on the excluded class;
- the generalisation: the same argument template runs for any factive, conjunction-distributive operator, so a solution should either cover the template wholesale or explain why knowledge is the interesting special case;
- the proof's own epistemic status: the argument appears to establish that certain propositions are knowably unknowable, and a solution should say whether that self-application is coherent and what, if anything, it shows;
- the knower: the proof is silent about who knows, when, and with what evidential access, so say whether exploiting that silence is a legitimate move or an evasion.

Then the part I care about most: is some subset of your listed requirements jointly unsatisfiable? If so, identify it and treat that fact as a result in its own right rather than a defect of the list. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate solutions. Keep the depth on the criteria and their motivating cases, since that is where the thinking in my essay will happen; I will write the scoring section myself. Please write your answer in British English.
