When I bring up Wang Yangming's unity of knowledge and action, my go-to defense against the obvious objection (people clearly know lying is wrong and lie anyway, akrasia etc.) is the skill analogy: moral knowledge is like knowing how to swim or play the piano. If someone claims to know how to swim but sinks every time, we just say they didn't really know, they only had secondhand information about swimming. Same with virtue: the person who "knows" filial piety but treats their parents badly never actually knew it -- they knew about it, the way someone can memorize a book about music without being able to play a note.

Audit this analogy for me. What's the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the water, the muscle memory, the coach, the mere book-knowledge, on the moral side? Where does it break? A few things I'm suspicious of myself: skills seem to be about ability while moral knowledge seems tangled up with reasons and justification; you can genuinely possess a skill and just choose not to use it today (a pianist who never touches the piano still knows how), which looks like exactly the knowledge-without-action case the analogy is supposed to rule out; and skill-knowledge plausibly has no truth-evaluable content while moral claims at least seem to. Also: does the analogy smuggle in a theory of what moral knowledge is (something in the heart/mind's dispositions rather than assent to propositions) and thereby assume the unity thesis rather than support it? And Wang's own framing (knowing the good is like loving a beautiful sight or hating a bad smell -- the response is internal to the knowing) isn't really a skill analogy at all, so tell me whether the swimming analogy is actually a faithful gloss of his position or a westernized substitute that changes what's being claimed. If it can't carry the anti-akrasia conclusion, is there a better analogy (perception? appreciating a joke?) that does better, or does the thesis have to be defended some other way?
