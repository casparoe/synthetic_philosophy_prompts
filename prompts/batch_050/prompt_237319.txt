So I help run a little politics-and-science reading group (we meet in a pub, very serious operation) and last week we did the gender-equality paradox -- the finding that countries scoring highest on gender-equality indices tend to have a smaller share of women among STEM graduates, not a larger one. The discussion was a total mess: one person said it proves occupational preferences are innate and freedom just lets them show, another said it's about STEM being a financial safety ladder that women in poorer countries need and women in rich welfare states don't, someone else claimed the whole thing is a statistical artifact of using proportions instead of absolute numbers, and a fourth kept insisting none of this settles anything about justice anyway because preferences themselves are shaped by the society doing the measuring. Everyone agreed with everyone and then contradicted them two minutes later.

I want to make a one-page handout for next time: a decision tree that sorts these positions (and any I've missed) by what people actually believe. Each branch should be labeled with a concrete claim someone can agree or disagree with without knowing any of the labels -- something like "if the correlation evaporates when you count absolute numbers of women in STEM rather than percentages, does your conclusion change?" rather than "are you a preference theorist?" The tree has to at least separate: the measurement skeptics; the view that equality reveals innate preferences; the economic-safety-net view; the view that falling formal barriers make informal stereotyping do more work, not less; and the position that the causal story, whatever it is, doesn't answer the justice question without extra premises (e.g. about wether preferences are endogenous). Around 6-10 leaves max, each with a short name and one sentence on the most awkward thing that position is committed to. If there are combinations of answers nobody seems to hold, note them and say briefly wether that's because the view is incoherent or just unpopular, and if two positions can't be pried apart by any yes/no question, say so instead of forcing it.

Two honesty requests. First, flag which branching questions are doing the real philosophical work and which are just factual questions wearing a trench coat -- I suspect the tree's most interesting splits are the normative ones, and if I'm wrong about that I'd rather hear it. Second, if the premise here is shaky -- say, if the paradox finding is fragile enough that building a taxonomy of what-follows positions is premature, or if the positions aren't really mutually exclusive in the way a tree assumes -- just tell me plainly instead of politely drawing the tree anyway.
