I am a retired molecular biologist who has spent the past two years reading philosophy of economics, and I keep colliding with the sentence "prices reflect all available information." Most recently it surfaced at a reading group on the 2008 financial crisis: one participant deployed it to shut down the suggestion that pre-crisis housing prices could have been systematically mistaken, reasoning that if everyone had known better, the prices would already have been different. I suspect that sentence is doing more work than any single claim could bear. Please lay out, as a menu I can bring to the group, the distinct precise theses this slogan might be expressing — for instance, the near-trivial claim that trading transmits beliefs into prices; the claim that future price movements are unpredictable from public data; the claim that no one can systematically earn above-market returns on public information; the stronger no-trade-style claim that rational agents with shared priors will not trade against one another on private information, so prices must aggregate it; the claim that prices are correct estimates of underlying value; and any normative claim that market prices ought to settle factual or policy disputes. For each reading, say whether it is platitudinous, substantive and defensible, or false, and indicate which readings, if any, would actually license my colleague's dismissal of bubble-talk — and which he was probably relying on without noticing.
