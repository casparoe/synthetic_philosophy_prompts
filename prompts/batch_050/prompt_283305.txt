I’m a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I’m developing a four-part series on whether labels such as “the Middle Ages,” “the Renaissance,” and “the Scientific Revolution” identify genuine historical formations or are mostly convenient—and sometimes distorting—retrospective partitions. I don’t want another general essay saying that all periodization is constructed. What interests me is a more precise question: when historians call something a period, are they claiming to have found a temporally extended “natural kind,” held together by causal mechanisms and characteristic patterns; describing a family-resemblance cluster whose boundaries vary by subject and region; or merely imposing a filing system whose usefulness should be judged pragmatically? The answer may differ for art, science, agriculture, state formation, religion, and ordinary social life, and it may also differ between contemporaries’ self-understanding and what becomes visible only afterward.

Given that framing, which open questions should the series prioritize, and in what order? Please defend a concrete agenda rather than offer a survey. For each proposed question, explain why it is upstream or downstream of the others, how it could be investigated without becoming a bibliographical literature review, what kinds of historical evidence would count for or against competing answers, and how its resolution should change public uses of period labels in textbooks, museums, journalism, and popular histories of science. I would especially like you to consider the possibility of “domain-relative period realism”: perhaps “the Renaissance” is a defensible causal entity for some elite cultural networks but not for most people’s material lives, while “the Scientific Revolution” may be real at the level of practices and institutions even if its dates and geographical center remain unstable. Which parts of that hypothesis are most worth testing?

Please rank the three or four most consequential questions using explicit criteria—importance, tractability, neglectedness, and the number of other disputes that depend on them—and discuss at least one major trade-off, such as global versus European evidence, intellectual elites versus broader populations, or causal explanation versus the history of the labels themselves. Also identify one question that receives disproportionate attention but would, in your view, resolve comparatively little. Finally, state what discovery or argument would most change your ordering: for example, evidence that contemporaries organized their institutions around one of these identities, that apparently synchronous changes had independent causes, or that the modern label has materially reshaped the archive from which historians now reason.
