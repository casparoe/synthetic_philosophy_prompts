I'm a staff engineer at a ~200 person SaaS company, and once a quarter I chair our "deletion review," where teams propose retiring stuff: code paths, features, internal tools, alerts, even old process rules like approval steps. Every meeting goes the same way. Someone says the thing is dead weight, someone else mutters "Chesterton's fence," and then we argue for 45 minutes and usually end up keeping it out of pure fear. Our monolith is turning into a museum and our on-call is drowning in alerts nobody understands, so pure fear isn't a great long-term strategy either.

I want to turn the fence principle into an actual procedure the review board can run in under 30 minutes per item, by people who are not philosophers and mostly haven't read anything. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure just stops with "keep it," and where it has to hand off to a human because the remaining question is about values or risk appetite rather than facts. I also want you to be honest about where the procedure will misfire, because I know it will.

Two wrinkles I'd like baked in:

1. The thing I'm actually scared of is what I've started calling silent load: work an artifact does that appears nowhere in docs, tickets, dashboards, or anyone's job description. The fence is invisible precisely because it's working. The procedure needs explicit steps for hunting silent load -- who to ask, how long to look, and whether we're allowed to run experiments (e.g., announcing a deprecation and waiting to see who screams) and when that's too reckless.

2. A lot of our fences' authors are gone. Left the company, sometimes left the industry. There's nobody to ask why the fence is there. The procedure needs a rule for authorless fences that's different from just "treat as load-bearing," or we'll never delete anything again.

Then run the procedure on three real items from my backlog:

- A feature-flag system built in 2016 that every engineer hates, which we're confident nothing uses anymore because the last flag was flipped two years ago.
- A manual approval step for refunds over $500. Finance swears it's useless because approvers just click yes, and honestly I proposed killing this one myself.
- A nightly batch job whose output no dashboard reads and no one can remember requesting, which costs us about $900/month in compute.

Ideally at least one of these comes out with an answer I won't like, because a procedure that always agrees with me is just me with extra steps. And tell me plainly what the procedure can't do -- what kinds of deletion decisions it's going to get wrong no matter how well we run it.
