Below is where I have landed on Scanlon's contractualism after working through the core theory and the literature on the aggregation problem, and I would like to know whether the package is coherent. My commitments:

1. An act is wrong if and only if it would be disallowed by principles that no one, motivated to find principles for the general regulation of behavior that others similarly motivated could also accept, could reasonably reject.
2. Whether a complaint grounds reasonable rejection is settled by pairwise comparison: the strongest burden a principle imposes on any single person is weighed against the strongest burden its alternatives would impose on any single person. Complaints are never summed across different people.
3. In a rescue case — one stranger stranded in one spot, five strangers stranded in another, I can reach only one group before both are lost, and no special obligations or other morally relevant differences obtain — I am required, not merely permitted, to save the five.
4. I may not kill one healthy person to harvest organs for five patients who will otherwise die, even when success is certain.
5. Contractualism does not demand perfect impartiality in practice: principles no one could reasonably reject will still leave me room to give extra weight to my own projects and to people I love.
6. When I can save only one of two strangers with equally strong claims, fairness calls for a procedure that gives each an equal chance (a coin flip, say) rather than selection by some irrelevant feature of the situation.

My question is whether this set hangs together. I suspect there is strain somewhere in it, but I cannot locate it myself, and I have no settled view about which commitment I would surrender. Please audit the set rather than lecturing on contractualism in general, and be specific about what commits me to what.
