Say you're advising a small foundation that wants to fund about five years of work -- a mix of research and pilot programs -- aimed at figuring out what to actually *do* about policing in mid-sized American cities. Not advocacy, they've already got opinions; what they want is the open questions whose answers would most change what a sensible reform/defund/abolish agenda looks like, so they can spend on those.

Candidate questions include stuff like: how much of what police currently do (traffic enforcement, mental health crises, homelessness response, noise complaints, wellness checks) could be unbundled to other responders without losing whatever public-safety benefit policing provides; whether the crime-reduction effect of police comes mainly from presence/deterrence, from clearance rates, from incapacitation via arrests, or from something else entirely; whether police unions, certification and decertification regimes, and qualified immunity are the real bottleneck such that everything else is deck-chair rearranging; and whether abolitionist proposals quietly presuppose that *some* institution still has to hold the monopoly on legitimate force -- wich would make abolition less a rejection of Weber's framework than a bet about who should hold it -- or whether they genuinely imagine a state (or stateless order) without that kind of concentrated coercive capacity at all. Plus whatever I'm not thinking of.

Which of these, or which others, are genuinely upstream -- in the sense that settling them changes the answer to several downstream questions -- and which are overrated, either because the answer wouldn't move policy much or because we roughly already know it?
