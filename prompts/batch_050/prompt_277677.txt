In my moral philosophy seminar next week I'm presenting a short paper arguing that promises bind only insofar as they give others expectations they would not otherwise have: roughly, the wrong of breaking a promise is exhausted by the wrong of frustrating an expectation you deliberately created, with no irreducible normative power or social convention doing additional work. The audience is a dozen classmates who have just read Hume's account of promises as an artificial virtue and Raz's argument that the obligation to keep a promise doesn't depend on expectations, plus our tutor, who I know is sympathetic to convention-based accounts. Prepare me for the objections I'm actually likely to get, phrased the way this audience would phrase them, in roughly the order they'll arise. For each, tell me what I should concede up front (I suspect deathbed promises and promises nobody relied on are trouble for me), what I should hold firm on, and where my reply is weakest. Include the objection I'm most hoping nobody raises. Finally, if my framing of the position conflates views that the literature keeps apart, or if the task as I've set it is misguided, say so plainly rather than politely working around it.
