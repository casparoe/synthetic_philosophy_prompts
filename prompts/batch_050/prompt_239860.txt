I chair the institutional review board for a university medical center, and I need a decision memo I can circulate before our vote next week. The proposal before us is a randomized, placebo-controlled trial of an experimental compound, code-named PX-41, in adult prisoners convicted of violent offenses. In preliminary work, PX-41 does not suppress aggressive impulses, change moral beliefs, or affect mood. What it appears to do -- for roughly six months after a short course of treatment -- is markedly increase the vividness with which subjects mentally simulate the future consequences of their own actions, including consequences for other people. The investigators frame this as the enhancement of an ordinary, content-neutral cognitive capacity: prospective imagination. They compare it to literacy programs and cognitive-behavioral therapy, and they argue that ordinary informed-consent norms apply.

The sponsor is the state parole authority. Enrollment would be offered to eligible prisoners as a voluntary pathway to "positive consideration" at parole hearings -- not a guarantee of release, and prisoners who decline retain their standard parole eligibility. Half of enrolled volunteers will receive placebo but will still receive the positive consideration. The board is split, and two objections in particular have stalled us. One member argues that any intervention aimed at a prisoner's moral personality is categorically unlike rehabilitation, and that consent given under a prison sentence is compromised no matter how the choice is formally structured. Another raises a finding from the pre-screening interviews that I think deserves more weight than it has gotten: because PX-41 appears to be content-neutral, amplifying simulation of consequences rather than redirecting what a person values, several subjects with high antisocial-trait scores reported that the enhanced simulations made planning future offenses feel more tractable. The investigators concede the drug might, in principle, make a committed wrongdoer a more careful one, and propose an exclusion criterion based on trait scores that their own statistician admits is unreliable.

Draft the board's decision memo: approve, approve with specified conditions, or reject. The memo should state a single clear recommendation the board can vote on; give reasons the investigators could actually respond to rather than general unease about enhancement; address directly whether the consent arrangement is coercive, whether offering positive parole consideration for placebo constitutes a wrongful inducement, and whether the content-neutrality finding changes the moral analysis or merely the risk-benefit arithmetic; and present the strongest case for the losing side honestly before answering it.
