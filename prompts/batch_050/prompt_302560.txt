Write an essay (roughly 1,200–1,600 words) on wild animal suffering and habitat destruction, taking the following passage from Chapter III ("Struggle for Existence") of Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859) as your point of departure:

"We behold the face of nature bright with gladness, we often see superabundance of food; we do not see, or we forget, that the birds which are idly singing round us mostly live on insects or seeds, and are thus constantly destroying life; or we forget how largely these songsters, or their eggs, or their nestlings, are destroyed by birds and beasts of prey; we do not always bear in mind, that though food may be now superabundant, it is not so at all seasons of each recurring year."

Darwin's point in this passage is not about welfare: the concealed destruction he describes is, for him, the engine of natural selection, and elsewhere in the chapter he suggests that death in nature is generally prompt and that the survivors are vigorous and happy. Your essay should press on the gap between Darwin's use of the observation and a welfare reading of it, and should address two connected questions.

First, does the biological picture Darwin sketches — massive juvenile mortality, seasonal scarcity, predation, and the reproductive strategy of producing far more offspring than can survive — support the conclusion that the aggregate lifetime welfare of wild animals is negative? Consider both sides seriously: the argument that most wild animals die young, often painfully, before accumulating much positive experience; and the counterarguments that many lives may contain net pleasure before death, that the subjective experience of small-brained or invertebrate animals is deeply uncertain, and that "dies young" does not by itself entail "suffers greatly." You should also consider whether population dynamics (the fact that numbers are set by resource limits, so that deaths and births roughly balance) matters to the welfare calculation, not just the manner of individual deaths.

Second, if the net welfare of wild animals in an ecosystem is negative, does it follow that habitat destruction — which reduces the carrying capacity of an area and hence the number of animals it supports — reduces total wild animal suffering? Weigh the obvious objection (habitat destruction inflicts painful deaths and displacement on currently living animals, and may simply shift mortality to harsher causes rather than spare anyone) against the population-level argument (fewer future animals born into net-negative lives), and consider whether the two effects can be compared at all given our ignorance about which deaths occur and how bad they are.

Do not end on a non-committal note. Reach and defend a definite verdict on both questions: whether Darwin's picture, updated with what we now know, supports net-negative wild animal welfare, and whether habitat destruction should be expected on balance to reduce or increase wild animal suffering. Indicate how confident you are in each verdict and identify the single consideration that, if resolved differently, would most change your answer.
