I direct methodology at a nonpartisan institute that publishes annual constitutional-health assessments of established democracies. Our analysts are generalists, not classicists, and each country assessment is a two-week desk exercise; the reports get press coverage, so our method has to be repeatable across analysts and defensible when it disappoints someone who wants a headline. I want to ground the assessment in the scheme Polybius lays out in Book 6 of the Histories. The passage I want the procedure built around is this (6.57, in Evelyn Shuckburgh's translation): "But in all polities we observe two sources of decay existing from natural causes, the one external, the other internal and self-produced. The external admits of no certain or fixed definition, but the internal follows a definite order." A little further on, describing a commonwealth past its zenith, he writes that "the desire of office and the shame of losing reputation, as well as the ostentation and extravagance of living, will prove the beginning of a deterioration," with the people then cast as authors of the change once they are convinced they are being cheated.

Please turn Polybius's claim -- that internal decay follows a definite, readable order while external shocks are ungovernable -- into a diagnostic procedure an analyst can actually run on a country dossier. Concretely: the ordered questions to ask, in the sequence his scheme implies; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; how you translate his indicators (rivalry and expense of office-seeking, shame at loss of standing, conspicuous wealth, the people's conviction of being cheated and their subsequent refusal to accept equal standing with their leaders) into features a modern analyst can check without importing their own politics; and a rule for routing events through the external/internal distinction first, since Polybius himself brackets the external, so that wars, sanctions, pandemics, and commodity shocks do not get misread as internal decay. Where his categories simply do not map onto modern institutions -- independent courts, central banks, professional parties, a permanent civil service, mass media -- say so at the exact step of the procedure, and mark where the procedure must hand off to judgment and where it should refuse to answer altogether. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on two hypothetical countries: first, one with spectacular norm violations and a leader who tests every institution, but intact countervailing bodies -- where, if your procedure is faithful to Polybius, it should resist declaring internal decay, which is an answer my funders and the press will not like, and I want to see it defended anyway; second, a quiet, procedurally smooth country where the early Polybian signs are advancing -- and say what follow-up evidence each verdict obliges the analyst to gather. Close by stating what the procedure cannot do: in particular, whether adopting Polybius's "definite order" commits us to his sequence being fixed and terminal, or whether the procedure can remain agnostic about that. Finally, say how confident you are in the procedure overall, and single out the one point in it you are least sure of -- my guess is it will be the translation of his indicators into measurable modern evidence, but tell me where your own doubt actually sits.
