Having recently retired from a career in experimental physics, I have been working my way through the philosophy of religion, and I have become stuck on the Molinist doctrine of middle knowledge. I should like someone to explain where I have gone wrong, because at present the whole apparatus strikes me as redundant.

My understanding is this. The Molinist holds that, logically prior to any decision to create, God knows, for every possible free creature and every possible set of circumstances, what that creature would freely do in those circumstances. God then creates whichever world, given this knowledge, yields the outcomes he intends. So, for instance, God knew that Peter, placed in the courtyard on that particular night, would freely deny Christ, and he created a world containing precisely those circumstances.

Here is my difficulty. In the laboratory, if I know precisely how a system will behave under given conditions, and I deliberately place it in those conditions in order to obtain a particular result, we would ordinarily say that I brought the result about, since the behaviour was fixed by the conditions together with the system's dispositions. The Molinist's God seems to me to be doing exactly this. Yet I gather Molinists insist that the picture is wholly compatible with libertarian freedom and quite different from the Calvinist one, in which God determines the outcome directly. Frankly, the difference looks cosmetic. If the counterfactual about Peter is true before God creates anything, and God then deliberately selects the relevant circumstances, Peter's denial appears to be settled twice over: once by the prior truth of the counterfactual, and once by God's choice of circumstances. Surely the honest conclusion is that Molinism is determinism with an extra epicycle.

I presume I have erred somewhere, as intelligent people have defended the view for centuries, but I cannot locate the error myself. Is my laboratory analogy the culprit — is there some disanalogy between selecting initial conditions for an apparatus and selecting circumstances for a free agent? Or have I misunderstood what middle knowledge is knowledge of? I confess I do not really see how it differs from ordinary divine foreknowledge, since in either case God knows what will happen, so I also cannot see what explanatory work the middle knowledge does that simple foreknowledge could not do just as well. Please set me straight.
