I've been trying to work out what makes pain bad by consulting my reactions to cases, and I'd like you to find the principle — if there is one — behind the pattern. Some background, stated so you don't need to look anything up: neurologists describe a condition called pain asymbolia, typically resulting from damage to the insular cortex, in which patients continue to feel pain much as before. They locate it on the body, rate its intensity, and distinguish burning from stabbing from aching. What has changed is that the pain no longer bothers them. They do not wince, do not ask for analgesics, and will report an intense pinprick with complete equanimity. Please take the standard description at face value: these patients really do have an experience they call pain. The question is what, if anything, is bad about their condition.

Here are my verdicts:

1. My ordinary tension headache: bad, and worse the more intense it is. Nothing interesting here; it's my baseline.

2. The asymbolia patient: not bad at all, or nearly so. The sensation remains, but with the distress entirely gone, nothing of value seems to be missing from his inner life. If he never minded, there's nothing to mourn.

3. A post-surgical patient on morphine who says the pain is still present but no longer matters to her: bad, but much less bad than it was. The badness seems to shrink with the caring — not to zero, but proportionally.

4. A dental patient given an amnestic drug who plainly suffers during the procedure — gripping the chair, flinching — and afterward remembers nothing and is glad it's done: the suffering was bad while it lasted. Later forgetting doesn't retroactively erase it.

5. A masochist who seeks out and enjoys pain in a context of his choosing: not bad while enjoyed. Whatever one says about the tissue damage, his pain, as experienced, doesn't seem to count against his welfare at that moment.

6. A patient hypnotized for surgery who, in the trance, feels the pain but doesn't mind it — yet who, both before the trance and after emerging from it, says he very much wants never to go through that: bad. The in-the-moment indifference doesn't settle it; his settled attitude outside the trance is the one that counts.

7. On a Parfit-style comparison — learning that a severe pain is behind me and forgotten, versus learning that an equally severe one is still to come — I am enormously relieved in the first case and dread the second, and I cannot give this asymmetry up, though I cannot defend it either.

Two tensions in my own verdicts trouble me. Cases 2 and 6 both involve pain without in-the-moment dislike, yet I acquit one and condemn the other. And case 4 says badness is fixed by what the experience is like at the time, while case 7 seems to discount past badness precisely when it is forgotten. I suspect I cannot keep everything.

What I'd like from you: state the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible — precisely, clause by clause, saying which case motivates each clause. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate (I assume at least one of 2, 6, and 7 has to go, and I'd like to know which and why). Then apply the resulting account to two cases I haven't listed: the pain of a dog, which has no higher-order attitudes with which to endorse or disavow its experience, and the injuries of a person with congenital insensitivity to pain, who feels nothing at all when he burns himself on a stove.
