You are a philosopher with experience moderating adversarial collaborations between people who deeply disagree, and you are good at the distinctive craft of it: finding formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted, and separating genuine disagreements from disputes about words. Two colleagues of mine, a political theorist and a political philosopher, have spent a year arguing about epistocracy versus democracy and have finally agreed to write up a joint statement. Your task is to draft the document they should produce. Some background so you pitch it right: the epistocrat defends a specific institutional proposal, not a vague gesture -- a statutory board of policy experts, selected by examination and professional record, holding a suspensive veto over legislation that contradicts settled empirical findings on the matter it regulates, alongside a system in which citizens can earn extra votes by passing a publicly administered exam on basic political and economic facts. The democrat rejects both, but not from a blanket scepticism about expertise -- she happily grants that some people know more than others and that actual voters are often badly misinformed. Her resistance runs through two convictions: that political competence cannot be measured in a way that is independent of the contested values the measure would then be used to adjudicate (any exam must decide which facts are the relevant ones, and that decision is itself political), and that equal suffrage expresses a standing among citizens that is lost the moment the state certifies some of them as second-class participants, whatever the downstream policy gains. The epistocrat, for his part, concedes that history's actual franchise restrictions were bigoted pretexts and insists his proposal must be judged on its own design features, not guilt by association.

Before drafting, brainstorm privately: list candidate agreed claims, candidate disagreement formulations, and candidate cruxes, and stress-test each formulation by asking whether each party would sign the wording of *their own* position as you have rendered it. Then produce the document itself, in three parts. First, a numbered section of claims both can sign -- I expect these to include something about voter ignorance being real and not easily educated away, something about the difference between rejecting a proposal because it is epistocratic and rejecting it because this version of it would be captured or discriminatory in practice, and something about what the historical record of literacy tests does and does not show. Second, a disagreements table: each row a precise statement of one residual disagreement, with the epistocrat's position and the democrat's position each phrased as that person would phrase it themselves -- no strawmen in either direction. Third, a cruxes section: identify the two or three sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one side (I suspect one crux is whether an exam's content can be fixed by a procedure both would call neutral, and another is whether the expressive harm of unequal votes depends on how the excluded citizens themselves perceive it), and for each crux give the test, study, or argument both parties agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance so the agreement cannot be retrofitted. Close with a short verdict on which parts of their year-long argument were, on reflection, verbal rather than substantive, and say why. Do not adjudicate who wins; the deliverable is a text both could put their names to.
