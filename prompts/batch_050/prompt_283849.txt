I sit on my hospital system's drug coverage commitee (we decide what our insurance arm will pay for in borderline cases). A neurologist has petitioned us to cover a stimulant for one of her patients, a 41-year-old air traffic controller who had chemo two years ago and never fully bounced back cognitively. Here's the wrinkle: her neuropsych testing shows a clear drop from her own pre-chemo scores (she happened to enroll in a research study back then, so we have solid baseline data), but her current scores are still comfortably inside the normal range for the general population, a bit above average even. Our policy covers "treatment of disease or injury" and explicitly excludes "enhancement of normal function," without defining either term. The committee is split down the middle: half says restoring her to her own baseline is obviously therapy for a treatment-related injury; half says you can't "treat" someone up to a level most people never reach, and if this drug taking a normal person up a notch is enhancement, then it's enhancement regardless of where she started -- plus covering it invites claims from anyone who feels they used to be sharper.

Draft the decision memo I'll circulate before the vote: approve or deny, with an actual bottom line. Whatever you recommend, engage the losing side seriously, and make explicit what general principle about the therapy/enhancement line you're leaning on -- in particular whether "normal" gets measured against the population or against the patient's own history. Then stress-test your principle against the neighboring case: someone with no injury history who scores at the 40th percentile and wants the same drug to get to the 70th. If your principle covers her too, say why that's acceptable; if not, say what morally relevant feature separates the two cases, since our lawyers will ask exactly that.
