My partner and I are reading Nāgārjuna's Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way in a book club (slow going, we're like a month in), and she came up with an argument last week that seems to prove way too much, but neither of us can find the step to cut. Here it is, cleaned up from the napkin:

(1) Any statement about how things are has to fall into one of four boxes: it says things are a certain way, or they're not that way, or they're both that way and not that way, or they're neither.

(2) Nāgārjuna's own method knocks out all four boxes for whatever you pick. Take motion. Motion can't exist: what's already been traversed isn't being traversed, and what hasn't been traversed yet isn't being traversed either, so there's nowhere the moving actually happens. Motion can't fail to exist either, because then nothing would ever change and there'd be no explaining how yesterday's ice is today's water. "Both" is a straight contradiction and "neither" is just gibberish.

(3) So no statement about motion is true. Not even "there is no motion," since that's just box two, which we already knocked out.

(4) There's nothing special about motion here; you can run the same four boxes for the self, for time, for causation, for whatever you like.

(5) So no statement about anything is true.

(6) But (5) is itself a statement, so it can't be true either — and yet the argument looks valid and each premise looked fine going in, so apparently reason just eats itself and we should all go home.

She takes this to be Nāgārjuna's actual teaching: thinking and talking are bankrupt, full stop. That can't be right — the guy wrote a whole book of verses, which is a weird hobby for someone who thinks statements are impossible. But when I push on the argument I keep getting stuck. I'd really like to keep (1), because the four boxes honestly do seem exhaustive, and I'd like to keep the idea that ordinary talk is mostly fine — I want to say "the bus is late" without commiting a metaphysical crime. Which step do I actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost me as someone who otherwise finds the emptiness stuff compelling?

One more thing that's bugging me: is the move inside (3), where "it's not the case that motion exists" gets treated as one of the views being refuted rather than as the conclusion of the refutation, doing something sneaky? If it is, I want to know exactly what, because I feel like the whole argument might live or die there.
