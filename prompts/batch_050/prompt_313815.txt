So I just read the Iliad with my reading group and I keep thinking about this sociology paper I half remember (I think Campbell and Manning?) about honor cultures vs dignity cultures vs "victimhood cultures" -- the idea being that honor cultures handle insults through personal violence, dignity cultures through ignoring slights or going to court when it really matters, and victimhood cultures through public appeals to third parties where claiming victim status is itself a way to get standing.

Read the Iliad through that lens. It's obviously an honor culture textbook case, but I want the reading to go deeper than "Achilles got insulted so he sulked." Like: when Achilles refuses Agamemnon's embassy in Book 9, is that honor logic working *correctly*, or is it Achilles breaking the honor system's own rules -- he's been offered the full compensation package the system prescribes and he still says no. And what about the fact that the whole plot is basically a public complaint drama? Achilles doesn't actually take revenge on Agamemnon, he appeals to his mom, who appeals to Zeus, which looks weirdly like the "appeal to powerful third parties" move the victimhood-culture paper describes. Is the poem secretly more complicated than the honor/dignity/victimhood trichotomy?

Also: the paper (as I remember it anyway) treats dignity culture as kind of the mature default and gets a lot of pushback from people who think honor cultures got something right about self-respect. Does the Iliad settle anything there? I'm genuinely torn -- Achilles' rage causes mass death and even he seems to half regret it by the end with Priam, but the alternative where he just shrugs off Briseis seems like it would have made him a doormat in that world. Don't just flatten the poem into an illustration of the theory; if the poem complicates the theory, say where.
