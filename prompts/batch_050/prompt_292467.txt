I've been keeping a private list of cases where an assertion strikes me as proper or improper, and I'd like to know what single norm of assertion, if any, lies behind my verdicts — or else which verdicts I should abandon. Here they are.

A colleague tells me, on the basis of the published schedule she's just read, that the 3:15 train to Glasgow has been cancelled. I take her assertion to be perfectly proper, even though schedules sometimes change after publication and she couldn't rule that out. By contrast, when a friend who has checked the winning numbers announces that his lottery ticket lost — a claim as probable as almost anything in daily life, but one he hasn't yet verified by scanning the ticket — I feel his assertion was improper, and that "it hasn't won yet, almost certainly" is what he should have said.

When a doctor, on strong but incomplete evidence, tells a patient "it's nothing serious," I think the assertion is improper even if the evidence would fully license a flat assertion of the same proposition in casual conversation — something about the stakes changes what's permissible. When a historian asserts a date she's misremembered, despite having checked it in three reliable sources that were all corrupted by the same printing error, I say her assertion was blameless and proper, even though what she said was false. Yet when a man asserts that his house will not burn down this year, reasoning solely from his insurance policy's generosity, I judge the assertion improper even if the house does in fact survive.

Finally: someone who says "it's raining, but I don't believe it is" asserts improperly no matter what the weather is doing, and someone who later says "I told you the bridge was safe, and it was, but I shouldn't have said it because I hadn't inspected it" seems to me to be making a coherent and correct self-criticism.

Is there one precise norm of assertion — a rule about what epistemic position a speaker must be in to assert outright — that delivers all of these verdicts? If so, state it exactly, show me which of my cases each clause of it is doing work on, and tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: a teacher who flatly asserts a well-attested historical claim she herself has never independently investigated, knowing only that it appears in every standard textbook. If no single norm covers everything, tell me which verdict you'd overturn, in whose favor the revision falls, and whether the surviving account is the knowledge norm, a truth norm, a justified-belief norm, or something more contextualist — and explain why the runner-up fails on my cases rather than merely on hypothetical ones.
