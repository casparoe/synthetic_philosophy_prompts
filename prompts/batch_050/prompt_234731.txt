I spend a fair amount of time on rationalist forums, and a while back I bookmarked a comment — by someone whose object-level philosophy I usually respect — summarizing the "wrong kind of reasons" problem from metaethics. I've been meaning to figure out whether the summary is trustworthy before I let it shape my understanding, so I'm reproducing it here:

"Fitting-attitude accounts say that for something to be good (admirable, amusing, enviable) just is for it to be fitting to favor it (admire it, be amused by it, envy it). The wrong kind of reasons problem is a knockdown objection: suppose a powerful demon credibly threatens to torture you unless you admire him. Then you have a reason to admire him — but obviously he isn't admirable. So reasons to have an attitude don't track the fittingness of the attitude, and value can't be analyzed in terms of fitting attitudes. Defenders usually reply by distinguishing 'right-kind' reasons from 'wrong-kind' ones and saying only the former figure in the analysis, but since the only way to tell which reasons are the right kind is to check whether the object actually has the value in question, the reply is circular and the analysis collapses. The upshot is that the buck-passing program is dead: value cannot be reduced to reasons, reasons must be prior, and 'fittingness' turns out to be just a placeholder for an unanalyzed normative notion dressed up as an explanation."

Something in here feels off to me, but I can't localize it. The demon example strikes me as a genuinely interesting case rather than a mere sophism, so I'd expect a fair summary to take it seriously — yet the leap from "here is a hard case" to "the whole research program is dead" seems fast, and the circularity charge seems like exactly the sort of move that sounds decisive in a forum comment but that a careful philosopher would have anticipated. I'd like an accuracy audit I can act on: which parts of this summary would a defender of fitting-attitude analyses accept as correct, which parts are misrepresentations (and of what kind — conflation, overstatement, an objection upgraded to a refutation, a technical distinction glossed in everyday terms), and where exactly the demon's-threat reasoning goes wrong if it does. Does the conclusion that reasons must be prior to value follow from the accurate parts, or does it depend on the inaccurate ones? And if I wanted to pass an ideological Turing test as a fitting-attitude theorist, what would the strongest honest version of the objection in this comment look like?
