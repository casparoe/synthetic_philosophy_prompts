The exam question was this: In the centipede game, two players alternately choose to take (ending the game, with the mover receiving the larger share) or pass (letting both piles grow and handing the move over). (a) Explain how backward induction leads to the conclusion that the first player takes at the very first opportunity. (b) Why has this conclusion struck many people as paradoxical? (c) Does the paradox cast more doubt on backward induction as a standard of rationality, or on the assumption that rationality is common knowledge between the players? Defend your answer.

And here is the student's answer, which I have to mark out of ten:

At the final node the second player faces a choice between taking, which gives her the larger pile, and passing, which gives her the smaller one, so if she is rational she takes. The first player can foresee this: at the penultimate node, passing would hand the move to someone who will take, leaving him worse off than if he takes now. Reasoning backwards in this fashion, each player takes at every node they control, and the game ends at the first move. This outcome is the unique Nash equilibrium of the game, since for any other profile of strategies some player could do strictly better by deviating. The paradox is that the equilibrium leaves both players much poorer than they would have been had they passed for a while. In this respect the centipede is essentially the prisoner's dilemma in sequential form: rational play lands on a Pareto-inferior outcome, and the mechanism is the same in both games, because taking at the first opportunity is the dominant strategy — it yields a slightly larger share whatever the other player intends. As for (c), the blame lies with the common knowledge assumption. Backward induction is just arithmetic once that assumption is granted, and arithmetic cannot be at fault. If the second player ever actually finds herself with the move, the assumption has already been falsified, so the theory simply has nothing to say about what she should do — which means the supposed paradox never really arises, since it only bites if we demand that the theory guide players through situations its own hypothesis rules out. Experiments in which subjects pass for several rounds before anyone takes confirm that actual people fall well short of the rationality the theory assumes.
