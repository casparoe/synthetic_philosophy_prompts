I serve on an independent research ethics committee that reviews randomized clinical trial protocols. We meet monthly, typically reviewing five or six trials per session — a mix of drug, device, surgical, and behavioral studies, some academic and some industry-funded. The members are clinicians, a biostatistician, a pharmacist, and two lay representatives; nobody has formal training in ethics, and we usually have under forty minutes of deliberation per protocol. When we get this question wrong in one direction, patients are randomized to treatments there was already reason to believe inferior; in the other, we block trials that would have resolved real uncertainty, and patients keep receiving treatments chosen by habit and marketing.

Our charter requires us to confirm that a trial is ethically permissible before randomization, and the concept we have inherited for this is equipoise. The problem is that it comes in at least three readings that diverge in practice. On the first, a trial is permissible when the individual enrolling clinician is genuinely uncertain which arm is better for the patient in front of them. On the second, it is permissible when there is honest, sustained disagreement within the relevant expert community about the preferred treatment. On the third, what matters is neither anyone's state of mind nor the sociology of the profession but the state of the evidence: the trial is permissible when the existing evidence does not warrant a conclusion that one arm is superior. These come apart constantly. Surgeons can be split evenly between two operations because disagreement tracks training lineage rather than data, even when the best available evidence moderately favors one. Conversely, a field can be unanimous on the strength of theory and anecdote while the actual evidence is thin. We need to know which of these we are committed to before we can be consistent, and whether the answer is one of them or some hybrid.

Please write us a decision procedure we can genuinely run inside a forty-minute review. It should give the questions in order; state what counts as a sufficient answer to each (what we should demand from investigators — prior systematic reviews, surveys of practitioner opinion, mechanistic rationale, and how to weigh these against each other); specify where the procedure terminates in rejection; and mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated, with an account of what a legitimate judgment call looks like at those points so that two different committees applying the procedure would usually agree. It must address three recurring complications: placebo controls where an effective treatment already exists; trials whose justification rests on expert disagreement that may itself be unresponsive to evidence; and the mid-trial problem — if equipoise licenses starting, does its erosion oblige stopping, and whose job is it to watch for that. The output of the procedure has to be writable as a one-page rationale a lay member can co-sign and that survives appeal by a disappointed investigator. End with a frank statement of what the procedure cannot settle.

Then apply it to three protocols, showing the procedure working step by step:

1. A twelve-week trial of a new antihypertensive against placebo. Approved effective drugs exist; the sponsor argues the new compound may be cheaper and better tolerated, and points to weak preliminary evidence of that.
2. A trial randomizing patients between two established surgical procedures for the same condition. Practicing surgeons are divided roughly evenly, but the division correlates strongly with where each surgeon trained, and the best observational evidence moderately favors one of the procedures.
3. A trial in a field where every expert we consult recommends the same standard intervention, but no adequate controlled study of it has ever been done and the supporting evidence is case series and physiological reasoning.

At least one of these applications should produce a verdict we will find uncomfortable — a trial we would like to approve that the procedure blocks, or one we would prefer to reject that it permits. Where that happens, say so explicitly rather than softening the procedure to avoid it, and tell us whether the discomfort is a reason to distrust the procedure or a reason to distrust our instincts.
