Write me a dialogue between two philosophers arguing about Kant and the murderer at the door. One is a proper Kantian who thinks lying is wrong full stop, even to the murderer. The other thinks that's mad, but is a sharp consequentialist rather than a lazy one, so they actually engage with the Kantian's reasons rather than just going "but obviously the victim dies".

Here's the twist I want baked into the conversation: at some point the Kantian floats what they call the reverse bluff — instead of lying about where your friend is hiding, you tell the murderer the exact truth ("he's in the cellar"), because you reckon the murderer will assume you're lying and go search the attic instead. The consequentialist should pounce on this: is the reverse bluff actually a lie? If it's permitted, hasn't the Kantian just smuggled in outcome-based reasoning through the back door? And if it's not permitted, what exactly is the prohibition even latching onto — the falsehood, the intent to deceive, or something else?

A few requirements:

- Both characters should be equally competent. Neither should win decisively, and neither should be a strawman — the Kantian in particular should come across as thoughtful rather than as a cartoon rules-robot.
- They should respond to each other's actual points. Real back-and-forth, building on what's been said, not two monologues stapled together.
- Feel free to have them introduce small variations on the case (e.g. what if the murderer is notoriously gullible, what if you merely say something literally true but misleading, what if you stay silent) where it helps them press a point.
- Keep it roughly 500-800 words, natural sounding speech, no narrators or stage directions beyond the names.
