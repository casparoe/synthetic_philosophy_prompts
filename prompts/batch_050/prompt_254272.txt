I manage a small team (11 people) at a software company and last month a joke in our Slack channel blew up badly enough that HR got involved. Nobody was named in the joke, but it was about a topic one person here is going through IRL, and now I've been roped into rewriting our "humour at work" guidelines, which honestly read like they were copy-pasted from somewhere. The current draft says: jokes are fine as long as they "punch up, not down"; no jokes targeting a specific person or group; self-deprecating humour is explicitly encouraged; and there's a seperate list of "sensitive topics" (illness, bereavement, layoffs, money problems) that are off-limits entirely, whoever the target is.

The more I stare at it the more it seems to assume a whole theory of what jokes ARE and what makes them hurt, and I'm not convinced it's one consistent theory. A friend who's into philosophy told me there are four classic theories of humour: superiority (humour is basically feeling above someone), incongruity (humour is noticing a pattern break), relief (humour lets off psychic pressure, the Freud-ish one), and benign violation (something's funny when it seems wrong and okay at the same time).

What I'd like: (a) reconstruct which theory or mix of theories our draft policy actually presupposes -- the punch-up rule smells like superiority theory and the sensitive-topics list smells like benign violation, but do the work and show me; (b) tell me which parts of the policy don't follow from any of these theories, and where the policy is underdetermined, i.e. several rival theories would license the exact same rule; (c) check whether the parts are even consistent with each other -- does the no-targeting rule assume humour is fundamentally a form of attack in a way that sits weirdly next to us also allowing absurdist wordplay that "targets" no one?; and (d) sketch what the policy would concretely look like if we rebuilt it on benign violation theory alone -- which rules would survive, which would change, what would get allowed that's currently banned and vice versa.

One more thing that's bugging me: relief theory seems to predict that banning taboo jokes completely backfires, because people need the release. Does our policy presuppose that's false, or is it just silent on it, and does the silence matter for whether the policy hangs together?
