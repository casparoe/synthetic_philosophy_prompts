I'm putting together the syllabus for my upper-level seminar on rational choice and I keep getting stuck on something that seems basic but I can't shake. Herbert Simon argued that real agents satisfice rather than maximize -- they set an aspiration level and take the first option that clears it. But if that's right, doesn't it follow that satisficing is strictly the more rational procedure? You stop searching as soon as you have something acceptable, so you're guaranteed an outcome you can live with, whereas the maximizer keeps burning time and attention hunting for the best and can always end up dissapointed that something better was out there. So why do decision theorists and economists still model agents as expected utility maximizers? Is it just inertia, or is there some theorem showing maximization is more tractable? And secondarily: if I want to teach students to be more rational in their own lives, should I basically be telling them to satisfice more, e.g. pick a threshold for apartments or job offers and grab the first thing that meets it?
