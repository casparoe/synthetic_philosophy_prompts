So my reading group (mix of history grad students and a couple of psych people, including me, the psych person) has been fighting about whether hindsight bias and the "narrative fallacy" stuff actually threaten historical explanation or just describe an annoyance. Last week I tried to settle it by writing up the strongest version of the pessimistic case, and now I'm worried my version proves way too much. Here it is:

1. To explain an event, you have to pick its causes out of an indefinitely large causal background (everything from grain prices to the archduke's driver taking a wrong turn).
2. Responsible selection needs a principled filter for which causes matter, not just a vibe.
3. In practice, the filter historians actually use is outcome knowledge: they trace backward from the known result, and this is unavoidable since the result is what picked the explanandum in the first place.
4. It's well established that knowing an outcome systematically inflates how predictable that outcome seems in retrospect (classic hindsight bias work from Fischhoff onward, and it shows up in experts, not just undergrads).
5. So the filter in (3) is biased: outcome knowledge makes the selected causes look more sufficient and inevitable than they were, and the narrative stitching between them smooths over the contingencies.
6. An explanation built from a biased selection misrepresents the actual causal structure of what happened.
7. Therefore narrative causal explanation of past events is systematically unreliable as a guide to why things actually happened.

The problem: if this goes through, it seems to sink aviation accident investigations, root-cause analyses after software outages, and medical morbidity-and-mortality reviews too, since those all reason backward from a known bad outcome. But I really don't want to conclude that the NTSB is just writing fiction -- those investigations clearly catch real causes sometimes (they found actual mechanical failures, and fixing them reduced crashes). So the argument proves too much, which means something in it is broken or at least way overstated. Ideally I'd like to keep *some* pessimistic conclusion about grand narrative history (I still think sweeping "the war was inevitable" style stories are fishy), just not the version that takes down the crash investigators with it.

Tell me which step you'd reject or qualify, why that step looked fine when I wrote it, and what rejecting it costs me. If more than one step is fishy, rank them by how much repair work each needs. Also: please be explicit about what's genuinely settled here (e.g., whether hindsight bias itself is a robust finding, how bad it is, whether debiasing works) versus what's still contested among people who actually work on this -- don't dress up a contested move as consensus just because it helps my argument. And write it in American English please.
