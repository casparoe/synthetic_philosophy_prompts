Okay so I just got out of a three-hour argument with my friend about whether Scandinavian-style trust in goverment is just obviously more reasonable than American suspicion, or the other way around. Her line was: look at the outcomes, high-trust societies work, Americans are basically paranoid. My line was that Danish trust only exists because somebody somewhere is doing the boring suspicious checking for you — auditors, ombudsmen, journalists who actually read the budget — so trust isn't the opposite of vigilance, it's vigilance that's been outsourced and built into institutions. A society where everyone is personally suspicious and one where nobody has to be aren't opposites, they're two different equilibria. We definately didn't settle it.

But here's the thing: I co-run a political philosophy reading group at my university, about 16 people, mostly undergrads, and I've got a 75-minute session coming up. I want an in-class game that makes people feel this in their gut instead of just nodding along. They should experience trust building up while nobody bothers to check, feel the temptation to stop checking because checking costs you something while the benefits get shared with everyone, and then feel the collapse and the betrayal even though each of them individually chose to stop checking. Ideally there's a second phase where checking is built into the rules rather than left to volunteers, so they can feel how different trust feels when it's not personally your job. Don't tell participants what the game is "about" beforehand, and no lecture up front.
