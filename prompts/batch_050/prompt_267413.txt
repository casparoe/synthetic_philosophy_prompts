Over the past year I have run into what feels like the same argumentative shape in three quite different places, and I want to know whether philosophy has a name for the shape itself.

First instance: my daughter asked me to tell her about something that nobody anywhere is currently thinking about. Whatever I came up with -- a particular lamppost in a city neither of us has visited, say -- became, in the moment I offered it, something at least two people were thinking about. The request is not impossible to satisfy in the way that drawing a round square is impossible; there are presumably billions of unthought-about things. It is impossible to satisfy by answering, because the answering destroys the very property the answer was supposed to display.

Second instance: in a meditation class the teacher asked us to turn our attention onto the part of the mind that does the attending. Every attempt seemed to install a fresh attender behind the one being observed, so the target receded at exactly the speed of the pursuit. Again, nothing contradictory about the attended attender existing; it just cannot be exhibited by the method prescribed.

Third instance: a colleague of mine, a mathematician, claimed he could prove that there are facts no human being will ever know, and his proof consisted of describing such a fact in careful detail. When I objected that he had just come to know it, he retreated to the position that he knew it only schematically, not concretely, and that the schematic knowledge was still knowledge of its unknowability. I left the conversation unsure whether he had made a deep point or performed a magic trick.

A friend who studied some philosophy tells me an eighteenth-century bishop built a once-famous argument on exactly this structure, aimed at people who believed material things could exist while perceived by no one, but my friend could not recall what the argument was called, and in any case I am asking about the general pattern rather than that historical argument.

So, three questions. One: does the general pattern -- call it a demand whose fulfillment erases the property the fulfillment was meant to display -- have an established name? Two: however it is named, I want the carving, not just the label. How does it differ from a straightforwardly impossible request like drawing a round square, where the defect lies in the thing requested rather than in the act of fulfilling; from the kind of self-defeat involved when someone sincerely asserts that they are not speaking; and from ordinary frustrating instructions such as being told to act naturally, which seem to me merely difficult rather than structurally self-erasing? If the general pattern has no name of its own, please coin one with a definition precise enough to settle those boundary cases. Three: my colleague insists the pattern is a rhetorical cheap shot that proves nothing about what exists, since exhibition and existence are different things; my own suspicion is that it marks a real limit of some kind, though I cannot say a limit of what. Which of us is closer to the truth, and does the answer change across my three instances?
