I'm a postdoc in normative ethics, and in six weeks I'm giving an invited talk to the grant-making committee of a mid-sized global health foundation that is considering me for a standing advisory role. The audience is about fourteen people: program officers trained in cost-effectiveness analysis who think in terms of cost per life saved, two former clinicians, a measurement lead with a management-consulting background, one academic bioethicist who I believe has contractualist sympathies, and a board observer whose job is to worry about how decisions look to major donors. The format is a twenty-minute talk followed by forty minutes of questions, and the stakes are practical: the committee is piloting a decision procedure for cases where two eligible programs cannot both be funded, and my talk is effectively a proposal that the pilot not default to simply funding whichever option the models project will save more lives.

My thesis is a deliberately unfashionable one: in genuinely forced choices, where saving one group or another is all-or-nothing and cannot be divided, justice does not require saving the greater number, and it can be permissible to settle the choice by giving each imperiled person an equal chance — a coin flip between saving one person and saving five. I plan to open with a constructed case: a one-time emergency grant that can either extend vaccine cold-chain infrastructure into a dense urban district (projected roughly 900 deaths averted) or into a scattered rural region (projected roughly 220), where the logistics genuinely cannot be split, and I argue that a weighted or unweighted lottery is defensible here in a way that always funding the larger projection is not. My sketch of the argument is the classic one: aggregation treats the losses to separate persons as if they pooled into a single larger loss, when in fact a loss to me is not made worse, from where I stand, by the fact that strangers elsewhere face losses of their own; the coin flip respects each person's claim rather than letting numbers swamp claims. I plan to concede up front that my view governs only forced choices, not routine program comparisons where funds are divisible — though I suspect this scope restriction will itself draw fire, since every budget decision is arguably a forced choice at the margin.
