For years now, in every ethics seminar and reading group I've been part of, the utility-monster objection to total utilitarianism has followed the same script and died in the same place. Someone presents the monster -- a being who gets vastly more utility per unit of resource than anyone else, so that maximizing total utility seems to require handing it everything -- and the utilitarian replies, with evident sincerity, some version of: yes, and if such a being really existed, giving it everything would be right; your discomfort is a bias toward creatures like yourself. The anti-utilitarian then says the conclusion is a reductio, the utilitarian says the intuition is just status-quo bias dressed up, and both sides leave the room feeling they won. Nobody has changed their mind about anything in the decade I've been watching this happen. What strikes me is that the two sides don't even disagree about how the thought experiment goes -- both accept that the monster maximizes total utility, both accept the conclusion is counterintuitive, both have heard every reply the other has. The argument is, in some sense, complete, and yet it settles nothing.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the debate has stalled because it is secretly a methodological disagreement that never gets stated as one. The anti-utilitarian is implicitly committed to something like: a moral theory must be answerable to any coherently describable case, so a monstrous verdict in a possible world is a genuine mark against it. The utilitarian is implicitly committed to something like: intuitions calibrated to actual human distributions of welfare have no authority over cases designed to be maximally alien, so the 'reductio' is just the intuition pump working as intended. If that's right, the two are answering different questions under one heading -- one about what the correct theory of the good is, one about what evidence thought experiments can supply -- and no further work on the monster itself will move anyone. But I may be wrong: maybe the real crux is a hidden technical question, like whether the monster scenario is even well-defined given how interpersonal utility comparisons work, or whether a being with that utility function could instantiate anything we'd recognize as welfare. Or maybe the disagreement is genuinely bedrock -- rival starting intuitions about whether equality matters independently of welfare, which neither side can argue for without begging the question.

So here is what I'd like: diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, working from what the two sides characteristically say rather than from the textbook summary of the objection. Is it verbal, factual, methodological, or bedrock-intuitional? Which specific moves in the standard exchange support your diagnosis? And given the diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress -- a distinction that would dissolve part of the dispute, a question the two sides could profitably argue about instead, an empirical or formal result that would actually bear on it -- or, if you think no progress should be expected, argue for that instead of just asserting it. I'm less interested in a verdict on utilitarianism than in an answer to the stranger question of why an argument everyone has fully heard convinces no one.
