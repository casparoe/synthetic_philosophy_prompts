Ok so a buddy of mine walked me into this argument last weekend and I've been going back and forth on it ever since. First the setup, the standard Death in Damascus case: a man meets Death at the market in Damascus, she tells him she has an appointment with him tomorrow. He can stay put in Damascus or flee overnight to Aleppo. Death's appointment was fixed before he decides anything, and she predicted where he'd go — in the strong version of the story, perfectly. So wherever he ends up going, that's where she'll be waiting.

His argument, as best I can reconstruct it:

1. Whatever the man chooses, his choosing it is (given Death's perfect prediction) conclusive evidence that Death will be in exactly that city. So at the moment of choosing, every option available to him is such that taking it means expecting to die there.
2. A decision problem in which every available option is, by your own lights at the moment of choice, tied to the bad outcome is a problem with no rational solution — each option condemns itself the moment you lean toward it.
3. So in Death in Damascus there simply is no rational choice, and decision theory has no answer to give. The case is a counterexample to the very idea of rational choice under correlation.
4. But nothing about the structure is exotic. Any situation where others can predict your behavior — poker, salary negotiations, an arms race, your partner deciding whether to trust you — has your choices correlated with outcomes via mechanisms fixed before you choose.
5. So the conclusion generalizes: there is no rational choice in most of the strategic and social situations we actually care about either.
6. That's obviously too much. So the problem must be in 1–2, and his verdict is that step 2 is where expected-value reasoning "refutes itself" and we should just abandon the whole framework.

Now, I'm fairly convinced he's proven too much somewhere, but I keep flip-flopping on where. Two things I'd like to hang on to if possible: that the man in Damascus has a genuine problem and the story isn't some cheap trick, and that ordinary prediction-heavy cases (negotiations, poker) are usually perfectly rationally navigable. His own preferred target is step 2, but I'm suspicious that the real slide happens somewhere in 3–5 instead, maybe in how the Damascus structure gets described in the generalization.
