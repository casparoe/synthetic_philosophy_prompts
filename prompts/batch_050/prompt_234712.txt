A friend and I recently spent an entire evening arguing about intellectual property, and she ended the night by walking me into an argument that I have been unable to find my way out of. I would like a careful diagnosis. The argument runs as follows:

(1) If someone produces something valuable through their own labor, without taking anything from anyone else, then they are entitled to control how that thing is used, and others act wrongly if they use it without permission.

(2) A new idea -- a theorem, a melody, a plot twist, an algorithm, an invention -- is produced through its originator's labor and is taken from no one; before the originator thought it, nobody had it.

(3) Therefore the originator of an idea is entitled to control how that idea is used, and anyone who uses it without permission wrongs them.

(4) A genuine entitlement to control use does not lapse merely because time passes or because many people would benefit from using the thing; we do not hold that ownership of a house or a plot of land expires after twenty years, or that it evaporates once squatters would put the property to good use.

(5) So intellectual property, if it is justified at all, should be perpetual, and it should prohibit any unauthorized use of the idea, including uses by people who arrived at the very same idea entirely on their own.

(6) But (5) is absurd: it implies that a mathematician who independently proves an already-proven theorem has wronged the first prover, that an engineer who reinvents a patented device in ignorance of the patent is a thief, and that the estates of long-dead playwrights could still be charging theater companies performance fees today.

(7) Since the absurdity must be traced to the premises, and (2) through (6) look like mere elaborations, the labor-based principle (1) is false: producing something valuable through your own labor gives you no entitlement to control its use.

Her conclusion strikes me as proving far too much -- if (1) is false, it is hard to see what could justify ordinary property in produced goods either, and yet I do not want to abandon (1) wholesale. My own suspicion is that something shifts in the move from (3) to (5), as though "controlling use of the idea" quietly changes what it excludes along the way, but I cannot pin it down, and my friend insists that any restriction short of (5) is an arbitrary concession rather than a principled one.

Please tell me which step or premise you would reject, explain why that step looked innocent when it is not (or why rejecting it is the least costly option even though the argument is valid), and say what the rejection costs -- in particular, what it commits me to regarding two features of actual intellectual property law that came up in our argument: that copyright does not penalize genuinely independent creation of an identical work, whereas patent law does not excuse independent invention. Finally, note whether your diagnosis vindicates my friend's closing claim that anything short of (5) is unprincipled, or instead gives a non-arbitrary stopping point. Please write your answer in American English.
