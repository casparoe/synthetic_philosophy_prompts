I run a philosophy of mind reading group, mostly grad students and a couple of sharp undergrads, about 8-10 people, and we meet for 90 minutes. We're doing intentionality soon and I want an activity for that session, not a discussion.

Background so you know where I'm coming from: there's a well-known thought experiment (Dennett's) about a vending-machine-style gizmo built in the US to accept American quarters — it checks weight, diameter, maybe magnetic signature. Say it gets shipped to a country whose coins happen to be physically near-identical to US quarters but worth way less, and it happily keeps clunking away on those. Question: what is the machine's internal state *really about*? Quarters? The foreign coins? Anything with that physical profile? Dennett's move is that there's no deeper fact to appeal to beyond design history and selection pressures, and that this lesson applies to us too, since our own "detectors" were shaped by evolution rather than by an intentional designer — so content is never "original" in the sense Searle wants, where minds have intrinsic aboutness and artifacts only borrow it from us.

Design me a hands-on activity that makes the group *feel* the slide from "obviously the machine means quarters" to "wait, there's actually nothing here to settle it," and then feel the vertigo when the same move gets applied to human brains. Some ideas of mine you can improve on or ditch: participants acting as coin-discriminators with rule cards they wrote themselves, an environment that silently shifts mid-game so their rule now picks out something else, then a structured dispute over what the rule "really meant" all along. Whatever you do, the indeterminacy has to emerge from what actually happens during play, not from me explaining it at the end, and I dont want to say Dennett or Searle's names until the debrief.

Include: concrete rules and materials, roles, a rough timeline for the 90 minutes, the debrief mapping specific moments of the game onto the original-vs-derived distinction and the evolution-as-designer point, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and an honest note about what this kind of activity can't stage (I suspect the fact that my participants have real intentions while playing a machine smuggles something in, but tell me if I'm wrong about that).
