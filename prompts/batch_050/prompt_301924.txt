Earlier this year I moved from the reserving side of my insurance firm into pricing, and one of my first assignments was to review a proposed product with a genuinely unbounded payout structure — think unlimited reinstatements on a catastrophe layer rather than a toy game. Our senior underwriter killed it in one sentence, saying we were essentially being offered the St. Petersburg paradox and being asked to charge a premium for it. I looked the paradox up, I can follow the philosophy of it reasonably well, but I keep having the feeling that the entire debate should be expressible in the vocabulary I work in every day, and I want to test that feeling.

Please act as a decision theorist who has also spent years in quantitative risk management, and take your time — I would rather have a careful, structured answer than a quick one. Begin by brainstorming the main moves in the literature: the setup of the game itself, Bernoulli's original diminishing-marginal-utility resolution, responses based on finite wealth and the casino's inability to pay, repeated-play and growth-rate arguments, bounded-utility responses, and anything else you consider load-bearing. Then restate each move as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: expected loss versus certain equivalent, risk loadings, probability of ruin, solvency constraints, Kelly-style growth rates, ensemble versus time averages, diversification and the law of large numbers across a book of business, and the pricing of heavy-tailed risks. Be precise about the mapping: do not tell me two notions are basically the same unless they really are.

The part I care most about is the residue report. For each resolution, tell me which claims translated cleanly, which you could only express by distorting or quietly strengthening them, and which parts of the philosophical debate have no counterpart at all in actuarial and risk vocabulary. Where nothing translates, I want your judgment on why: does the gap expose something my framework genuinely lacks that a pricer needs, or does it suggest the philosophers are arguing about something my framework has rightly dissolved? For instance, I have a standing suspicion that the question of what it would be rational to pay for a single play stops being well-posed the moment you insist on specifying a bankroll, a time horizon, and a ruin constraint — but I want that suspicion stress-tested, not confirmed.

Finally, be explicit about your confidence and its failure modes. Identify the step of the translation you are least sure of, and state what argument or consideration would lead you to reverse your verdict on the residue — in particular, what would convince you that my framework, rather than the paradox, is the thing that is missing something.
