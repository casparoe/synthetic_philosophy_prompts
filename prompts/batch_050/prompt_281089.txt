White Horses and Restricted Domains: A Semantic Reading of the White Horse Dialogue

The white horse dialogue, attributed to Gongsun Long and preserved in the text bearing his name, defends the apparently absurd thesis that a white horse is not a horse. Most modern readers, following a long line of dismissive commentary, take the piece to be a sophism that trades on the ambiguity between the "is" of identity and the "is" of predication: trivially, the white horse is not identical to the horse, and trivially, any white horse is a horse. On this diagnosis the dialogue has no philosophical content beyond the equivocation itself. I want to resist this dismissal, though not in the usual way. I will argue that the dialogue is best read as an inquiry into the reference of compound expressions, and that its negative thesis is an early recognition that nominal modification does not always behave as simple intersection.

Consider the dialogue's three main lines of argument. First, the term-composition argument: "horse" serves to name a shape, "white" serves to name a color; the expression combining color and shape therefore names something different from what the expression for shape alone names. Second, the differential-satisfaction argument: if one requests a horse, a yellow or black horse will do; if one requests a white horse, they will not; hence what "white horse" answers to is not what "horse" answers to. Third, the exclusion argument: "white horse" excludes horses of other colors, while "horse" excludes nothing; an excluding term cannot co-refer with a non-excluding one.

The standard charitably-minded reconstruction makes all three arguments proofs that the class of white horses is a proper subclass of the class of horses — a result the interlocutor never doubted. My proposal is to take the arguments at their metalinguistic word. The particle rendered "is not" in the thesis is a negator without a copula; the thesis asserts a difference between two expressions' referents, not a classification of animals. So read, the dialogue probes the conditions under which a compound noun co-refers with its head noun, and its answer — never, when the modifier adds content — anticipates a genuine problem in semantics: modification is not always intersective. A fake gun is not a gun; a large mouse is not large, full stop. Gongsun Long, on my reading, has seen that the logic of "white horse" cannot be read off mechanically from the logic of "horse."

It may be objected that I have chosen the worst possible example, since "white" is the paradigm intersective modifier: white horses are exactly the intersection of white things and horses. This objection is correct, and it is precisely the point. Because "white" is intersective, the thesis that a white horse is not a horse is false as a claim about animals — but the dialogue's arguments, which are entirely about what terms answer to, never touch animals at all. Gongsun Long's achievement is to isolate extension-of-expression from classification-of-things using the one modifier where the two can be cleanly separated, because here the separation costs nothing empirical.

A note on the mass-noun interpretation associated with Chad Hansen is in order. On that reading, classical Chinese nouns function like English mass nouns: "horse" names horse-stuff, and white-horse-stuff is a proper part of horse-stuff, whence the thesis. I am unpersuaded, for the dialogue's differential-satisfaction argument turns on counting and on which animals satisfy a request — reasoning that presupposes individuated horses rather than quantities of stuff. My semantic reading accommodates this argument directly; the mass-noun reading must explain it away.

I conclude that the dialogue is neither sophistry nor a proof of class inclusion. It is a compressed treatise on the semantics of modification, whose deliberately outrageous thesis marks the gap between what a compound expression refers to and what its head refers to — a gap that, as non-intersective adjectives show, is not a mere artifact of ancient grammar.
