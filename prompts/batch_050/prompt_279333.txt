I am writing an essay for a philosophy of probability reading group and would like your help with its central case study. Please read Sophocles' Oedipus Rex through the lens of I. J. Good's theorem on the value of information. Good's result, in rough terms, is that a rational Bayesian agent who can acquire information at no cost, and who remains free to choose among the same acts afterwards, can never be worse off in expectation for acquiring it: expected utility with the evidence is at least as high as without it, since one can always ignore what one learns. The play looks like a standing refutation of this. Oedipus pursues the truth about Laius' murder patiently and at every stage is urged to stop -- by Jocasta, by Tiresias, effectively by the herdsman's reluctance -- and the knowledge he finally obtains destroys his family, his eyesight, and his crown. Nor is this the play's only episode of costly inquiry: the earlier oracle at Delphi gives Oedipus partial information about his fate, and his attempt to act on it by fleeing Corinth is precisely what brings the fate about.

I want a reading that is scrupulous in both directions. On the philosophical side: identify exactly which of the theorem's conditions the play violates, and do not let the essay get away with the lazy claim that the play simply disproves Good. Consider, in particular, the ex ante versus ex post distinction; the fact that Oedipus has bound himself by a public oath to punish the killer, so that he lacks the theorem's crucial option of disregarding what he learns; and whether the Delphic episode shows information changing the agent's choice set rather than merely his beliefs. On the literary side: say where the reading illuminates particular scenes -- Tiresias' reluctance, Jocasta's attempt to dismiss oracles, the shepherd's interrogation -- and where it distorts the play, since Sophocles is presumably interested in fate, shame, and self-knowledge rather than in decision theory. Conclude by saying what, if anything, the play contributes to the philosophical question that the theorem's proof alone cannot: is there a sense, available to tragedy but not to Bayesianism, in which some knowledge is worse than ignorance even for an agent who reasons perfectly?

Please write in British English, in a formal register suitable for the essay I have described.
