I am a novelist at work on a book about two rival families of antique dealers whose feud over a disputed estate spirals over three generations -- lawsuits answered by lawsuits, sabotage answered by sabotage, until both families have spent far more than the estate was ever worth and neither can remember a version of themselves that wasn't fighting. At a dinner party, an economist friend told me I had unwittingly written a dramatization of the dollar auction: the game in which two players bid for a dollar, with the twist that the runner-up must also pay their bid and receives nothing, so that once the bidding passes fifty cents each player is rationally compelled to keep escalating toward a loss, and the only winning move was never to play. She used the analogy to make a stronger claim about my manuscript: that my characters are not wicked or tragic but merely trapped in a payoff structure, and that any attempt I make to give them a moment where they could reasonably walk away would ring false, because walking away is precisely what the structure forbids.

I would like you to audit the analogy for me, because my novel's architecture may depend on it. Begin with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the auctioneer, the dollar, the bids, the rule that the loser also pays, and the point at which passing the prize's value becomes invisible to the players? Then take the mapping apart. In the dollar auction the bids are forfeited money, but in a feud the costs are time, reputation, and self-respect, which people experience as investments that must be made good rather than as money already lost -- does that difference make escalation harder or easier to escape? In the game the prize is fixed at a dollar, but in my story the estate accrues meaning as it is fought over, so that winning it eventually means vindication rather than furniture; does a prize whose value grows with the bidding strengthen the analogy or break it? And the game has exactly two players with no exit and no audience, whereas my families have children who watch, spouses who plead, and a town that gossips -- do third parties function as escape hatches the game lacks, or as additional bidders?

Finally, press on my friend's conclusion. If the disanalogies matter, does it follow that a credible exit scene is possible after all -- and if so, what would have to be true of a character for walking away to read as growth rather than as a failure of nerve the plot hasn't earned? And if the analogy largely holds, what is the novelist's honest way to end a dollar auction: a death, a bankruptcy, an external shock that changes the rules, or the quiet recognition by one player that the game was the relationship all along? I am less interested in being told that my friend was right or wrong than in knowing exactly which load-bearing parts of the analogy can carry a novel and which cannot.
