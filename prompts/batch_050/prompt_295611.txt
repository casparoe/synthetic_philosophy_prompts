Analyze the 1972 U.S. Supreme Court case Wisconsin v. Yoder—where Amish parents were permitted to withdraw their children from formal schooling after eighth grade despite a state requirement of attendance until age sixteen—as a conflict between parental licensing and the child’s right to an open future. Treat parental licensing as the view that the state may restrict child-rearing authority to those meeting minimum standards of competence and care, and the open-future principle as the claim that children have an interest in preserving a reasonable range of adult options. Map the interests of parents, children, the Amish community, and the state; consider whether compulsory education protects children’s future autonomy or instead imposes a contestable conception of the good life; and assess whether the Court safeguarded the children’s prospects or merely converted parental and communal authority into a veto over them. Finally, use the case to test the theories themselves: does Yoder reveal ambiguity in what counts as an open future, an excessive state-centeredness in licensing proposals, or a legitimate limit on both principles?
