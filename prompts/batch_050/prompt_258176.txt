Earlier this year, my younger brother became convinced that the 2020s wave of hospital overcrowding was staged, and that official mortality statistics are fabricated wholesale. We grew up sharing everything, and I assumed a careful conversation with good sources would bring him around. Instead, every piece of evidence I offered was absorbed into the theory: the more authoritative the source, the deeper the conspiracy must run. What unsettled me most was not his conclusion but the structure of our exchange — I kept trying to give him reasons, and he kept treating my reasons as exhibits. I had the disorienting sense that we were not disagreeing about a proposition but standing on different ground.

This experience sent me back to Wittgenstein's On Certainty and to G. E. Moore's famous anti-skeptical writings, which Wittgenstein's notes constantly engage. In the remarks around §§94–99 of On Certainty, Wittgenstein develops the image (which I paraphrase, not quote) of our beliefs as a river: some propositions function like hardened river-bed rock, others like water that flows over it, and the boundary between the two can shift — what was once bedrock can be carried away, and water can deposit new sediment that hardens. Around §§141–142 he adds that what we believe is never a single proposition but a whole system in which the parts lend one another support, so that nothing is tested in isolation. And in §§105–116 he criticizes Moore's way of responding to the skeptic: Moore's confident assertions that he knows, for instance, that he has two hands strike Wittgenstein as misusing "know," since such convictions are better described as things that stand fast for Moore, beyond the reach of justification and doubt alike.

Against that background, here is my question. Compare Wittgenstein's hinge-based account of what grounds our certainty with Moore's common-sense insistence that he simply knows the things skeptics deny. In particular:

1. Moore and Wittgenstein both want to refuse the skeptic, but they seem to refuse in different ways — Moore by asserting knowledge on the far side of doubt, Wittgenstein by relocating certainty outside the knowledge game altogether. Which refusal, if either, better describes what was actually happening between my brother and me? Was he doubting within a shared river-bed, or had his river-bed itself shifted?

2. Wittgenstein's image allows that the bed of the river can move. Does that give me any rational foothold for hoping my brother's convictions could change — or does it imply that what separates us is not the kind of thing reasons can bridge, since reasons only flow along a bed that is already shared?

3. If Wittgenstein is right that conviction is always systemic, does Moore's strategy fail precisely because it treats certainty as something that can attach to a single proposition, spoken in a single assertoric voice? Or is there a way to read Moore charitably as making a Wittgensteinian point avant la lettre?

Please answer with a genuine comparison rather than a summary of each thinker, and take a position on question 2 in particular, since it is the one I cannot stop turning over.
