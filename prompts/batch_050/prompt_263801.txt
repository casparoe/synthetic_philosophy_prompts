**Exam question:** "Explain how, on Davidson's view, radical interpretation is possible. Your answer should cover the evidential situation of the radical interpreter, the role of a Tarski-style theory of truth as a theory of meaning, and the work done by the principle of charity. Then assess: is the principle of charity, as Davidson conceives it, strong enough to secure the determinacy of interpretation, or does Quine's worry about indeterminacy survive?"

**Student's answer:** Davidson's radical interpreter starts from scratch: no dictionary, no shared language, no prior knowledge of what the speaker believes. The only evidence available is behavioral — which sentences the speaker holds true, and under what observable conditions. The interpreter's task is to construct, from this evidence alone, a theory that simultaneously assigns meanings to the speaker's sentences and beliefs to the speaker. Davidson's proposal is that this takes the form of a Tarski-style theory of truth for the speaker's language: a finite axiomatic theory that generates, for each sentence, a theorem specifying the conditions under which it is true. Since the theory is compositional, it handles the infinity of sentences from finite resources, and because meaning and belief are interdependent — you cannot know what a sentence means without knowing what the speaker believes, or attribute a belief without knowing what their words mean — the interpreter must solve for both at once, fitting the whole theory to the total pattern of hold-true attitudes. What makes this solvable is the principle of charity: the interpreter proceeds on the assumption that most of what the speaker believes is true, i.e., that the speaker largely agrees with the interpreter about the world they share. Without this assumption the data underdetermine interpretation; with it, the interpreter can project from observed correlations between utterances and conditions to assignments of meaning. Charity, as Davidson presents it, is a methodological heuristic the interpreter elects to adopt — a useful working assumption that could in principle be replaced by other constraints, such as detailed knowledge of the speaker's neurophysiology, were such knowledge available. On determinacy: Quine argued that translation is indeterminate because incompatible translation manuals can fit all the behavioral evidence. Davidson's answer is that this apparent threat dissolves once we demand that interpretation be charitable, since the requirement of maximizing agreement narrows the acceptable theories to one. Where two charitable interpretations still conflict, Davidson insists one must be wrong, because disagreement at that level would show that at least one interpreter has failed to find the speaker intelligible. So the determinacy Quine denied is restored, and radical interpretation is possible in the strong sense of delivering a uniquely correct theory of meaning for any speaker.
