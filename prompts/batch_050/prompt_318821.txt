I’ve been reading the old rationalist-blog arguments about torture versus dust specks, and I got into a slightly confusing argument with a friend who works in public health. Modern health agencies routinely aggregate very small benefits or harms across enormous populations: a screening programme might be approved because it prevents a few expected deaths, even though it gives millions of people a brief period of anxiety, a needle stick, or a day of unnecessary inconvenience. In cost-effectiveness calculations, those tiny welfare losses can be added together and compared with a few severe outcomes. Yet the same institutions would regard it as monstrous to impose a severe harm on one identifiable person in order to save an equivalent aggregate amount of welfare elsewhere — even if the arithmetic were transparent and the severe harm were, on some measure, less bad than the total of all the tiny ones.

What does this regulatory practice presuppose about aggregation, rights, and the separateness of persons? Does it commit us to the view that arbitrarily many minute harms can collectively outweigh one catastrophic harm, or can it instead be explained by distinctions between statistical and identifiable victims, imposed and incidental harms, or harms chosen by institutions and harms merely permitted? Please reconstruct the strongest implicit moral theory behind the practice, note where it is philosophically underdetermined, and test it against the extreme torture-versus-dust-specks case rather than treating that case as a mere intuition pump. Also say how public-health practice would have to change under each rival view. I’d like the answer in British English, and I’m less interested in names or citations than in getting the conceptual joints seperately and clearly.
