I sit on the curatorial board of a mid-sized national museum of social history, and we are finalising the interpretive text for a permanent gallery provisionally called *The Politics of Hunger*, which covers three famines side by side: Ireland in the 1840s, Bengal in 1943, and China between 1959 and 1961. The board is deadlocked over the gallery's framing, and I have been asked to produce a decision memo for our next meeting. The majority draft currently hangs the whole exhibition on a thesis drawn loosely from Amartya Sen's entitlement approach: that mass starvation is rarely, if ever, a simple shortage of food, but a collapse in people's ability to command food, and therefore that famines of this scale are best understood as political failures rather than natural disasters. The draft goes further and includes a wall panel stating, in effect, that famines do not occur in functioning democracies with a free press, because electoral accountability and public criticism force governments to act.

Two objections have paralysed us. First, our three consulting historians (one per famine) argue the unified framing flattens genuinely different cases: the Irish famine began with a real blight and unfolded under a parliament and a press that reported it as it happened, which sits awkwardly with the democracy panel; Bengal followed a cyclone and a wartime context; and scholars still dispute how far the Chinese catastrophe was intended, concealed, or simply the result of procurement policy compounded by bad weather. They want case-by-case labels that present each famine as a contested event with natural and political components in different proportions. Second, a diaspora community organisation has formally requested that the gallery apply the word genocide to one of the three famines, and has hinted at a public campaign if we refuse; our director is sympathetic in principle but worries that using the word for one famine implicitly absolves the other two, and that we lack the standing to adjudicate a term whose legal and scholarly usage is unsettled.

Please draft the board's decision memo. It should recommend, with reasons, (a) whether to keep the unified Sen-inspired thesis as the gallery's spine, adopt the differentiated case-by-case approach, or some specified hybrid; (b) what to do about the democracy-and-free-press panel given the Irish problem, including the option of keeping it with qualifiers; and (c) whether the word genocide should appear anywhere in the gallery, and under what conditions a museum should defer to, or override, affected communities on contested terminology. The memo must give the strongest version of the losing arguments on each point, since the historians and the community organisation will both see it, and it should end with wording the board could actually vote on. Where your recommendation depends on a judgement call about contested history or contested concepts, say how confident you are in that judgement, and single out the point in your memo where you are least sure you have it right.
