So here's a thing I've wanted for years after reading yet another centipede game thread that went nowhere: the backward induction people and the "but common knowledge of rationality is self-undermining" people keep talking past each other, and I want the output to look like an actual adversarial collaboration, not another debate.

Draft the joint statement that a two-camp adversarial collaboration on the centipede game would produce. Camp A is the Aumann-style epistemic camp: common knowledge of rationality implies the backward induction solution, full stop, take at the first node. Camp B is the doubt camp: common knowledge of rationality can be destroyed by the very moves it predicts won't happen, so once you're a few nodes in, the epistemic ground has shifted and continuing to pass can be rational. (Both camps should be treated as sophisticated -- no strawmanning Camp A as "has never considered off-path beliefs" or Camp B as "doesn't understand induction.")

Structure it like this:

1. Agreed claims: a numbered list of things both sides sign. These need to be non-vacuous -- not "the game has an equilibrium," but stuff like exactly what backward induction establishes and exactly what it presupposes, what both sides make of the empirical fact that experimental subjects mostly don't take at node one, and what each formalism can and can't express about beliefs at unreached nodes.

2. A disagreements table: each row states the disagreement in a sentence both sides accept as neutral, then each side's position phrased the way that side would phrase it. Flag which rows are genuine disagreements and which you've concluded are verbal (e.g. hiding in what "rational" or "knows" is doing), and say why.

3. Cruxes: for each genuine disagreement, the sub-question such that settling it would actually move at least one side, plus -- and this is the part I care most about -- a concrete test, theorem, or thought-experiment both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If you want, use a modified centipede as shared test case, like one where a noisy referee randomly passes with small probability so off-path beliefs stop being pure hypotheticals -- but say whether the camps think this variant dissolves the paradox or just relocates it.

Don't adjudicate. If at the end you think the two camps would each walk away believing they won, that's fine, but the statement should make it obvious where exactly the win-condition differs.
