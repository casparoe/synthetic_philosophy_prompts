I teach a first-year undergraduate seminar on philosophy of history (about 18 students, one 75-minute session), and we are about to start Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, with its three modes: monumental, antiquarian, and critical. Rather than lecture the triad first, I want an activity in which students enact all three modes themselves and feel the pull of each before ever hearing the names. Design a structured exercise built around a shared archive you will specify: a fictional small town or college approaching some anniversary, with a packet of 10-14 invented documents (founding charters, a beloved founder's letters, records of an expulsion or a strike the institution would rather forget, petty administrative trivia, wartime conduct, etc.). Divide the class into three groups and give each a different commission with different incentives -- for instance, one group must write the keynote for the centenary celebration, one must curate the permanent exhibit for the town museum, and one must advise a present-day council on whether to keep honoring the founder -- such that each group, just by taking its task seriously, is driven toward one of the three modes: reaching for great exemplars to inspire action, reverently preserving everything simply because it is ours, or putting the past on trial and condemning parts of it. Spell out the document packet in enough detail that I can actually print it (each document should contain something that reads differently under each commission), the group instructions, the timeline for 75 minutes, and what each group produces and presents.

Then design the debrief: a mapping from what each group spontaneously did -- what they selected, what they suppressed, what tone they took toward the dead -- onto monumental, antiquarian, and critical history, including the characteristic excess of each mode (the monumental mode's mythmaking and contempt for the merely actual, the antiquarian mode's suffocating piety that can no longer distinguish what deserves to live, the critical mode's ingratitude toward the very past that made the judges possible). I also want the debrief to surface Nietzsche's claim that all three serve life and that each becomes harmful when it dominates, and to set up the question of what the supra-historical and the unhistorical might add. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (for example, students treating the critical group as obviously the good guys, or the archive being too thin for the antiquarians), how to handle it, and one feature of Nietzsche's account that this activity structurally cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in discussion afterward. Please write the answer in American English.
