So I got into an argument with my sister last weekend about the whole "Rosie the Riveter" thing. You know the story: during WWII millions of American (and British, and Soviet) women flooded into factories, shipyards, offices, flying ferry planes, all of it, and were constantly told this was their patriotic duty. Then the war ends and within a few years the same media ecosystem pivots hard to telling women their real fulfillment is in the home — the suburban housewife ideal of the 1950s, the era Friedan later wrote about, women fired or pushed out so returning soldiers could have jobs, ads suddenly about vacuum cleaners instead of welding torches. My sister's take was basically: see, society just manufactures femininity to fit whatever the economy needs at the moment. I pushed back that this is too simple, but honestly I didn't have great arguments.

Anyway it occured to me that this episode is almost a laboratory test for Beauvoir's famous claim that one is not born but becomes a woman. So: analyze the wartime-to-postwar swing through the lens of The Second Sex. Do the full mapping — what are the mechanisms by which, on her account, a girl or woman is made into "woman" (upbringing, myths, religion, marriage as institution, women's own complicity and bad faith, the positioning of woman as Other), and which of those mechanisms were actually doing the work in each phase: the wartime mobilization, the postwar pushback. Does the episode support her view, complicate it, or expose something she got wrong? I'm especially interested in awkward points: Beauvoir is writing in 1949, right in the middle of this swing, so is her theory a good explanation of it or itself a product of it? How does she handle the women who didn't want to go back into the home, or who never fit the housewife mold at all (working-class women, Black women, who had always worked)? And does the sheer speed of the swing — a decade, maybe less — vindicate the "becoming" thesis or actually embarrass it, since a deep social construction arguably shouldn't be able to reverse that fast?

Structure it like this: first "The mapping" (around 300 words, matching her mechanisms to the wartime and postwar phases), then "Where the theory shines" (~150 words), then "Where it strains" (~200 words, including the speed objection and the diversity objection), then "What the episode does to the theory" (~150 words — does it confirm, revise, or embarrass her account), and finish with "Bottom line" (a few sentences, and actually commit to a verdict on whether my sister is right).
