I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of law, and I am writing the take-home essay for our unit on the legal enforcement of morality. I want a single essay question built around the following passage from Chapter IV of John Stuart Mill's On Liberty (1859):

"The acts of an individual may be hurtful to others, or wanting in due consideration for their welfare, without going the length of violating any of their constituted rights. The offender may then be justly punished by opinion, though not by law. As soon as any part of a person's conduct affects prejudicially the interests of others, society has jurisdiction over it, and the question whether the general welfare will or will not be promoted by interfering with it, becomes open to discussion."

Here is what I need the question to do. Students in this course can all recite the harm principle and can all summarize the Hart–Devlin debate in slogan form. The question must make that recitation useless. I want it to turn on the distinction Mill draws in this passage between punishment "by opinion" and punishment "by law" — and on whether that distinction can bear the weight Mill (and later Hart) puts on it once one takes seriously two pressures: first, Devlin's claim that a shared morality is the cement of society, so that private immorality may threaten society's cohesion and hence its members' interests; second, Mill's own well-known anxiety, elsewhere in On Liberty, that the tyranny of prevailing opinion can be as crushing as legal penalty. If informal social sanction can ruin a life as thoroughly as a fine or a prison term, is the opinion/law line a principled boundary or merely a choice between enforcement technologies? And if Mill concedes that society may enforce its morality by opinion, has he already granted Devlin the premise that morality is society's business, quibbling only about the instrument?

Please write: (1) the essay question itself, phrased as it would appear on the exam, answerable in roughly 90 minutes or about 800–1,000 words, with one or two sentences of guidance telling students what a complete answer must engage; (2) a model answer of about 700 words that takes a definite position rather than surveying views — it should reconstruct the strongest version of the opinion/law distinction, state Devlin's challenge precisely (the disintegration thesis, not the weaker claim that society is entitled to feel disgust), and assess whether Hart's rejoinder — that a society's changing its moral code is no more its destruction than an individual's change of belief is the individual's death — actually meets the challenge or concedes it under a different description; and (3) a marking scheme out of 20 points built on five must-hit points, stating the marks each is worth, plus a one-line note for each on the characteristic way weaker candidates fumble it (for example, treating Devlin's argument as mere majoritarian squeamishness, or attributing to Mill the view that self-regarding conduct may not be criticized at all). The marking scheme should make clear what separates an A answer from a B answer in terms of specific argumentative moves, not polish.
