You are a philosopher of art who has also spent years advising acquisition committees, and you are good at the specific skill of extracting someone's operative standards from their past calls rather than from what they say their standards are. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming freely before you commit to anything.

Here is my situation. I serve on the acquisitions committee of a small museum with a colleague, Marta, whose votes I am trying to predict. We are about to consider a difficult object, and rather than ask her directly (long story involving committee politics), I want to reason from her track record. I will give you five verdicts she has delivered over the years, in her capacity as a curator and collector, and then the new case.

Her prior verdicts:

1. A celebrated painting long attributed to a major Dutch Golden Age master was exposed as a twentieth-century forgery by a skilled forger. Marta said it had lost essentially all of its claim on our esteem and should come off the wall -- while insisting that nothing about the paint, the surface, or its appearance had changed, and that anyone who now found it "less beautiful" was fooling themselves. Its value, on her view, had collapsed even though its look had not.

2. A Renaissance panel in which roughly a third of the visible surface is nineteenth-century repainting over the original composition: she voted to keep it displayed as a genuine work of the original master, with the restoration disclosed on the label. Full membership in the collection, honestly described.

3. A bronze cast from a sculptor's original plaster model: she said casts produced by the foundry the artist employed, during the artist's lifetime, are originals, while casts struck from the very same plaster by the artist's estate decades after his death are not, however faithful -- even though the artist never personally touched the lifetime casts either, foundry workers being the ones who did the pouring.

4. A flawless, molecule-for-molecule replica of an unremarkable decorative chair she owns: she said she would swap them without a moment's hesitation and could not see what would be lost.

5. A student copy after Rubens, honestly labeled, which she finds genuinely lovely: she was glad to hang it in her home but said its value was "decorative only" -- not the kind of value that anchors a collection, and not a candidate for acquisition even at a trivial price.

The new case: a canvas that, on the best current connoisseurship, was painted largely by a talented pupil in a great master's workshop, under the master's supervision, in his studio, and was sold by the master under his own name, in his lifetime, to a buyer who understood himself to be buying the master's work. (This was common practice in the period; the modern category of the fully "autograph" work had not yet hardened.) No one was deceived by the standards of the time, though a modern label reading simply "Master, title, date" would, by our standards, mislead.

My questions, in order:

(a) Lay out at least three distinct rules that are each consistent with all five of Marta's prior verdicts -- no gerrymandering that simply restates the verdicts as a list -- and state what each rule predicts about the workshop canvas. I want rules that genuinely diverge on this case, not three phrasings of one rule.

(b) Give me your best single guess about how Marta will vote, your reasons for preferring the rule that delivers it, and which of the five prior verdicts is the closest analog to the new case and why.

(c) Describe a further case -- one I could plausibly put to her at some point -- whose verdict would do the most to discriminate among your remaining candidate rules, and say what each rule would predict about it.

(d) If, on reflection, you think her track record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say so plainly, and tell me what principle she would be adopting with each possible vote, so that I at least know what I am asking her to commit to.

Two requests about manner. First, be explicit about how confident you are in (b) and why -- I would rather have a calibrated guess with the source of residual doubt named than false precision. Second, if you think this whole exercise rests on a confused premise -- for instance, if you doubt that people like Marta actually operate with stable extractable rules of the kind I am assuming, or if one of the verdicts as I have reported it cannot be made coherent -- push back on that directly rather than politely complying with a task that cannot be done well as posed.
