So I'm drafting a short paper for a workshop on the philosophy of emotion in the spring, and I'd love some comments before I embarass myself in front of the crowd. The paper tries to stake out a position in the old fight between judgment theories (emotions as evaluative judgments, roughly the Solomon/Nussbaum line) and perceptual theories (emotions as perception-like representations of value, think Prinz or Tappolet). Below is the current draft of the core argument -- please tell me (a) whether I'm characterizing the two camps fairly, (b) whether my main objection to the perceptual view actually lands, and (c) whether my positive proposal is coherent or just a judgment theory wearing a fake mustache. Specific counterexamples very welcome.

Draft: Judgment theorists say fearing the dog just is judging it dangerous. The standard objection is recalcitrance: the arachnophobe who sincerely judges spiders harmless still fears them, and we don't want to convict her of outright contradiction. Perceptual theorists pounce on this. Fear, they say, is like seeing the Müller-Lyer lines as unequal: it persists even when you know better, because perceptual systems are encapsulated from belief. Neat. But here's my worry, and I don't think it's been pressed hard enough: perceptual illusions are motivationally inert. The Müller-Lyer lines don't grab your attention, don't keep you up at night, don't issue any felt pressure to do something. Recalcitrant fear does all of these. So the illusion analogy explains persistence but not grip. The perceptual theorist has explained why the spider still *looks* threatening, so to speak, but not why it still *matters* to me.

My proposal: emotions are what I call prescriptive perceptions. Fear doesn't just present the dog as dangerous; it presents it as to-be-avoided. The content is partly imperative, built right into the experience rather than added on by a separate desire. This gets me three things at once: motivation comes free (the mandate is constitutive), persistence comes free (it's still perceptual and encapsulated), and rational assessment comes free (imperative content can be apt or inapt, just as descriptive content can be accurate or not). I think this also nicely explains the phenomenon of being angry at one's own anger -- you're experiencing a mandate you simultaneously refuse to endorse.

Now, the trouble I'm having: grief. What on earth is the action mandate in grieving a dead parent? There is nothing to be done, and that's arguably the whole point of the experience. If grief has no prescriptive content, either my account fails for a central case or I have to say grief isn't (only) an emotion, which sounds like cheating. Right now I'm flirting with the line that grief prescribes something like attending-to-the-loss, but that feels thin. Tell me how bad this is, and whether the whole prescriptive-perception move collapses into judgment theory once you spell out what 'imperative content' even means for a perceptual state.
