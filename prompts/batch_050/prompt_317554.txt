Act as a philosophy-of-probability expert who also gets how data scientists think. I'm running a reading group for practicing data scientists (smart, stats-fluent, but most have never worried about foundations) and my session is on Bertrand's paradox as an attack on the principle of indifference. Plan the session for me — I want the plan, not the session content itself.

Before drafting, brainstorm a bit about where these people are coming from, then structure it as:

- The dependency order: what has to be in place before the paradox even lands as a paradox and not just a puzzle. E.g., I suspect they need to feel the pull of "ignorance means uniform" first, but maybe something else has to come before that.
- The carrying example: pick ONE. The classic random-chord-on-a-circle setup, or something closer to their day job (like "choose a random probability threshold between 0 and 1 for a classifier" vs. choosing it on the log-odds scale). Tell me which, and what the example must and must not have to do its job.
- The misconception map: these folks will say things like "priors are subjective anyway so who cares," "maximum entropy fixes this," and "there's a true frequency, just measure it." Map which of these gets raised at which point in the arc, whether to preempt or let them fall into it, and how each should be absorbed.
- The check at the end: how do I know they've gotten the real lesson (parameterization-dependence of "uniform") rather than just memorized "geometry problems are ambiguous."
- What to deliberately leave out at this level (Jeffreys priors? reference priors?) and what to say when someone asks.

Think carefully about the order — I'm convinced sequencing is 80% of the work here.
