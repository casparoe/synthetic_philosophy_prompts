So I was rereading the Preface of Kant's Groundwork for the Metaphysics of Morals (1785, in the T.K. Abbott translation) and I got myself into an argument that I'm pretty sure proves way too much, but I can't find the step to cut. Here's the passage that set me off:

"Everyone must admit that if a law is to have moral force, i.e., to be the basis of an obligation, it must carry with it absolute necessity; that, for example, the precept, "Thou shalt not lie," is not valid for men alone, as if other rational beings had no need to observe it; and so with all the other moral laws properly so called; that, therefore, the basis of obligation must not be sought in the nature of man, or in the circumstances in the world in which he is placed, but a priori simply in the conception of pure reason;"

And here's the argument I built off it:

(1) Per the passage, anything that matters morally -- the basis of obligation, and I'd assume moral worth too -- can't rest on "the nature of man" or "the circumstances of the world," i.e., on anything empirical.
(2) Whether some particular person acted from duty or from a warm fuzzy feeling is an empirical fact about their psychology. It's exactly the kind of thing anthropology or introspection would settle.
(3) So nothing about a person's actual motives can bear on moral assessment.
(4) But Kant also says an action only has genuine moral worth if it's done from duty, not just in line with duty -- which is a claim about motives.
(5) So Kant either contradicts himself in the space of like twenty pages, or (4) can't mean what it seems to mean.
(6) The charitable reading: motive-talk in (4) is just shorthand for something about the form of the maxim, and actual psychology drops out entirely.
(7) But then praise and blame should never track why someone did something, only what they willed formally -- so the person who helps others out of spontaneous sympathy and the person who grinds out help purely from duty deserve identical moral esteem, full stop. And that seems nuts.

I'd rather keep (4) meaning something real about motives if at all possible, and I'd rather not convict Kant of contradicting himself, but mostly I want to know which step is actually doing the damage and what it costs to reject it. My best guess is something goes wrong around (3) but I cant articulate what.
