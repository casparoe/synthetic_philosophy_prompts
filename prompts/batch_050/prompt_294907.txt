I am a novelist in the early stages of a book in which two families collide: one rooted in an old honor culture of feuds, dueling codes, and hospitality obligations, the other in a contemporary professional milieu where slights are handled through complaints, procedures, and third-party adjudication. To keep both sides from becoming caricatures, I hired two consultants to argue out the underlying moral question, and they have spent six months talking past each other. One holds that dignity culture — the culture that treats each person's worth as inherent and routes conflict through impartial institutions — is straightforwardly morally superior to honor culture, which she regards as a violent, patriarchal reputation-game that strong states rightly abolished. The other replies that honor culture cultivated virtues dignity culture has quietly lost — self-command, the willingness to stand behind one's name, refusal to outsource one's grievances — and that what she calls progress is partly the rise of a victimhood culture in which status accrues to those who publicize their victimhood, and in which feuds have not vanished but migrated into reputational pile-ons conducted by strangers.

Write the document they should have produced instead of arguing: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration. It should contain, first, a numbered list of claims both parties can sign without reservation — I suspect these include that all three moral cultures are strategies for handling conflict and offense rather than character types, that each thrives under particular institutional conditions (honor where protection is scarce, dignity where third parties are trusted, victimhood where sympathetic audiences are abundant and attention is a currency), and that no culture is free of coercion, only differently organized coercion. Second, a table of their remaining disagreements, each phrased neutrally enough that each party would accept the wording of her own position and of her opponent's — taking care to separate genuine disagreements from ones that dissolve once, say, honor is disambiguated into reputation, self-respect, and code-bound conduct. Third, a list of the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side.

For each crux, give a concrete case or body of evidence both would accept as bearing on it, and record each party's prediction in advance. I suspect, but you should feel free to revise, that the cruxes include these: whether honor is an intrinsic good or merely an instrumental adaptation to the absence of reliable protection (a case both would accept as bearing on this might be what happens to honor norms when functioning institutions arrive — do people shed them with relief, or mourn them?); whether the aggregate ledger favors the old order or the new once one counts dueling deaths and feud casualties against incarceration, anonymity-enabled harassment, and the mental toll of complaint-driven status competition; whether victimhood culture is a genuinely distinct moral culture or dignity culture amplified by new attention incentives; and whether asking which culture is morally superior even makes sense apart from asking which institutions a culture presupposes. The document will become the shared briefing my two consultants hand to my fictional families, so it must be something real partisans could actually sign.
