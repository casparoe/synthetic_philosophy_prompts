I serve on the ethics committee of a mid-sized psychiatric hospital, and we have been asked to rule on a case that has split the staff. A 38-year-old patient with bipolar I disorder, during a stable period and with documented decision-making capacity, signed a psychiatric advance directive -- essentially a Ulysses contract -- stating that if she became manic, she was to be hospitalized involuntarily and treated with lithium even over her objections at the time. The directive includes a revocation clause of her own design: she can cancel it, but only in writing, only with thirty days' notice, and only after two independent psychiatrists certify she has capacity. She has now been brought in during a manic episode, is refusing medication, and is loudly and articulately demanding that the directive be torn up, saying -- and this is the part that has the treating team genuinely unsettled rather than merely frustrated -- that the woman who signed it had no right to sell her future self into confinement, any more than a sober person can consent in advance to being restrained whenever she next gets drunk.

The committee is divided three ways. One faction says the directive is binding: it was executed with more information, more capacity, and more safeguards than most decisions we honor, and the whole point of a Ulysses contract is that it binds precisely when the present self demands release -- deferring to the manic refusal makes such contracts logically impossible. A second faction says the present refusal must win: the person in the bed is the one who bears the consequences, she is not psychotic and can give reasons, and a competent person's contemporaneous refusal of treatment normally overrides everything short of danger to others. A third faction, which I find tempting but worry is a fudge, wants to treat the episode itself as incapacity and decide on best-interests grounds, sidestepping the precommitment question entirely.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend one of these positions (or a genuinely distinct fourth, if you think the framing is wrong) as the committee's formal recommendation, with a concrete directive the treating team can act on for this admission and a policy statement for future advance directives, including what should count as adequate safeguards when such directives are drafted. It must engage the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly -- not the strawman version -- and it should grapple with the identity question the patient herself raised: whether, and in what sense, a past self may legitimately govern a present one who disowns her, and whether that question is even the right one for a hospital to be answering.
