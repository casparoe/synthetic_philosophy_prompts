I write for a general-interest magazine on science and ideas, and my desk keeps running into the same editorial problem: philosophers will tell me, on the record, not just what people believe about some contested moral question but what the right answer is — that a practice is wrong, that a duty exists, that one value outweighs another. Empirical experts' claims I know how to handle; I can check data and triangulate. Moral claims from a named authority feel different, and my editors disagree about whether a journalist should ever simply pass them along.

Write me a decision procedure I can run, in under half an hour, whenever a source offers me a moral verdict I might relay to readers: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and the conditions under which I may report the verdict as one I have accepted on the expert's say-so versus only as one the expert holds. The procedure should be grounded in what the philosophical literature on moral testimony actually treats as the central problem — the widely noted asymmetry between accepting empirical claims and accepting moral claims on testimony, and the idea (however contested) that moral knowledge is defective or second-rate when it comes without the understanding of why the claim is true. Distinguish at least these cases in the procedure itself: a verdict backed by broad expert consensus, a verdict from a single philosopher on a live dispute, a moral judgment smuggled inside an otherwise empirical claim, and the question of whether my own grasp of the expert's reasons — not just their credentials — changes what I'm permitted to print. Then run the procedure on three worked examples, one of which should yield a restriction I will find inconvenient for deadline journalism. Close by marking which steps the procedure cannot settle mechanically and must leave to editorial judgment.

Maximum 800 words.
