I'm trying to sort out my intuitions about when the state may compel people to serve, and whether letting money into the picture makes things better or worse. Here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases:

1. An all-volunteer professional army, paid decent wages, whose ranks end up filled mostly by people from poor backgrounds because it's their best available option: fine. Not ideal, but fine.

2. A draft lottery where everyone aged 18-25 is equally exposed, no exemptions, no buyouts, in a genuine war of national survival: acceptable, if anything is.

3. Same war, same lottery, but draftees can pay a fixed fee or hire a substitute to serve in their place (the Union allowed something like this in the Civil War): clearly wrong. Somehow *much* worse than both 1 and 2, which is weird when you think about it.

4. Peacetime conscription where only a fraction of each cohort is actually needed, chosen by lottery: not acceptable, even though each person's chance of being taken is lower than in case 2 and the risks are way lower.

5. A Swiss-style militia setup: every man serves, or does civilian service, and those who do neither pay an annual exemption tax of a few percent of income: I can't fully decide, but it doesn't feel nearly as bad as 3, even though it's kinda just a buyout with extra steps.

6. Mandatory jury duty, compensated at a pittance, with courts excusing people for hardship or professional obligations: fine. It's coerced and underpaid and I still think it's completely fine.

7. A year of mandatory civilian national service for everyone at 18 (hospitals, elder care, infrastructure), no military dimension at all: bad. Worse than jury duty, even though the physical risk is zero and the social benefit is real.

8. A Finland-style arrangement where conscientious objectors may do alternative civilian service, but it's deliberately set longer than military service, partly to test sincerity: pretty ugly.

My first pass at a principle was: compelled service is OK when the burden is spread universally and equally, and money corrupts it when it lets people buy their way out. That nicely vindicates 2 over 3. But it leaves 1 unexplained -- in the volunteer army the poor effectively serve *because of* money and the rich effectively buy out by not needing the wage, and yet I think 1 is fine. It also condemns 5 harder than I want to, since the Swiss tax is literally a fee for not serving. And it can't distinguish 6 from 7: both universal, both coerced, neither buyable, and my verdicts differ. Duration? Stakes? I can't tell.

So: is there a principle that captures most of these verdicts? If yes, state it precisely and tell me wich clause each case is doing the work for. If no single principle gets all of them, tell me which verdict you think is the weakest and has to go, and why. And either way, I'm curious what your principle says about a case I didn't list: a draft where anyone can opt out by paying into a fund that's used to raise volunteer wages until enough volunteers show up to make the draft unnecessary.
