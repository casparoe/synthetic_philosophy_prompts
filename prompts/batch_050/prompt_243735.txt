I've been reading about fitting-attitude and buck-passing accounts of value, on which for something to be admirable just is for it to have properties that give us reason to admire it. The standard demon case, though, seems like a clean counterexample. Suppose a demon credibly threatens to torture me unless I admire his cruelty. Then I have a strong, perhaps decisive, reason to admire the cruelty, even though there is nothing admirable about it; the threat generates a reason for the attitude without generating any value in its object. So reasons for attitudes and value can come entirely apart. Given this, why does the literature describe the "wrong kind of reasons problem" as a difficulty to be managed within buck-passing accounts -- by stipulating that only reasons of the right kind figure in the analysis -- rather than as a straightforward refutation of them? Isn't the "right kind of reasons" clause simply an ad hoc patch on a theory that the counterexample has already falsified?
