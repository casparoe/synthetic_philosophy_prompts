A few years ago I read Thinking, Fast and Slow and then fell down a total rabbit hole on the sunk cost fallacy, and I've basically turned it into a personal policy that I also push on everyone around me: once money, time, or effort is spent, it's gone, so it should never factor into any decision, period, full stop. I manage a small internal grant fund at a research institute (about a dozen two-year projects running at any given time), and at our annual review I tell PIs the correct procedure is simple: look only at what the project will deliver from today forward relative to its remaining budget, and if anyone starts talking about how much we've already put in, that's the fallacy talking and we ignore it. I've even started applying the rule to my own life -- last month I walked out of a movie halfway through on princple, even though I was actually kind of enjoying it, because I'd already "paid" the ticket either way.

Recently two people pushed back and it's rattled me a bit. A colleague said that when she decides whether to renew a project, the fact that one team needed 200k to get to a working prototype while another needed 60k tells her something real about the teams, so "ignore the past" means throwing away perfectly good data. Separately, one PI argued that finishing his half-failed project matters for the institute's credibility with the ministry that funds us, and that the money already spent is somehow "part of the deal going forward." My gut says both of these are just the sunk cost fallacy dressed up in fancier language, and that any clean decision procedure has to be blind to the past by definition.

So: is my blanket rule actually right? If it isn't, where exactly does it go wrong -- what is the precise thing the sunk cost fallacy forbids, and which of my colleague's and the PI's arguments (if either) is legitimate forward-looking reasoning rather than the fallacy in disguise? And what should I actually tell PIs at the next annual review, given that some of them are definitely using "we've come so far" as a shield for failing projects? I'd like a crisp bottom line: end your answer with a last line starting with "Verdict:" saying whether I should keep my rule as-is, modify it, or drop it, plus one sentence stating the modified rule if there is one.
