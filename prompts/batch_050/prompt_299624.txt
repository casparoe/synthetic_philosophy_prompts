I TA for a political philosophy seminar (juniors and seniors, about 16 students, one 75-minute session a week) and we're in the middle of a unit on public reason and religious arguments in politics. My students can recite the basic Rawlsian idea -- that when we justify coercive laws to each other, we owe each other reasons that don't depend on any one controversial religious or philosophical doctrine -- but they treat it as obviously correct in a way that makes me think they haven't felt why religious citizens (and philosophers like Wolterstorff) find it so alienating and maybe rigged. They also blur together two very different proposals: the idea that laws need reasons everyone can share (consensus) and the idea that each citizen can justify a law from within their own doctrine, so long as the law itself doesn't single anyone out (convergence).

Design me a structured classroom activity that makes them feel the bite of this, not just nod at it. My rough sketch: a mock town council has to pass a real-ish ordinance (maybe: whether the public school's sex-ed curriculum must include an abstinence module, or whether town funds can support a religiously run homeless shelter with hiring preferences), with a rotating "public reason referee" who can strike any argument that fails the constraint -- and the constraint changes between rounds, so the same speaker is fluent in one round and nearly mute in the next. But you should tear up my sketch if something better makes the point. I want roles and concrete rules (what counts as a strikable argument, how the referee is picked, what happens if a player thinks a strike was unfair -- can they appeal? to whom?), a payoff or stakes structure that doesn't require money, a timeline for the 75 minutes, and the debrief questions that map specific moments in the game onto the consensus-vs-convergence distinction and the "asymmetry" objection that secular reasons get counted as public while religious ones don't.

Two more things. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it -- e.g., I'm guessing students will quickly learn to launder religious reasons into neutral-sounding ones ("this isn't about my faith, it's about... community values") and I'm not sure if I should treat that as cheating or as the most instructive moment of the session. Second, be honest about what the activity can't reproduce: my sense is that a game where being "silenced" costs you points can't capture what it's like to be asked to bracket something identity-constituting when real political losses are at stake, but maybe there's a way to get closer to that than my sketch does.
