ok so for the past few months I've been collecting "ways out of Buridan's ass" -- first for a comment thread, now it's turning into a post. The setup, to be clear about what problem I mean: an agent faces two options that are, by its own lights, exactly equally good. Not "hard to tell apart," not "probably about equal" -- exactly tied, and the agent knows it. Classic version: the donkey between two identical bales of hay starves because there's no reason to go left rather than right. Modern versions: two identical items on a shelf, two moves in a game that are provably worth the same, picking between two job offers after your spreadsheet says dead even.

The moves I already have, which I do NOT want repeated:

1. Randomize. Flip a coin. In decision-theory terms a mixed strategy over tied options has the same expected value as either, and it terminates, so deliberation ends. Sometimes the right move is outsourcing the choice to noise.
2. Deny the premise: exact ties basically never happen in the wild. Any real pair of options differs somewhere, and enough introspection or computation finds the difference, so the donkey is a philosophers' fiction and not a practical problem.
3. The picking/choosing distinction: some selections don't need reasons at all. Rationality demands you take the best option when there is one; when options tie, just picking either is fully rational and needs no justification. The donkey only starves if it wrongly thinks it needs a reason.
4. Salience and focal points: grab whatever asymmetry the environment hands you (left before right, top shelf first, alphabetical order) as a conventional tiebreaker even while admitting it's normatively arbitrary.
5. Bounded rationality: deliberation costs time and compute, and the expected gain from figuring out which tied option is better is zero by hypothesis, so continuing to deliberate is strictly irrational. Grab one and move on.
6. Dissolution by indifference: if you're genuinely indifferent, you can't go wrong, so there is no decision problem to solve. The "paradox" is an artifact of describing the situation as if a reason were required.

I want a seventh resolution that is genuinely different from these -- not a rewording, not a special case, and not a mashup ("randomize because deliberation is costly" is just 1 glued to 5, don't bother). It has to engage the exact-tie case head-on rather than dissolve it by saying ties are rare or unreal.

Then be honest with me: does your seventh move actually work, or does it collapse into one of the six under pressure? And if you think the real answer is that the list is complete and no seventh move exists, you can argue that instead -- but only if you have an actual argument, not just a vibe that I've got them all.

Write it in American English, and keep it casual -- this is for a blog post, not a journal submission.
