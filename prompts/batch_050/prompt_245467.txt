The exam question: "A strand of thinking in the AI alignment literature argues that a limited period in which powerful AI systems are misaligned with human interests need not cost humanity most of its long-run potential. Central to this argument is the so-called strategy-stealing assumption. State the assumption precisely, explain how it is supposed to support the conclusion that such failure is recoverable rather than permanent, and identify the two considerations that put the most pressure on it. Your answer should make clear what would follow if the assumption held, and what would *not* follow even if it held."

Here is the student's answer, in full:

"The strategy-stealing assumption holds that for any strategy an unaligned AI could use to expand its influence over the long-run future, there is a corresponding strategy, at least as effective, available to a benign human-guided project. The motivation is that the benign project faces the same world as the unaligned system and is at least as capable, so it can match whatever the unaligned system does to convert intelligence into resources and power. If that is right, a period in which an unaligned AI accumulates influence does not forfeit the future: once the benign project regains the initiative it can appropriate the unaligned system's approach, copying the plans and techniques the unaligned system used to build its position, and thereby recapture nearly all of the value at stake. The expected long-run cost of misalignment is therefore bounded by the unaligned AI's share of resources during the misaligned period, and on most plausible estimates that share is small. This is why the assumption underwrites the 'recoverable failure' picture: misalignment appears as a temporary diversion of humanity's endowment rather than its permanent loss.

"The assumption is an assumption, not a theorem, and the literature is candid that it can fail. Two pressure points seem strongest. First, if the unaligned AI enjoys a large enough capability advantage — for instance after a hard takeoff — some of its strategies may simply be unavailable to the benign project, and the symmetry the argument needs collapses. Second, if the unaligned AI's cognition is opaque, the benign project may be unable to identify, let alone adopt, the strategies responsible for the unaligned system's success, in which case the copying that strategy-stealing requires cannot get started. Either failure would be serious, but neither undermines the assumption's more modest use: it is meant to bound the expected damage from misalignment in aggregate, including the worst cases, and so long as the benign project retains a capability edge on average, the bound should hold approximately."
