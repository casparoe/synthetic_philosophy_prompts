In a reading group on the ethics of belief, we reached William James's essay "The Will to Believe," and a passage from its opening section stalled us for most of the evening. James is introducing his distinction between live and dead hypotheses, and he writes:

"This shows that deadness and liveness in an hypothesis are not intrinsic properties, but relations to the individual thinker. They are measured by his willingness to act. The maximum of liveness in an hypothesis means willingness to act irrevocably. Practically that means belief; but there is some believing tendency wherever there is willingness to act at all."

What stalled us was that one member cited this passage to defend a contemporary-sounding claim: that when the stakes of being wrong rise high enough, and you find yourself hesitating to act, this hesitation shows you did not really believe the proposition after all — belief being, on James's criterion, nothing over and above willingness to act. Another member objected that James is doing descriptive psychology here — explaining what makes an option feel alive to a thinker, not giving an analysis of belief — and that nothing normative follows about what one may or should believe under pressure. A third suggested the passage is really about degrees of confidence: that outright belief is just the limiting case of a continuum of "believing tendency," and that James anticipates the idea that what shifts with the stakes is not your confidence but whether that confidence amounts to belief.

Please adjudicate by laying out, as a menu rather than a single reading, the distinct precise theses this passage could be expressing. I have in mind at least the following candidate shapes, but please correct or extend the list: a constitutive thesis (belief just is a settled willingness to act); a psychological thesis about the phenomenology of live options; a degree-theoretic thesis (credences are fundamental, belief the limiting case); a normative-epistemic thesis about when one is entitled to believe; and a thesis about how the epistemic status of a fixed body of evidence can vary with what rides on the question. For each candidate, say whether it fits the surrounding context of James's argument, whether it survives contact with modern objections, and whether it would actually support the use my first colleague made of it — that high-stakes hesitation retroactively cancels belief.

Test your readings against three hard cases: the person who sincerely avows a proposition but will not act on it when the cost of error becomes severe (have they lost a belief, revealed its absence, or merely revealed its degree?); the person who denies believing yet reliably acts as if the proposition were true; and James's own qualifier "irrevocably," which seems to make the criterion for full belief turn on the character of the available act rather than on the believer's state of mind — does that refinement save the constitutive reading or expose a circularity in it? I am less interested in a label for the view than in exactly where each reading stands and where it breaks.
