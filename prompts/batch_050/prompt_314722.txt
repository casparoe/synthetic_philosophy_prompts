I am a professor of philosophy setting the agenda for a five-year interdisciplinary research program on societal collapse as a concept. The program's test cases are the usual ones — the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya lowlands, and Rapa Nui — and the money is real but limited: we can fund serious work on perhaps three questions, not ten. The field sits uneasily between archaeology, historical social science, and philosophy of science, and my worry is that it runs on an undertheorized concept of its central object. One tradition measures collapse as a rapid loss of sociopolitical complexity; popular accounts treat collapse as failure brought on by environmental mismanagement; and a revisionist strain in the study of Late Antiquity holds that the so-called fall of Rome was a gradual transformation that most contemporaries would not have experienced as catastrophe. Even the canonical cases are contested: the older narrative of ecological self-destruction on Rapa Nui has been challenged by archaeological work suggesting a reasonably stable population until European contact and the diseases that came with it.

Here is the candidate list of open questions I am working from:

1. Operationalization. Can collapse be given a measure that does not smuggle in the researcher's values — complexity loss, population decline, political fragmentation, cultural discontinuity — and do these dimensions actually dissociate? Are there cases where hierarchy collapsed while ordinary welfare rose, and if so, is "collapse" one phenomenon or several?
2. Normativity. Whose bad is a collapse? If a simpler successor society feeds its children better and works them less, in what sense did anything go wrong, and does the concept have a built-in bias toward the perspectives of literate elites?
3. Explanation. Is collapse a distinct explanandum requiring its own theory — endogenous rigidity, declining marginal returns on complexity, cascading failure across coupled subsystems — or is it just ordinary social change observed at a coarse timescale?
4. Prediction. Do early-warning indicators borrowed from dynamical systems theory (critical slowing down, rising variance and autocorrelation before transitions) transfer from ecosystems to societies, and would a positive answer license anything about contemporary risk?
5. The continuity challenge. Given that every "collapse" case also looks like a transformation under a different description, what would it take for the concept to be falsifiable rather than merely a way of narrating decline?

Which three of these deserve the program's funding, and in what order?
