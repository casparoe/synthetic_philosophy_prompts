Retired chemist here, fairly new to philosophy, and I've been reading around Locke's claim that personal identity is basically a matter of memory -- you're the same person as some past self just in case you can remember being them. I've been collecting the standard objections and I think I have most of them: Reid's brave officer (the officer remembers the boy but the old general doesn't remember the officer, so transitivity breaks), Butler's charge that appealing to memory just presupposes the identity it's meant to explain, the false-memory problem (I could sincerely 'remember' doing things I never did), the fission problem (if my psychology gets copied into two people, memory can't make me identical to both), and the gap problem (we forget huge stretches of our lives, so by the criterion I'm not the person who did most of what I did). Give me one more objection to the memory criterion that isn't a rewording or a special case of any of these -- ideally one that leans on something empirical about how memory actually works, since that's my home turf -- and tell me whether you think it survives the usual fixes like replacing strict remembering with overlapping chains of memory.
