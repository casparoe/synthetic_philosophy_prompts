I cover mathematics and the sciences for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a piece about how mathematicians decide which conjectures are worth spending years on. While reporting it I've noticed a style of argument that comes up constantly, that practitioners treat as completely routine, and that I cannot find a name for. It goes roughly like this. Take some famous unproven claim — say, that every even number is the sum of two primes, or that there are infinitely many prime pairs two apart. The mathematician doesn't try to prove it. Instead they say: pretend primes were scattered among the integers by chance, with each number n being prime with probability about 1/ln n. Then estimate how often the conjecture's requirement would be met under that pretense — for a given even number, how many ways of splitting it into two pieces would land both pieces on primes — and treat the relevant events as if they were independent coin flips, so the probabilities just multiply. If the expected count is enormous, the conjecture is 'surely true' and people work on proving it; if the expected count converges to something tiny, the conjecture is probably false and people work on disproving it. The same template gets applied to claims about equations having integer solutions: estimate the chance a random tuple works, sum over all tuples, and read the answer off whether the sum diverges or converges.

What strikes me is that everyone I've interviewed performs this move, no one calls it anything more specific than a heuristic, and yet it seems to be doing real epistemic work — it redirects careers and grant money. It's clearly not a proof, and the people doing it will cheerfully admit that the underlying assumption is literally false: primes are not independent of each other, since knowing a number is even already tells you something, and my sources mentioned that there are known cases where this whole style of reasoning gives a confidently wrong answer that later had to be patched by adding correction factors. But it's also not mere gut feeling, because the calculations are precise and different people get the same numbers. It sits in some uncategorized space between proof, statistical inference, and hunch.

My questions: does this configuration — assigning degrees of belief to deterministic mathematical claims by modeling the objects as random and presuming the relevant constraints vary independently — have an established name in mathematics or philosophy, and a literature I could describe without turning my article into a textbook? How does it differ from adjacent things I do have names for, like Bayesian credence assignment, informal plausible reasoning of the sort Pólya wrote about, and ordinary statistical modeling, given that here there is no actual randomness in the world to model? And if there is no settled name for the general pattern (as opposed to its specific instances in number theory), I'd like you to propose one, define it precisely enough that a reader could tell which arguments fall under it, and mark its boundary: what features must be present, what nearby forms of reasoning are excluded, and under what conditions the pattern is trustworthy versus known to break.
