ok so my coworker has a whole thing about how generational labels are pseudoscience, and she asked me to read a draft of a blog post she's writing. i don't know enough demography to push back but some of it feels too neat. here's the key paragraph:

'Social scientists have largely debunked the idea of generations. The cutoffs are arbitrary -- nobody seriously thinks a person born December 31, 1980 has a fundamentally different outlook from someone born a week later, but that's exactly what the Gen X/Millennial line implies. Since the boundaries don't track anything real, generations are basically astrology for people with master's degrees, and Pew Research essentially admitted this when they announced the labels do more harm than good. On top of that, every generation in recorded history has complained that young people are lazy, entitled, and disrespectful, which tells you the so-called generational differences people obsess over are really just age effects dressed up as cohort effects. The right conclusion is that nothing distinctive about "your generation" survives scrutiny: people differ by age, class, and country, not by birth cohort.'

how much of this would an actual sociologist or demographer sign off on? i'm inclined to agree with her that the cutoffs are arbitrary, but the move from "arbitrary boundaries" to "nothing real here" seems fast, and i have a vague memory that certain events really do leave a mark on a whole cohort -- like people who graduated into the 2008 recession having worse earnings for years afterward. so: go through it claim by claim. what's accurate, what's a fair-but-loaded framing, and what's an actual misrepresentation of how demographers think about this? and does her final conclusion (nothing distinctive survives, it's all age/class/country) follow from the parts that are right, or does it sneak in something extra?
