A colleague and I have been arguing about the Death in Damascus case for weeks -- the version where Death keeps an appointment book, infallibly records where each person will be on the appointed day, and the servant, upon learning this, must decide whether to stay in Damascus or flee to Aleppo, knowing that wherever he goes, Death's book already says so. In the course of the argument I've staked out a set of positions, and I'd like an audit of whether they can be jointly held before I build a paper around them. Here is where I've landed. First, in the infallible version of the case, the servant who undertakes the costly, exhausting flight to Aleppo is doing the rational thing -- and the servant who stays in Damascus on the grounds that it cannot matter anyway, since the book is already written, is making a rational mistake; resignation in the face of a fixed future is a failure of practical reason, not an insight into it. Second, I one-box in Newcomb's problem, and my rationale is the standard evidentialist one: since the prediction is already made, your choice cannot causally influence what's in the boxes, but it is excellent evidence of what's in them, and rationality consists in choosing so as to give yourself the best news. Third, whether Death is literally infallible or merely has a perfect track record so far should make no difference to what the servant ought to do, since the two hypotheses are indistinguishable to him from the inside. Fourth, I hold that every well-posed decision problem has at least one rationally correct option -- rationality may permit ties, but it never simply fails to deliver a verdict; there are no holes in what reason demands. Fifth, if the servant flees and meets Death in Aleppo, he made no mistake: the bad outcome does not retroactively convert his decision into the wrong one, since the rationality of a choice is fixed by the situation at the time of choosing, not by how things turn out.

My colleague claims this package is incoherent, though she won't say where, and I suspect she's bluffing. I can see that my first and third commitments might look uneasy together, and that my second commitment has a reputation for causing trouble everywhere it goes, but I genuinely cannot locate the problem -- each commitment strikes me as something a reasonable decision theorist holds, and several of them seem to be held by the very same people. What I want is a proper audit: identify the genuine tensions in this set, ordered by severity, and for each one state precisely which commitment has to be abandoned or weakened, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the package. Equally important, tell me which apparent conflicts dissolve on inspection -- pairs that look incompatible but turn out not to be, with the reasoning shown -- because I don't want to amputate a commitment over an illusion. If it turns out that some pair of my commitments jointly rules out every standard decision rule on offer, I want that stated plainly rather than gestured at.

Please write your assessment as continuous prose, the way you'd write a long email to a colleague -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and don't restate my commitments back to me in list form; just work through the analysis in order of how much damage each finding does.
