Can you explain how Williams’s integrity objection and the one thought too many complaint come apart, using a slightly annoying case I made up?

A trauma surgeon has to choose between spending the next hour operating on her wife or on two strangers, and can only save the patients she treats. She chooses her wife immediately, without first checking whether impartial morality permits the preference. A philosopher friend says this shows one thought too many: the problem would be pausing to ask for moral permission. But another friend says that misses Williams’s deeper point. Even if the surgeon did pause, the real issue would be whether her attachment to her wife is one of the ground projects that makes her life hers, rather than merely a private preference competing with an impersonal ranking of outcomes.

Please distinguish at least three complaints that might be getting mashed together here:  
1. a complaint about what the agent consciously thinks in the moment,  
2. a complaint about what reasons ultimately justify the action, and  
3. a complaint about what kind of person the agent would have to become in order to reliably follow an impartial theory.

Then explain where integrity does independent work. I’m especially interested in whether the objection is really against deliberation itself, against deliberating at the wrong level, or against a theory that can only retroactively authorize a motive it treats as morally suspicious. Also discuss whether an explicit moral commitment can itself be part of someone’s integrity, so that a devoted utilitarian aid worker might have the opposite problem if they ignored an impersonal calculation.

Use the surgeon case plus two contrasting variations: one where intimate partiality seems obviously corrupt, and one where stopping to reflect seems morally admirable rather than alienated. I sorta want an account of when reflection is one thought too many and when failing to reflect is one thought too few.
