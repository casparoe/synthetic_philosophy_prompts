I spent forty years as a geologist (sedimentology, mostly) and only got into philosophy in retirement, so forgive me if I walk into something well-trodden here. But I built this argument after reading about functionalism and multiple realizability, and it seems to prove something absurd, and I can't find the loose board.

(1) Functionalism says a mental state just is a functional role: a pattern of inputs, outputs, and internal states standing in the right relations. That's the whole selling point, since it explains how minds could be realized in brains, computers, Martians, whatever.

(2) A functional organization is fully specified by that abstract pattern — the state-transition table, basically. Nothing about the stuff doing the realizing matters.

(3) Take any finite transition table and any physically rich system — say the stone wall at the bottom of my garden, which has an enormous number of molecular states. Over any stretch of time the wall passes through a long sequence of distinct physical states. There will always be some mapping that assigns wall-states to table-states so that the wall's actual sequence lines up with the table's transitions.

(4) If the system's actual states line up with the table under a mapping, then the system realizes that functional organization. That just is what realization comes to, if (2) is true.

(5) So the wall realizes the functional organization of, pick your favorite, someone doing their tax return.

(6) So by (1) the wall has the mental states of someone doing their taxes. My wall is presently puzzling over deductions. This is daft, so the argument proves too much.

(7) Conclusion: functionalism, or at least multiple realizability as usually stated, is false.

Now, I'd rather keep (1) and (2) if I can, because the alternative seems to be chauvinism about carbon, which as a scientist I find embarassing. And (3) looks to me like plain combinatorics, the sort of counting argument we'd have accepted without blinking in my old department. So I suspect (4) is where the rot is, but every time I try to say what's wrong with it I end up either begging the question or smuggling biology back in through the window.

Could you lay the answer out in this order: first "Where it breaks" — one or two sentences naming the exact step. Then "Why the step looked fine" — a paragraph being fair to it, since I clearly fell for it. Then "Ways out and what they cost" — the meat of it, maybe three or four paragraphs, comparing at least two repairs and saying what each one gives up. Finally "Verdict on my wall" — short, plain language, no hedging.
