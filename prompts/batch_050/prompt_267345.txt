I'm giving a colloquium talk in about three weeks defending a time-slice view of rationality, roughly in the spirit of Brian Hedden: all rational requirements are synchronic. There's no standing requirement to be consistent with your past self, no rational norm of following through on intentions or plans, and diachronic patterns like money-pumpability or procrastination don't by themselves show any irrationality -- rationality only ever evaluates your state at a moment, given your beliefs and desires then.

The audience: a mid-sized philosophy department, nobody who works on decision theory. The core of the room is normative ethics and action theory people, most of whom take Bratman-style planning agency as basically settled and think intentions come with their own rational norms (means-end coherence, consistency, stability). There are a couple of Kantians who'll want to talk about willing the end and self-governance. And there's one philosopher of economics who is the only person in the room who'll actually know the money pump literature cold, so their question will be a different species from everyone elses.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'm actually going to get from this audience -- not the ones I'd get at a formal epistemology workshop -- ordered by how much damage they'd do to the talk. Each objection phrased the way that audience member would actually phrase it (the Bratmanian, the Kantian, the philosopher of economics), with a two-minute reply, and a note on where the reply is soft or where I'd have to concede ground.

A few things I especially want covered:

- The money pump argument for transitivity is my obvious weak point. The standard worry: on my view each step of the pump looks locally rational, so apparently nothing irrational happens even as the agent is predictably bled dry. "Bite the bullet" isn't going to fly with this crowd, I need a better answer, or at least a better way of framing which bullet I'm biting.
- The planning people are going to say that without diachronic requirements, resolute choice in temptation cases becomes rationally arbitrary, and sticking to a diet or a resolution is no more rational than abandoning it at every moment.
- Andreou-style procrastination: each individual delay is locally harmless and locally rational, the catastrophe is only visible across time. That seems engineered to hurt me.
- Someone will definitely ask what happens to trust, promising, and being a reliable colleague if there's no rational pressure to follow through.

Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
