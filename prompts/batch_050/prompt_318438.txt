Okay so I'm a novelist -- literary-historical stuff, third book, and this one has a big fictional empire in it that has to come apart by the end. Not fantasy exactly, more like a Rome-ish world with the serial numbers filed off. My editor's main note on the draft was that the collapse reads "like a plot point, not like history," which is fair but not super actionable.

Here's the thing that's been rattling around my head. I was rereading Gibbon, Decline and Fall, and at the end of chapter 38, in the part he calls the General Observations, he does this move where he basically says the ruin of the empire is simple and obvious to explain, and the real puzzle is the opposite -- not why Rome fell, but why it managed to last as long as it did. He flips the whole question around. I don't have the page in front of me so I'm paraphrasing, but that's the gist, and it kind of broke my brain a little, because everything in my draft is set up to explain the fall and nothing is set up to explain the three hundred years of muddling through that came before it.

So what I want is this: take the actual historiographical debate about why Rome fell (or didn't fall, depending who you ask -- the transformation crowd, the barbarians crowd, the climate and plague crowd, Gibbon's own "immoderate greatness" line of thinking) and turn it into a procedure I can run on my fictional empire. Like a checklist or a set of questions in order that I can sit down with for an afternoon and actually use on my outline. Something like: what's the tax base doing, what happens when an emperor dies, does anyone inside the story even experience this as a "fall" or only the people writing about it two hundred years later, that kind of thing -- but you tell me the right questions, and the right order, and what each question is supposed to rule in or out.

Some specific things I need it to handle:

1. There should be points where the procedure just says "stop, this is a thematic choice, not a mechanics question" -- because I suspect a lot of fall-vs-transformation is really about what I want the book to mean, and I don't want a checklist pretending it can decide that for me. Mark those points explicitly.
2. There should be at least one hard "no" -- some configuration of my plot where the procedure tells me the collapse as written is not believable no matter how good the prose is, and I have to restructure.
3. Take Gibbon's inversion seriously as a step in the procedure: make me answer, in worldbuilding terms, what was holding this thing together for the centuries before my plot starts, and tell me what goes wrong in a novel if the writer skips that step.
4. Then actually run the procedure on two versions I'm deciding between: (a) the empire gets overrun by invaders from the steppe in about thirty years, one generation sees the whole thing; (b) there is no single fall at all, the empire just sort of becomes a different country across 200 years and only the later characters talk about an empire "that used to exist." I suspect the procedure will prefer one of these and I'd like to see that happen honestly, even if it's not the one I want.
5. End with a frank note about what a checklist derived from real historiography can't capture about fiction -- like, pacing and reader sympathy are not things historians worry about.

Please write the whole thing in American English, and keep it practical -- I will be printing this out and taping it to the wall next to my desk, so fewer seminar words, more "do this, then this." Thanks!
