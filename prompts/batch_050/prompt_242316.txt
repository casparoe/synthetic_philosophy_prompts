I'm taking an upper-level seminar in metaphilosophy this term, and our unit on conceptual engineering has left me stuck on an argument I reconstructed from our readings and class discussion. It seems to show that ameliorative analysis -- the Haslanger-style project of deliberately revising concepts like woman, race, or knowledge so that they better serve our legitimate purposes -- is either pointless or self-undermining. Since I find the project appealing, I take it the argument goes wrong somewhere, but I can't locate the fault. Here it is:

(1) An inquiry into a concept -- say, an analysis or a proposed revision of it -- counts as being about that very concept only if it preserves the concept's meaning; otherwise the inquiry has simply changed the subject and is about something else.
(2) The stated motivation for ameliorating a concept is that the concept, as it stands, is defective: it does ideological or theoretical work we have reason not to want done, and that work is carried by what the concept means and how it sorts things.
(3) If the defect is carried by the concept's meaning, then removing the defect requires changing the meaning.
(4) So a successful amelioration produces a successor concept with a different meaning from the one we started with.
(5) By (1), that successor is not the concept we set out to fix -- we've stopped talking about woman or knowledge and started talking about something new.
(6) Therefore ameliorative analysis faces a dilemma: either it preserves the meaning and thereby keeps the defect it was meant to remove, or it removes the defect and thereby forfeits its claim to be about the original topic at all.
(7) And the second horn is worse than it looks, because the original motivation -- that our practices concerning the old concept were unjust or confused -- is left entirely untouched by a new concept that happens to be better behaved.

This strikes me as proving too much: if sound, an analogous argument would apparently show that no conceptual revision is ever genuinely continuous with what came before, including the revisions of concepts like fish, planet, or atom that we regard as unambiguous scientific progress. If forced to choose, I'd rather abandon premise (1) than accept (7), but I suspect the interesting move is elsewhere.

Which premise or step should I reject, and why does it look plausible? Please also price the alternatives: what do the standard moves in this literature -- distinguishing topic from meaning, appealing to a concept's function rather than its content, or the distinction between what a concept says and what it does -- each have to concede in order to resist the argument?
