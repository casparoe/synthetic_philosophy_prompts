Some context first. I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for donkey's years, and for the past three years I've sat on the grants panel of a small donor-funded outfit (EA-adjacent, let's say) that gives out five grants of £10–20k twice a year, picked from roughly forty applications. There are five of us on the panel, all volunteers, none of us philosophers. We get about twenty minutes of discussion per shortlisted application plus one long evening a month of reading. At the moment we score everything on a weighted rubric — basically shut-up-and-multiply with extra steps — and then spend hours arguing about the near-ties anyway, because the rubric output and our felt sense of the decision keep coming apart.

The passage that keeps nagging at me is from Plato's Protagoras (356d–357a, in the old public-domain translation hosted on the Perseus site). Socrates says:

"Whereas the art of measurement would have made this appearance ineffective, and by showing us the truth would have brought our soul into the repose of abiding by the truth, and so would have saved our life."

And a few lines on:

"Well then, my friends, since we have found that the salvation of our life depends on making a right choice of pleasure and pain—of the more and the fewer, the greater and the smaller, and the nearer and the remoter—is it not evident, in the first place, that measurement is a study of their excess and defect and equality in relation to each other?"

His claim, as I read it: every agonising choice is really a botched measurement, and once you have the measuring art the appearances lose their power and the choice dissolves. What I want is for you to turn that into something my panel can actually run, while also stress-testing whether it's true. Concretely, write us a decision procedure for the shortlisted cases where the room is split, with these features:

1. An opening filter that sorts each disputed choice into (a) a measurement problem in disguise, where more information or better aggregation would genuinely dissolve the disagreement — say what the filter questions are, in order, and what counts as a passing answer, and (b) a genuinely hard choice, where the options are on a par in the Ruth Chang-ish sense — neither better, nor worse, nor exactly equal, so no amount of measuring ever settles it. Say what evidence would show a choice is really type (b) rather than type (a) we haven't measured hard enough yet, since that's the move Socrates would accuse us of fumbling.
2. For type (a): what extra work the panel must do, given our twenty-minutes-an-application constraint, and when we're allowed to declare the measurement hopeless and reclassify.
3. For type (b): a legitimate stopping rule that neither fakes precision (no taking the rubric to three decimal places) nor abdicates (no coin flips, no chair-decides). If the best available answer here is that the panel should just pick and stop agonising — because deliberation can't manufacture a reason that isn't there — say so plainly and tell us what to write in the minutes so donors get an honest account rather than a post-hoc scoresheet.
4. Somewhere in the procedure, use the "nearer and remoter" bit of the passage properly: the near-looking-bigger effect is a real failure mode for us (scope insensitivity, the applicant we interviewed last, the charismatic one in the room). I want a step that checks whether our felt sense of a choice is appearance doing its work.

Then run the finished procedure on three cases, one of which should give an answer the panel won't like:
- A: a meticulous researcher with a strong track record doing modest, proven work, versus B: a first-timer with a speculative project that looks ten times as valuable if it works and probably won't.
- Two finalists, both clearly fundable: an animal-welfare researcher and a community organiser, where every attempt to put them on one scale feels like a category error rather than a hard sum.
- The committee favourite: an applicant the whole panel warmed to at interview, where the scores say he's middling. I suspect the procedure will tell us the warmth is the power of appearance and to discount it, or else that this was never a hard choice and we've wasted three meetings on it — work out which, and don't flinch.

Mark the points where judgement cannot be eliminated and the procedure has to hand off to a person, be honest about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure, and finish by saying what this procedure cannot do for us — I'd rather know its limits than discover them infront of a donor. Aim for 1,200–1,500 words, not counting the quoted passage.
