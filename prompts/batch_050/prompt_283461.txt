I am a retired research chemist. After thirty-five years in industrial laboratories I have, in retirement, drifted into philosophy, and I now help run a monthly discussion group at our university's continuing-education institute: roughly sixteen participants of mixed background, ages thirty to seventy-five, meeting for ninety minutes.

Next month's topic is loyalty, whistleblowing, and the ethics of betrayal. My interest is not merely theoretical. Twice in my career I watched colleagues agonize over whether to report data manipulation by people they had worked beside for years, and what struck me both times was that their anguish had almost nothing to do with weighing consequences and nearly everything to do with the felt texture of betrayal — had the culprit been a stranger in another department, the same act would have cost them a phone call. I want the group to feel that asymmetry in their own choices before we ever open a text, because I suspect that without the feeling, the philosophy reads as a puzzle about cowardice rather than what it actually is: two genuine goods in collision.

Design an in-session activity for us, subject to the following requirements.

1. No lecture beforehand and no naming of the theme. The experience must come first; the vocabulary of loyalty and betrayal should only be introduced in the debrief, once participants have something to attach it to.

2. A genuine bonding phase. Before any dilemma appears, participants must build real, if brief and modest, collegiality — a cooperative task with a small shared payoff, something that makes a team feel like a team in twenty minutes. The loyalty they later confront must be theirs, not a hypothetical someone's.

3. Private, graded information. Each participant should learn, in confidence, of a transgression committed within the game world, with the severity of transgressions varying across participants. Specify the gradations and how they are distributed.

4. Here is the constraint I care most about, and I ask it as an experimentalist. I want the activity to function as a crude controlled comparison. Across rounds, vary the identity of the transgressor — a teammate one has just worked beside, a stranger from another team, the institution running the game itself — while holding severity roughly constant; and separately, vary severity while holding the relationship constant. Each round, participants decide whether to report to a rotating authority, with reporting decisions recorded. The point is that the room should be able to see, in its own revealed choices rather than in anything I assert, which variable was doing the work: how bad the act was, or whose act it was.

5. Real but trivial stakes: points, a shared pot, something concrete, but nothing that could not fit in a biscuit tin.

6. A debrief plan that maps specific moments of the session onto the philosophical distinctions I want surfaced: loyalty to persons versus loyalty to institutions or principles; whether disclosure to an outside authority even counts as betrayal when it is owed; and the thought, which I find the most unsettling of the three, that the very psychology that makes loyalty exploitable by corrupt organizations is also what makes shared projects possible at all. Tell me which observable moment of the activity carries each of these.

7. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and what to do about it. I can already foresee some candidates: a participant who reports everything immediately and flattens the comparison; participants who treat the points as an optimization problem and never let the dilemma bite; a bonding phase too flimsy to generate real reluctance. You will foresee others.

8. Finally, be candid about what this format cannot reproduce. Twenty minutes of paper-tower building is not twenty years of shared grant applications, and a jar of biscuits is not a pension. Tell me what I must supply in the debrief to bridge that gap without reverting to a lecture.

One caution: several of our members are retired professionals who will have buried versions of my own story. If any feature of the activity risks opening something raw at the table, I would rather know in advance and plan for it than discover it on the night.
