So I got into this long argument with my brother-in-law over christmas about whether you should ever believe something that goes against everything you've personally experienced. He was making fun of people who fall for miracle stories and healing crystals and whatever, and I was like, fair enough, but then I remembered something I read a while back in David Hume's Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, in the section called "Of Miracles," and now I'm stuck on it and want an essay that sorts it out for me.

Here's the bit that bugs me (paraphrasing, I don't have the book in front of me). Hume talks about an Indian prince living somewhere hot who hears travellers say that in cold countries water turns so solid you can walk on it, and he refuses to believe them. And Hume says the prince reasoned justly! Not that he was being stubborn or dumb — that given his experience, refusing was the reasonable thing. But obviously the prince was WRONG. Ice is real. So Hume seems to be saying you can be perfectly rational and land on a false belief, and the weirder the claim is relative to your experience, the more rational your disbelief is, even when the claim happens to be true.

So write me an essay, maybe 800-1000 words, on this question: does the prince example actually help Hume's case against believing miracle reports, or does it accidentally blow a hole in it? Some angles I've been chewing on, feel free to ignore or add your own:

- If the prince reasons justly and still ends up wrong, then "reasoning justly from your experience" doesn't get you truth, it just gets you... whatever your experience happens to support. Doesn't that mean Hume's whole approach can just as easily protect a false belief as block one? The prince's disbelief in ice and the religious person's disbelief in, idk, evolution or whatever seem structurally identical.
- On the other hand, maybe the prince's error is fixable in a way the miracle case isn't. Travellers could eventually bring back ice, or take him north. What does that difference (testability? repeatability? the fact that frost is regular once you move?) do to the argument?
- Where does this leave me in actual life? I don't want to be the prince about stuff outside my bubble (I've never seen a black swan or been to Australia, technically), but I also don't want to be the sucker who believes every wild claim because "hey, the prince was wrong once."

Take an actual position on whether the example strengthens or undermines Hume, and defend it — don't just survey both sides and shrug. And at the very end, give me a one line bottom line starting with "Verdict:" that says in a sentence whether the prince example helps or hurts Hume's argument against miracles.
