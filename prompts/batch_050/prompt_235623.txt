I help run a small reading group on the ethics of deception, and we've hit a recurring frustration: participants keep sliding between two distinct questions -- what *counts* as a lie, and whether lying is morally worse than merely misleading -- and treating a verdict on the first as if it settled the second. I'd like a test case that pries these apart cleanly. Please construct a single, realistic scenario -- an office, a tenancy dispute, a family dinner; ordinary stakes, no philosophers, no contrived machinery, one paragraph at most -- in which one speaker makes two assertions, U1 and U2, as parts of a unified attempt to steer a hearer into a false belief. Engineer it to meet three constraints: (a) U1 is in fact false, but the speaker believes it true (the speaker has been misinformed); (b) U2 is in fact true, but the speaker believes it false (their information is out of date in a way that happens to favor their plan); and (c) in both cases, the specific false proposition the hearer is meant to walk away with is carried by an obvious inference from what is asserted, not by the asserted content itself.

Then demonstrate that the scenario meets the spec by working through three accounts of lying: the traditional account requiring objective falsity plus intent to deceive; an insincerity-only account, on which lying is asserting what one takes to be false regardless of how things actually stand; and an account that confines lying to what is literally said and files everything carried by Gricean implicature under mere misleading. For each account, state which of U1 and U2 it classifies as a lie and why -- if I've specified the case correctly, the three accounts should deliver three different partitions. Next, identify the structural features doing the classificatory work (I take there to be two: the wedge between believed and actual truth-value, and the wedge between what is said and what is implicated), and show what happens when each wedge is removed in turn: does collapsing the speaker's beliefs into the facts make the first two accounts converge while leaving the third unmoved? Finally, address the moral question the case is built to raise, since that's the point of the exercise: on reflection, is either utterance worse than the other, does any of the three taxonomies track your answer, and how would a defender of the view that lying is categorically worse than misleading most plausibly attack the scenario rather than concede its lesson?
