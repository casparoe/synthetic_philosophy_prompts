I am a novelist designing the biology of an invented world, and the plot has forced on me a question I cannot evade: what makes some collection of living things one organism rather than several? Three creatures of my world sit on the border. The first is a lifelong pairing of two unlike species: neither partner can feed, move, or reproduce alone, and the pair is transmitted intact from parent to offspring. The second is a sessile colony of genetically identical polyps sharing one gut and one nerve net; any torn-off fragment regrows a complete colony. The third is a migratory swarm whose members are born of different mothers and share no tissue, yet mature, mate, and perish on a single synchronized schedule, reproducing only in one coordinated mass event.

My notebook already lists the standard tests for individuality: genetic uniformity of the parts; passage of every generation through a single cell; early sequestration of a germ line; internal policing that suppresses competition among the parts; immune discrimination of self from non-self; and metabolic interdependence such that no part survives alone. I need one further test, different in structure from all six — not a refinement of one, not a conjunction of two, and not the same idea applied at a new scale. State it plainly; say which of my three creatures it counts as individuals and which as mere collectives; and tell me honestly whether it withstands the counterexamples you can raise against it, or whether it collapses back into a test I already have. In the book, the answer settles whether the burning of a swarm is a murder or a massacre.
