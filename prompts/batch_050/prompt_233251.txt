So my friend Marcus and I got into it last night until like 1am over Thomas Szasz — the psychiatrist who spent his whole career arguing that mental illness is a myth. His line, roughly: illness properly means something wrong with the body, so-called mental illnesses have no demonstrated bodily lesion, so the label is a metaphor at best, and psychiatry mostly functions as social control, converting what he called problems in living into medical conditions so institutions can coerce people without having to justify it the way they'd have to justify punishment. I was defending Szasz harder than I actually believe just to wind Marcus up, and then this morning I realized the case sitting on my desk at work is basically this arguement made flesh.

I sit on the appeals panel of my graduate school — we hear appeals of administrative decisions like involuntary leaves and dismissals. The case in front of us: a second-year PhD student, R., sent about a dozen late-night emails to the entire department over one weekend accusing a senior professor of fabricating data, in pretty wild language, and then stood up at a colloquium the next week and read a four-page statement aloud until security walked him out. No threats, no violence, nobody is claiming otherwise. The grad school put him on involuntary health leave under a policy that was clearly drafted for suicide-risk situations, and made his return conditional on a psychiatric evaluation clearing him. He's appealing.

Here's the Szasz-shaped wrinkle. A campus psychiatrist who met R. twice wrote that he presents as grandiose and possibly paranoid and recommended further assessment. There's no test, obviously — there is no test. And R.'s fraud accusation is apparently not nothing: I can't discuss details, but his complaint kicked off a genuine internal inquiry that's still running. So his appeal, in essence, is: you can't get me on the merits of my conduct, so you're medicalizing me instead — if I disrupted a colloquium, charge me with misconduct and let me answer the charge, but this health leave lets you sidestep all of that. Which is Szasz almost verbatim. Meanwhile the senior colleague on my panel says the school has a duty of care, that behavior like this is exactly how serious episodes announce themselves, that we'd be negligent to wait for something worse, and that illness has never required a litmus test anyway — plenty of accepted conditions lack one.

I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting and I'm honestly torn; last night settled nothing except that Marcus is stubborn. Please draft the opinion for me. It has to recommend one concrete course of action — uphold the leave, reinstate R. outright, or reinstate him with conditions that you spell out — with a time frame attached. And I need it to take the losing side seriously: the strongest version of it, not a token paragraph before dismissal.

Structure it like this, in this order:

- Recommendation, right up top — one short paragraph, the actual motion I'd move, no throat-clearing
- What I'm treating as settled — ~100 words, the facts and procedural posture I don't want relitigated in the meeting
- The case for upholding the leave — ~150 words, steelmanned
- The case for reinstating him — ~150 words, steelmanned
- Where the Szasz point bites and where it doesn't — ~200 words, and here I specifically want you to seperate the question "is this an illness?" from the question "may the school do this anyway?", because I suspect those come apart and that that's the real crux
- Conditions and next steps — ~100 words, concrete, with deadlines where relevant

About 800 words total. Tone: serious enough to circulate to deans, but written by a human, not by a compliance office.
