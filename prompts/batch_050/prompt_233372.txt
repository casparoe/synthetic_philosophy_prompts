I'm grading homework for an undergraduate philosophy course. The question on the assignment was:

"Define Thomas Nagel's 'control principle.' Using the example of two equally negligent drivers, only one of whom happens to hit a pedestrian, explain the problem of resultant moral luck. Does Nagel offer a solution to this problem, and if so, what is it?"

Here is the student's answer:

"The control principle holds that we are morally assessable only to the extent that what we are assessed for depends on factors under our control. Resultant luck concerns luck in how our actions turn out. Consider two drivers who drive home equally drunk: one arrives safely, while the other strikes a child who runs into the road. We intuitively judge the second driver far more harshly, yet the difference between them is entirely a matter of luck, which violates the control principle. Nagel distinguishes resultant luck from three other kinds: circumstantial luck (luck in the situations one faces, such as being an ordinary German in the 1930s), constitutive luck (luck in one's temperament and dispositions), and causal luck (luck in being determined by antecedent causes). Nagel's solution to the paradox is that we should confine moral judgment to what is genuinely within the agent's control — the will itself — and stop holding people responsible for outcomes that depend on luck. He grants that this is psychologically difficult but argues that consistency requires it."
