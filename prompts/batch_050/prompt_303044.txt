I'm a high school junior on my school's student newspaper, and I'm writing an op-ed about a comedian who got criticized for making jokes about a disaster that happened only a few weeks earlier. In a column defending him, another writer reached for the old line that comedy is tragedy plus time, as if that settled whether the jokes were acceptable, or even predicted that they'd eventually be celebrated. The slogan feels right to me at first hearing but dissolves the moment I press on it, and I want to press on it properly before I quote or attack it in my piece.

Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims this sentence could be making? I can see at least the outlines of several: a psychological claim about how painful experiences get converted into amusement as they recede; a sociological claim about what audiences will in fact laugh at and when; a normative claim about when joking about suffering becomes permissible (which would make "too soon" a real moral category rather than a complaint); and an aesthetic claim that temporal distance improves comedy the way aging improves wine. For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false, and give me the counterexample that settles it — because I notice that a pratfall is funny instantly, with no time elapsed, while some tragedies seem to get less jokable, not more, as the decades pass.

Also unpack the load-bearing words. What does "tragedy" have to mean for the slogan to work — actual catastrophe, or any misfortune down to stubbed toes? Does "plus time" mean time is sufficient, merely necessary, or just one variable standing in for distance, surprise, or the joke-teller's relationship to the event? And does the slogan predict that jokes get funnier the more time passes, which seems empirically wrong about jokes regarding the Black Death?

Finally: which of these readings does the columnist I'm responding to actually need for his defense of the comedian to go through, and does that reading survive your analysis? I'd like to know whether the slogan, on its strongest interpretation, supports him, condemns him, or is simply silent about the case.
