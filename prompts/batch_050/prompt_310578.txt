I teach a first-year undergraduate seminar on language and social life, with about twenty students from a dozen countries and no background in linguistics or philosophy. In one ninety-minute session I want to give them Grice's machinery of conversational cooperation and then push it until it strains, using politeness as the stress. The passage I want the session to pivot on is from Grice's Logic and Conversation (the 1967 William James Lectures, reprinted in Studies in the Way of Words), where he illustrates the maxim of Quality: "I expect your contributions to be genuine and not spurious. If I need sugar as an ingredient in the cake you are assisting me to make, I do not expect you to hand me salt; if I need a spoon, I do not expect a trick spoon made of rubber."

What interests me pedagogically is that the analogy smuggles in an assumption: Grice evaluates a contribution against the purpose of a joint enterprise, so whether an utterance counts as "salt instead of sugar" depends on what the exchange is for. I want students to end the session able to argue, from the framework itself rather than from cultural stereotypes, that a blunt Dutch-style correction and a Japanese-style circumlocution might both be fully cooperative, differing in what the participants take the enterprise to include — while a white lie told to a job interviewer is spurious in a way the aunt's admired sweater is not.

Please plan the session rather than deliver it. Specify the order in which the pieces must come and what each depends on (my current thought is: shared-purpose inference before maxims, maxims before flouting, flouting before the Quality passage — but tell me if that is wrong); the one exchange to build the session around and what it must and must not contain; the misconceptions students will predictably bring — that the maxims are etiquette rules, that implicature is just hinting or sneakiness, that any false sentence is a Quality violation so irony and white lies collapse together, that calling something "cooperative" excuses it morally — and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact; a closing exercise that would show the idea has landed rather than been memorized; and which refinements (cancellability, generalized versus particularized implicature, formal politeness theory) I should explicitly declare out of scope for this level.
