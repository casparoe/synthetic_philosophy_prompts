I am preparing a short paper on whether everyday conventions can generate genuine moral obligations rather than merely social expectations. The issue arose after several trips during which friends disagreed about tipping in restaurants, taking turns in informal queues, and crossing streets against a signal when no traffic was present. I would like a consistency audit of my current position, not a general survey of these debates.

My commitments are these: (1) a convention has no moral force simply because it is conventional, and a legal rule has no moral force simply because it is legal; (2) participation in a convention can nevertheless create an obligation when others reasonably organize their conduct around it and noncompliance shifts a concentrated cost onto them; (3) a visitor to the United States who knowingly eats in a full-service restaurant ought to leave the customary tip, even if they regard the tipping system as seriously unjust, because refusing to tip ordinarily penalizes the worker rather than the employer who designed the wage arrangement; (4) a visitor may refuse to comply with a seriously unjust local convention even when other people have reasonably come to rely on it, since reliance cannot by itself launder injustice; (5) crossing an entirely empty street against a signal is morally permissible even where it is illegal and strongly disapproved of locally, provided no driver, cyclist, child, or other pedestrian is likely to alter their behavior in response; (6) a person who joins a queue can acquire obligations before receiving any benefit from it, and therefore cannot justify jumping ahead merely by saying that they have not yet benefited from the queue’s orderly operation; and (7) visitors and locals ordinarily have the same convention-based duties, although a visitor’s ignorance may make them less blameworthy for violating one.

Please identify which commitments genuinely conflict, which merely appear to conflict, and which conflicts can be resolved through distinctions already available in the set. I am especially interested in whether my treatment of tipping and unjust conventions uses the idea of reasonable reliance inconsistently, and whether my queue and jaywalking verdicts rely on different accounts of how obligations arise. Rank any tensions by severity, state exactly which commitment would have to be revised in each case, and conclude by recommending the least costly overall revision without assuming in advance which commitment I should abandon.
