in my reading group on classical pragmatism last week someone tried to shut the whole tradition down with the line "truth is just what works" -- and then piled on: so on pragmatism a comforting lie that never gets found out is true, astrology was true back when it worked for the Babylonians, and Peirce is basically just doing motivational-poster philosophy. I've read a bit of Peirce and I'm pretty confident he wouldn't cop to any of this, but I can't cleanly articulate which part of the slogan he's rejecting and which part he might actually endorse.

So: treat "truth is what works" as a menu rather than a sentence. Lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making -- I'm thinking candidates like: true beliefs tend to be useful; whatever it is useful to believe is thereby true; truth is what inquiry would settle on in the long run; the meaning of a concept is exhausted by its conceivable practical effects; maybe others I'm missing. For each reading, say (a) whether it's a platitude, false, or genuinely substantive, (b) whether Peirce could accept it given the pragmatic maxim and his doubt-belief picture of inquiry (belief as a habit of action, doubt as the irritation that gets inquiry going), and (c) which reading is the one my reading-group guy actually needed for his gotcha to land -- and whether that reading is even one a pragmatist has to hold.

Two kickers while you're at it. First: does "works" in the slogan mean works for me, now, or works for the community of inquirers, indefinitely -- and does flipping between those two do all the slogan's rhetorical work? Second: Peirce is a fallibilist, every belief is in principle revisable. Does that sit comfortably with the "settled in the long run" reading, or is there a real tension there (if we can never know we've reached the end of inquiry, in what sense is the long run doing anything)? I have a feeling people paper over this.
