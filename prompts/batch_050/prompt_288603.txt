In the Introduction to What is Art? (1897, trans. Aylmer Maude), Leo Tolstoy recounts visiting a Moscow gallery with an artist friend and finding a book of elegantly executed erotic pictures. He writes:

"I ventured to suggest that the subject-matter of the pictures was objectionable. But my companion (who prided herself on being an artist) remarked with conscious superiority, that from an artist’s point of view the subject was of no consequence. The pictures being very well executed were artistic, and therefore worthy of attention and study. Morality had nothing to do with art."

Tolstoy's own positive account, developed later in the book, treats art as the transmission of feeling from artist to audience, and judges it both by how infectious it is (which he takes to depend on the individuality and clarity of the feeling and, above all, the artist's sincerity) and by whether the feelings transmitted are worthy ones that unite people rather than divide or merely titillate them. Taking that account as given, please do two things. First, rate each of the following on a scale from 0 to 10 for how much genuine art it contains by Tolstoy's lights, with a sentence or two of justification for each score: (a) the erotic picture book described above; (b) a harvest folk song sung by workers who learnt it from their grandparents; (c) a technically dazzling advertising jingle composed on commission to sell soft drinks; (d) an experimental opera whose meaning its composer refuses to explain and which only professional critics claim to understand; (e) a crude, clumsily drawn icon made by a devout village painter for his church. Second, say whether your ranking shows Tolstoy's criteria to be a workable way of distinguishing art from non-art and good art from bad, or whether it instead exposes a fatal conflation in the passage — between a work's being well made and its being valuable — and, if the latter, which of the five cases does most damage to his position.
