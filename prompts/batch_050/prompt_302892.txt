In three weeks I have to give the big after-dinner speech at the retirement party for my old history professor, who is a medievalist retiring in the same year our department voted to stop offering its famous three-course sequence: the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution. The new sequence is called Connected Histories, 1000-1750, and half the room thinks this is long overdue because those labels are later inventions that flatten a messy thousand years, while the other half -- including alumni donors and her own oldest friends -- thinks the college has definately abolished her life's work and renamed it something bland. She is being funny about it, but I know it stings.

Write the speech, around ten minutes, for this mixed room: historians who will notice every shortcut, relatives who basically think the Renaissance was when art got good, and one very sharp retired medievalist who does not want piety. I want it built around a concept she used in class, which I want you to develop and slightly push beyond how she used it: chronological zoning. Her idea was that periods are like zoning laws -- nobody drew a line in the actual landscape, but once institutions respect the line, real things happen because of it. I want the speech to ask what happens when the zoning map starts making people think every neighborhood inside the line is the same, or that nothing crosses the border.

The speech has to do some careful work, not just be nice. It should concede that Middle Ages only makes sense as a middle seen from later, that rebirth talk was partly self-advertising, and that calling something a scientific revolution can make a slow reorganization of practices sound like a weekend coup. But it also has to resist the lazy conclusion that, because the labels were invented, the differences were invented too. If there were real changes in institutions, images, books, instruments, and habits of argument, what exactly did the bad names get right?

End on what it means to retire a period without retiring the people who studied it. No citations, no bibliography, no name-dropping big historians -- I want the argument to stand without authority.
