In my graduate seminar on consciousness we have spent the term on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness (Frankish, Dennett) and its realist critics. For the final essay, I want students to engage with a position of my own construction, which I call selective illusionism: the view that introspection systematically misrepresents phenomenal consciousness only in degree and structure, not in kind. On this view, experience seems richer, more determinate, and more unified than it really is, but its seeming to exist at all is not itself an illusion. Compare selective illusionism with thoroughgoing illusionism, which holds that even the apparent existence of phenomenal properties is a matter of misrepresentation. Which of the two better explains why the intuition that there is something it is like to be conscious is so recalcitrant, given that the selective view grants that intuition a kernel of truth while the thoroughgoing view must explain it away entirely? And is the selective position stable, or does it either collapse back into realism about some phenomenal properties or else get driven by its own argumentative pressures toward the thoroughgoing version? Please work through at least two concrete test cases -- I suggest the apparent richness of peripheral visual experience and the felt awfulness of pain -- and state explicitly what, on each view, is veridically represented, what is misrepresented, and what the misrepresentation consists in.
