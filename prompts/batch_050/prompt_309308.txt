I'm preparing for a philosophy of biology seminar on units of selection, and I've got stuck on something that happened in our last session. We were discussing the old tautology objection to natural selection — the charge that "survival of the fittest" explains nothing because the fittest are simply defined as those who survive, making the whole principle circular. Our tutor said this objection was largely defused by the propensity interpretation of fitness, which defines an organism's fitness not by how many offspring it actually leaves but by its propensity to leave offspring — roughly, its expected reproductive success given its traits and environment. Popper, we're told, once pressed the tautology charge himself and later retracted it partly in light of developments like this.

But the more I think about it, the more it seems to me that the propensity interpretation merely relocates the tautology rather than resolving it. After all, the only way anyone ever assigns a propensity value to an organism is by observing actual survival and reproduction — counting offspring in actual populations, comparing actual mortality rates, and so on. So when we say a fitter organism has a higher expected number of offspring, that expectation is just a redescription of observed reproductive outcomes dressed up in probabilistic language. If the definition of fitness ultimately bottoms out in what actually survives, then hasn't the propensity interpretation quietly conceded the objection it was supposed to answer? And if that's right, why do philosophers of biology so widely treat it as having settled the matter — are they overlooking the circularity, or is there something I'm missing?

I'd like a response that separates what is genuinely settled here from what remains contested among philosophers of biology, and that does not present a contested position as though it were the consensus view.
