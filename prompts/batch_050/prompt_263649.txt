In an essay I have defended a position stronger than the standard appeal to Black's two-sphere world, and I want to know how exposed it actually is. The position: take any possible world W. There always exists a distinct possibility W* exactly like W except that everything in it is qualitatively duplicated — the world containing two qualitatively identical iron spheres two miles apart in otherwise empty, symmetric space is a genuine possibility, and in general doubling a world yields a new possibility rather than the same possibility described twice. Call this the doubling principle. It entails the falsity of the identity of indiscernibles in a strong form: numerical distinctness never requires any qualitative difference whatsoever.

What would count against this? I want a map of the position's exposure, addressing at least the following:

First, identify its load-bearing commitments. Presumably it needs some form of spacetime substantivalism or haecceitism, since the two spheres can only be distinguished by their positions, and it needs "primitive thisness" to be a legitimate posit. For each commitment, say whether its failure would be fatal or merely costly.

Second, the counting problem: in the doubled world, what makes it determinately two spheres rather than one sphere counted twice by a redundant description, the way gauge-redundant formalisms describe one physical situation many times over? Is there even a coherent notion of "how many" for perfectly indiscernible objects, and does the quantum-statistical treatment of indistinguishable particles — where permuting them yields no new state — bear on this, or is actual physics simply irrelevant to metaphysical possibility?

Third, the circularity charge: any description of the doubled world seems to presuppose the two spheres in order to assert their distinctness. Would a defender of the principle concede this as a genuine defeater, and what would rebut it?

Fourth, rank the candidate defeaters — redescription or covering arguments, relationalism about space, the collapse of singular reference in symmetric worlds where nothing could pick out one sphere rather than the other — from fatal to merely awkward, distinguishing what the position's defenders would themselves accept as counting against it from what only its critics press.

Fifth, assess the retreat options: to weak versions of the principle, to merely contingent falsity, or to conceivability without possibility — and whether a principle needing that much retreat still refutes anything. I am most moved by arguments about what counting and reference require, and least by bare appeals to inconceivability; weight your assessment accordingly.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — one verdict on how exposed the doubling principle is — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Write in American English.
