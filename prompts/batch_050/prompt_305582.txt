After our local reading group last week two friends of mine got into this long argument at the bar about whether Buddhist ethics is secretly just consequentialism, and they both refused to give an inch. Neither of them is a scholar, to be clear, they're both the type of person who reads LessWrong and SSC and has done a couple meditation retreats, so maybe half-remembered stuff mixed with decent reasoning. Here's roughly how it went, as best I can reconstruct:

Dana's opening move: obviously it's consequentialism. The whole point of the path is ending suffering for all sentient beings, and whether an action counts as "skillful" (kusala) is judged by whether it leads away from craving and suffering. That's judging acts by their outcomes, just with a psychology-heavy axiology instead of the usual hedonic utilitarianism.

Eli: no, what makes an act karmically good or bad is the intention (cetana) behind it, and intentions aren't consequences. If I try to save someone and fail, my karma is the same as if I'd succeeded. A consequentialist has to say the act turned out bad because of bad luck; Buddhism says the moral quality was already fixed at the moment I formed the intention.

Dana: that's just trait-consequentialism wearing a robe. Intentions get moral weight because cultivating certain intentions has the best downstream effects on your character and future behavior. The karma doctrine is basically an empirical claim that intention is the lever where intervention pays off best. The evaluation still bottoms out in consequences.

Eli: okay, but the precepts are hard rules. Don't kill, full stop. A real consequentialist kills one to save five. Dana then brought up some story she'd heard, apparently from the Mahayana literature, where the Buddha in a past life kills a bandit who was about to murder a whole ship full of merchants, and the text presents it as the compassionate thing to do. She said this proves consequences trump rules when push comes to shove.

Eli said one edgy story can't overturn the mainstream treatment of intention, and moreover: acting from genuine goodwill that accidentally harms someone isn't karmically bad, while acting from greed that happens to benefit people still is. Moral evaluation sits upstream of outcomes, not downstream. Dana's last move was to say that if Buddhism counts as non-consequentialist just because it cares about mental states, then every theory that cares about anything becomes "consequentialism" and the word stops meaning anything.

Can you go through the exchange point by point and tell me who's actually right about each step — including whether that ship story does the work Dana thinks it does — and what, if anything, both of them are missing? I'm not asking which team to join, I genuinely can't tell where the disagreement stops being empirical and starts being semantic.
