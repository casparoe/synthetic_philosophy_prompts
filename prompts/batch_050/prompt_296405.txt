I am a PhD student in moral philosophy, and my dissertation includes a chapter on moral trade: cases in which agents with conflicting moral convictions cooperate so that each secures more of what her own view counts as good, as when two effective altruists who disagree about which cause matters most agree to split their donations, or when two voters with opposed moral ends agree to swap votes across districts. For a graduate seminar I am co-teaching next term, I want to give participants a decision tree that sorts them into a position on when, if ever, such trade is morally permissible — sorted by what they actually think, not by which label they find attractive.

Please write the tree with the following features:

1. Every branch node should be a single question the participant answers yes or no (or picks between two specified options), and each question should be answerable by someone who has never encountered the literature on moral trade. Where possible, phrase the node as a concrete case rather than an abstract principle — for instance, a question about whether you may facilitate an outcome you take to be seriously wrong in exchange for your counterparty facilitating one you take to be seriously good, rather than a question about 'complicity' in the abstract.

2. The following positions must appear as leaves, each with a short name and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment: (a) the view that moral trade is permissible whenever it is mutually beneficial by each party's own lights; (b) the view that trade is impermissible because knowingly enabling what you judge wrong is itself wrong, whatever the compensating benefit; (c) the view that trade is permissible only insofar as disagreement has rationally reduced your confidence in your own moral view; (d) the view that trade is permissible only between parties who share at least a thin common morality, so that trading with someone whose ends you consider monstrous is ruled out even when the arithmetic favors it; (e) the deflationary view that most alleged moral trades are really just preference bargains wearing moral language, so there is no distinctively moral question here at all. Add further leaves if the logical space demands them.

3. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list above: first the questions, then the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space. Note any unoccupied combination and say briefly whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely undefended.

4. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, and say what further question would be needed to pry them apart.

Five to seven levels of branching is the right size for a one-page handout. Please do not include citations or a reading list; I will supply references myself.
