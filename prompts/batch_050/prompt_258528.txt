Last weekend I spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend about what metaphors mean, and I want to stress-test the position I ended up defending.

My position, roughly Davidsonian, is this: a metaphorical utterance has no meaning beyond the literal meaning of the words used. When someone says that time is a thief, or that an argument is a war, there is no hidden metaphorical proposition for hearers to decode; the sentence means something straightforwardly false or trivial, and everything of interest -- the noticing of resemblances, the reframing, the emotional coloring -- is something the utterance makes us do, not something it says. My friend held the opposed view that metaphors carry genuine content of their own, grounded in systematic mappings between conceptual domains, such that understanding "she dismantled his argument" involves actually cognizing arguments under the structure of buildings or machines, and that this content explains why such expressions are informative, inferentially productive, and translatable between speakers.

We made little progress, so here is what I want: an honest map of my position's exposure. What would have to be true for it to be wrong? Which empirical findings about how metaphors are processed, acquired by children, or distributed across languages would count decisively against it, and which would merely be awkward -- things I could absorb by relocating them to pragmatics? Conversely, what would my friend need to show, rather than assume, to establish that metaphorical mappings are content rather than aftermath?

One specific exchange I want adjudicated. My friend proposed that if novel, never-before-heard metaphors turned out to be interpreted in ways systematically predicted by entrenched conventional mappings -- so that coinages behave less like fresh pragmatic promptings and more like instances of standing semantic structure -- then my view loses. I countered that no such result could be cleanly obtained, because any pattern she cites could be redescribed as savvy hearers exploiting shared background knowledge. Who is right about what such a finding would show, and is my redescription move a legitimate defense or an immunizing maneuver that renders my position untestable?

Finally, address the standard escape hatch my side relies on: classifying troublesome metaphors as "dead," hence literalized, hence no longer counterexamples. Does conceding that metaphors die into literal meaning quietly concede that they once had meaning of their own? And overall: is the view I defended genuinely risking something, or has it been qualified into safety? Please distinguish what defenders of my position would themselves concede as a defeater from what only its critics treat as one.
