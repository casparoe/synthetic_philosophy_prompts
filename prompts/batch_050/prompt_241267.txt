I'm a PhD student in political theory, and this term I'm TAing an upper-level undergraduate seminar on obligations to future generations. I've been asked to design the long essay question for the final exam, on the nonidentity problem. In previous years the exam simply asked students to explain and evaluate Parfit's argument, and the results were useless for grading: strong and weak students alike produced near-identical summaries of the standard Depletion and Risky Policy examples, and we couldn't separate students who understand the problem from students who have memorized its vocabulary. I want a question that makes students *use* the relevant distinctions rather than recite them.

Please write the exam question, a model answer, and a marking scheme, subject to the following constraints. The exam is 90 minutes for this question; students write roughly 1,000–1,200 words; the question is worth 40 marks. The question should be built around a concrete case you construct yourself — not Parfit's own cases — in which a policy choice affects which people will later exist (for instance, a government choosing between two long-run energy or environmental policies with different patterns of relocation and family formation), and it should include a deliberate wrinkle: at least one sub-decision in the case should be a same-people choice rather than a different-people one, so that students must notice that the nonidentity problem does not apply uniformly across the whole case. The exam is closed-book, so nothing in the question, model answer, or marking scheme should depend on students recalling particular passages, page references, or secondary literature.

The marking scheme should allocate the 40 marks across components and give band descriptors (first / 2:1 / 2:2). More importantly, it should name the specific moves a top answer must make — for example: correctly explaining why, in different-people choices, the people who end up badly off cannot claim to have been made worse off than they would otherwise have been, given that their lives are worth living; distinguishing the nonidentity problem from the distinct claim that we have no duties to people who don't yet exist, and from worries about statistical versus identifiable victims; applying the same-people/different-people distinction to the wrinkle in the case rather than discussing the problem only abstractly; and presenting at least two strategies for resisting the conclusion that the policy choice is morally permissible (e.g., an impersonal-badness principle, or the claim that one can wrong someone without harming them) while identifying a real cost of each. It should also name the specific errors that should cost marks — for example: sliding from "no one was made worse off" to "no wrong was done" without argument; treating the nonidentity problem as arising only when population size changes; conflating it with the mere-unlikelihood of any particular future person existing; or dragging in the repugnant conclusion or the mere addition paradox where they don't bear on the case. The model answer should be genuinely achievable under exam conditions, not a survey of the literature.
