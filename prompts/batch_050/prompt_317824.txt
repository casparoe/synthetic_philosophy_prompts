I am a retired biochemist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been making my way through the philosophical literature on rational choice, and I belong to a small reading group that meets monthly to argue about these matters. At our last meeting I watched two longtime members -- one a former actuary, one a retired engineer who now donates a large share of his pension to charity -- have a genuine falling-out over how the engineer ought to direct this year's giving. I stayed out of it, and I honestly cannot decide who had the better of the exchange, so I would like a professional philosopher with expertise in decision theory to adjudicate it carefully. Please take your time, first thinking through what each side's strongest underlying principle might be, and then evaluate the moves as they were actually made.

The engineer announced that he is giving most of this year's money to an organization trying to reduce the probability of a globally catastrophic engineered pandemic, and the actuary objected. The exchange went roughly as follows. (1) The actuary argued that the donation is irrational: the probability of such a catastrophe, and of this particular organization meaningfully reducing it, cannot be estimated from any stable frequency or mechanism, so the engineer is multiplying a number he effectively invented by an enormous payoff and calling the product knowledge -- arithmetic on guesses is still a guess. (2) The engineer replied that any rule instructing us to ignore sufficiently small probabilities is either an arbitrary threshold or a violation of principles the actuary himself accepts, like the continuity of rational preference; we do not, he said, refuse to reason about low-probability, high-stakes events elsewhere in life, and refusing here is just squeamishness dressed up as rigor. (3) The actuary said his objection was never about smallness but about groundlessness: where probabilities are anchored in data or well-understood mechanisms, expected value reasoning is fine, but the engineer's estimate is dominated by the probability that the entire chain of argument behind it is mistaken somewhere, and that is a discount the engineer has refused to apply. (4) The engineer answered that this proves too much: even the actuary's beloved well-measured charities -- bednets, cash transfers -- rest on models, extrapolations, and assumptions that could be wrong, so by the actuary's own standard nearly all philanthropy collapses into guesswork; and ignoring catastrophic risk altogether amounts to assigning it a probability of exactly zero, which is a far more dogmatic epistemic act than assigning it a small, honestly hedged one.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible-but-incomplete, with a brief justification grounded in decision theory. Then give your overall verdict: who is right about which specific points, and what -- if anything -- both parties are missing (for instance, whether there is a principled way to handle probabilities that rest on judgment rather than data, and whether the dispute is really about the mathematics or about something upstream of it).
