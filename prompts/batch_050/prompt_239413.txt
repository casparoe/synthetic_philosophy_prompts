I am a PhD student in philosophy who also serves as style editor for a graduate-run journal in the humanities, which sends me roughly forty manuscripts a year. I want to operationalize the central device of Douglas Hofstadter's satirical essay known as the "Person Paper" — the thought experiment of an alternate English that encodes race everywhere our English encodes sex, making the arbitrariness of our own defaults suddenly visible — as a reusable test for deciding which usages our copyeditors should flag and which they should leave alone. Please write a decision procedure a copyeditor with no philosophical background can run on a manuscript in under an hour: the questions to ask about each candidate usage, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; where the procedure terminates in a change or a pass; and where it hands the decision up to me because the race–sex parallel breaks down or the question is one of values rather than mechanics. It should handle at least: generic "he"; occupational terms like "chairman" and "freshman"; asymmetric honorifics; terms like "guys"; words whose sexed etymology no longer registers in use ("seminal," "hysterical"); contexts where sex is genuinely relevant to the content; and cases where removing a marked term would erase information the journal has reason to keep, such as historical quotations and sex-specific award names. Then run the procedure on three sentences of your own construction, including one where it delivers an answer a progressive style editor will not like, and say plainly what a procedure of this kind cannot settle.
