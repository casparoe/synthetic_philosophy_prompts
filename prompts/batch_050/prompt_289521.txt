I am a novelist, and for my current book I have been reading the literature on the Ship of Theseus and personal persistence — endurantism, perdurantism, psychological continuity, brute-physical views — and I am struck by how thoroughly stalled the whole debate seems. Every argument appears to have a standard reply, and the reply a standard counter-reply, and the cases each side treats as decisive (fission, teleportation, gradual plank-by-plank replacement) are simply denied as data by the other side. As a working hypothesis, here is my novelist's suspicion: the disagreement is not really about what identity is, but about what identity-talk is *for* — the sides are reverse-engineering a concept that exists to serve practical functions (responsibility, grief, property, narrative coherence) and mistaking disagreements about which function matters most for disagreements about metaphysical fact. I ran a rough version of this test myself: I gave early readers two drafts of a chapter in which a character is gradually reconstructed after an accident, and readers' verdicts about whether she survived tracked nothing metaphysical — they tracked whether the plot still needed her. Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) your diagnosis of what kind of disagreement the persistence debate actually is — verbal, substantive, or several questions sharing one name — with reasons drawn from how the positions actually argue; (2) an assessment of my hypothesis about identity-talk serving practical functions, including its strongest objection; (3) whether my reader-anecdote is evidence for anything or merely reflects literary conventions rather than ontological intuitions; (4) what, given your diagnosis, would count as genuine progress — a distinction, a formal result, an empirical finding, or abandoning the question; (5) your honest verdict on whether the impasse is a sign that the question is ill-posed or simply that it is hard.
