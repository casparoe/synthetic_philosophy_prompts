So a few weeks ago a friend talked my ear off about L.A. Paul's work on transformative experiences — the idea that certain life choices (having a kid, becoming a vampire, getting a cochlear implant after being deaf from birth, emigrating somewhere totally different) are both epistemically transformative, in that you can't know what the experience is like until you have it, and personally transformative, in that the experience rewrites the very values and preferences you'd use to evaluate it. Which means the standard decision-theoretic move — imagine the outcomes, assign utilities, maximize expectation — breaks down, because the person doing the imagining won't be the person living the result. I found this weirdly upsetting and also kind of hilarious, and I want to commission a piece of satire for a small zine some friends and I are putting together.

Write the FAQ page (roughly 600-700 words, headed questions with deadpan corporate answers) for a fictional consultancy called something like "Rational Futures Advisory Group" that has taken Paul's argument with total, punishing seriousness: it advises clients that no major life decision can be rationally made, and sells products and services to help them cope with that fact. The humor should come from actually understanding the philosophy, not from mocking a strawman — the FAQ should distinguish the epistemic from the personal transformation (e.g., explaining that skydiving is fine because it only changes what you know, not who you are, while parenthood does both), take seriously the problem that your post-choice self might endorse what your pre-choice self would have rejected and vice versa, and have fun with edge cases like the cochlear implant question, where the people with the best evidence about the experience are the ones already transformed by it. Good joke fodder might include: a paid service where a consultant flips a coin for you, premium tiers where the firm merely *describes* outcomes without recommending them, testimonials from clients whose transformed selves filed complaints, a disclaimer that the decision to hire the consultancy was itself transformative and therefore irrational, and one question at the end where the firm quietly breaks character by conceding that maybe choosing for the sake of discovering who you'd become is itself a way of deciding. Stay deadpan throughout — no winking — but keep it legible that this is a reductio of treating the argument as a recipe for paralysis, not of Paul herself.

Begin with the piece itself, no preface or commentary about what you're doing.
