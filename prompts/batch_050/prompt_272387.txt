I'm designing the closing exercise for a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and rather than assigning another problem set I'd like the students to leave with something stranger. Write a villanelle about the Löbian obstacle to self-trust: the predicament of a formal system, or an idealized reasoner modeled on one, that would like to adopt as a standing principle that whatever it proves is true, but cannot safely do so. By Löb's theorem, if the system proves of some claim that its provability would guarantee its truth, then it can already prove the claim itself; so a blanket rule of self-trust, if the system could assert it of itself, would collapse into a license to prove anything at all, falsehoods included.

The two refrains should carry the philosophical weight rather than merely decorating it. Make one refrain a self-referential sentence at the heart of the puzzle -- a line that asserts its own provability -- and the other the agent's aspiration toward trusting its own derivations, so that their alternation across the tercets re-enacts the regress of checking: every attempt to certify the system from inside itself leans on the very trust it is trying to earn. In the closing quatrain, where the refrains finally stand side by side, land the puzzle's genuinely surprising punchline: a sentence that merely claims to be provable turns out, remarkably, to be provable after all, so that a reasoner extending itself trust on credit finds the loan called in at once, and the collateral is everything. The poem should be accurate enough that a logician would recognize the machinery -- in particular, the contrast between a sentence declaring itself unprovable, which goes unproved, and one declaring itself provable, which does not -- while remaining a good poem for a reader who has never heard of Löb's theorem: a meditation on what it costs a mind to vouch for itself.
