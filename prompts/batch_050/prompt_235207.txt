So last night I got into this absurdly long argument with my friend Priya about Dennett's Chase and Sanborn case -- the two coffee tasters who both come to dislike Maxwell House, where one says the taste itself changed for him and the other says the taste is the same but his standards drifted. Priya thinks it's obvious there's a fact of the matter about which one is right and Dennett is just being a killjoy. I tried to defend Dennett and ended up writing this short essay at 1am to organize my thoughts, but now I'm worried I've either misunderstood the original case or overextended it. Can you give me comments? Here's the essay:

---

Dennett's Chase and Sanborn are usually read as a demolition of qualia, but I think the better reading is narrower and more interesting. Chase says the flavor of the coffee has gone bad on him; Sanborn says the flavor is what it always was, and it's his appreciation that soured. The point isn't that nothing happened inside either man -- something clearly changed. The point is that "did the taste change, or did my reaction to the taste change?" has no answer beyond everything either man could possibly report, remember, or do, and both stories fit all of that equally well. There's no further fact to be wrong about.

The standard objection is that each man just *knows*, from the inside, which kind of change happened to him. But this is where I want to push a bit further than the usual discussion. Consider a third taster, call her Okafor, who works with grief rather than coffee. A decade ago her mother died, and at the time the grief had a certain searing quality. Now, remembering it, she's unsure: does recalling the grief bring back the same feeling, or does it bring back a photograph of the feeling -- the facts of it, the shape of it, but not the sting? When she says "it doesn't hurt like it used to," is that because the present-day echo is genuinely weaker, or because her current self can no longer run the old feeling at all, and mistakes that inability for the feeling's mildness?

I think Okafor's case shows the indeterminacy isn't a philosopher's toy. If there's no fact of the matter about whether her grief is diminished or merely inaccessible, then the common consolation "time softens the pain but the love is the same" is not quite coherent -- it presupposes a stable inner item, the grief, that persists while our access to it varies. And Chase and Sanborn show why no such item can be identified independently of the reactions it's supposed to explain. The conclusion isn't that grief and coffee flavor are illusions. It's that our concept of "the feeling itself" is a splice of behavioral, memorial, and dispositional threads, and when those threads come apart -- as they do slowly, in everyone, all the time -- the concept simply gives out, and there is nothing left for introspection to be right or wrong *about*.
