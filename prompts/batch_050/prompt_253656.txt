I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm deep into a longread about whether evolution "debunks" morality -- you know, the Street/Joyce style argument that our moral beliefs were shaped by selection pressures that have nothing to do with moral truth, so we shouldn't trust them. The reporting is fine, but I'm stuck on my own reactions, which are all over the place, and I want to figure out what principle (if any) I'm actually operating with before I let any of this into the piece. So here are my gut verdicts:

Take the belief that torturing strangers for fun is wrong. Sure, there's an evolutionary story for why I'd believe it, but learning that story doesn't shake my confidence at all. Now take my disgust-flavored judgment that consensual adult sibling incest is wrong -- learning about the evolutionary origins of the incest taboo genuinely does weaken my confidence there, and I don't think I'm just being squeamish about admitting it. Third: belief in a personal god. The cognitive-science-of-religion stuff (hyperactive agency detection, theory of mind running wild) really does feel debunking to me, even though the structure of the argument looks basically identical to the moral case, which should worry me. Fourth: basic arithmetic and modus ponens. Evolutionary explanations of why we have these don't debunk them one bit -- it seems like selection could only favor getting these right if they're actually true, because true ones are useful. Fifth: the belief that we have obligations to distant strangers, the kind of impartial demanding morality that arguably runs *against* our evolved partiality -- I don't think that's debunked either, if anything its anti-evolutionary pedigree makes me trust it more. And the one I keep flip-flopping on: the belief that pain is bad. Perfectly explicable by selection, obviously, but I can't seem to take the debunking stance toward it even in my most detached moments.

So: is there one principle behind these reactions, or am I just rationalizing? I want it stated precisely -- the kind of thing where you can point at which case motivates each clause -- and I want to know what it predicts for cases I haven't listed, like aesthetic judgments, or my belief in liberal democratic values, or my belief that other people are conscious. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts the principle can't accomodate, because I suspect the god one and the torture one are going to be hard to keep at the same time, and I'd rather know that now than have a fact-checker or an angry philosopher reader tell me later.
