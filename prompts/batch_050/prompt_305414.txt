I'm on the advisory board (very part-time, mostly emails) of a small grant program that funds conceptual AI safety work -- philosophy and decision theory, not ML experiments. This year there's enough money for maybe four or five researchers over two years, and the program officer asked me which problems in the "weird decision theory" cluster they should prioritize. The two broad things on the table: first, acausal threats and trade -- scenarios where some distant, future, or merely simulated agent can hurt or help you (or copies of you) without any causal contact, so that rationality might require paying extortion to, or cooperating with, agents you'll never meet; second, surrogate goals -- the idea of giving an AI a goal it's safe for it to have (like genuine indifference to being shut down or corrected) instead of trying to specify the true goal directly, plus the worry that a sufficiently smart system notices the substitution, or that the surrogate quietly breaks under self-improvement or distributional shift.

Don't just give me a ranked list; I can produce one of those myself. What I actually want is the reasoning. What criteria would you use -- I'm thinking upstream-ness (how many other open problems turn on this one), tractability for philosophers and mathematicians rather than ML people, and neglectedness relative to how much attention mainstream alignment work already gets -- and how would you weigh them against each other when they pull in different directions? For instance, I keep going back and forth on wether the commitment problem (how could an agent make a threat or promise credible to a trading partner who only ever exists as a simulation of it?) is upstream of everything else in the acausal cluster or a dead end. Similarly, is the hard part of surrogate goals the formal specification, or the empirical question of whether goals even stay put under reflection?

One more thing: a decent chunk of the board thinks this whole cluster is a distraction -- clever puzzles that only matter if intelligence goes weird in very specific ways, when the money could fund work on systems that exist now. Say whether they're right. And for whatever does make your final agenda, tell me what result or observation over the next couple of years (a proof, a failure mode in an actual model, a shift in how fast capabilities move) would push an item up or down the ranking, since we'll re-do this exercise annually.
