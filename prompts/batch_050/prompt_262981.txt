I cover cosmology and fundamental physics for a general-audience science magazine. After a conference last month, I listened to two of the panelists — a cosmologist and a philosopher of physics — carry an argument about Boltzmann brains through dinner without either conceding anything. I need to write about this dispute and would like it adjudicated first, move by move, since I genuinely cannot tell who came out ahead.

(1) The cosmologist's opening move: any cosmological model in which Boltzmann brains — briefly existing observers produced by random fluctuations, complete with false memories — vastly outnumber evolved observers like us must be rejected outright. On such a model, nearly all observers with your exact current experiences have fabricated memories and a chaotic immediate future, so the model entails that your evidence is unreliable, including the evidence you took to support the model itself. A theory that destroys its own evidential basis is disqualified, no matter how well it otherwise fits the data.

(2) The philosopher replied that this is dressed-up reasoning from unusualness: you cannot refute a theory by pointing out that your situation would be atypical if the theory were true, just as you cannot refute a large universe by noting how improbable your exact location in it is. Moreover, the claim that Boltzmann brains "dominate" is not a prediction the physics makes on its own — it depends on a chosen measure for counting observers across an infinite or eternal cosmos, and no one has a non-arbitrary measure. The problem, he said, lives in the bookkeeping, not in the cosmology.

(3) The cosmologist answered that the measure point cuts the other way: the Boltzmann-brain worry is precisely what disciplines the choice of measure. Physicists routinely and without objection use typicality reasoning elsewhere in cosmology, and abandoning it selectively, only when it threatens a favored model, is special pleading. A measure that predicts we are statistical flukes with unreliable evidence is a bad measure, full stop.

(4) The philosopher's final move: the self-undermining argument proves too much. Followed consistently, it would let us rule out cosmological models from the armchair, before examining the data, and it smuggles in an a priori principle — we must be typical observers — that has no independent justification. Boltzmann-brain domination is at most one consideration among others bearing on a model, not a knockdown refutation.

Please rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating, and say who is more right overall. Whatever verdict you reach, state explicitly what would change your mind: which argument or consideration, if made, would flip your assessment of the key move, or where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
