I'm building a little interactive thing for a science centre (mostly kids, but curious adults linger too) where visitors answer questions about some animal or gadget and get told what "kind of mind" it has, roughly along the lines of Dennett's tower of creatures: things that only evolve solutions over generations, things that learn from their own trial and error during a lifetime, things that can test options in their heads first so the bad ideas get filtered out without the organism paying for them, and things that boost all of the above with culturally inherited tools like language.

What I want from you is the decision tree behind the exhibit. It should take a visitor from "here is a thing that behaves" to one of those four kinds, with a few ground rules:

- Every branch has to be a question about observable behaviour or experimental results, phrased so you can answer it without knowing the labels. So never "does it have inner simulation?" but something like "it faces a problem it's never seen, with no time to experiment — does it get it right more often than chance?".
- I need to know which distinction you put first and why. Intuitively "does it change within its own lifetime?" comes before anything about simulation, but convince me or correct me.
- The leaves are the four kinds, plus at least one honest "the scheme struggles here" leaf.

The hard part, and the bit I actually care about: run these cases through your tree and tell me where they land and where the tree creaks. Bacteria colonies and slime moulds (variation happens, but only across the population, no individual learning). A honeybee doing the waggle dance. An octopus that clearly learns within its lifetime but shows no sign of rehearsing options mentally. A chess engine, which in some sense tries out thousands of moves in its "head" before committing — does brute tree search count as letting candidate moves die instead of itself, or is that a category error? And the awkward one: a large language model chatbot. It's saturated with the accumulated products of human culture, so it looks like a fourth-rung creature by inheritance, but it's not obvious it ever tests a hypothesis internally before speaking, or that a bad idea could ever "die" for it in any meaningful sense. If the chatbot reveals a genuine hole in the taxonomy — like a rung the ladder skipped, or a rung that turns out not to presuppose the one below — say so and tell me whether to add a fifth leaf or redraw the ladder.

Also flag any pair of leaves your tree can't seperate behaviourally, and whether that's a real gap in the taxonomy or just a limit of what a museum visitor can observe in two minutes.
