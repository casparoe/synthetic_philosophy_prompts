I've noticed a recurring pattern of disagreement and I'd like to know whether it has an established name. Some background: I grew up in the United States, spent six years working in Germany, and now moderate an online discussion group with members from both countries. Again and again I see the same clash. An American member will describe doing something technically forbidden but obviously harmless -- crossing an empty street against the light at two in the morning, letting a friend without a ticket onto a train platform to say goodbye, ignoring some workplace form that nobody has read in a decade -- and will treat the fact that no one could possibly be harmed as the end of the matter. The German members don't dispute that no one was harmed. Instead they say things like: then the rule should be changed, but until it is changed you follow it; or: if everyone decided for themselves which rules apply to them, the whole arrangement collapses; or, most interestingly, that a child's presence changes everything because children learn rules before they can learn judgment. The Americans hear this as fetishizing order. The Germans hear the Americans as arrogant -- as claiming a private exemption from a burden everyone else carries. Neither side is making a mistake I can point to, and the arguments never connect; they just talk past each other.

Here is the pattern I want named, more abstractly: a recurring moral standoff in which one party evaluates an act by its consequences for the people actually affected, and the other evaluates it by what would happen to the practice -- the shared institution of the rule itself -- if the act were generally permitted, and each party regards the other's question as simply beside the point. I don't think this is just rule-consequentialism versus act-consequentialism, because the German side isn't making a philosopher's argument about ideal rule sets; it's closer to a gut conviction that rule-following is itself a form of respect for other people, and that deciding unilaterally which rules deserve obedience is a kind of cheating even when nobody is hurt. It also isn't quite what psychologists mean by authoritarianism, since the same people will happily campaign to repeal a rule they consider stupid -- they just won't break it in the meantime. And I've read a little about Kohlberg's stages, but both sides here seem perfectly capable of principled reasoning; they just locate the principle in different places.

Please take on the role of a moral philosopher with a strong background in comparative moral psychology, and think carefully rather than reaching for the first label. Before answering, briefly brainstorm the nearest established concepts in ethics and moral psychology -- I suspect there are three or four candidates -- and for each one state precisely where the pattern I described matches it and where it breaks. Then give me your verdict: is there an established term for this exact standoff, or for each of the two moral styles involved? If not, coin one, define it rigorously, and give me a few boundary cases -- situations that nearly qualify but don't, and an explanation of why -- because I want to be able to recognize the real thing and not just wave the label at every argument about traffic lights.
