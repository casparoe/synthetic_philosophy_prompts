So I'm in this reading group that does one "applied ethics" session a month and next time it's on judging historical figures, and we were each asked to write down our settled positions in advance so the others can attack them. Here's roughly where I've landed, and I'd like to know before I embarrass myself whether the package actually hangs together:

(1) The "man of his time" defence doesn't work for Jefferson on slavery. Abolitionist arguments were in circulation, he understood them as well as anyone, he basically conceded the moral point in his own writing, and he kept enslaving people anyway because it was profitable and convenient. When someone knows the arguments and acts against them anyway, that's the paradigm case of blame. (2) Moral progress is real and it's mostly driven by small minorities pushing against a complacent majority, which means most people in most eras -- including ours, including me, an occasional meat-eater who knows the factory farming arguments perfectly well -- are blameworthy for something normalized, and future generations will be right to judge us harshly for it. (3) At the same time, blame requires a fair opportunity to know better: someone raised somewhere a practice is genuinely unthinkable, where every trusted authority endorses it and the contrary view has never been articulated in their vicinity, isn't blameworthy for going along. I want to keep this for, say, an ordinary medieval peasant. (4) Statues and public honors celebrate specific contributions, they don't canonize character. So Churchill's statue stays despite his views about Indians and his role in the Bengal famine, because what's being honored is 1940, not the whole man. (5) Columbus is different and gets no statues: partly because he was condemned even by his own era's lights (the Spanish crown had him arrested and sent back in chains), but mostly because the supposed achievement isn't one -- the continent was already inhabited, and Europeans had reached it centuries earlier anyway.

Is this consistent? I suspect (4) and (5) might be doing different jobs but I can't quite see where. Please audit it properly: flag the pairs that genuinely pull against each other, ordered by how serious the damage is, and for each one tell me specifically which commitment has to give and what the cheapest repair costs me. Also tell me which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, because I'd rather not concede ground I don't have to. And if one of my arguments proves too much -- i.e. a principle I use against one figure would convict people I explicitly want to acquit -- that's exactly what I want caught.
