Okay, so I'm putting together the reading for my probability & induction seminar and I've decided the students are getting way too comfortable with Bertrand's paradox as a party trick — three parametrizations, three answers (1/2, 1/3, 1/4), shrug, move on. I want a dialogue that actually fights about what the paradox shows. Write it in British English (mind your -ise endings) and aim for somewhere around 800-1000 words.

The two characters:

- One defends the dissolution view: the paradox only arises because the phrase "random chord" fails to specify a randomization procedure, and once a procedure is fixed — or once the problem carries enough physical symmetry, as in Jaynes-style well-posedness arguments, where invariance under translations, rotations and scale pins down a unique distribution — there simply is no residual problem. The principle of indifference survives, properly disciplined.

- The other defends the sceptical view: the paradox shows indifference is unfixable, because every "discipline" just reapplies indifference one level up. You solved the chord problem by demanding invariance under a symmetry group? Lovely — but the choice of that group over its rivals was itself an appeal to indifference over parametrizations, so you've relabelled the question-begging rather than removed it.

The dialogue has to work through three test cases, in whatever order feels natural:

1. The classic chord setup, where the sceptic should force the dissolutionist to say exactly which unanswered question survives once you concede each parametrization is a different experiment — is the paradox then an illusion, or just ambiguous?
2. The straws-on-the-floor case: someone physically tosses straws onto a circle chalked on the ground. The dissolutionist will say this settles everything; the sceptic should press on whether it settles the *original* question or merely swaps it for a better-behaved one.
3. A case I want injected to stop them rehashing the standard moves: a factory machine generates chords by one of the three classic mechanisms, but the specification plate has worn off and nobody knows which. The characters should fight over whether applying indifference *over mechanisms* is legitimate, whether it triggers a regress that vindicates the sceptic, or whether it's precisely the point where the dissolutionist's position bites back — since refusing to assign any probabilities here seems like a cost of its own.

Ground rules: both characters are equally sharp, neither is a strawman. They must respond to each other's actual arguments, not restate their opening positions at increasing volume. Each side has to concede at least one genuine point somewhere. And don't let them hug it out at the end — the dialogue should close by locating the precise crux they still disagree on, stated cleanly enough that a student could take a side.
