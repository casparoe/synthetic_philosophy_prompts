Two of my oldest friends are getting married next month, and I have been asked to give the toast at the reception. They met as graduate students co-teaching an undergraduate seminar on nineteenth-century utilitarianism, and both went on to write dissertations in ethics, so the paradox of hedonism is, between them, something like a shared native language: the familiar observation, associated in their field with Sidgwick and with Mill's account of his own youthful crisis, that happiness aimed at directly tends to recede, and arrives instead as a byproduct of pursuing things other than itself. The guest list reflects this. Roughly a third of the room will be philosophers who know the paradox by name and will notice instantly if I get it wrong; the other two thirds are parents, aunts, and childhood friends who have never heard of Henry Sidgwick and should not be made to feel that they need to. The toast should run about five minutes.

Here is the difficulty I want the speech to solve. Nearly every wedding toast gives the couple some version of the same advice -- make each other happy, put the other person's happiness first, never stop working at your happiness together -- and the paradox says that this advice, taken literally, is self-defeating. I want a toast whose central advice to the couple survives the paradox rather than politely ignoring it. Two failure modes to avoid. First, the lazy reading on which one must simply refuse to care about or think about happiness at all; that would be a chilling thing to tell newlyweds, and it is not what the paradox actually shows. Second, the obvious regress, which the philosophers in the room will be waiting for: aim at the marriage so that you will be happy is still aiming at happiness, only sneakily. The toast should confront that regress openly enough to satisfy them -- perhaps by distinguishing what one attends to from why one acts, or by treating the paradox as a problem about constant monitoring rather than about commitment -- while never once sounding like a seminar.

A few pieces of material you may use or discard. The couple's first real conversation was an argument, conducted over bad conference coffee, about whether Mill's crisis was a genuine conceptual discovery or simply depression wearing a conceptual costume. Each of them has independently told me that the other is the only person whose company makes them forget to check how the evening is going. And the sleep analogy -- that happiness, like falling asleep, resists direct willing -- is one they both use in teaching, so it will land as an inside reference rather than a cliché. The toast should close with an actual toast, a sentence the entire room can raise a glass to that does not quietly retract everything that came before it. Warmth first, precision a close second: it must move the aunts and withstand the philosophers, in that order of priority.
