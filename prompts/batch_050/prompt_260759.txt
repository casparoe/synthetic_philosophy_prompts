I'm a junior in high school, and my bioethics elective has been doing a unit on how health systems allocate scarce resources. We learned the basic idea of a QALY -- one year of life in full health, with years at reduced quality of life counting fractionally -- and how agencies use cost-per-QALY to decide which treatments and programs to fund. I tried to turn the unit's reasoning into a rigorous argument for a debate tournament, and I seem to have proven something monstrous. Here it is.

(1) A year of healthy life has the same value no matter who lives it -- a child, a retiree, a patient lying in front of us, or a stranger we will never be able to name.

(2) If two outcomes differ only in how many such years they contain, the outcome containing more is better, and if one option available to us produces the better outcome, that option is the one we should take.

(3) Money spent on one health measure cannot also be spent on another, so the choice between programs is real and zero-sum.

(4) Therefore, by (1)-(3), health resources should go wherever they purchase the most healthy years.

(5) Rescuing identified victims -- the miners trapped underground, the child who needs a three-million-dollar drug, the climber airlifted off a mountain -- purchases far fewer healthy years per dollar than prevention does: vaccines, highway barriers, screening programs.

(6) Therefore, whenever the choice is between a rescue and a prevention program, the money should go to prevention.

(7) This choice recurs every budget cycle, indefinitely. At the moment of choosing, next year's victims are always mere statistics -- no one is trapped yet. So the rational policy is to fund only prevention, every year, forever, and never to fund rescue at all.

(8) So when the miners are actually trapped, the right thing to do is to let them die and buy the highway barriers instead; a society that keeps rescuing people is acting wrongly, and the rescue crews are doing something bad.

(9) But (8) is insane. Nobody decent could let the miners die on live television to buy guardrails, and I don't believe the crews are doing wrong. So something between (1) and (7) has to give.

Here is my problem: every step looked airtight when I wrote it, and I can't find the crack. My teacher says the mistake is "somewhere in how the argument treats time and identity," but she won't say more because she wants me to figure it out. Two things I'd strongly prefer to keep: premise (1), because I think denying it is just prejudice with extra steps, and the idea that the number of people saved matters for something -- I don't want to end up saying we should be indifferent between saving one person and saving a million.

So: which step do I reject? Please explain why that step looked valid when it isn't (or why it's the least costly premise to surrender if the argument is valid), and then price the alternatives -- what do I have to give up if I instead reject (2), or (5), or the move from (6) to (7)? Finally, tell me whether your diagnosis still lets me say anything sensible about cases where the rescue is genuinely, extravagantly expensive -- the million-dollar-a-day intensive care patient, say -- or whether fixing the argument forces me to conclude that no rescue is ever too costly, which I also find hard to swallow.
