I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and a colleague presented me with the following argument during a workshop on the epistemology of introspection. Each premise strikes me as defensible, yet the conclusion is one I cannot accept, since it would force a choice between trusting empirical science and trusting its results. I would like to know exactly where the reasoning fails, why the failing step looked innocent, and what rejecting it commits me to.

(1) All empirical evidence ultimately consists in how things seem to an observer: instrument readings, reaction times, and questionnaire responses count as evidence for someone only insofar as they appear to that person in some way.
(2) The only access anyone has to their own seemings is introspection; there is no other route by which a seeming becomes available to the subject whose seeming it is.
(3) So all empirical justification is mediated by introspection.
(4) If a faculty that mediates all justification were not generally reliable, then no belief formed through it could be justified, since there would be no independent standpoint from which to check its deliverances.
(5) Some beliefs are justified -- including the empirically established belief that subjects frequently misreport their own experience.
(6) Therefore introspection is generally reliable.
(7) A faculty is generally reliable only if it is reliable in its central, everyday applications; a thermometer accurate only under unusual conditions is not a reliable thermometer.
(8) Reporting the character of one's ongoing experience -- for instance, that one's visual field is rich and detailed in the periphery, or that one was just thinking about nothing in particular -- is the central, everyday application of introspection.
(9) So such reports are generally reliable.
(10) But work on change blindness, inattentional blindness, and confabulated self-report indicates that reports of exactly this kind are often mistaken.
(11) So either the empirical findings in (10) must be rejected, which seems perverse given (5), or the argument goes wrong somewhere before (9).

I would most like to keep both the evidential standing of empirical science and the findings in (10). Which step do you reject, why did it seem plausible, and what does rejecting it cost -- in particular, does it reopen the skeptical worry raised at (4), or concede something important to the skeptic about introspection?
