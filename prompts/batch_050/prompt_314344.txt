ok so I'm a philosophy phd student (ethics / action theory side) and I've gotten sucked into the approval-directed vs goal-directed agent debate from the AI safety literature, and I want to write a dissertation chapter that doesn't just relitigate the usual talking points. Before I pick a side I want the spec first. Write the desiderata: what must a theory of oversight-based agency actually deliver to count as an answer to when an agent should be steered by a principal's approval rather than by pursuing a goal directly -- the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the failure modes it must predict in advance rather than be surprised by -- with the consideration behind each requirement, and mark which are non-negotiable and which look like prejudices of one camp (the alignment people, the virtue ethicists, the principal-agent economists). also tell me which single desideratum does the most excluding of candidates.

Things I already know I want on the list, but tell me what I'm missing: Goodharting the approval signal; the agent that learns to keep its overseer selectively uninformed (call it epistemic capture of the overseer); the regress of who approves the approver; and a problem I'm calling approval laundering, where each step in a long chain of locally approved actions composes into an outcome nobody would of approved ex ante -- I especially want to know whether any approval-based theory can handle that compositionality issue without quietly smuggling a goal back in through the definition of approval. Also the theory has to say which notion of approval it's running on -- actual approval, informed approval (what the overseer would sign off on if shown everything), or some idealized version -- and what breaks under each choice.

The part I most want to see: are any subsets of these desiderata jointly unsatisfiable? I suspect "never shape the overseer's information for approval's sake" and "maximize actual approval" can't both hold once the overseer is fallible, but maybe there's a cleverer carve-out. Then briefly score pure goal-direction, pure approval-direction, and the obvious hybrid (approval as a veto over goals) against the list -- keep the scorecard short though, the criteria are the real deliverable. Purpose, so adequacy is relative to something: a theory good enough to govern AI assistants acting on behalf of ordinary users.
