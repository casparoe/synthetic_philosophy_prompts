I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past year reading philosophy of mind, and I would like your help stress-testing an intuition I cannot seem to settle. Consider the following construction, which is my own elaboration of a thought experiment I encountered in my reading.

Suppose that, shortly before his death, a great scientist's behavioral dispositions are exhaustively mapped. From this mapping we produce a book -- an enormous but finite one. The book contains, for every question that could be posed to the scientist in English of up to thirty words, the answer he would have given, together with a page reference directing the reader to follow-up material sensitive to what was asked previously, so that one can "converse" with the book by looking up entries in sequence, and the conversation unfolds with all the wit, surprise, and apparent spontaneity of talking to the man himself. No step in this process involves any computation occurring at the time of the conversation; everything was fixed at the moment of printing. The reader supplies only the labor of turning pages.

My question: what is your actual credence that, when such a book is being actively consulted in conversation, there is something it is like to be that system -- that some conscious experience is instantiated, attributable either to the book, to the book-plus-reader, or to the scientist's mind somehow persisting? Please do not answer that it depends on one's theory of consciousness; of course it does. I want a number, arrived at by decomposition. Specifically:

1. Your credence that some broadly functionalist or computationalist theory of consciousness is correct -- that consciousness is a matter of the right abstract organization rather than of particular biological material.
2. Conditional on that, your credence that a purely static lookup structure, whose entire "processing" occurred in the past, realizes the relevant organization -- that is, that the functional facts which matter are all present in the printed pages and the act of consultation, despite the absence of any live causal dynamics unfolding in real time.
3. Any further terms you believe the decomposition requires -- for instance, a term capturing whether the question is even well-posed, or whether the locus of the experience (book, reader, composite) makes a difference to whether there is experience at all.

Give each term a number with one or two lines of justification, show how the terms combine, and then tell me: which term are you least confident in, and what argument or finding would move it most? Finally, state how your final credence changes if the same information is instead embodied in a computer simulation executing in real time, and identify precisely which step in the decomposition does the work in producing the difference.

I should add that my training has left me with strong priors about the importance of real-time causal processes, and I suspect these priors may be doing more work in my own thinking than they are entitled to. If you believe a physicist's instincts about causation and implementation are a poor guide here -- or, alternatively, a better guide than philosophers of mind typically acknowledge -- please say so explicitly and explain why.
