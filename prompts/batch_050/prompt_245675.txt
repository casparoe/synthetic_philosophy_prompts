I direct a small science-policy institute with enough funding to run two serious multi-year projects over the next five years, and our board has decided the theme will be behavioral genetics and its public understanding, with heritability as the organizing concept. We have a standing list of candidate projects, and I want a defended prioritization rather than another survey of the literature.

The candidates: (1) the "missing heritability" problem -- why SNP-based estimates fall so far short of twin-study estimates for many traits, and what resolving the gap would actually settle; (2) the persistent public and journalistic inference from "trait X is highly heritable" to "trait X is unchangeable by policy," which is a logical error, yet survives decades of correction -- a project would investigate why the error is so robust and what framing actually defeats it; (3) the equal-environments assumption and the additivity assumptions built into variance decomposition, and whether twin-study estimates are trustworthy enough to bear the policy weight placed on them; (4) the within-group versus between-group fallacy, i.e., the inference from high heritability within populations to genetic explanations of differences between populations; (5) the poor portability of polygenic scores across ancestries and what that implies for both the science and its communication; (6) the observation that heritability estimates are population- and time-specific statistics that shift as environments and policies shift, and what follows for how such numbers should be reported at all.

Which two of these deserve our money and attention, and which should we explicitly decline, even though they are unresolved? Please order all six if you can, defend the ordering with explicit criteria (intellectual importance, tractability for an institute of our size, how much downstream confusion each question generates, and how much of the public debate silently depends on it), note where the criteria pull against each other, and say what evidence or events over the next five years would most change your ranking.
