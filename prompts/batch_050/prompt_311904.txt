In a reading group I run on behavioral genetics, the same analogy surfaced three weeks in a row, from three different people: heritability is a pie chart. The claim goes that when researchers report, say, that a trait is 60 percent heritable, they are telling us how a trait in a person is divided up — genes baked 60 percent of the pie, environment contributed the remaining 40 — and that the chart therefore tells you how much room there is to move the trait by changing the environment. One member of the group, an education-policy master's student, has been leaning on this analogy to argue that interventions aimed at highly heritable traits are mostly a waste of money: if genes baked most of the pie, she says, no amount of recipe-tinkering by policymakers is going to change what comes out of the oven. I suspect the analogy is doing more work than it can bear, but when I pressed her, I realized I could not articulate precisely where it fails without hand-waving, so I would like a proper audit.

Please assess how far the pie-chart analogy actually holds. Start with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the pie itself, the slices, the percentages on the slices, the baker, and the act of re-baking — given that heritability is technically a ratio of variance components estimated within a particular population at a particular time, rather than a fact about how any individual's trait was produced? Then work through the breakdowns, and for each one say whether it is a harmless simplification or a load-bearing failure. The cases I have in mind: heritability estimates change when the range of environments in the population changes (so the "chart" seems to be redrawn by circumstances the analogy treats as external to it); a trait can be highly heritable and yet highly malleable, as with height rising across generations under better nutrition, or with conditions whose effects are severe in the untreated but preventable by a simple dietary change; and a high heritability within each of two groups is routinely taken to license conclusions about why the groups differ, which the analogy makes tempting but which I believe is a further step. Finally, address the use my group member is putting the analogy to: once the disanalogies are on the table, does her pessimism about environmental intervention survive, or was the pie chart carrying the whole argument? If you think a better analogy exists for explaining heritability to a general audience — something that captures what the statistic actually measures without the misleading connotations of fixed proportions and baked-in outcomes — propose it, and say what predictions or intuitions it gets right that the pie chart gets wrong.
