I spent four decades as an experimental physicist, and over that time I invoked Occam's razor almost reflexively: in referee reports, in model selection, in telling graduate students not to keep adding free parameters. Since retiring I have begun reading philosophy of science, and I was pleased to learn that the razor has received a formal defense through algorithmic information theory — hypotheses treated as programs, simplicity as program length, with convergence results suggesting that a simplicity-weighted prior eventually favors the truth. But the same reading has unsettled me. Program length is defined only relative to a choice of universal machine; the apparent complexity of a hypothesis seems to shift with how the data are encoded; and I now cannot say what I was actually asserting all those years when I told someone their theory was "too complicated."

Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that the injunction to prefer the simpler theory might be expressing — for instance, a metaphysical claim that nature is simple, an epistemic claim that simpler hypotheses are more likely true, a methodological rule that pays for itself without being truth-conducive, or a merely pragmatic tie-break between empirically equivalent rivals. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; whether the algorithmic-information formalization genuinely vindicates it, and whether the machine-dependence of Kolmogorov complexity and the encoding-dependence of descriptions undercut that vindication or leave it intact; and whether it would have justified the uses I actually made of the razor, namely preferring a curve with fewer parameters and rejecting an ad hoc auxiliary hypothesis introduced to save a failing model. Please also say plainly where counting parameters and measuring program length come apart, since I suspect my intuitions were trained on the former while the theorem concerns the latter.
