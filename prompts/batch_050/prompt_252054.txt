I manage IT at a mid-sized company, and at a meeting about deploying productivity-monitoring software on employee laptops — keystroke logging, periodic screenshots, flagged-keyword alerts — a colleague ended the discussion by saying that if you have nothing to hide, you have nothing to fear. I felt the move was illegitimate but couldn't articulate why on the spot. Lay out the distinct precise claims that slogan could be making: for instance, an empirical claim about who is actually harmed by monitoring, a normative claim about what privacy is for and what it protects, a claim about the trustworthiness and competence of whoever holds the collected data, and any further readings you think are genuinely distinct from these. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or straightforwardly false, and whether — if granted — it would actually justify dismissing the employees' objections in this case. Conclude by identifying which reading my colleague's rebuttal most plausibly depended on, and whether that reading survives two complications: that the watching party is the one who gets to define what counts as something to hide, and that information harmless under present management may not remain so.
