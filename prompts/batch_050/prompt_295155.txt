I'm preparing a unit on the badness of death for an undergraduate philosophy of death course, and I want a decision tree that walks a student from the initial intuition that death is a grave misfortune to a named position in the literature. Build it as follows:

- Around 7–9 branching questions, each phrased as a concrete case or claim a student can react to without knowing any labels. At least three of the questions should use cases along these lines: whether falling into a century of dreamless unconsciousness and then waking would be a misfortune comparable to dying; whether it would have been better for you had you been born decades earlier than you in fact were; whether a harm must befall its victim at some time at which the victim exists.
- Leaves should include at least: the Epicurean view that death is not bad for the one who dies; a deprivationist or comparativist view on which death is bad because of the life it prevents; a Lucretian view that accepts the symmetry between prenatal and post-mortem nonexistence and bites the bullet; a view that breaks the symmetry by appealing to what is and isn't metaphysically possible for the person; and a view on which death harms the person but at no particular time, or only given eternalism about time.
- For each question, indicate which answers lead toward which leaf, and note any combination of answers that no named position occupies — with a brief diagnosis of whether the gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate.

Close with a short note distinguishing what is effectively settled among philosophers who work on this topic from what remains genuinely contested; in particular, do not present any contested move — such as a particular way of rejecting the symmetry argument, or a particular solution to the timing problem — as though it were the consensus.
