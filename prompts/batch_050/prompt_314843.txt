You are a comparative political scientist with philosophical training, advising a small, non-partisan democracy-monitoring institute. The institute wants to publish its own annual judgement about which countries are undergoing democratic backsliding, rather than simply citing existing indices, and the director has asked you for a specification document before any indicator work is commissioned: the desiderata that any adequate concept of democratic backsliding must satisfy. Think carefully about this. Begin with a short brainstorming phase in which you list a dozen or so candidate requirements without filtering, then prune, merge, and organise them into a final ordered list.

For each desideratum on the final list, state the requirement precisely and give the case or consideration that motivates it. Draw on the following repertoire of cases, and feel free to add others: Hungary after 2010, where institutional change proceeded largely through legal channels by a government with a parliamentary supermajority; Poland between 2015 and 2023, where a period of judicial and media capture was followed by an electoral transfer of power and partial repair; Turkey, where an attempted coup was followed by emergency rule and a constitutional shift to a presidential system; and the United States around 2020-21, where an incumbent sought to overturn an election result but courts, state officials, and legislatures mostly held. Your desiderata should grapple with at least the following problems, though you should not treat this list as exhaustive. The baseline problem: backsliding relative to what -- a country's own recent past, or some ideal of democracy -- and can a country that was never very democratic "backslide" further? The legality problem: must the concept count changes achieved by lawful, even constitutional, means, and if so how does it avoid collapsing into "any institutional change we dislike"? The symmetry problem: how the concept is to be insulated from the charge that it is a partisan label applied asymmetrically to one side of the political spectrum, given that incumbent governments of every stripe sometimes claim a popular mandate to weaken checks on their power. The intent problem: whether backsliding requires deliberate executive aggrandisement or whether drift, decay, and polarisation without a central author also count. The resilience problem: whether a failed attempt at subversion, thwarted by institutions, constitutes backsliding, its near-miss, or evidence against it. The reversal problem: whether subsequent repair retroactively changes the classification of the earlier period, and whether the concept is binary or a matter of degree. And the adjudication problem: who gets to apply the concept -- since coders, opposition parties, and incumbent governments will all reach for it -- and whether it must be operationalisable with reasonable inter-coder agreement without reducing to whatever the coders happen to think.

Mark each desideratum as non-negotiable or as a preference likely to vary between camps, and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding inadequate concepts. Most importantly, flag any pair or subset of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied -- for instance, whether political symmetry can be reconciled with substantive content, or sensitivity to legalistic capture with coder reliability -- and explain why, since an inconsistency in the list would itself be the most useful finding for the institute. Conclude with a brief pass (no more than a few sentences each) on how three broad families of approach fare against your list: minimalist electoral definitions, behavioural and norm-centred accounts of the kind associated with Levitsky and Ziblatt, and large-scale expert-coded institutional indices such as V-Dem or Freedom House. Keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scorecard. Write in British English throughout.
