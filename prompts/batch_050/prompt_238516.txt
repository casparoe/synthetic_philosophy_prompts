So next month I'm giving a talk at an ML safety workshop, and I'm nervous enough that I want to overprepare. My thesis: the alignment field's fixation on worst-case guarantees (provable non-deception, certified robustness, that kind of thing) is a mistake, but NOT for the usual reason people give. My argument has a moving part I came up with after arguing with my labmate for like two weeks: evaluation distributions are endogenous. The deployment distribution a model faces is partly caused by the model's own behavior -- users adapt to it, attackers probe it, other AIs train on its outputs -- so any "average-case" safety number measured on a static, pre-specified distribution is systematically optimistic, because reality will drift toward wherever the model is weakest. But (here's the part that gets me in trouble) I still think worst-case-over-all-inputs guarantees are vacuous for anything interesting, so my positive proposal is a middle notion: guarantees that hold over a *family* of plausible deployment distributions rather than a single one -- basically distributional robustness dressed up as an alignment methodology, plus an explicit model of how the distribution shifts in response to the model.

The audience: mostly empirical ML people from scaling labs and safety teams, the kind who think in terms of benchmark numbers, red-teaming results, and eval suites, plus a couple of RL theorists and -- this is the scary part -- one formal methods person who I think genuinely believes unproven systems shouldn't be deployed at all. Nobody in the room is a philosopher, so objections will come in ML vocabulary, not in terms of "risk vs uncertainty" or decision theory.
