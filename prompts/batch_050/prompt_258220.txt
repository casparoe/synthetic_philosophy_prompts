At a department happy hour last week I listened in on an argument between two postdocs, one in econophysics and one in decision theory, that I still can't settle for myself. It started with a coin-flip bet: each round you stake your entire bankroll; heads you gain 60% of your stake, tails you lose 50%. Dana pointed out the arithmetic expectation is positive -- a 50/50 average of 1.6 and 0.5 gives a mean multiplier of 1.05 per round -- so a risk-neutral agent should take the bet every time. Marcus replied that this is exactly where ensemble thinking goes wrong: if you actually play repeatedly, compounding the bet, your wealth multiplies by sqrt(1.6 × 0.5) ≈ 0.894 per round in the typical trajectory, so almost everyone who plays long enough goes broke, and the rising ensemble average is an illusion sustained by a vanishingly small number of wildly rich outliers. Dana conceded the arithmetic but insisted the conclusion was wrong-headed: the law of large numbers hasn't been refuted, the positive expectation is a genuine fact about the bet, and the real mistake in Marcus's scenario is bet sizing, not betting -- using a Kelly-style criterion and staking roughly one-sixth of the bankroll each round turns the time-average growth rate positive, so ergodicity considerations don't tell you whether to bet, only how much. Marcus pushed back that this understates the point: the whole lesson of the time-average perspective is that expected value is the wrong objective for any process an individual actually lives through, since you get one trajectory, not an ensemble, and that this explains why things like insurance can be rational despite negative expected value without invoking any arbitrary utility curvature. Dana answered that this last move gives the game away: maximizing the time-average growth rate of a multiplicative process is mathematically just maximizing expected log wealth, so the "new" framework is ordinary expected utility theory with a particular concave utility function wearing a trench coat, and the claim that three centuries of decision theory rested on an error is marketing rather than mathematics.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the main moves above -- Dana's opening EV argument, Marcus's typical-trajectory rebuttal, Dana's Kelly-criterion response, Marcus's one-trajectory objection, and Dana's final repackaging charge -- tell me whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or right only in a qualified sense, and justify each verdict in a few sentences. Then tell me, at the deepest level, what the disagreement is actually about: is it a dispute about mathematics, about the correct objective function for a single agent embedded in time, or about nothing at all once both sides make their implicit assumptions explicit? If there is a consideration both parties are missing -- for instance, about whether the relevant process is genuinely multiplicative, or about what justifies one growth objective over another -- I'd like to know that too.
