I cover physics and the philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine, and each winter I help run an intensive study week in which working journalists and a handful of graduate students in physics take up one conceptual problem in depth. This year's problem is gauge theories: which parts of the mathematical formalism represent something physically real, and which parts are mere redundancy in our description — what philosophers of physics call surplus structure. I am responsible for the final written assessment, and I would like you to design it.

The students will have covered the following material: classical electromagnetism formulated in terms of the scalar and vector potentials, with gauge transformations presented as redescriptions of one and the same physical field; the Aharonov–Bohm effect, in which a measurable phase shift depends on the potential in a region the charged particle never enters; and the geometric reformulation in which the potential is treated as a connection and the effect is attributed to the holonomy of that connection around a loop. They will not have been told which interpretation of the potential is correct. The exam lasts 90 minutes and is marked out of 40.

Please produce three things.

First, a single essay question that cannot be answered by reciting the slogan that gauge symmetry is unphysical. The question should require students to (a) state a precise criterion for when an element of a theory's formalism counts as surplus structure, (b) apply that criterion to the vector potential in classical electromagnetism, (c) explain why the Aharonov–Bohm effect appears to threaten the verdict reached in (b), and (d) adjudicate between at least two live interpretive responses — for instance, realism about the potential, realism about gauge-invariant holonomies, or a non-separable treatment of the electromagnetic field — by identifying a consideration that genuinely discriminates between them, such as locality, separability, or what is doing the explanatory work in the effect, rather than by appealing to the standardness of any view.

Second, a model answer of roughly 600 words, written to the standard of a top script.

Third, a marking scheme allocating the 40 marks across components, and doing so in a way that makes explicit what separates an upper-tier answer from a mid-tier one. For the upper tier, name the specific moves the answer must contain — for example, recognising that the holonomy reading purchases gauge-invariance at the price of a form of non-locality or non-separability, and acknowledging this trade rather than presenting one interpretation as cost-free. For the lower tiers, name the specific errors that should cap the mark — for example, treating the Aharonov–Bohm effect as showing that the potential acts locally on the particle at its location; claiming that gauge transformations alter observable quantities; conflating gauge dependence with mere convention about units; or defining surplus structure so loosely that every theoretical entity qualifies.

Two constraints. The question must be answerable from the material described above alone: no appeal to particular papers, named authors, or quotations, since the students have no library access during the exam and I will not ask them to rely on citations. And the question should be stated in language a careful non-specialist can parse, since half the examinees are journalists rather than physicists.

End your response with a final line beginning "Crux:", containing one sentence that states the single move which most distinguishes a first-rate answer from a merely competent one.
