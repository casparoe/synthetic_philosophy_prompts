I'm a high school student in my school's philosophy club, and I've run into a pattern in arguments that I can't find a word for, though I suspect philosophers have discussed it to death. We've spent the last few weeks on arguments for God's existence, and three of them seem to share the same skeleton.

First, the famous one from Anselm: define God as the greatest being that can be conceived, observe that a being that actually exists would be greater than one that is only imagined, and conclude that God exists. Second, the version where existence is said to belong to God's nature the way certain properties belong to a triangle's nature, so that denying God's existence is supposed to be a contradiction on par with denying what a triangle is. Third, and most interesting to me, Gödel's modal proof: it sets up a technical notion of "positive" properties, treats necessary existence as one of them, defines a God-like being as one possessing every positive property, and derives that such a being necessarily exists. In that one, at least, the work doesn't seem to be done by the definition of God directly, but by what gets counted as positive in the first place.

The pattern I keep noticing is this: the argument's real action happens entirely at the setup stage, in what gets built into a definition or a classification, and the actual derivation afterwards is just bookkeeping -- careful, valid bookkeeping, but bookkeeping. This seems subtler than ordinary circular reasoning, where a premise more or less restates the conclusion. None of these premises flatly say "God exists." Instead the vocabulary is arranged so that existence falls out once you accept the definitions, and objecting feels like refusing to play the game rather than finding a bad move in it.

So my questions: Does this failure mode (if it is one) have an established name, and how is it different from plain begging the question on one side and from equivocation on the other? Is Kant's complaint that existence is not a real predicate the same objection as mine, a different one, or a diagnosis of why the move is tempting? Does Gödel's version genuinely escape the pattern by routing existence through an independently characterized class of properties, or does it just relocate the setup work one level up, into the axioms about positivity? And if the thing I'm describing fits no established concept exactly, what would you call it -- with a definition precise enough to say whether it also covers "defining into existence" cases outside theology, like a perfect island or a necessarily existing number? I'd rather get the boundaries right than get a label.
