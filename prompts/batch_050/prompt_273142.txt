Having retired from a career in experimental physics, I have lately been working my way through the philosophy of time, and I keep returning to Gödel's 1949 argument concerning rotating cosmological models. As I understand it, Gödel showed that certain solutions to Einstein's field equations permit closed timelike curves — worldlines along which an observer could, in principle, return to an event in their own past — and he concluded from this that the objective lapse of time, the idea of reality as a succession of nows coming into being and passing away, cannot be a genuine feature of the world but must be a perspectival artefact of observers situated within it.

Please write a dialogue between two philosophers of physics who disagree about whether this argument succeeds. The first defends Gödel's conclusion: even granting that our actual universe is not a rotating one, the sheer consistency of spacetimes containing closed timelike curves demonstrates that temporal becoming cannot belong to the fundamental structure of reality. The second resists the inference: at most, she maintains, Gödel established that certain spacetimes admit no global time function, and moving from the mathematical existence of such solutions to the unreality of temporal passage in our universe requires a modal premise that stands in need of independent defence rather than one delivered by general relativity itself.

So that the exchange does not merely rehearse the standard moves, I would like them to work through two specific pressure points. First, a hypothetical laboratory case: suppose engineers could construct a small, spatially bounded region containing closed timelike curves, embedded in an otherwise ordinary universe. Does the Gödelian conclusion then hold only within that region? If so, does this show that the lapse of time, if it exists at all, is a local rather than global affair — and would that consequence comfort or embarrass each of them? Second, have them press on the question of what the argument actually requires: is the mere conceptual coherence of time travel enough for Gödel's purposes, does he need physical realizability somewhere in the space of solutions, or must closed timelike curves exist in our universe in particular?

A few stipulations about form. Both participants should be equally sharp and well-informed; I do not want either position caricatured. They should engage with what the other actually says, conceding points where concession is due and exploiting genuine weaknesses rather than talking past one another. The register should be that of a careful seminar discussion, and the dialogue should run to roughly one thousand words. It need not end in agreement — I would rather it conclude with a precise statement of where the disagreement is finally located, if it cannot be resolved, than with an artificial reconciliation.
