A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about supertasks, and we got stuck on a diagnostic question rather than on whether supertasks are possible. Her view: whatever is paradoxical about Thomson's lamp stems entirely from the fact that nothing in the infinite sequence of switchings determines the lamp's state at the final moment -- the description of the task is simply incomplete, and the "paradox" is just an unanswerable question masquerading as a contradiction. My view: underdetermination is not the real source of trouble, and there are supertask-style setups whose outcomes are perfectly determinate yet which are every bit as paradoxical.

I would like you to construct a thought experiment that settles this in my favor, if it can be settled. Specifically, design a supertask meeting all of the following specifications:

1. Infinitely many operations are completed in a finite interval (say, by noon, with operations at ever-halving intervals before noon, in the standard idealization).
2. The state of the system at noon is fully determined by the setup -- not left open the way the lamp's on/off state is left open.
3. The setup is internally consistent: unlike, say, a machine whose instructions require it to be in two incompatible states at noon, your scenario must not smuggle in a contradiction. Every specification you give could, per the physics of the story, be jointly satisfied.
4. Despite (2) and (3), the outcome is still genuinely paradoxical-seeming -- the kind of result a reasonable person balks at accepting, not a merely surprising one.

Constraints: keep the scenario itself to one paragraph; use no components beyond ordinary objects (balls, urns, marbles, lamps, switches and the like); make no claims that violate conservation of energy or require infinite velocities beyond the usual supertask idealization of ever-faster discrete operations; and do not stipulate the verdicts -- the paradoxicality has to fall out of the scenario, not be asserted of it.

After the scenario, do the following, in order. First, verify each specification: state the schedule of operations, show exactly why the noon state is determined (what rule or limit fixes it), and show that no contradiction is anywhere specified. Second, locate the paradox: name the precise feature of the case that does the work -- my suspicion is that it is a discontinuity, the fact that the state of the system at noon is not the limit of its states at the approach to noon, so that the completion of the task requires the system to "jump" -- and explain why that feature suffices to generate the paradoxical air even though every individual operation is unobjectionable. Third, run the comparison my friend would demand: state what Thomson's lamp and your case have in common, state where they differ, and say what your case shows about her diagnosis -- does underdetermination turn out to be necessary for supertask paradox, sufficient for it, or neither? Fourth, perform the removal test: modify the scenario so that the noon state coincides with the limit of the pre-noon states, and report whether the paradoxicality disappears or survives. If it survives, revise your diagnosis of the load-bearing feature accordingly. Finally, give the strongest reply available to my friend: how would someone who thinks supertasks are simply impossible, full stop, attack the scenario -- and does that attack concede my point about the source of paradoxicality while denying the coherence of the whole genre, or does it actually reinstate her diagnosis?
