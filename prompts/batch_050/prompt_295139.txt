I am a novelist by trade, and later this month I have agreed -- against my better judgment, but the organizer is an old friend -- to lead an evening session for a local circle of amateur musicians and music-lovers on the subject of what, if anything, music is about. The group is intelligent but philosophically untrained: several play instruments seriously, none has read more than a page of philosophy, and I can count on ninety minutes of attention at most. I want to anchor the session in Schopenhauer's account of music, since it seems to me the most ambitious attempt to explain why music moves people who could not say what it depicts. I do not need Schopenhauer explained to me; I need a plan for explaining him to them. Specifically: in what order must the pieces be introduced? My sense is that his claim that music stands apart from all the other arts is unintelligible, or worse, sounds like empty praise, unless the audience first grasps his picture of the world as representation, the will that lies behind it, and his account of how painting, sculpture, and poetry work by presenting Ideas -- but perhaps I am wrong about what has to come first, and perhaps there is a way in that postpones the metaphysics until the audience is already invested. Please tell me what the correct dependency structure is, and why.

Second, I need the carrying example. I want one concrete musical case that bears the whole weight of the idea that music is not a copy of appearances, nor even of the Ideas, but something like a direct presentation of the will itself -- a case where the audience can feel the difference between music expressing this or that particular grief, tied to a story, and music expressing grief-as-such. Should the example be a well-known instrumental movement, a sung passage where the words pull one way and the music another, or a comparison between a piece with a program and a piece without? Tell me which to choose, what the example must have and must conspicuously lack, and what to do about the complication that most of my listeners will know more music than I do and will immediately supply counterexamples from repertoire I have never heard.

Third, the misconceptions I should expect and where in the sequence to head each off. I anticipate at least these: that Schopenhauer means music is the composer's diary in sound; that he is simply ranking the arts and putting music at the top out of personal taste; that the view implies program music is always a corruption (and what that means for opera and song, which several members of the group love); and that talk of the will is just an outdated word for emotion. Tell me which of these I have correctly diagnosed, which I am missing, and the precise point in the evening at which each is most economically defused -- some presumably before they harden, others perhaps only after the carrying example has landed. Finally, give me one question I can pose to the group at the end whose answers would show me whether the idea has actually taken hold, and tell me honestly which parts of the view -- the metaphysics of the will, the technical claims about melody and harmony -- I should deliberately leave unsaid at this level, while flagging to the group that I am leaving them unsaid.
