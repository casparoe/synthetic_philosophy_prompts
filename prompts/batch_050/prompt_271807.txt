I'm a high school junior doing an independent study on medical ethics, and I keep running into a configuration I can't find a name for, which is frustrating because it seems like it must be a standard topic. I'll describe it with two cases. First: a woman in her fifties, sharp and healthy, signs documents saying that if she develops dementia, she does not want certain life-prolonging treatments. Twenty years later she has advanced dementia, seems cheerful, and when asked says she wants to keep living. The hospital treats her old signature as binding and discounts her current stated wish. Second case: the same woman, at fifty-five, signs a document saying "in ten years, give me treatment X regardless of what I say at the time." Everyone agrees this second document is worthless -- if she objects at sixty-five, still fully competent, no one would dream of enforcing it. What strikes me is the pattern across the two: the earlier self only ever wins when the later self has been ruled out of the conversation. The past version of a person is treated as the authoritative voice precisely in the situations where the present version can't competently speak -- and never when she can.

Does this configuration have an established name? I want to be clear about what I'm asking, because I suspect it's easy to gesture at nearby ideas without landing on this exact one. I know there's a concept for binding yourself in advance against your own future weakness -- the sailor who has himself tied to the mast before the sirens sing -- but that seems different, because there the future self is expected to be tempted or irrational, whereas in my first case the later woman is simply a different sort of person whose wishes get disqualified wholesale. I also know there's a general principle about honoring what someone wanted before they lost capacity, but as far as I can tell that's usually stated as straightforward respect for the past self, without anyone flagging the conditional structure I've noticed: that the respect activates only upon the present self's disqualification. So my question is whether anyone has named the thing itself -- the arrangement where an earlier self's authority is contingent on, and perhaps even constituted by, the later self's being deemed incompetent.

If there is a name, I'd like to know how it differs from each of the nearby concepts I've sketched, and which parts of the literature actually grapple with the conditional structure rather than just the honor-the-past-wishes part. If there isn't a name, I'd like you to coin one and define it carefully, including its boundary cases: does it cover parents' decisions for children (where the present self is disqualified by age rather than decline)? Does it cover a living person's wishes controlling what happens to their corpse, where there is no later self at all? And I'd also like a verdict on the coherence of the practice: if the earlier self is only authoritative once the later self has been discounted, is it really the earlier self we're respecting, or is all the real work being done by our judgment that the current person doesn't count? A bare label without that analysis won't satisfy me.
