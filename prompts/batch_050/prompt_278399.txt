I host a long-form podcast on science and ideas, and in two weeks I am interviewing a philosopher who defends the view that omissions are genuine causes: that a gardener's failure to water the flowers caused their death in precisely the same sense that pouring weedkiller on them would have, and not merely in some secondary, courtesy, or moralized sense of 'cause.' The opposing camp holds that causation is a matter of physical production -- some transfer or connection between events -- and that describing inaction as a cause confuses our practices of assigning responsibility with the world's causal structure. My guest is aware of all the standard objections and has well-rehearsed answers, so I need questions that get past the opening statement of the view.

Draft ten questions in interview order. Each must engage the substance of her position rather than invite a restatement, and I want the sequence to build: later questions should be able to presuppose her answers to earlier ones. At minimum, the run of questions must put the following pressure points on the table. First, the connection intuition: by what mechanism does an absence do causal work, and is her account committed to counting non-events as relata of causal relations? Second, overgeneration: if the gardener's omission caused the death, so did the Queen's, the mail carrier's, and mine, since each of us also failed to water -- does the view really imply that millions of omissions caused one death, and if so, is that a cost or a feature? Third, the worry that absences are individuated relative to expectations, norms, or what ought to have happened, and that building such facts into the causal order makes causation anthropocentric. Fourth, the relation between her metaphysics and ordinary moral and legal practice: does accepting omissions as causes settle anything about blame, or does she hold the two strictly apart, and what happens to her view if they come apart in a hard case? Fifth, prevention structures: cases where an omission is prevented from preventing something, where the 'cause' is linked to the effect only through other agents' refraining.

For each question, write the follow-up I should have ready, keyed to the reply she is most likely to give given the commitments of the view -- not a generic 'can you say more?', but the natural next pressure point if she takes the obvious line. Flag any question where you expect her to respond by redescribing the case in production-causation vocabulary rather than answering, and tell me how to phrase the follow-up so that move becomes visible to the audience. Conclude the sequence with two questions on the empirical side: what she makes of experimental findings about how ordinary speakers actually deploy causal language for omissions, and whether a systematic mismatch between her theory and folk judgment would trouble the view or merely diagnose the folk.
