I work as a junior policy analyst for a mid-sized city's mayor, and I'm facing a dilemma I can't sort out. Our office is pushing a large downtown redevelopment plan, and last week I was asked to prepare the foot-traffic figures that will go to the public. No one asked me to invent numbers. What I was asked to do is present real, verified data in a way I believe is designed to mislead: the office wants to define the measurement window to exclude two bad years, use a baseline almost nobody would choose, and describe the result as showing that "downtown is dying." The redevelopment plan itself may genuinely be a good idea, but my supervisor waved off my objection with the argument that politics runs on persuasion, that framing facts is what every administration does, and that the end justifies it. Something about this still feels categorically different from ordinary political spin, and I want help understanding what that difference is.

I've been reading Hannah Arendt's essays "Truth and Politics" (1967) and "Lying in Politics" (1971) for a political theory seminar I'm auditing, and I think they speak to my situation, though I'm not sure how. As I understand her, Arendt distinguishes between the rational truths of logic and mathematics, which we can compel assent to, and factual truths about what contingently happened, which are fragile in a peculiar way: they rest on testimony and records, and once a political power starts treating facts as raw material to be rearranged for convenience, it is not merely deceiving an audience but attempting, in her account, to rewrite the world itself — an attempt she thinks eventually entangles the liar as well, since the fabulist ends up having to live inside and defend his own fabrication. My question is how to apply this. On her view, is my office's selective-but-accurate framing of genuine figures already the kind of fact-rewriting she warns about, or is it a defensible exercise of persuasion among competing opinions, which she seems to regard as the legitimate currency of politics? And what would Arendt make of my supervisor's argument that a worthy policy goal licenses the framing — does she think the ends-means reasoning even makes sense when what is being spent is public trust in facts?

Please lead with your bottom-line advice in the first sentence — whether I should comply, refuse, or push for a middle way, and under what conditions — and then give the reasoning, drawing on the Arendtian framework as well as any relevant distinctions it may be missing (for instance, whether a principled line can be drawn between framing and fabrication, and what the practical obligations of a civil servant are when that line is disputed). No preamble and no restating of my question.
