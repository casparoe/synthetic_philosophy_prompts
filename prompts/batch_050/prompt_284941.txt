I've been arguing with a friend about when the word genocide actually applies, and I realized I should figure out my own verdicts first before accusing him of misusing it. So here are my gut calls on a set of cases, real and hypothetical, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, underlies them. The Holocaust and the Rwandan genocide of 1994: clearly genocide, paradigm cases. A hypothetical dictator who massacres hundreds of thousands of suspected political opponents drawn from every ethnic group in his country: mass murder, but not genocide. A regime that engineers a famine in a particular ethnic region it views as disloyal, killing millions, while insisting it has nothing against that ethnicity as such and would happily spare loyal members: yes, genocide. A government that forcibly transfers an indigenous group's children into boarding schools with the aim of eradicating the language, religion, and culture, but kills no one: yes, genocide. The strategic firebombing of enemy cities in a war, with a civilian death toll comparable to the famine case: no, not genocide. A leader who orders an ethnic minority expelled from a territory, knowing thousands will die in the process, but with no aim of exterminating them: no, that's ethnic cleansing, a grave crime but a different one. And Cambodia under the Khmer Rouge: yes, I think, though I notice this sits uneasily with my verdict on the hypothetical dictator, since the victims there were largely fellow Cambodians targeted as class or political enemies rather than as a distinct people.

My tentative principle, shaped partly by what I know of Lemkin's original, broader idea and partly by the narrower postwar legal definition that centers on intending the destruction of a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group, is roughly this: genocide is a campaign intended to make a people cease to exist as a people -- destruction of the group qua group, whether by killing its members or by destroying what makes them that group. On this view, the perpetrator's ultimate motive (winning a war, consolidating power, taking land) is irrelevant, the death toll alone settles nothing, and cultural destruction can qualify even without bloodshed. What I'd like from you: test this principle against my seven verdicts, clause by clause, and tell me precisely which verdicts it accommodates and which it forces me to drop -- I suspect Cambodia is where it breaks, and if so, tell me whether to drop the verdict, repair the principle, or accept that common usage of the word is simply looser than any principle I could endorse. Then tell me what the surviving principle says about two cases I haven't listed: first, a general who destroys a region knowing full well that an ethnic group will thereby be effectively wiped out, but who is genuinely indifferent to that outcome rather than aiming at it; second, a policy of coerced sterilization designed to shrink a minority over generations without a single death. Finally, if my everyday usage and the legal category come apart anywhere, say which of the two my principle is actually tracking.

Please be direct with me about the exercise itself, not just the principle. If some of my verdicts reflect convention or political momentum rather than anything principled, say which ones and why. If you think the whole project of drawing a bright line around this one word is misguided -- that the moral seriousness of atrocities doesn't track the label, or that the legal and moral questions here can't be answered together -- I'd rather hear that argued plainly than receive a tidy principle that papers over the problem.
