A friend of mine works on AI safety, and over dinner he tried to sell me on a scheme he called amplification. Take a careful, well-intentioned person; whenever they face a question too hard to answer in the time available, let them consult fresh copies of themselves, each given a shorter time budget and a narrower slice of the problem; those copies may consult copies of copies in turn, until the leaf questions become trivially easy; then stitch the answers back together. Because every participant is literally the same trustworthy person, he argued, the composite can only ever want what that person wants, so alignment is preserved by construction; and because every sub-question eventually becomes easy, competence is preserved as well. The final step, distillation, trains a fast, cheap model to imitate the whole deliberation, so the expensive tree never has to be run again. I told him he had reinvented middle management, and we agreed I would express my skepticism in the medium it deserves.

Write the employee handbook of a company that operates exactly this way — give it a blandly corporate name — in which the founder is the only human being on the payroll and every analyst, reviewer, manager, and auditor is a short-lived copy of him working under a strict time budget. Deadpan HR prose, around 700 words, organized as numbered sections (Onboarding; Your Shift; Delegation and the Org Chart; Quality Assurance; The Alignment Guarantee; Distillation and Severance; and so on). The handbook should take the scheme's promises entirely seriously on the surface while the fine print quietly concedes the problems: sub-copies who cannot ask clarifying questions because their manager-copy's shift has already ended; "independent review" conducted by people who share every one of your blind spots, so that errors correlate perfectly; the fact that nobody in the tree has ever read the whole question; the Quality Assurance department being three copies of the intern with four minutes each; the distillation step producing a successor that has learned the company's confident tone rather than its reasoning; and a severance clause noting that, as a copy, you will not exist long enough to file a complaint. The Alignment Guarantee section should be simultaneously the most reassuring and the least reassuring thing in the document.

The satire has to come from getting the scheme right — exaggerating features it actually has rather than inventing flaws — so that someone who takes the underlying research program seriously can recognize their own position in it and, ideally, concede that the jokes are fair. Keep the irony deadpan but legible: the handbook must never editorialize, every concession should arrive as routine policy, and a reader should be able to tell what is being ironized without the narrator ever winking. It should be readable and funny to someone who has never heard of AI safety, and sharper for someone who has.
