I sit on the board of a regional transport authority (mid-sized UK city, about 800k people). We're choosing between two ways of fixing a dangerous junction cluster and it has turned into an unexpectedly philosophical fight, so I want a memo I can circulate before the vote.

Option 1 (the "specks" option, as one colleague keeps calling it): signal reprogramming plus minor roadworks across the whole network. Nobody is seriously harmed, but the modelling says the average commuter loses around 70 seconds per journey for the 18 months of works, with roughly 1.4 million journeys affected each weekday. Aggregate that and you get an enormous number of person-hours, which half the board keeps quoting at me.

Option 2 (the "torture" option): compulsory purchase and demolition to widen the junction directly. Nine households lose their homes — compensated at market value plus ten percent, but two are elderly residents who've lived there over fifty years and say the money is entirely beside the point — and one family-run garage doesn't survive relocation on anyone's projection. So: a handful of identifiable people harmed very seriously, versus a vast number of people mildly inconvenienced.

Draft a decision memo for the board recommending one option (or approving one with conditions). In it:

- Take an actual position on whether the aggregation argument settles this. Can a sufficiently vast number of tiny harms outweigh a few severe ones, or is there something like a threshold below which specks simply don't add up to torture no matter how many you pile on? Don't just gesture at the dilemma — apply it to our specific numbers.
- Deal with a wrinkle our modelling team raised late in the day: the delays aren't distributed evenly. Because of how routes overlap, about 3% of commuters would pass through several affected junctions daily and could rack up 20+ minutes of extra travel per day for the entire works period. Does the "tiny harms to many" framing survive that, or does accumulation turn some of the specks into something morally heavier, and does it change the recommendation either way?
- Give the strongest honest version of the losing side. If you recommend the network option, take seriously the objection that we'd be sacrificing identifiable, named people to a spreadsheet. If you recommend demolition, take seriously the objection that we'd be letting one visceral, photographable case override a massively larger total of harm just because it's spread too thin to picture.

Two things about how to write it. First, British English throughout, and keep the tone suitable for a board paper rather than a philosophy seminar. Second — and I mean this — if you think some part of how I've set this up is confused, say so plainly in the memo rather than politely going along with it. For instance if you think the whole premise that the board should be doing this kind of aggregation at all is wrong, and we should instead be applying side-constraints about what you may do to identifiable people regardless of totals, then argue that directly. I'd rather be told the question is malformed than recieve a beautiful answer to a bad one.
