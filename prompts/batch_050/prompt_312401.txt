i'm in a grad seminar on philosophy of psychology and we've spent basically the whole term on the imagery debate, pictorial vs propositional formats and all that. our professor refuses to just tell us where she stands, she only gives verdicts on experiments one at a time, and i'm trying to reverse-engineer her actual view before i write my final paper. her verdicts so far, as best as i've got them in my notes: on the cube-rotation studies (reaction time proportional to the angle of rotation) she said this is weak evidence for picture-like imagery, since people know what it is for an object to rotate and can pace themselves accordingly. on the map-scanning studies (time to "scan" between imagined landmarks proportional to distance) same verdict, roughly "they memorized the layout, of course they can act out the distances." on the zooming studies, where it's harder to report a mouse's whiskers when its imagined standing next to an elephant than next to a fly, she dismissed it entirely, said it's just what people know about mice and elephants and nothing about format follows. on the Perky-style results, where vividly imagining an image makes you worse at detecting a faint real image you don't even know is there, she said this is the genuinely interesting kind of result because you can't produce it just by knowing facts. on the finding that people generally can't flip an ambiguous figure (duck-rabbit type) while only holding it in imagination, she said this shows mental images are NOT like pictures in at least one respect, since a real picture on your desk can be looked at again and reversed. and on the neuroimaging showing retinotopic activation in early visual areas during imagery she said "suggestive, not decisive," since activation patterns alone don't settle questions of format.

so here's the ask. next week we're workshopping a (hypothetical, invented for the seminar) new result: congenitally blind subjects memorize a tactile map, and when later asked to travel routes on it in imagination, their response times scale linearly with the distances, same as sighted subjects with visual maps. i want a prediction: what does she say about this study, and does she count it as evidence for depictive imagery? tell me which rule you think she's actually been running across those six verdicts, what other rules fit the same track record just as well, and what each of the serious candidate rules predicts about the blind-map case. if her six verdicts genuinely underdetermine the answer, say so plainly, tell me what principle she'd be committing to with each possible verdict, and name the one further experiment that would pin her down. also be explicit about how confident you are in the prediction overall and flag the single point in your reconstruction you're least sure about -- i'd rather know where the story is shaky than get a clean one.
