I'm a science-and-ideas journalist writing a short column about why people hate uncertainty even when the expected payoff is basically the same. Everyone reaches for the same toy story: Ellsberg’s urn with 30 red balls and 60 that are black-or-yellow in some unknown proportion gets compared to a casino where the odds are printed on the felt. The analogy I keep hearing from policy people is stronger: a risky bet is like roulette in a licensed casino, but an ambiguous bet is like a sealed envelope from a stranger that contains either cash or a bill, and the stranger may have watched you walk in. They use it to argue that citizens are not being irrational when they resist things like geoengineering, AI triage in hospitals, novel vaccines, or prediction-market budgeting if experts cannot hand them tidy probabilities; on this analogy, demanding openness first is just ordinary prudence, not vibes-based panic.

Audit that analogy for me. What is supposed to map onto what: urn colors to outcome space, printed casino odds to known frequencies, the stranger to institutions with incentives, the envelope to model uncertainty, the sealed part to whatever is not merely stochastic but contested or strategically produced? Where does it break, what does it quietly smuggle in about adversarial motives, asymmetric downside, trust in regulators, and the difference between missing information and information someone has reason to hide? Which disanalogies actually matter for the anti-new-tech conclusion and which are just atmosphere? If a better everyday analogy can make ambiguity aversion look neither stupid nor magically wise, propose that too. Keep it under 600 words, lead with the one-sentence verdict, then give the audit; casual tone is fine, but don’t turn it into a reading list.
