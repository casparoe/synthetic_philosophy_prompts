On a rationalist forum I follow, someone was discussing a one-shot, anonymous prisoner’s dilemma between two separately instantiated copies of the same LLM. No communication, no enforcement, no future rounds, and each copy knows the other has the same weights and prompt. Someone replied: “Defection is only rational when the other player isn’t you.” It got a bunch of upvotes and then the thread moved on.

I’ve read enough LW-adjacent decision theory discourse to suspect this one-liner is smuggling in several different claims, but I can’t cleanly separate them. Using the standard payoffs -- 3/3 for mutual cooperation, 5/0 if one defects while the other cooperates, and 1/1 for mutual defection -- lay out the main precise theses the slogan could mean. For example: ordinary dominance reasoning, evidential reasoning about similar agents, claims about the copies’ outputs being correlated, claims about identity or self-interest, and claims that only work once iteration or retaliation is smuggled back in.

For each reading, say what exactly it assumes, whether the slogan follows from it, whether it changes the game’s payoffs or merely the decision procedure used to choose among them, and whether it is trivial, plausible but limited, or just confused. Also address the annoying middle cases: near-copies rather than exact copies, models trained differently but given the same prompt, and agents whose reasoning is similar without being identical. Is there any precise version that supports cooperation in the strict one-shot case without relying on morality or on secretly turning it into an iterated game? I’d like a menu of readings with verdicts, not just “it depends on your decision theory,” which is true but not very helpful.
