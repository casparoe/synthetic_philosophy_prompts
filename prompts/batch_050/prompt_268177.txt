I co-organize a philosophy reading group, and for our final session of the term we are trying something ambitious: an adversarial collaboration between our two most entrenched factions on the ethics of belief. One faction defends the strict evidentialist line associated with W. K. Clifford's essay The Ethics of Belief — roughly, that it is wrong, always and for everyone, to believe anything on insufficient evidence, with epistemic negligence carrying real moral guilt (their touchstone is Clifford's shipowner, who stifles his doubts about his vessel's seaworthiness and lets it sail). The other faction defends the permissivist line associated with William James's The Will to Believe — roughly, that when a person faces a genuine option (live, forced, and momentous) that evidence cannot settle, deciding by passional nature is not only unavoidable but lawful, and that some truths (their touchstone is James's mountaineer, whose belief that he can make the leap is part of what makes the leap possible) depend on prior faith. The two sides have spent three sessions talking past each other, and I have concluded the problem is that they have never had to write anything down together.

Draft the joint statement they could both sign at the end of a good-faith collaboration. It should have two parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims — and I want real agreements, not platitudes: I suspect both camps can sign claims about the shipowner-type case, about belief not being directly voluntary, about the difference between believing against the evidence and believing ahead of it, and about the evidentialist norm governing inquiry even if it does not govern every moment of deliberation, but I want you to find the exact formulations rather than assume mine are right. Second, a disagreements table with one row per residual disagreement, where each disagreement is stated twice, once in words the Clifford faction would accept as a fair statement of their own view and once in words the James faction would accept for theirs, followed by the crux (the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side), the case or argument both sides agree bears on that crux, and each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Two constraints shaped by how our sessions have gone wrong. First, please include at least two test cases of your own construction that go beyond the standard religious-belief and mountaineer examples — one involving a self-fulfilling or self-stultifying belief with concrete stakes (a researcher deciding whether to trust her long-shot hypothesis through the years of work needed to test it is the sort of thing I mean), and one involving a purely intellectual question where believing ahead of the evidence carries no practical benefit at all — and record in the table how each faction's position treats them. Second, the statement must explicitly mark which of the apparent disagreements the collaboration dissolved as merely verbal (I strongly suspect the dispute over what counts as evidence, and the dispute over whether James's permissiveness applies only where the intellect is genuinely undecided, are candidates) versus which survived as genuine. Where a disagreement survives, the crux column must name something specific enough that my two factions could at least agree on what would count as losing.
