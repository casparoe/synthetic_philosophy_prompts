So I just had this two-hour argument with my friend Sam about the reference class problem and we got nowhere, so I'm dumping my gut verdicts here and hoping you can tell me what principle (if any) I'm actually committed to. Background: Venn makes this point in The Logic of Chance that for a single case -- say one particular sickly man of a certain age -- you can sort him into tons of different classes (men his age, men with his disease, men with his disease living in his country, etc.) and each class gives a different frequency, and there's no principled rule for which class to use. Fine. Here's where my gut lands on cases:

1. An actuary pricing life insurance for a 45-year-old smoker by looking at death rates for 45-year-old smokers: totally fine, that's just good business.
2. Saying "50/50" about this particular coin flip even though the flip is physically determined: fine.
3. A court reasoning "the defendant is a young man from a neighborhood where 3% of young men commit this crime, so the prior he's guilty is low (or high, if the stats ran the other way)": feels deeply wrong, even though it's the same move as the actuary.
4. "What's the chance AI causes a catastrophe this decade?" -- people just make up numbers and I think there's no real reference class so the question is broken. Sam says this proves too much.
5. The chance that my specific flight tomorrow crashes: I'm fine answering with the frequency for commercial flights, even though I could narrow it (this airline, this weather, this plane model) indefinitely and at some point narrowing feels worse, not better.
6. Assigning a probability to whether a particular deterministic-but-unrun program halts: I want to say the question still makes sense, which sits awkwardly with (4).

Is there one principle that delivers all six? Something like "use the narrowest class with adequate statistics" handles some but I think breaks 3 and maybe 4. If no principle gets them all, tell me which verdict you'd give up and why, and what your principle says about a case I didn't list -- say, a doctor telling a specific patient her personal odds of surviving surgery. Also please be clear about what's genuinely settled in the stats/philosophy-of-probability literature here versus what's still contested -- don't present one school's fix (frequentist, propensity, whatever) as the consensus answer if there isn't one.
