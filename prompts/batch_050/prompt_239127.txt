I am trying to determine what criterion lies behind my intuitions about when mathematics genuinely explains a natural phenomenon rather than merely representing or calculating it. Consider these cases. First, take a simplified version of the cicada case: a 13- or 17-year life cycle overlaps less frequently with predator populations whose cycles last two, three, or four years than nearby composite life cycles would. My verdict is that primality is genuinely part of the explanation of the life cycle’s success, although natural selection explains how the trait became established. Second, grant that a hexagonal arrangement minimizes the perimeter needed to divide a surface into equal-area cells. If actual honeycomb cells initially approximate circles and are then reshaped by the physical properties of wax, I do not think the minimization theorem by itself explains the hexagons, though it might explain their selective advantage or why the physically resulting pattern remains stable. Third, suppose a city’s bridges form a network in which too many landmasses have an odd number of connections, so no route can cross every bridge exactly once. Here the parity result seems to explain the impossibility directly: no further physical investigation could reveal a route unless the network were changed. Fourth, when differential equations are used to calculate the resonant frequency of a suspension bridge, I am inclined to say that the mathematics is indispensable for prediction but does not provide an additional explanation; the bridge’s elasticity, mass distribution, and external forcing do the explanatory work. Fifth, a fitted predator–prey model predicts oscillations because a matrix associated with the model has complex eigenvalues. If its parameters have no independent biological interpretation, I deny that the eigenvalues explain the oscillations; but if those parameters track reproductive and predation rates, and changes in them reliably alter the population cycles, I become much more sympathetic. Finally, in the sunflower case, I want to distinguish between explaining why a golden-angle arrangement is advantageous for packing and explaining how the plant’s local developmental processes produce that arrangement.

Is there one precise principle, or perhaps a small family of principles, that captures these verdicts? I would like a criterion that does not count mathematics as explanatory merely because an equation occurs in a derivation, but that can still accommodate the cicada and bridge-network cases, where the explanatory role seems more substantial. In stating the principle, please identify which case motivates each clause and address the difficulty that mathematical truths are necessary: we cannot straightforwardly ask what would have happened if a parity theorem or primality fact had been false while all physical facts remained fixed. If no principle can save every verdict without becoming ad hoc, say which verdict should be abandoned. As a test case, apply the resulting account to a traffic model in which gridlock follows from the arithmetical fact that average inflow exceeds road capacity. Finally, explain what bearing the distinction has on the indispensability argument for mathematical realism: if some scientific explanations require mathematical facts in your strong sense, does that support belief in mathematical objects, or only in the explanatory usefulness of mathematical structures?
