The question, as it appeared on the problem set: Consider the gamble in which your wealth is multiplied by 1.5 if a fair coin comes up heads and by 0.6 if it comes up tails, repeated once per year. (a) Define the ensemble average and the time average of a growth process, and state what it means for such a process to be ergodic. (b) Compute the ensemble-average growth factor and the time-average growth factor per round for this gamble, and explain why they differ. (c) A recent research program known as ergodicity economics, associated with Ole Peters, argues that choices under uncertainty should be evaluated by the time-average growth rate they generate rather than by expected value, and presents this as a replacement for the expected-utility tradition going back to Bernoulli's treatment of the St. Petersburg paradox. Assess this claim: what does it get right, and what, if anything, does it overstate?

The student's answer: (a) The ensemble average of a growth process is the average taken across many parallel realizations at a single moment in time — here, the average over a large population of players after n rounds. The time average is the average taken along a single realization as it unfolds over time. A process is ergodic when the two averages coincide, which essentially requires the process to be stationary: its statistical properties must not change over time, so that a sufficiently long single trajectory explores the same distribution the ensemble is drawn from and its time average converges to the ensemble average. (b) The ensemble-average growth factor per round is 0.5(1.5) + 0.5(0.6) = 1.05, so expected wealth grows at 5% per round. The time-average growth factor is the geometric mean √(1.5·0.6) ≈ 0.949, so a typical trajectory shrinks by about 5% per round — the product 1.5·0.6 = 0.9 shows that one win and one loss, in either order, leave you 10% poorer. The gap arises because the dynamics are multiplicative: wealth compounds, so what accumulates is the logarithm of wealth, and the long-run growth rate is the average of the log growth factors rather than the log of the average, an instance of Jensen's inequality. The ensemble average is sustained by a small and shrinking minority of extraordinarily lucky trajectories, while the vast majority of players lose money steadily. (c) The program is right that an individual lives one trajectory through time rather than an ensemble of parallel lives, and that under multiplicative dynamics expected value is therefore the wrong criterion for individual choice: the gamble above has positive expected growth and is nonetheless ruinous for almost everyone who keeps playing it. It is also right that this resolves puzzles like the St. Petersburg paradox without invoking psychology — Bernoulli explained the paradox by observing that people overweight the small probability of an enormous prize, whereas the time-average analysis arrives at a finite valuation directly from the dynamics of repeated play. But the claim to have replaced expected utility theory is overstated, because maximizing the time-average growth rate under multiplicative dynamics is mathematically equivalent to maximizing expected logarithmic utility, so the proposal amounts to Bernoulli's criterion in disguise and the old framework survives inside the new one. Moreover, for additive dynamics the time and ensemble averages coincide, so the critique has no force there.
