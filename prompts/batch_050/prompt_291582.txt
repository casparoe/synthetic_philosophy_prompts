I am a PhD student in philosophy of physics, and my department's podcast runs an occasional series of "impossible interviews" in which a colleague portrays a historical figure and fields questions on their work. I have been assigned the episode on Kurt Gödel, specifically his 1949 work on rotating cosmological solutions to Einstein's field equations -- the so-called Gödel universe, with its closed timelike curves, and his notorious inference that these solutions show time to be ideal rather than real. Our actor has prepared by studying the standard moves Gödel makes: the idea that because our universe might have been rotating in a way that permits travel into one's own past, the notion of an objective lapse of time is not even necessary and therefore not a genuine feature of reality; and his suggestion that this vindicates something like Kant's view of time as a form of intuition rather than a property of things in themselves. My worry is that the usual interview questions -- did you really believe in time travel, what did Einstein think -- will produce a shallow episode.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer the interviewee is most likely to give, that press where the philosophical literature actually presses. I want at least one question on the modal step in the argument: why should the mere possibility of a universe without a unique time-lapse show that time in the actual universe fails to be objective, given that our universe does not appear to be rotating? I want one on whether Gödel's conclusion conflates two things that come apart -- the existence of a global ordering of events into earlier and later, and the existence of an objective lapse or passing of time -- and whether his solutions really threaten the first rather than only the second. I want one on the objection that closed timelike curves may be physically pathological in ways that undermine their philosophical weight, for instance if quantum effects prevent their formation, and whether the argument from possibility survives if nature turns out to forbid such universes after all. And I want one on the claimed Kantian upshot: whether establishing that time is mind-dependent in the relevant sense follows from the physics, or whether a realist about spacetime can simply absorb Gödel's results and say that time in rotating universes is merely stranger, not unreal. Each follow-up should anticipate the characteristic Gödelian reply -- for example, his insistence that a property of reality that fails to hold in a physically possible world is no property of reality at all -- and press it further rather than changing the subject.

Before drafting, adopt the persona of a philosopher of physics who specializes in Gödel's cosmological work and the scholarship on its reception, including Einstein's ambivalent response. First, briefly brainstorm -- in a few sentences -- which objections to Gödel's argument are strongest and which of his replies are most robust, so the questions track genuine pressure points rather than journalistic curiosities. Then produce the questions and follow-ups, numbered, each one phrased as I would actually ask it on air, and flag any question where the historically plausible answer is genuinely uncertain so I can prepare alternatives.
