I'm a novelist and I'm adapting the old Baghdad death tale for my next book — you know the one: a servant bumps into Death in the market, takes her startled gesture as a threat, borrows his master's horse and gallops off to Samarra, only for Death to remark to the master that she was merely surprised to see the servant in Baghdad, since she had an appointment with him that very night in Samarra. I mentioned the plot down the pub last week and two mates of mine went at it for nearly an hour. I kept schtum, but I genuinely need to know who was right, because the ending of my book sort of depends on it. Priya did philosophy at uni, Marcus writes thrillers. Roughly how it went:

Marcus opened: the servant did the only rational thing going — if Death looks surprised to see you, you leg it. The story is a tragedy of dramatic irony, not a riddle, and calling his flight irrational is just philosophers being clever.

Priya: it doesn't matter what you'd instinctively do. If Death keeps her appointments — if she flawlessly predicts where you'll be tonight — then riding to Samarra is no better than staying in Baghdad, and no worse; both roads end at the same appointment. So there is no rational choice available to him at all, and that, not the irony, is what's actually interesting.

Marcus: then rationality has come apart from blame, and I know which one I care about. The man is blameless whatever he does, and a theory that says a blameless man had nothing rational to do has gone wrong somewhere, not him. Anyway, flawless prediction and genuine choice can't both be real — if he truly could have stayed, Death truly could have been wrong.

Priya: prediction isn't puppetry. Death doesn't make him ride, she just forecasts the ride, and a forecast can be dead certain without forcing anyone's hand — same as I can be certain you'll order the usual. And blamelessness doesn't entail rationality: he's blameless precisely because nothing was available to him, not because his choice was sound.

Marcus: fine, but look at what actually happens in the tale — his flight is what gets him to Samarra. The prophecy fulfils itself through his attempt to dodge it, same as Oedipus. It's a story about evasion causing the thing evaded, not about prediction.

Priya: you've merged two different stories. In the Oedipus structure the evasion causes the fulfilment — a causal loop. Here the Samarra appointment predates the flight; Death's surprise shows she wasn't expecting him in Baghdad. His ride doesn't bring the appointment about, it just coincides with it. Fixed future, not feedback loop. If the tale is the second kind, his deliberation was genuinely futile; if it's the first kind, staying home breaks the loop and he should never have saddled the horse.

Adjudicate for me, and be commited about it — take a definite side at each step and say why, rather than laying out the options and leaving me to pick. I want exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up: one point per round of the argument saying who wins it, one on who wins overall, and the fifth on the one thing both of them are missing that I could build into the novel.
