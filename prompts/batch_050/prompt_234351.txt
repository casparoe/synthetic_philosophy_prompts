I teach perception at both the undergraduate and graduate level, and each year I return to the cognitive penetrability debate with a sense of diminishing returns. Since at least the late 1990s, one side has defended the claim that beliefs, desires, and expectations can genuinely alter perceptual experience itself -- citing effects like the apparent impact of racial categorization on face perception, of value judgments on perceived brightness, and of linguistic categories on color discrimination -- while the other side insists that every putative case can be redescribed as a change in attention, in spatial selection, in pre-perceptual input, or in post-perceptual judgment and memory rather than in perception proper. My sense is that the stock of experimental paradigms has grown considerably while the stock of arguments has not: each new result is met by the same repertoire of deflationary redescriptions, and each deflationary redescription is met by the accusation that it renders the anti-penetrability thesis unfalsifiable.

I would like a diagnosis of why this debate has stalled, not a verdict on who is right. In particular: is the disagreement fundamentally verbal, hinging on where one draws the boundary of 'perception' -- so that the two sides agree on all the empirical facts but label them differently? Is it a disagreement about standards of evidence, with one side requiring a kind of case (penetration with no attentional or judgmental confound) that may be impossible to produce in principle? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the real work, say about the functional architecture of the visual system, that neither party's armchair arguments can settle? Or are the sides simply answering different questions under one name -- one about the phenomenology of experience, the other about the informational encapsulation of early vision? I have my own suspicion, which I offer only as a hypothesis: that the impasse rests on the absence of any agreed criterion, independent of the penetration cases themselves, for distinguishing perceptual states from post-perceptual judgments, so that each side can classify the disputed cases to suit its theory. Please assess that hypothesis alongside the alternatives, grounding the diagnosis in what each side actually argues rather than in caricature, and conclude by telling me what would count as progress given your diagnosis -- a conceptual distinction, an experimental design, an argument that the question should be replaced -- or, if you think no progress should be expected, explain what structural feature of the disagreement guarantees that.
