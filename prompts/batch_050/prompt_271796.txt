I run a small philosophy-and-music discussion group (we meet in a pub, half of us are musicians and half are philosophers, plus one guy who refuses to commit to any position ever). Two members have been arguing about sad music for over a year now, and I want you to write the document they should have produced by now instead of just repeating themselves louder.

A bit of framing first: answer as a professional philosopher of aesthetics who also knows some music theory and is reasonably familiar with the psychology of musical emotion. Think carefully about this before writing anything final — it might help to brainstorm what the real cruxes are likely to be before you start drafting, and only then write the document.

Here are the two positions, in my words:

Member A holds that sad music makes us sad. When we call a piece sad we are reporting (or predicting) its power to arouse sadness in listeners, and the genuinely interesting question is why anyone would voluntarily seek that out.

Member B holds that music is sad roughly the way a slumped posture or a drooping face is sad: it expresses sadness without making anyone sad. On her view we hear the contour, tempo, register and dynamics as resembling how a sad person moves, speaks and breathes, and the music needn't arouse any sadness at all — indeed a seasoned listener can judge a piece utterly heartbreaking while feeling perfectly calm.

Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two. It should contain:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims — things both can sign without wincing. E.g. presumably that we do unhesitatingly describe instrumental music in emotion words, that at least some listeners report genuine felt sadness (tears, chills) in response to music, that people deliberately seek out sad music, and that within a given musical culture listeners' sadness-judgments about a piece are fairly robust. You decide what else belongs there and what doesn't actually survive scrutiny.

2. A disagreements table (markdown is fine): each disagreement stated in one precise sentence per row, with a column for how A would phrase her own view and a column for how B would phrase hers — each wording something that side would actually accept, no strawmen.

3. A cruxes section: at least three sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case or observation both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction about it in advance. The statement must handle these four cases, though you're welcome to add more:

- The dry-eyed connoisseur: a veteran listener who reliably identifies which pieces are sad, agrees with other listeners' ratings, and reports feeling nothing at all. Whose problem is this, and what does each side predict about how common such people are?
- The Inverted Funeral March: take the profile of a typical slow, low, descending dirge and re-render it fast, high, major and staccato. What does each side predict listeners will say about it, and what would it show if listeners still hear a trace of sadness in it?
- Chills and tears that arrive on passages which don't seem sad at all — a sudden harmonic shift, a loud climax, a soaring melody. Is that a counterexample to one of them?
- The Sad Synthesizer: a piece engineered to contain every feature theorists associate with sadness in music, which nonetheless leaves every test listener unmoved and unconvinced. Does this falsify either view, and if not, what would falsify them?

4. A short verdict on which parts of their disagreement you think are actually verbal — e.g. whether some of the fight dissolves once you distinguish two different senses in which a piece can be called sad — and which parts are genuine and survive.

Don't bother with citations or named authorities; the group wants something two actual stubborn people could sign, not a bibliography. Keep the tone of the document neutral even though I'm obviously on B's side.

Finally, add a brief note at the end (not part of the joint statement) saying how confident you are in the agreed-claims section — which of those claims are on solid ground and which are you somewhat guessing at — and single out the one point in the whole document you are least sure of, and why.
