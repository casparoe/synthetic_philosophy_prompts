I am a retired experimental physicist — forty years in low-temperature work — who came to philosophy only after leaving the laboratory, and I still keep notebooks. One of them has generated a question I cannot find treated in any of my reading, and I would like to know whether the thing it records already has a name.

The notebook. For nine years I have logged every instance in which a possession of mine was lost, broken, or stolen: the object, my distress that day (rated on a scale of my own devising), my written prediction of how I would feel about the loss six months later, and whether I replaced the object within a year at roughly its original cost. Three regularities have emerged. First, the immediate distress is genuine and often sharp — mislaying the fountain pen I had used daily since my postdoc cost me two nights' sleep. Second, my predictions are reliably wrong: I forecast durable sorrow and feel, six months on, almost nothing. Third, and most striking to me, I almost never replace the item — in fewer than a fifth of cases — even where an identical one is cheap and a telephone call away. Two observations serve as controls. Objects I deliberately gave away produced no distress at all, so the pain attaches to losing, not to not-having. And the one object I would have restored at almost any price — a bowl thrown by my late sister — is precisely the one for which replacement is impossible. The pain, in short, does not appear to measure any standing valuation; a standing valuation would announce itself in the disposition to reacquire. It looks like a pure cost of the event of loss, leaving no trace in my subsequent wants.

Reading Adam Smith's The Theory of Moral Sentiments (Part I, Section I, Chapter I, "Of Sympathy"), I came upon a sentence that seemed to explain my notebook better than anything in the economics I had been reading:

"For as to be in pain or distress of any kind excites the most excessive sorrow, so to conceive or to imagine that we are in it, excites some degree of the same emotion, in proportion to the vivacity or dulness of the conception."

Smith is describing sympathy with other people, but the mechanism he proposes seems indifferent to its target: at the moment of loss I vividly conceive the self that still has the pen, or the self that must now manage without it, and on Smith's account that vivid conception alone excites real emotion. Once the event recedes, the conception dulls, and the emotion — having never been a valuation — simply stops. I am aware that commentators on behavioral economics credit Smith with anticipating loss aversion, on the strength of his observation later in the book that a decline in our condition grieves us more intensely than an equivalent improvement gratifies us; but it is this earlier sentence, not that remark, that seems to me to do the explanatory work.

Three requests. First: does the configuration in my notebook have an established name? It is plainly adjacent to loss aversion, to the endowment effect, to hedonic adaptation, and to what I believe is called impact bias, yet it appears to be none of them — the first two describe the moment of comparison and are silent about the missing aftermath; adaptation predicts the fading of the grief but does not register that the initial pain never corresponded to a durable preference; impact bias concerns mispredictions, whereas my puzzle would remain even had I predicted correctly. If a name exists, give me the nearest concepts and state precisely where the phenomenon I describe departs from each; if none does, coin and define one, and mark its boundary cases — in particular, whether grief for a person is a limiting case of the same thing (reacquisition being impossible by nature) or something different altogether. Second: if Smith's mechanism is the correct explanation, does that settle, or merely sharpen, the dispute over whether loss aversion is a bias to be corrected or a legitimate preference to be respected? A feeling produced by the vivacity of an imagining rather than by the value of a thing sounds like a candidate bias, yet I can also hear the defense: a transient preference is still a preference. Third, as a laboratory man: what observation or experiment could, even in principle, decide between those two verdicts on first-person evidence of my kind — or is the distinction one that no such evidence could ever force? I will not be satisfied with a bare label in answer to the first question, nor with a both-sides summary of the second.
