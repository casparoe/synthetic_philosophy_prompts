For the final assignment in my course on rational choice and social justice, we've each been asked to submit a personal position statement on decision-making under uncertainty, and I want to stress-test mine before handing it in. Here are my commitments, as I'd defend them:

1. In decisions under genuine ignorance -- where no probabilities can be defensibly assigned -- maximin is the rational rule, and it's rationally required, not merely one permissible option among others.
2. Rawls's difference principle is the correct standard for the basic structure of society, and the original position argument succeeds precisely because the parties deliberate under ignorance, which makes maximin the appropriate rule for them.
3. Harsanyi's rival argument fails at its first step: behind the veil of ignorance there are no probabilities, and the principle of indifference doesn't license manufacturing a uniform probability distribution over positions you might occupy. So his case for average utilitarianism never gets going.
4. Where probabilities are available, subjective expected utility maximization is the right decision rule, and Allais-type preferences are straightforwardly irrational -- they violate the sure-thing principle with stated probabilities in hand, and that's just an error.
5. Minimax regret should be rejected as a decision rule because its verdicts are menu-dependent: adding a new option to the choice set can change which act minimizes maximum regret even though nothing about the original options has changed, and a rule whose verdicts depend on irrelevant alternatives can't track rationality.
6. Ellsberg-type preferences are rational. Preferring bets with known probabilities over bets with unknown ones isn't a bias; it's a reasonable response to ambiguity.
