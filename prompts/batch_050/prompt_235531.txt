For the final meeting of my graduate seminar on the philosophy of language, I would like to close with a villanelle about Davidson's project of radical interpretation, and I want the form to carry the argument rather than merely decorate it. The two refrains should be the two commitments the radical interpreter cannot set down: that the speaker is mostly right about the world, and that any theory of what the speaker's words mean must answer at every step to the evidence of what the speaker holds true, and under what observable circumstances. Their return in each tercet should enact the theory's central claim — that charity is not a heuristic the interpreter could discard once the language has been cracked, but the standing condition of there being a language to crack at all.

The body of the poem should work through the project in sequence: the opening tercets the scene of the interpreter armed with nothing but utterances and circumstances; the middle tercets the holism, the impossibility of fixing what a speaker believes independently of what their words mean, so that belief and meaning must be solved for together; the closing tercets triangulation, two creatures and a shared world as the only thing that could give utterance content in the first place. The final quatrain should land the conclusion that unsettles students: there is no inner code being guessed at, no fact of meaning over and above what the best charitable interpretation delivers.

Nineteen lines, strict villanelle form, no prose preface. It should be accurate enough that someone who works on Davidson would not object, and still a good poem to a reader who has never heard of him.
