A few years ago I came across Steven Pinker's argument in The Better Angels of Our Nature that the world has become dramatically less violent, and ever since I've watched intelligent people argue past each other about it. One side points to long-run declines in homicide rates, battlefield deaths, and practices like torture and public execution; the other replies that the whole claim hinges on contested choices — measuring deaths per capita rather than in absolute numbers, treating the world wars as statistical outliers rather than as evidence, and defining violence narrowly so that things like mass incarceration, colonial domination, or preventable poverty don't count. What I genuinely can't figure out is what kind of disagreement this is. Is there a fact of the matter about whether the world is becoming less violent that better data could settle, with the two sides simply disputing the numbers? Or are they really disagreeing about what should count as violence and which measure is morally relevant — in which case all the empirical work might be beside the point? Please diagnose the structure of the dispute rather than take a side in it, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a particular kind of evidence, or an honest admission that the question as posed has no single answer.
