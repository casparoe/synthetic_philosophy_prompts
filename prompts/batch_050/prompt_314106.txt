I've been arguing with my brother-in-law about this since Thanksgiving and I want to settle it properly. He designs the app for a credit union and recently added a feature where, if you try to wire a large amount of money to a brand-new payee late at night, the app makes you wait fifteen minutes and watch a short video about common scams before it lets you proceed. I said that was obviously fine, maybe even good design. He then admitted that the same app buries the "close your account" flow three menus deep and makes you talk to a chatbot first, and I said that was obviously sleazy. He asked me what the difference was, and I realized I couldn't say -- both are just friction inserted into a default path to change what people do. So I started collecting my gut verdicts on influence-y cases and I want someone to tell me what principle, if any, is underneath them, because I suspect my intuitions are a mess.

Here are the verdicts. (1) A cafeteria putting the fruit at eye level and the chips in the back: totally fine, doesn't even need a sign. (2) A state switching organ donation to opt-out instead of opt-in: fine, probably a great policy. (3) The credit union's scam-warning friction: fine. (4) The credit union's buried account-closure flow: not fine. (5) Graphic photos of diseased lungs on cigarette packs: fine. (6) A doctor deliberately exaggerating the risks of skipping a vaccine because she knows the patient is bad with probabilities and will make the "right" choice only if scared: not fine, even though the patient ends up healthier. (7) An employer enrolling people in the retirement plan by default with easy opt-out: fine. (8) An employer enrolling people by default in a program that donates 2% of each paycheck to the company's favorite charity, opt-out available but annoying: not fine, and it bugs me that I can't articulate why, since (2) is also a default set partly for the benefit of third parties. (9) A high school course that teaches students to recognize dark patterns and marketing manipulation so they can resist them: not just fine, but somehow better than the nudges, and I'd like to know if there's a real distinction there or if I'm just romantic about education.

So: is there one principle that captures all of these, or close? Some candidate axes I keep circling are whether the influencer benefits, whether the person could have noticed and reversed it, whether it works on reason or around it, and whether the person already endorsed the goal it pushes them toward -- but none of them seem to get everything, in particular (2) versus (4) and (5) versus (6) are killing me. Please state the best principle you can find precisely, with a clause for each case that motivates it, and be honest about which of my verdicts it has to throw out rather than contorting to save all nine. Then tell me what it predicts for two cases I genuinely can't call: a meditation app that notices I'm spiraling at 2am and quietly reshuffles my feed so I stop seeing the content that was winding me up (no disclosure, no opt-out I could find), and a thermostat that reports my energy use to my neighbors on a little leaderboard. If your principle says one of those is fine and that strikes you as a reductio, say that too.
