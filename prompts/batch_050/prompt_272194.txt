My study group came up with this last nigth and it seems to prove way too much, namely that demandingness objections are never a good reason to reject a moral theory, full stop. But the integrity objection felt like the most compelling thing in ethics when I first read it, so I figure something's off. Here's the argument:

(1) A moral theory is refuted if fully complying with it would, in some possible situation, require an agent to give up the people and projects that make her life worth living.
(2) In some possible situations consequentialism requires exactly that — give until you're nearly as badly off as the people you're helping, abandon your life's work for relief work, and so on.
(3) So consequentialism is refuted. (Fine so far, that's just the standard objection.)
(4) But the same holds for every moral theory with any content: the Kantian must not lie to the murderer at the door even when refusing costs her everything she loves, the contractualist must keep her promises even when keeping them ruins her, the virtue ethicist must stand her ground like the courageous person does even when standing there destroys her family. Pick any theory and I can cook up a gruesome enough scenario where compliance is crushing.
(5) So every moral theory is refuted, i.e. nothing is really morally required of anyone.
(6) But we're not error theorists — some things really are wrong.
(7) So (1) is false, and the sheer demandingness of a theory is never by itself a reason to reject it.

(7) can't be right, but each premise looks defensible on its own. Which step do I reject? I'd most like to hang on to (1) in some form, since without something like it I don't see how demandingness talk gets off the ground at all. Tell me why the bad step looked innocent, and what it costs to reject it instead of one of the others.
