I teach ancient philosophy and moral epistemology, and I am drafting an essay — provisionally titled Two Lives at a Time: Comparison as Calibration in Plutarch's Moral Epistemology — for an edited volume on exemplarity and moral education. Below is the current draft, followed by the specific kinds of comments I am seeking. Please engage as a demanding but constructive referee. Where you correct my claims about Plutarch, do so from your own knowledge of the Lives: I need the correction and, where possible, the relevant life or proem, not citations.

Draft:

1. The standard picture. Philosophers who defend moral learning from biography usually argue that rules and principles underdetermine action, that exemplars fill the gap, and that narrative lets us rehearse the exemplar's deliberations. Plutarch is the tradition's patron. In the proem to the Alexander he announces that he is writing biography rather than history, and that a small matter — a phrase, a jest — can disclose character where pitched battles and great sieges do not. In the proem to the Aemilius Paullus and Timoleon he says he began the Lives for the sake of others but continued them for his own, treating the record of the past as a mirror in which to shape his conduct. On the standard picture, the life is a transparent container of moral evidence and the reader an extractor.

2. The compression problem. A life is not evidence until someone has compressed it. From a continuous stream of conduct the biographer selects a handful of anecdotes, and the selection is guided by a prior conception of what character is and which of its expressions are revealing. Plutarch's own manifesto makes the point vivid: his method is to hunt the small, character-disclosing detail, which means a detail enters the record because it already fits a theory of disclosiveness. In philosophy of science we say observation is theory-laden; for biography I want to claim something stronger, which I call constitutive selection. The theory does not merely color data that exist independently; it determines which data exist at all. The reader who believes she is deriving virtue from the evidence of lives is often receiving the biographer's moral taxonomy laundered through the archive.

3. The calibration thesis. The essay's main claim: Plutarch's distinctive contribution is not the life but the pair. Most of the Parallel Lives couple a Greek with a Roman and close with a formal comparison. A one-point measurement cannot be checked; a two-point measurement externalizes the instrument's axes. When the same biographer applies his selection principles to two lives and then weighs them against each other, his criteria of selection become visible as criteria. The surviving comparisons often redistribute praise and blame in ways the narratives alone would not predict, and sometimes expose the instrument's biases, as when the scales seem pressed in one partner's favor. So the pair functions as a calibration device: the reader learns not only what Plutarch concluded but how his evaluative instrument behaves, and can correct for it. The true unit of moral instruction in the Lives is the comparison, not the individual biography.

4. Hagiography as the degenerate case. The saints' lives of late antiquity and the medieval period are the limiting case on the other side: a single subject, maximal compression toward the ideal, no comparison, and a selection principle that admits only the edifying. On my account, hagiography is not bad biography because it idealizes — Plutarch idealizes, and he is regularly accused of leniency toward his subjects — but because it suppresses the calibration signal. A reader of Athanasius' Life of Antony receives the instrument's reading with no way to inspect the instrument. The hagiographer's reply is obvious: idealization is the point, since exemplars must be better than actual people if they are to pull us upward, and an exemplar corrected for bias pulls less hard. I want to take this seriously, because Plutarch's own justification for including vicious subjects — in the proem to the Demetrius he compares them, if memory serves, to the drunken helots the Spartans displayed to their sons so the young would learn by looking what to avoid — shows that he too thought exemplars teach simply by being contemplated.

5. Objection: the curation regress. Comparison relocates the compression problem rather than solving it. Plutarch chose the pairings; Demetrius stands beside Antony because the theatrical parallels served the lesson; a hagiographer could pair two saints and produce a formally identical comparison with no epistemic gain. My reply: the regress is real but not vicious, because each explicit comparison leaks criteria. A paired hagiography would still tell us what its author takes sanctity to consist in, and would give the reader two data series against which to see it. Calibration does not require an unbiased instrument, only an instrument whose bias is expressed in its output. Still, I concede the gains diminish when paired subjects are selected to agree.

6. Diagonal reading. The pedagogical upshot: readers should manufacture comparisons the biographer did not supply — read Plutarch's Antony against the hostile Augustan tradition that survives elsewhere; read Alcibiades against Nicias, his rival in the Sicilian debate, rather than against Coriolanus, his assigned partner; read a saint's life alongside a contemporary critic. Call this diagonal reading: the reader performs the calibration rather than inheriting it. If the calibration thesis is right, this is not an optional exercise for the suspicious but the primary way biography teaches.

Comments requested:

1. Accuracy. Flag anything in my claims about Plutarch's text or practice that is wrong, overstated, or under-hedged — the characterization of the proems, the claim that the comparisons redistribute verdicts, the helots anecdote, the alleged leniency. Name the relevant life where you can.

2. The calibration thesis. Does the two-point-measurement analogy earn its keep, or is it decoration over the banal advice to compare sources? Give the strongest version of the curation regress you can, and tell me whether my diminishing-gains concession in section 5 actually answers it.

3. The compression problem. Is constitutive selection genuinely stronger than ordinary theory-ladenness, or a relabeling? If the difference is real, is it distinctive of biography, or does it hold of any curated archive — clinical case studies, judicial opinions, anthropological fieldnotes — and would that generality weaken or strengthen my argument?

4. Hagiography. Is the contrast too tidy? Have I conceded enough to the reply that exemplars must exceed reality to be motivating? Consider whether idealization itself might carry a calibration signal of a different kind — and whether section 4 can survive that possibility.

5. Referee's report. Close with the two or three objections a hostile referee would lead with and one structural suggestion for the revision. If some part of the argument closely tracks an existing position in the scholarship, note the resemblance in general terms; do not invent a reference.
