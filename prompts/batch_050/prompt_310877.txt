I'm in an environmental ethics reading group (I'm an engineer by training, so I'm the token non-philosopher), and this month we're doing a chapter from a popular book on climate ethics. The chapter introduces Derek Parfit's "nonidentity problem," and I want to check whether the book's presentation is trustworthy before I repeat any of it in discussion, because the conclusion it draws strikes me as suspiciously convenient. Here's the author's presentation, as best I can reconstruct it: When we choose between long-term policies — say, between aggressively decarbonizing now and continuing with business as usual — our choice affects not just the conditions future people live in but which people come to exist at all, since different policies lead to different people meeting, conceiving at different times, and so on. So the people born under the worse policy could not have existed under the better one. Since they exist, and their lives are (we may suppose) worth living, they are not worse off than they would have been under the better policy — under that policy, they'd never have existed at all. Therefore nobody in the future is harmed by our choosing the worse policy. And from this, the author draws the lesson that the nonidentity problem "defuses" much of the moral urgency of climate change: our real obligations concern people who already exist or will exist regardless of what we do, and the rhetoric of "harming future generations" rests on a conceptual confusion that Parfit exposed.

The author is a respected science writer rather than a philosopher, and the chapter reads as confident and careful — there's no obvious blunder I can put my finger on. But something about the move from "no particular future person is made worse off" to "so the moral stakes of climate policy are lower than advertised" feels like a sleight of hand, and I half-remember from a podcast that Parfit himself treated the nonidentity problem as disturbing rather than as good news.

So: is this a fair summary of Parfit's argument and its implications? Please separate (a) what is an accurate statement of the reasoning, (b) what is a common simplification or a step that goes beyond what Parfit argued, and (c) anything Parfit himself explicitly rejected. In particular, I'd like to know whether the conclusion about climate obligations survives once the summary is corrected, what resources Parfit thought we have for condemning the worse policy even if no identifiable person is harmed, and whether the distinction between harming someone and wronging them (or making things worse, impersonally speaking) does any work here. If the popular version conflates claims that the philosophical literature keeps apart, please say so plainly — I'd rather be corrected now than repeat a misreading in front of the group.
