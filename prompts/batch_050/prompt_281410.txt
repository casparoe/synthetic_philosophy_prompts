You are a philosopher of mind with particular expertise in the philosophy of pain and the metaphysics of value, and I would like you to take on a careful design task for me. I am putting together the materials for a graduate seminar on the badness of pain, and I have become dissatisfied with the way the standard clinical cases are used in the literature. Patients with pain asymbolia, or patients who have undergone certain frontal lobe procedures or are under morphine, reportedly say things like: the sensation is there and it is recognizably pain, but it does not bother them. The trouble is that these reports are contested, the patients often have confounding deficits, and philosophers on every side simply read their preferred verdict off the case. So instead of leaning on real clinical reports, I want a controlled fictional case built to order, where the relevant variables are cleanly separated and the verdicts have to be derived rather than assumed.

Please construct a thought experiment meeting the following specifications. (1) The subject experiences a sensation whose phenomenal character is stipulated to be introspectively indistinguishable from an ordinary, moderately severe pain, and the case must be designed so that this stipulation is earned rather than cheap: the subject should be competent, sincere, attentive, and able to make fine-grained comparisons, and you should build in some feature of the scenario (a procedure, a comparison protocol, a history) that would silence the suspicion that the subject is merely confused about what ordinary pain feels like. (2) The motivational profile must be cleanly separable from the phenomenology: the case should be constructed so that a phenomenal theory of pain's badness (pain is bad because of how it feels, full stop) and a conative or motivational theory (pain is bad in virtue of the intrinsic aversion it involves, the built-in desire that it cease) deliver opposing verdicts, and a third theory of your choosing -- an evaluativist view, a desire-based theory of well-being, or an attitudinal hedonism -- delivers a verdict that comes apart from both. (3) Keep the scenario to one paragraph, clinically plausible in broad outline, with no exotic physics and no stipulating the verdicts: the dissociation has to fall out of the setup. (4) Before writing the final scenario, brainstorm briefly -- sketch two or three candidate designs, note where each fails, and then present the one that survives.

After the scenario, do the analytical work. State the verdict each of the three theories gives and derive it from the theory, not from your own intuitions about the case. Identify the single design feature that is doing the work of separating the verdicts, and tell me what happens if that feature is removed: does the case collapse back into an ordinary pain, and if so, along which dimension? Then give the strongest counterattack available to the theory that fares worst -- for instance, the phenomenal theorist's move that either the phenomenal character is not really intact or that the case trades on conflating the badness of pain with the subject's caring about it -- and assess whether that counterattack succeeds or merely relocates the disagreement. Finally, a candid verdict of your own: does the case, if it works, show that aversion is necessary for pain's badness, or only that it is necessary for pain's mattering to the subject, and is there a real difference between those two claims?
