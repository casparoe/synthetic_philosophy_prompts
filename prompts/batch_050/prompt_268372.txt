Next semester I'm running an undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of mathematics (14 students, one 90-minute session per week), and we're about to spend two weeks on Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations and the claim that mathematics is quasi-empirical -- that theorems get refined through a dialectic of proof, counterexample, and revised proof rather than being established once and for all by a watertight deduction. My students are mostly math majors, which is precisely the problem: they have been trained to see a finished proof as the end of inquiry, and when they read Lakatos they tend to nod along at the history while privately thinking that real, modern, rigorous mathematics doesn't work like that anymore. I want them to feel, in their own decisions, how a seemingly solid result can force you to choose between protecting the conjecture and protecting the proof.

Design me a session activity that stages this without any lecture beforehand. My rough idea, which you should develop or replace as you see fit: groups work with the relationship between the vertices, edges, and faces of polyhedra -- first arriving at a conjecture themselves, then being handed, one at a time, objects that stress it (e.g., a polyhedron with a hole through it, one built from two solids touching at a single vertex, one with an interior cavity), so that each group must decide at each stage whether to exclude the object as not really the kind of thing the conjecture was about, to shrink the conjecture's scope, or to go back and find which hidden assumption in their own reasoning did the damage. Give me concrete materials (exactly which objects to prepare, with their V, E, F data, so the arithmetic is right), the sequence in which to hand them out, and a scoring system with points or tokens that makes the strategic dilemma real -- I want it to be genuinely tempting to save points by redefining the problem away, because I suspect that temptation is itself the most Lakatosian thing in the room.

The debrief should map specific moments of the session onto Lakatos's repertoire of responses to counterexamples (monster-barring, exception-barring, lemma incorporation, the emergence of proof-generated concepts), naming these only after the fact, and should connect what happened to the quasi-empirical claim: in what sense did the groups' theorem end up better, and in what sense did it end up merely narrower? Also tell me what will predictably go wrong -- for instance, mathematically sophisticated students who know Euler's formula in advance and skip the conjecture stage, or groups that resolve every counterexample by definitional fiat -- and how to handle each. Finally, be frank about which feature of Lakatos's account the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply by talking: I suspect the historical timescale and the way genuine refutations arise from within the proof's own machinery, rather than being handed to you by a facilitator holding a card, are candidates, but tell me what you think is lost and how to compensate.
