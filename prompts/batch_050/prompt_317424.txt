I am a graduate student in philosophy presenting a paper next month at our department's early modern workshop, and I would like you to help me survive the question period. You should take on the role of an experienced historian of early modern philosophy with secondary training in the history of mathematics — someone who has sat through many such workshops and knows which questions sink a paper and which ones can be turned to the speaker's advantage. Think carefully about my audience before drafting anything, and feel free to brainstorm objections before selecting the final list.

The paper argues that Thomas Hobbes's notorious mathematical crusades — his repeated attempts to square the circle, his refusal of Euclid's definitions of point and line, his insistence in De Corpore that a line is the path traced by a moving body rather than a breadthless length, and his twenty-year print war with John Wallis — are usually treated as the crank hobby of a great philosopher past his prime, but should instead be read as his metaphysics doing exactly what it was built to do. My central exhibit is a passage from Chapter IV of Leviathan, "Of Speech," where Hobbes, attacking the scholastic vocabulary of abstractions, writes: "there being nothing in the world Universall but Names; for the things named, are every one of them Individuall and Singular." In the same breath he mocks the Schoolmen's coinages "Entity, Intentionality, Quiddity, and other insignificant words." My claim is that the Euclidean point and line are precisely the sort of abstraction this passage rules out: if nothing exists but individual, singular bodies, then there are no extensionless points and no breadthless lengths for geometry to be about, and geometry must be reconstructed as the science of magnitudes generated by motion — which is just what Hobbes spent his later decades doing. On my reading, the Wallis dispute was not a senile mathematician refusing to admit error but a genuine clash over what mathematics is about, and Hobbes lost the mathematical battle while fighting a coherent philosophical war. I came to this topic through a seminar on the reception of Euclid in the seventeenth century, where I noticed that the standard story simply skips from Descartes's analytic geometry to Newton without asking why Hobbes, a serious intellect, dug in his heels so completely.

Here is the audience: mostly early modern philosophy specialists, including two genuine Hobbes scholars and several Descartes and Leibniz people; plus two historians of mathematics from our sister department who attend everything and revere Euclid; no one present works on seventeenth-century mathematics full time. Everyone in the room will know that Hobbes claimed to have squared the circle and that Wallis demolished him in print; the mathematicians will know the quadrature was doomed on internal grounds, not merely in hindsight. Please prepare my Q&A sheet: the eight or so objections I should actually expect from this specific audience — not generic objections to historical theses — ordered by how much damage each would do if I answered badly, each phrased the way the person raising it would phrase it (the Hobbes specialist, the historian of mathematics, the generalist). For each objection, give me what I should concede up front, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and a candid note on where that reply is soft. I particularly want to know whether the Leviathan passage can bear the weight I am putting on it, given that its target is scholastic universals rather than mathematical objects, and whether the entailment I claim — from nominalism and materialism to a specifically kinematic geometry — is one the audience will grant or one they will force me to defend as an interpretation of Hobbes rather than a mere inference from him. Finally, tell me the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and how I should answer it if someone does.
