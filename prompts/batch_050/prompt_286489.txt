My uncle is a lawyer and over dinner he argued that Gödel's incompleteness theorems settle an old debate in jurisprudence: no legal system can be both complete and consistent, so there will always be cases the law can't decide, or the law will contradict itself somewhere. He treats the legal system as a formal system -- statutes are the axioms, precedents are the theorems, judges are the proof procedure. A philosopher friend told me afterwards that this is a category error, because Gödel's theorem only bites on systems strong enough to do arithmetic, and the law isn't one. Can you actually audit the analogy rather than just side with my friend? Give me the correspondence table -- what maps onto axioms, inference rules, provability, the Gödel sentence -- then go through the theorem's real preconditions one by one and say which a legal system plausibly meets and which it clearly fails (I'd guess the fixed-and-effective axioms bit and the consistency bit are trouble, but tell me). Then the part I actually care about: my uncle's conclusion, that undecidable cases are unavoidable, might be right even if Gödel doesn't deliver it -- so say whether it survives on other grounds, and if there's a better analogy or argument that captures what he's reaching for, give me that instead.
