I work in the risk office of a regional infrastructure authority, and part of my job is to give a preliminary ethics screening on proposals that would expose the public to new risks nobody signed up for: rerouting freight traffic through residential streets, permitting delivery drones over playgrounds, siting a solvent-storage depot a kilometer from a school, raising the speed limit on a road with cyclists. My screening isn't the final word -- an engineering review and a board vote come later -- but if I flag a proposal as ethically defective it usually dies, so I carry real weight, and right now I exercise it by gut feel. I recently got pushback from a project manager who pointed out, correctly, that nearly everything we approve imposes some nonzero risk on someone who never consented, so "someone could be hurt who didn't agree to this" can't by itself be a reason to block anything. I conceded the point and then realized I had no principled answer to the follow-up: fine, so which risk impositions are permissible and which aren't, and how do you tell before the fact rather than after the lawsuit?

Please write me a decision procedure I can actually run on a proposal in about an hour, grounded in what moral and political philosophy has to say about imposing risk without consent, and honest about how much of the theory survives translation into a checklist. It should be a sequence of questions in order, with what counts as an adequate answer to each, and clear stopping points: where the procedure terminates in an automatic no, where it terminates in a pass, and where it explicitly hands the question to a human judgment that no checklist can settle -- I want those handoff points flagged rather than papered over. The procedure needs to grapple with the distinctions the literature treats as load-bearing, not just gesture at them: setting and justifying a de minimis threshold when any positive number is contestable; whether it matters that the risk-bearers are also the beneficiaries (the neighborhood gets the faster deliveries); whether notice-plus-opportunity-to-avoid is any kind of substitute for consent, and when avoidance is in practice impossible; how to handle cumulative risk, where each proposal is trivially small but the background total is not; whether concentrated risks on a few should be weighed differently from diffuse risks on many; whether a standing offer of compensation after the fact does any justificatory work before the fact; and the reciprocity thought that we all impose small risks on each other constantly and mostly get away with it. Where two respectable theories would run the procedure differently -- say, a reasonable-rejectability test versus a straightforward expected-harm ceiling -- say so and tell me which one to use by default and why, rather than averaging them into mush.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show the working: (1) the freight rerouting, which cuts logistics costs for the whole region but adds roughly two expected pedestrian deaths per decade on a street that is mostly low-income and has no alternate route; (2) the delivery drones, which pose a very small risk of serious head injury to a very large number of park users, none of whom benefit much; and (3) the solvent depot, where the risk is tiny, the school cannot realistically relocate, but the operator offers an escrowed compensation fund and the town's tax base badly needs the facility. At least one of these should be a case where the procedure returns a verdict I will find uncomfortable in either direction -- approve something that feels wrong, or kill something that feels obviously fine -- and when it does, don't soften it; explain whether the discomfort is the procedure failing or my intuition failing, and how I'd tell the difference. Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of proposals it will systematically get wrong, and what I should do when one of those lands on my desk.
