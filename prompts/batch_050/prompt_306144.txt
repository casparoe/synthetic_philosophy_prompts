For the past two years I have served on the professional-standards panel of a mid-sized underwriting firm, and I have been asked to draft the panel's written opinion in a case we must resolve at our next meeting. The timing is awkward, because only last weekend I spent an entire evening arguing about the underlying issue with an old friend, and I came away unable to fully answer his position. I will describe the case, then what I need.

One of our senior risk analysts publishes daily probability assessments for discrete events our clients care about -- storms making landfall before a date, court rulings, defaults -- and standing contracts are priced off his published numbers. Over the past season, a junior trader noticed that these numbers routinely failed to hang together: mutually exclusive and exhaustive outcomes were assigned probabilities summing to well over one, and on several occasions a specific outcome was assigned a higher probability than a broader outcome that contains it. The trader quietly assembled combinations of contracts at the analyst's own implied prices such that the firm would lose money no matter what happened, and by the time compliance caught the pattern the desk had lost a real but modest sum. Notably, no one disputes the analyst's individual judgment calls as forecasts; several of his one-off predictions were excellent. His defense before the panel is that his numbers were sincere expert judgments, that arithmetical tidiness is a demand of betting markets rather than of good judgment, that the loss flows from the firm's own rule letting contracts trade at his published prices, and that sanctioning him means punishing him for being bet against rather than for being wrong about anything.

My friend's position, pressed over several hours, was essentially the analyst's: being so situated that someone can construct a guaranteed profit against you shows at most that you are an incautious person to trade with, not that your beliefs are defective. I disagreed but could not give a satisfactory account of why the vulnerability reflects on the beliefs themselves and not merely on the bettor.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate to the panel, roughly 700 to 900 words, in formal memo style with the bottom line stated up front. It must recommend one of our three available dispositions -- a formal finding of deficient professional judgment, no sanction with a recommendation that the firm change its pricing rule, or required retraining and a monitoring period -- and it must do several things beyond the recommendation: state the general standard the panel should apply when an expert's stated numbers are internally inconsistent but individually defensible; explain why susceptibility to a guaranteed loss bears, or fails to bear, on the quality of the underlying judgments rather than merely on trading prudence, giving the best version of my friend's objection before answering it; distinguish, if any distinction is warranted, between probabilities that fail to sum to one and probabilities that violate containment, since the panel will ask whether these are equally serious; and address whether blame, if any, is shared by the trader or by the firm's pricing rule. The panel includes non-specialists, so the reasoning should not lean on technical vocabulary or named authorities; it should rest on considerations an intelligent lay member can weigh.
