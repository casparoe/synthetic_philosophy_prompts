I'm writing a science column and I keep catching myself using the movie-screen picture of consciousness: light hits the retina, the signal gets processed, and then at some point it gets "shown" on a screen in the brain where *you* -- the audience of one -- watch it and react. I know this is the picture Dennett spent a whole book attacking as the "Cartesian theater," and that his alternative is the multiple drafts model, where there's no single place or moment where the show plays, just competing drafts of what's happening getting revised all over the brain, and probing the system at different times gives you different answers about what was experienced.

Here's my problem. Every popular explanation I can find uses the theater picture to *explain* the attack (here's the naive view, now watch Dennett demolish it), and then swaps in multiple drafts as the fix. But the theater analogy keeps sneaking back in whenever I try to actually write about, say, delay in perception, or change blindness, or the fact that you can confidently report a scene that wasn't there. So audit the analogy for me:

1. Give me the full correspondence table for the theater picture as people actually use it -- what plays the role of the screen, the audience, the projectionist, the showtime, the moment the lights go down. Include the parts people don't say out loud, like where in the brain the audience is supposed to be sitting.

2. Where exactly does it break? I want the specific disanalogies that do philosophical work -- the homunculus regress, the assumption that there's a finishing line where processing becomes experience, the "what happens before it reaches the audience is unconscious, after is conscious" cut -- not just pedantic ones like "brains don't have seats."

3. Here's the part I really need: is multiple drafts itself just a better analogy with its own smuggled cargo? Drafts implies an editor and a final published version, and Dennett insists there's neither. So tell me what the drafting metaphor imports that the theory disowns, and whether the model collapses without the metaphor or just gets harder to explain.

4. Finally: the conclusion I want to write is "there's no single moment when a perception becomes conscious, so your intuition that you either saw the thing or you didn't is wrong." Does the analogy audit actually support that, or is the theater picture so baked into how I framed the question that I can't get the conclusion out without it? If there's a better analogy for a general audience -- something that captures the no-single-showtime point without a screen or an editor -- propose one and show it handling the change blindness case.

Casual is fine, and feel free to tell me the column premise itself is confused if it is.
