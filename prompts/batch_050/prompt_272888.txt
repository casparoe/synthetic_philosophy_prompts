Luck egalitarians compensate for brute luck but not option luck, and they typically mark the difference by asking whether the disadvantage traces back to a choice the agent could have avoided. Construct a thought experiment that pressures this test at its weakest joint: a single realistic scenario (domestic, medical, or occupational; no trolleys, no amnesia, no stipulated demons) in which two people make the same choice, face the same risks, and suffer the same bad outcome, yet luck egalitarianism must compensate one and not the other -- where the only difference between them is the unchosen origin of the appetite for risk that produced the choice (say, one was raised to be bold and the other became bold through their own past decisions). After the scenario, show the work: state the verdict the theory delivers for each person and why, identify exactly which feature of the case forces the divergent verdicts, and say whether that feature is one the theory is committed to treating as relevant or merely one it has not ruled out. Then give the luck egalitarian's best two moves for resisting the case (for instance, folding preference formation into the option-luck ledger, or restricting compensation to outcomes rather than dispositions) and say what each move costs the theory elsewhere. Maximum 700 words.
