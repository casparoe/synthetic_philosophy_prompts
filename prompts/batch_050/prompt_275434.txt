I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm currently drafting a long feature on a deceptively ordinary question: what entitles someone to say something flat-out, rather than hedge it, qualify it, or stay silent? The reporting has left me with a notebook full of little episodes -- some from interviews, some from my own newsroom and dinner parties -- where my judgments about what was properly said don't line up in any obvious way. I'd like to know whether a single principle about the norm of assertion sits underneath them, and if not, which of my verdicts I should give up.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. (1) A colleague announces "The 8:15 is cancelled," having read it off the departure board a minute earlier: plainly proper. (2) Before the draw is announced, someone tells a ticket-holder, "Your ticket didn't win," purely on the odds: improper, even though it's 99.99% likely and will almost certainly turn out true. (3) A friend says "There's a pharmacy on the corner" from a hazy memory she'd have admitted was a guess; there is one: improper, despite being true. (4) A doctor tells a patient "Your results are negative," but the file she's reading was swapped with someone else's; by luck the patient really is fine, and any competent physician holding that file would have said the same: I find this proper -- blameless -- even though her grounds don't connect to the fact. (5) A manager asserts "The candidate we're hiring drives a Saab," reasoning from her settled belief that Jones will be hired and that Jones drives a Saab; in fact Smith gets the job, but Smith, unbeknownst to anyone, drives a Saab: improper, despite being true, justified, and believed. (6) "It's raining, but I don't believe it is": incoherent to assert even though it could, in principle, be true. (7) "I think the 8:15 is cancelled" or "As far as I know, it's cancelled": proper in exactly the situations where the flat-out version wouldn't be, so hedging seems to buy something. (8) Passing along what a scrupulously reliable source told me, without flagging it as secondhand: generally proper, even though the evidence sits with them, not me.

Two pairs in particular resist reconciliation. Cases (2) and (4) both involve probabilistically excellent grounds that fall short of certainty, yet I condemn the first and excuse the second. Cases (4) and (5) both involve a speaker whose grounds fail to connect to the truth in the right way, yet I excuse one and condemn the other. So: is there a coherent norm of assertion -- knowledge, truth, justified belief, something more exotic like being in a position to know, or a standard that shifts with the stakes of the conversation -- that captures most of this pattern? Please state your candidate principle precisely, note which case motivates each clause of it, and test it against three situations I haven't formed a verdict on: a weather forecaster asserting tomorrow's high on the basis of a model; a courtroom witness who sincerely misremembers; and a researcher asserting a result in a paper whose data she collected but never personally re-checked. And be honest about which of my eight verdicts your principle forces me to abandon, rather than bending the principle to save them all.
