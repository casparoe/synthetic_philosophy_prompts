So I run a small reading group on 19th century political economy and we keep getting stuck on the same fight: what on earth was late Mill's actual position on socialism? Next month we're doing the posthumous Chapters on Socialism, and I want to hand out a decision tree that sorts people into the main interpretive camps by what they make of the text, not by which label they find fashionable. Act as a historian of economic thought who has taught Mill for years, and build me that tree.

The tree should start from a passage we're going to anchor the whole session on (I'm paraphrasing since I don't have the book in front of me): in one of the later chapters, Mill takes up the standard objection that under a system of common ownership and equal distribution nobody would have any motive to exert themselves, and he pushes back by arguing that this objection overestimates how much present-day effort is really driven by personal gain and underestimates other springs of human action. So the first branch should be something like: do you read this as Mill endorsing the socialist arrangement, or as him steelmanning it before rejecting it on other grounds? From there, the branches should split on the questions that genuinely divide interpreters: whether his enthusiasm for worker cooperatives is a way station to full socialism or the actual endpoint (i.e. competition kept, capitalists gone); whether the apparent tension with his earlier defenses of competition and private property is a real change of mind, a change in emphasis only, or evidence of someone else's influence on the manuscript; and whether the unfinished, fragmentary state of the Chapters means no stable view can be extracted at all. Phrase every node as a question about the text that someone could answer without knowing which camp it feeds into.

The leaves I definitely want included: Mill as basically still a liberal whose socialist-sounding passages are aspirational rhetoric; Mill as a genuine liberal or market socialist whose end-state is an economy of competing worker co-ops; Mill as a convert whose late views contradict his earlier self; and the deflationary reading that the fragment is too incomplete to pin him down at all. Add any camp I've missed, note which combinations of answers nobody occupies and give a one-sentence diagnosis of why (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored), and flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate rather than fudging it. Each leaf should also get one sentence on the piece of textual evidence that makes that reading awkward. Aim for maybe six to eight branch nodes total, and feel free to brainstorm about the structure before you commit to it -- the exact ordering of the questions matters more then you might think for a handout like this.
