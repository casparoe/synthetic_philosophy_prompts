My daughter is being ordained as a minister next month, and I have been asked to speak for five minutes at the dinner afterward. The complication is that I am an atheist of a fairly specific kind: I am persuaded by the naturalistic study of religion, the view that religious belief and practice are products of ordinary human cognitive and social machinery — agency detection running ahead of the evidence, theory of mind, coalitional psychology, the social functions of ritual and costly commitment — rather than responses to anything supernatural. My daughter knows this. We have argued about it, affectionately, for a decade, and she chose her path with her eyes open about what I think.

The audience will be mixed in the worst way: her new congregation, most of whom know my views and some of whom are wary of me; her seminary friends, who will hear every dodge; my wife, who just wants me to be kind; and my daughter herself. Please write the speech.

Two hard constraints. First, I will not say anything I take to be false — no suggestion that God called her, that I now sense something she senses, that this day is blessed in any literal register. Second, I will not condescend: nothing that frames her vocation as a beautiful illusion, a socially useful mistake, or a noble fiction I am graciously permitting her. The first constraint rules out the easy warm things to say; the second rules out the easy honest ones.

What I am hoping for is a speech in which the naturalistic view does the structural work rather than being hidden or apologized for — one that finds, inside that view, something I can genuinely and specifically admire about what she is doing, stated in terms I actually believe, and that lands for her congregants without requiring them to share my premises and without asking me to borrow theirs. If part of the difficulty is that the naturalistic description of religion is one no religious person experiences their religion as, address that in the speech rather than around it.
