I'm putting together the ethics-of-deception week for my undergraduate seminar, and I keep running into the same wall. My students all come in with the strong intuition that lying is worse than merely misleading, and I want to force them to articulate what a theory of that difference would even have to look like before I let them defend one. So here's what I want from you: write out the desiderata that any adequate account of the (purported) moral difference between lying and misleading has to satisfy -- the cases it has to deliver a verdict on, the distinctions it can't just collapse, the facts about language and communication it has to stay consistent with -- with the motivation behind each requirement, and flag where you suspect two desiderata can't be jointly satisfied, because honestly that would be the most interesting result.

A few things to seed your thinking, since I've been collecting problem cases for years. There's the bald-faced lie, where both parties know nobody is deceived (the student who's obviously been absent insisting they were in class) -- it looks like a lie but deceives no one, so is the wrong of lying really about deception at all? There's paltering: answering with literally true statements designed to produce a false belief ("the car runs great," knowing the transmission is shot). There are cases where the misleading seems *worse* than the equivalent lie -- stringing someone along with technically-true statements so they reach the false conclusion through their own inference feels like it recruits the victim into their own deception, and any theory that says misleading is always the lesser wrong has to eat that. There's testimony under oath and advertising copy, where "technically true" seems to earn you nothing morally. And there's the background question of whether the theory is allowed to lean entirely on the linguistic saying/implicating distinction, or whether that's just relocating the moral question rather than answering it -- because surely the moral status of a boundary in pragmatics isn't self-justifying.

For each desideratum, tell me the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a prejudice of one camp, and mark which ones you think are genuinely non-negotiable versus negotiable. Then tell me which single requirement does the most work in eliminating popular positions (including the deflationary one that there is no moral difference at all and the whole debate is a mistake). If it turns out the desiderata an ordinary-intuition-respecting theory would need are jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly and show the collision. Please write in American English, and feel free to use a compact table for the list itself if that helps, but the why-behind-each-requirement should be prose.
