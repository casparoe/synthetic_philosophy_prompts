Two winters ago I helped distribute donated supplies in my town after a flood, and I have been turning over the same question ever since: when there is not enough to go around, should I help my own people first, or should a stranger's need count exactly as much as my cousin's? A friend who reads old Chinese philosophy told me I had stumbled into an ancient disagreement -- Mozi's doctrine of impartial care, where everyone is to be valued equally regardless of kinship, against the Confucian view that love is properly graded, strongest toward family and radiating outward in weakening circles like ripples from a stone. I do not want a lecture on who was right. I want to feel the weight of the choice. So I would like you to write me a short story, roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, that dramatizes this conflict.

Here is the setting to build from, though you may adjust details freely. A river valley town keeps a communal emergency granary, stocked by every household in good years and opened only in bad ones. The protagonist -- a widowed woman who has been elected keeper of the granary keys -- must manage it through a winter in which a fever crosses the valley and the grain runs short. The town has a rotation list, drawn up in peacetime, that determines the order in which households receive grain; it is deliberately blind to who is related to whom. But the keeper also holds personal discretion over a small reserve for emergencies the list cannot foresee. Across the story, give her three escalating decisions: one early and easy, one in the middle where following the list means letting someone she loves go hungry, and one at the end where both the list and her heart seem to point toward something she cannot bear. Include a character who defends the rotation list as the only form impartial care can take once real people with real favorites are involved, and a character who argues that love thinned out evenly over everyone is no longer love at all. Neither may be a fool or a villain; each should be right about something the other is wrong about, and each principle should exact a visible, concrete cost when actually lived rather than merely argued.

A few requirements. Write in close third person from the keeper's perspective. Do not let any character cite ancient philosophers or use terms like "Mohist" or "Confucian" -- the ideas should live entirely inside the situation. The ending must land on an actual decision made under pressure, not a conveniently arranged compromise in which the fever breaks or new grain arrives just in time. End the piece with a final line in exactly this form -- \answer{impartial care}, \answer{graded love}, or \answer{unresolved} -- stating which principle the story's final decision vindicates, if either.
