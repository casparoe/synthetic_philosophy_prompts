Last week, a long-running code review thread at my company ended with a colleague writing, in block capitals for emphasis, "meanings just ain't in the head!" — a slogan I later learned comes from Hilary Putnam's 1975 paper "The Meaning of 'Meaning'." The dispute was about a pure function in our codebase. I had claimed that what the function "does" is fully fixed by its source text; he replied that nothing in the text alone settles what the code means, since the very same bytes would compute something different on a different architecture or linked against a different standard library. He then pivoted: philosophers, he said, established decades ago that meaning is partly a matter of environment rather than internals, and this result carries over to software, so my position was not just wrong but refuted. Some searching tells me the slogan is associated with something called the Twin Earth thought experiment, but the popular summaries I found are loose enough that I cannot tell what claim the slogan is actually making, or whether my colleague's use of it is the claim or just the vibe.

What I want is a disambiguation, not another summary of the thought experiment. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing. At one end there seem to be near-trivial readings — word tokens and inscriptions are physical things located outside skulls; nobody can tell what a word refers to just by introspecting. In the middle there seem to be stronger theses about what determines a term's reference, such as the claim that two internally identical speakers could refer to different substances, or that a linguistic community's experts do some of the referential work for ordinary speakers. At the far end there seem to be radical readings: internal psychological states are simply irrelevant to meaning; there is no such thing as content individuated by what is inside a person; a perfect physical duplicate of me might mean nothing by anything. For each reading, please classify it as a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, a contentious extrapolation, or flatly false, and say what the standard Twin Earth–style considerations do and do not establish for it.

Finally, apply the menu to my situation. Which reading does my colleague need for the philosophical premise of his argument, and which reading does he need for the further inference that the semantics of my function is not fixed by its source? If the reading he needs is stronger than the one the slogan's pedigree actually supports, say so explicitly. I have no formal philosophy training but I am comfortable with logic, so please state each thesis precisely and briefly define technical terms like narrow content or supervenience when you first use them.
