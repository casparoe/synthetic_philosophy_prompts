I'm a third-year student currently enrolled in a seminar on Victorian liberalism and the British Empire, where we recently worked through Mill's On Liberty alongside some of his writings on India and his long career at the East India Company, including his defense of Company administration against its replacement by direct Crown rule. Around the same time, my English literature course assigned Forster's A Passage to India, and I can't shake the sense that the novel is almost a controlled experiment on Mill's theory of colonial governance. On the one hand, Mill held that despotism could be a legitimate mode of rule for peoples he judged not yet capable of being guided by free discussion, provided it aimed at their improvement, and he worried specifically that exposing India to the pressures of the home electorate and of resident English colonists would degrade administration -- the Company's meritocratic, specialist bureaucracy was, for him, a shield against precisely the kind of insular, status-conscious English community that Forster depicts in the Chandrapore club, with its Turtons and Burtons and their Bridge Parties. On the other hand, the novel seems to suggest that even a sympathetic official cannot remain insulated: Fielding begins as something like Mill's ideal administrator, educated and detached from club opinion, and yet by the end the machinery of the accusation against Aziz and the trial has forced him into positions that destroy his standing with both communities.

I'd like a careful reading of the novel through Mill's account of legitimate colonial rule that takes both sides seriously. Does Forster vindicate Mill's institutional diagnosis -- that the problem is untrained settlers and club society contaminating what could otherwise be benevolent expert administration -- or does the novel show that the contamination is inevitable, that administrators posted abroad become settlers in effect, so that Mill's insulation strategy cannot work even in principle? More radically, does the Marabar Caves sequence, where the echo reduces every utterance and every distinction to the same sound, function as an attack on the civilizational hierarchy that underwrites Mill's whole framework -- the very distinction between those capable of improvement by discussion and those who must be ruled for their own good? I'm also interested in Adela's retraction at the trial: can her insistence on what she actually saw, against the collective narrative of the club, be read as Millian individuality asserting itself inside a despotic social structure, and does that rescue Mill or condemn him? Finally, the novel's closing suggestion that genuine friendship between Fielding and Aziz is not possible under existing conditions seems to bear directly on Mill's promise that temporary paternal rule culminates in self-government and equality -- does Forster's pessimism about the timing expose a problem Mill never faced, namely that the ruling power has no internal reason ever to declare its wards ready? Please don't just catalogue parallels; I want to know where the novel resists the Millian reading, and what, if anything, the dramatization adds that Mill's own arguments cannot supply.
