Two friends of mine in a philosophy reading group got into an argument about whether Buddhist ethics is basically consequentialist, and it's been bugging me. Round 1: Maya said yes -- the Buddha's justification for precepts like not lying is that lying leads to suffering for yourself and others down the road (karma included), and the entire point of practice is reducing dukkha, so the structure is plainly outcome-based. Round 2: Ben said no -- the Buddha identifies karma with intention (cetanā), so what carries moral weight is the state of mind you act from, not the results; two outwardly identical acts with different intentions are morally worlds apart, which no real consequentialist would accept. Round 3: Maya replied that this just makes it motive-consequentialism -- intentions matter because acting from greed, hatred, and delusion reliably produces bad outcomes, and the tradition pushes compassion precisely because compassion works. Round 4: Ben tried a weirder move: consequentialism needs welfare that adds up across persons, but Buddhism denies there are any persisting persons -- so "the greatest good for the greatest number" has no numbers to count. Round 5: Maya said this actually helps her: if the boundary between selves is ultimately illusory, there's no principled reason to weigh my suffering over yours, so impartial maximizing falls right out (she said Parfit argued something along these lines). Round 6: Ben said she can't have it both ways -- either persons exist enough to have welfare worth maximizing, or they don't, and then the whole aggregative project loses its subject. Please go through it move by move, say who's right at each step and why, and who's closer to right overall.
