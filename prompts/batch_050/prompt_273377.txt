My grandmother lived through the Bengal famine of 1943 as a teenager in Calcutta, and next month our community center is unveiling a small memorial plaque about it, and somehow I've been volunteeered to give the dedication speech, maybe five minutes. Here's my problem. The plaque, which was decided by a comittee months ago and is already engraved, says something like "In memory of the victims of the Bengal famine disaster." My grandmother spent the rest of her life furious at exactly that word. She'd read Amartya Sen's argument that famines are failures of entitlement rather than failures of food supply, and she felt it in her bones: she'd tell us the rice was there, you could see it, it was in the shops and the warehouses, her family just couldn't afford it anymore as prices ran away from wages. So on her telling the famine wasn't a disaster the way a cyclone is a disaster; it was something specific people and institutions did, and failed to stop, under a colonial government that didn't answer to the people starving. She'd say calling it a disaster is how everyone gets to shrug.

At the same time, this is a memorial dedication, not a seminar and not a war-crimes tribunal. The audience will be maybe sixty people: elderly relatives of survivors, a bunch of local schoolkids who did a project on it, the councillor who paid for the plaque, a couple of historians, and some neighbors who just think of it as a terrible tragedy and would feel personally accused if I turned the thing into an indictment. I want the speech to be honest to what my grandmother knew -- that three million people starved within sight of food, and that this is a different kind of fact about the world than "the harvests failed" -- without hectoring a grieving room or litigating the Raj in front of ten-year-olds. The Sen point should do real work: it should shape what I can say about how the dead died (not "claimed by" anything), what "never again" can honestly mean when the cause was political rather than natural, and how to handle the word "disaster" carved in granite right behind me -- do I address it head on, reframe it gently, or leave it alone? Please write the speech (around 600 words) and then add a short note explaining the choices you made, especially the plaque one.
