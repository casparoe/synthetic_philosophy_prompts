Since retiring from the lab I've got into philosophy of perception, and I keep circling the question of whether we literally see things through photographs. Let me give you my gut verdicts on a run of cases and you tell me what principle, if any, captures them.

Looking at a colleague through the lab window: obviously seeing them. Through a mirror, or binoculars, or my old telescope: still seeing them, I'd insist. A live CCTV feed of the car park: I want to say yes, though I notice I'm less certain. A photograph of my grandfather from 1930: here's the funny thing, I do feel I'm seeing him, not just a picture of him. A photorealistic painting of him: no, definitely not, even if I couldn't tell it apart from the photo. An ultrasound scan of a foetus: something in between, and I can't articulate why. A colour-coded image reconstructed from radio-telescope data, where no light ever hit anything: much more like the graph case than the photo case. And a graph automatically plotted from temperature sensors: plainly not seeing anything, even though it's just as mechanically caused by the world as a photo is.

The photo versus the indistinguishable painting is the pair I can't reconcile, and the ultrasound sits awkwardly between the CCTV feed and the radio image. Is there a principle that gets all of these, or do I have to give one up? And what would it say about the case I keep flip-flopping on: watching a recording of last night's match?
