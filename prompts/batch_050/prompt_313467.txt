So I'm a retired materials scientist, took up philosophy about a year ago after my wife signed us up for a reading circle, and I've been lurking in a ethics discussion group online. Last week two regulars on there got into a proper argument about grown kids and aging parents, and both kept invoking this friendship-model idea from Jane English, the notion that adult children don't owe their parents repayment for past sacrifices the way you'd owe a debt, but rather owe whatever flows from an ongoing friendship, which is a different and thinner thing. I'd like an outside verdict because I genuinely can't tell who won.

The back-and-forth, as best I remember it:

(1) Anne said the friendship model proves that her brother-in-law is doing nothing wrong: he loves his mother, they talk every couple weeks, he visits at Christmas -- and on the friendship model that's exactly what a good friend does, so the people guilting him about not moving back to help with her care are just smuggling the debt model back in.

(2) Beatriz replied that this proves too much, because friendship itself isn't costless: if your actual friend of forty years broke her hip and you were the only one nearby, a decent friend would reorganize their life somewhat, not just call every couple weeks. So the friendship model, properly understood, can demand quite a lot, and the brother-in-law fails even as a friend.

(3) Anne said no, the whole point of English's move is that friends can't be obligated -- a favor done out of obligation stops being a favor, friendship is by definition voluntary, so any "you owe her as a friend" talk is a contradiction, and the most you can say is that he's a disappointing friend, not a failing one.

(4) Beatriz said that's an equivocation on "voluntary": friendship is voluntary at the level of entering and exiting the relationship, but once you're in one it has internal norms -- you don't get to keep the title of friend while opting out of everything that makes the title mean something. Either he's not really her friend (fine, but then say that) or he is, in which case the friendship itself generates the duties he's dodging.

Could you adjudicate move by move? Rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say briefly why, and then tell me what both of them are missing, if anything. Lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence -- who came out ahead -- then give the reasoning, no preamble and don't restate the question back at me.
