Last week I was buying coffee and a jacket, and both carried certification labels -- one from a fair trade organization, the other from a scheme that audits overseas factories and certifies that no sweatshop labor was used. I got into an argument with two friends about what these labels actually amount to, and we realized we disagreed about something deeper than branding. Here is the practice as I understand it: independent bodies inspect suppliers in countries like Bangladesh or Vietnam, check that wages meet some standard above the local minimum (sometimes a so-called living wage), that hours and overtime are limited, that workers can organize, and so on; brands pay for the audits and advertise the certification; consumers, in turn, pay a premium or boycott uncertified brands. The puzzling background fact is that the economists in my reading group keep insisting that the factory jobs in question are typically the best options available to the workers who take them -- people migrate and queue for them, the alternatives are worse-paid agricultural work or no work at all, and every transaction is entered voluntarily by adults who know their own circumstances better than any auditor does.

So my question is: what theory of exploitation does the certification practice presuppose? It seems to presuppose that a transaction can be wrongful -- wrongful enough that buying its products taints the consumer -- even when both parties benefit relative to their alternatives and the worse-off party consents. Which underlying view does the practice need to be true: that there is a fair division of the benefits of cooperation fixed independently of what the parties agree to, so that consent does not license taking the lion's share? Or that consent given under desperate background circumstances is somehow defective and therefore does not do its usual moral work? Or something else entirely, like a duty not to benefit from another's vulnerability? I would also like you to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. For instance, some certification standards only require compliance with local law and basic safety, and maybe that much presupposes nothing more exotic than an anti-fraud and anti-harm principle; it is the wage requirements that seem to need a thicker theory. Finally, tell me whether the practice is internally coherent, and what would have to change if we adopted the rival view -- apparently held by some of my friends -- that a mutually beneficial transaction between consenting adults cannot be wrongfully exploitative at all. Would certification collapse into ordinary quality assurance? Would the boycotts have to stop? And is it a problem for the whole practice that nobody demands analogous certification for low-wage domestic work, like gig delivery or agricultural labor, where the same logic would seem to apply?
