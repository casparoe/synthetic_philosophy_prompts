I never studied philosophy or politics or anything, I left school at 18, so sorry if this is a dumb question. Earlier this year I took over as the volunteer coordinator of a community workshop / tool library that a guy named Martin ran for 22 years before he moved to Spain. He left behind a laminated binder of house rules, and some of them are obviously sensible (no using the table saw alone) and some of them nobody can explain at all. Like: you can't borrow anything during the last week of the month. Red-handled tools stay in the building, no exceptions. New members do a month of "shadowing" before they can check anything out, even if they're a retired carpenter. There's a rule that the kettle must be unplugged and the plug put in a specific drawer, which Martin apparently enforced like it was life or death.

At the monthly meeting people keep proposing we just scrap the weird ones wholesale, and honestly I was on board until I fell down an internet rabbit hole about this idea people call Chesterton's fence -- roughly, if you come across a fence in a field and you can't see what it's for, you don't get to tear it down until you've figured out why someone built it, because the reason might still be operating even if you can't percieve it. That made me pause. But then I found the actual Chesterton essay and also ended up reading a chunk of Edmund Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790) on some public domain site, and near the start Burke says to his French correspondent:

"I do most heartily wish that France may be animated by a spirit of rational liberty, and that I think you bound, in all honest policy, to provide a permanent body in which that spirit may reside, and an effectual organ by which it may act"

This kind of broke my brain because everyone quotes Burke as the "don't change anything" guy, but here he's saying you're actually *obligated* ("bound") to build something -- a permanent body, an effectual organ -- for the spirit of liberty to live in. So first question: am I reading this right, that Burke isn't against change at all, he's against change that just demolishes structures without leaving a working structure behind? What does he mean by an "effectual organ" exactly? And does that mean the fence idea, done properly, shouldn't be a keep-it-or-scrap-it vote at all, but something with a third option where you replace the fence with something that does the fence's job on purpose?

Second, the main thing I actually need: turn this into a procedure I can run at meetings. We're talking one rule-change proposal every meeting or two, low-ish stakes but real (someone could get hurt, and also if I get this wrong twice the old guard will stop trusting me), and I have maybe 20 minutes of meeting time plus homework between meetings. I want:

- the questions to ask about any inherited rule, in order, before we're allowed to vote on removing it
- what counts as having "found the reason" for a rule -- like, is "Dave who was here in 2009 says it's because of the break-in" good enough, or do I need more than one source
- the genuinely hard case: what does the procedure say to do when nobody can find ANY reason? Does the fence principle really say keep it forever? That seems crazy and also seems to be what Chesterton says
- how to handle a rule where we found the reason and the reason is definitely dead (e.g., hypothetically a rule about rewinding something when we haven't owned the equipment for a decade) versus a rule where the reason is dead but the rule might have grown a second, different job since
- where the procedure just can't decide and I have to use judgement, and how to be honest in the minutes that that's what happened

Then run the procedure on three of our actual proposals so I can see it work: (1) scrapping the paper sign-out book now that we have a checkout app, (2) scrapping the no-borrowing-in-the-last-week-of-the-month rule, which everyone hates and which I suspect is a dumb reason I'll find out about (ask me why I suspect), (3) the new-member shadowing month, which a couple of members have started calling gatekeeping and honestly might be -- this is the one where I want the procedure to be capable of telling me something I don't want to hear, i.e. keep a rule I find embarassing, and also capable of telling me a rule really is just inherited snobbery with no function, because if it can't ever conclude THAT then it's not a procedure, it's just a fancy way of never changing anything, which is exactly the failure mode the Burke quote seems to be warning against?

Also please tell me where the whole fence way of thinking misfires even when applied correctly -- I assume smart people have complained about it -- because I don't want to walk into the meeting armed with a heuristic I only know the good side of.
