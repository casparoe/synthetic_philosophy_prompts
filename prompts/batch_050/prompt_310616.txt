So here's the deal: I retired a few years ago after forty-odd years in neuropharmacology, and I've been making up for lost time by sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local university. Right now we're on the badness of pain, and I volunteered to put together a set of cases for our next meeting -- little scenarios, clinical or imaginary, that are supposed to tell us something about *what* makes pain bad (how it feels, the fact that we hate it, whatever). Here's what I have so far, and I have the sneaking suspicion that all four are really pulling on the same lever, namely pulling pain and disliking apart:

1. Pain asymbolia -- patients (after certain kinds of brain damage, cingulotomy being the famous one) who say they still feel the pain, can locate it, describe its intensity, but simply don't mind it and don't try to avoid it.
2. The morphine case -- patients on morphine who report that the pain is still there but that it no longer bothers them. Sounds like the same trick as (1), but I kept it because the drug case at least leaves open that the sensation itself changed.
3. The masochist -- someone who seeks out and enjoys certain pains, which is supposed to be trouble for anyone who says pain is bad in virtue of being inherently disliked.
4. The distracted soldier -- the old observation that wounded soldiers in battle sometimes don't notice their injuries until the fighting is over, which suggests attention or context does something to how bad pain is.

What I want is a fifth case that tests a genuinely *different* axis of the problem -- not another variation on "pain present, aversion absent," since I clearly have that covered. Ideally it would bear on a question my four cases don't touch: say, whether a pain nobody ever finds out about could be bad, whether the badness lives in the moment or in what the pain does to a life, whether intensity and duration matter in the same way, or anything else I've failed to think of. It should be at least empirically plausible -- I'm the token scientist in this group and I'll be embarassed if I bring in something physiologically impossible. Tell me what the case is, which theory of pain's badness it helps or hurts, and honestly whether it actually works or just sounds clever.
