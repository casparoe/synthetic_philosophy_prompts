I am preparing an eighteen-month postdoctoral research proposal on Quinn-style self-torturer cases and the rationality of intransitive preferences. My guiding idea is that the debate often runs together three different phenomena: a fixed but non-transitive ordering, pairwise discrimination that is too coarse to support transitivity, and a rational change in the standard by which later options are assessed. I want to investigate whether a sequence of locally justified choices can be globally irrational even when no individual step is irrational, and whether the answer changes when preferences are indexed to the agent’s choice history.

The proposal should develop a framework of history-indexed preferences, where a preference is evaluated relative to the options already taken, the commitments thereby created, and the information available at that point. Apply the framework to a carefully specified spectrum case involving barely perceptible increases in pain, irreversible choices, and escalating rewards. Vary the case along dimensions such as whether the endpoint is known, whether the agent can stop, whether precommitment is possible, and whether the final reward remains worth having once the accumulated suffering is considered.

Write a polished two-page proposal containing:

1. A precise research question, avoiding the vague formulation of whether intransitive preferences are rational.
2. An explanation of why the main existing responses—accepting rational intransitivity, imposing transitivity as a requirement of practical rationality, assimilating the case to a sorites paradox, or treating the agent as irrational for failing to look ahead—leave the central issue unresolved.
3. The proposed distinction among fixed intransitivity, limited discriminability, and rational diachronic revision, with examples showing why the distinction matters.
4. A tractable research plan divided into realistic phases over eighteen months, including conceptual analysis, construction of variations on the case, and comparison of several possible rationality constraints.
5. The anticipated contribution: not merely another verdict on the self-torturer, but an account of when local rationality does or does not transmit across a sequence.
6. A discussion of what would count as failure—for example, if history-indexing merely relabels standard dynamic inconsistency, or if every plausible constraint either trivially restores transitivity or licenses clearly self-defeating sequences.
7. A brief statement of why the project could be completed by one researcher without experimental work or a large technical apparatus.

Do not include a bibliography or invent citations. You may refer to familiar positions and arguments by name; I will add references later.
