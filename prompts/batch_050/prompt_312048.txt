So my friend and I got into it again last night over whether delusions are actually beliefs. She's a straight doxasticist: if someone with Capgras sincerely insists his wife has been replaced by an impostor, defends the claim when challenged, and organizes his emotional life around it, then it's a belief — full stop, weirdness be damned. I keep pushing the line that delusions fail too much of belief's functional profile (patients often don't act on them in the ways you'd expect, they're strangely compartmentalized, they shrug off counterevidence in a way ordinary beliefs don't), so they're better understood as something else, maybe a kind of imagining or acceptance mistaken for belief. Write the joint statement we should be able to co-sign if we turned this into an adversarial collaboration: a list of claims about delusions we can both accept as stated, then the remaining disagreements phrased so each of us would endorse the wording of our own side, then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would genuinely move one of us — and for each crux, one case or observation (real patient phenomena or hypothetical evidence is fine) that we agree bears on it, with what each of us would predict about it in advance. One thing I want the statement to settle: whether the standard two-factor story (an anomalous experience plus a second deficit that explains why the bizarre explanation gets adopted and kept) is actually neutral between us, or whether it quietly presupposes her side. Keep the whole thing under 700 words.
