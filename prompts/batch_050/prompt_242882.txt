I am a novelist at work on a book whose true protagonist would ideally be a valley — its river, its soils, its weather — with the human story unfolding as something the valley mostly outlasts. My model and my stumbling block is War and Peace, and I would like a reading of it through Braudel's distinction between the near-motionless time of geography and climate, the slower rhythms of economies and social structures, and the rapid time of events and individuals. Tolstoy's essayistic chapters insist that battles and emperors are surfaces rather than causes, and he reaches for a figure from the calculus, history as the integral of countless infinitesimal human wills. But a sum of aggregated persons is not Braudel's world of material structures largely indifferent to persons, and I want to know whether the novel registers that difference or only its theory does. Where does the fiction itself render slow time — the winter that destroys the retreating French, the recurrent rounds of estate life, the epilogue's submergence of the war generation in marriage, children, and harvests, perhaps even the indifferent sky above Andrei at Austerlitz, which is after all a piece of geography? And where does the fiction betray the thesis: does Andrei's death, or Pierre wandering Borodino, win back for the individual everything the essays confiscated? Finally, the craft question underneath it all: can a form built from scenes, choices, and deaths represent the longue durée at all, or only smuggle it in through weather, repetition, and genealogy — and what does Tolstoy's solution cost him?
