I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized robotics firm, with no formal philosophy training. At our last retrospective, a colleague joked that our navigation agent's core defect is that it refuses to admit it lives inside the building it keeps mapping. That remark sent me down a rabbit hole on what's apparently called embedded agency: the observation that a real agent is part of the world it models, rather than the clean textbook setup in which an agent sits outside its environment, perceives it, acts on it, and whose own deliberation floats free of the physics it studies. The failure modes are wonderfully undignified. An agent can't hold a perfect model of a world that contains the agent holding the model, since the model would have to include itself, and itself-including-itself, and so on. It has to reason about the very computation that constitutes its reasoning. And its sense of choosing between alternatives sits awkwardly with the fact that its decision is determined by the same physical process it's busy modelling.

I'd like you to write me a satirical internal incident postmortem, roughly 600 to 800 words, in the house style of a large tech company: blameless, deadpan, structured with sections like Summary, Impact, Timeline, Root Cause, and Action Items. The incident: our navigation agent, after months of stable operation, glanced at its own camera feed, recognised itself in the building's floor plan, and entered a resource-exhaustion spiral attempting to compute the consequences. The postmortem should trace the failure back through the genuine problems of embedded agency, rendered as engineering defects. For instance: the planner spawned a full simulation of the building that necessarily included a process running a full simulation of the building; the scheduling module treated the robot's own future deliberation as an exogenous variable to be queried rather than a computation to be performed; a senior engineer repeatedly dismissed the agent/environment boundary as a legacy assumption but was overruled by product management, for whom it is a business requirement.

Two constraints. First, the humour must come from the real difficulties of the subject, exaggerated but recognisable, so that a reader familiar with the embedded agency literature feels the satire is fair rather than a strawman: self-modelling, logical uncertainty about one's own outputs, and the queerness of counterfactual reasoning when the reasoner is part of the causal order should all show up wearing lanyards. Second, keep the irony entirely deadpan: no winking, no punchlines flagged as jokes, and no mention of philosophy anywhere in the document. The Action Items section should close the piece, each item being a genuine philosophical position disguised as an engineering ticket, such as formally accepting that the map cannot contain the cartographer and deprioritising the matter indefinitely.
