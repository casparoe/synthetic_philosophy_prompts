Last weekend a friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, and I would like help formulating my side of the dispute properly. We ended up agreeing on the theorem itself: with three or more alternatives and unrestricted preferences, any deterministic single-winner voting rule in which every alternative can in principle win, and which no voter can ever profitably manipulate by misreporting their ranking, must be a dictatorship. What we disagreed about, sharply, is what follows from it.

Her position is a kind of dissolution. The theorem, she says, shows only that for every reasonable rule there exists some ballot profile at which some voter would gain by voting insincerely. But identifying such a profile requires the would-be manipulator to know everyone else's ballot exactly. Real voters know almost nothing about the full profile; at best they have polls, punditry, and hunches. So, she argues, a rule should count as strategy-proof for all practical purposes if no voter can identify a profitable misreport from the information voters actually possess. On that standard she thinks rules like Borda or instant-runoff are effectively honesty-inducing, and that the theorem is best read as a result about omniscient voters rather than about democracy.

I believe she is wrong, but over the course of the evening my objections kept collapsing into vague gestures at tactical voting, and I came away unsatisfied with my own performance. Please argue my side rigorously: that relaxing the theorem's implicit background of complete information does not dissolve the problem of strategic voting, and that my friend's move from dominant-strategy incentive compatibility to something like "no identifiable manipulation from realistic information" is either an empirical claim that fails, a subtle change of subject, or both.

A good answer should do the following. First, state precisely what the theorem does and does not assume about voters' knowledge, since I suspect the dissolution trades on an ambiguity there. Second, present the strongest version of her position before attacking it — it deserves better than the summary I have given. Third, engage with the fact that profitable manipulation typically requires only coarse, publicly available information rather than the full profile, and with the role of parties, campaigns, and published polls as institutions that aggregate and broadcast exactly the information manipulation needs. Fourth, consider whether recasting the problem in terms of Bayesian rather than dominant-strategy incentives rescues her point or sharpens mine. Finally, say something about what her standard would imply if it were correct — for instance, whether it proves too much by certifying as honest rules that are transparently and routinely gamed, and whether it would classify sincerity itself as irrational whenever polling is cheap.
