I've been reading around the ethics of blame for a seminar, and I keep coming back to the question of "standing" -- the idea that someone can lose the right to blame another person for a wrong even when the blame would be accurate. I've been stress-testing my own intuitions on cases, and I'd like to know whether there's a coherent principle underneath them. My verdicts: A man who is himself currently cheating on his wife angrily condemns a friend for cheating -- he lacks standing; there's something objectionable about his blame even though what he says is true. A man who cheated a decade ago, confessed, and genuinely repaired his marriage now condemns cheating -- he has standing; his past doesn't disqualify him. Someone who drove the getaway car blames the person who actually went inside and committed the robbery -- no standing; complicity seems to undermine blame just as hypocrisy does, though for a different reason. A stranger with an impeccable record as a parent criticizes an acquaintance's parenting at a party -- no standing, even though she's never done what she's criticizing; it's none of her business. A journalist whose own finances are clean investigates and publicly condemns a politician's corruption -- standing, clearly, and the fact that it's her job seems to matter. Now the pair I can't reconcile: I said the reformed cheater has standing, but I also have the intuition that a person who has simply never cheated -- not from virtue, but because they've never once been tempted -- has *less* standing to condemn a cheater than the reformed man does, which is strange, since the never-tempted person never did anything wrong at all. And a final verdict that complicates things further: if the currently cheating husband's blame is objectionable, I still think the friend he condemns ought to feel the force of the criticism and change his behavior -- so whatever is wrong with the blame, it isn't that the blame is false or that the target is off the hook.

Here is what I want from you. State a principle -- or, if one won't do it, a small set of principles -- of when someone has standing to blame, stated precisely enough that I can see which case motivates each clause. The literature gestures at conditions like non-hypocrisy, non-complicity, and the blame being one's business; tell me whether some unified principle subsumes these or whether standing is irreducibly plural. Say explicitly which of my verdicts your account cannot accommodate and which one you think I should give up if forced -- my sense is that the never-tempted-versus-reformed pair is where a clean theory will break, but I'd like to see whether you agree. Then apply your account to two cases I haven't given verdicts on: a politician who voted for a war now publicly blaming her colleagues for how it was conducted, and a friend who spent years enabling my drinking now blaming me for it. Finally, tell me how your account explains my last intuition -- that the hypocrite's blame can be simultaneously out of place and yet something its target should take seriously -- or, if it can't explain it, say so.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- whether you think a single principle captures my verdicts, and if so which -- and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
