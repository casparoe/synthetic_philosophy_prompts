I’ve been kicking around an argument with some friends from the old rationalist-blog diaspora, and it feels like it either exposes a deep problem with “gains from trade” between moral systems or sneaks in a bad move that I can’t quite locate.

Suppose a city contains three durable factions with genuinely different value systems. The welfarists mostly care about reducing suffering. The autonomy group mostly cares about people being free from interference. The sanctity group mostly cares about avoiding practices it sees as intrinsically degrading. None of them is just mistaken about the empirical facts, and none can persuade the others. The city therefore creates a Moral Exchange Board. Instead of fighting over every policy one at a time, each faction submits a cardinal value function over outcomes, and the Board designs packages of concessions: perhaps the autonomy faction accepts a public-health mandate, the sanctity faction accepts a legal practice it dislikes, and the welfarists accept restrictions on some suffering-reducing intervention, with each side receiving more total value by its own lights than it would get from ordinary political conflict.

Here’s the argument:

1. If people with conflicting preferences can both become better off by their own lights through trade, then refusing the trade purely because the other side benefits is usually irrational and often morally objectionable.

2. The same logic should apply when the “preferences” are moral value functions rather than tastes. A welfarist giving up something in one policy area in exchange for a larger welfarist win elsewhere is still making the world better by welfarist standards, and similarly for the other factions.

3. If each faction assigns greater expected moral value to the whole package than to continuing political conflict, then the package is a moral Pareto improvement across the value systems represented in the city.

4. Rejecting a moral Pareto improvement just because one faction must do something its own morality strongly condemns would amount to treating that faction’s local taboo as more important than the total amount of value at stake.

5. So the Board should sometimes prescribe package deals in which each faction knowingly does or permits things it considers seriously wrong, provided the concessions purchased elsewhere are sufficiently valuable according to that faction’s own value system.

6. Once that principle is accepted, neutrality requires the Board to count every sincerely held, stable value commitment as tradable moral weight. It may discount incoherent or empirically mistaken commitments, but it cannot exclude commitments merely because the majority finds their content repellent.

7. Therefore, if a fourth faction sincerely assigns immense positive value to other people being humiliated, frightened, or made to suffer, the Board must treat the frustration of that faction’s values as a real cost and should sometimes compensate it with concessions from everyone else, just as it compensates the other factions.

8. But that result is absurd. It rewards people for adopting or cultivating morally horrible commitments and lets the prospect of offense become a kind of extortionate bargaining chip.

9. So something is wrong with the attempt to generalize ordinary gains-from-trade reasoning into a general procedure for compromise between value systems.

I would very much like to preserve the intuitive rationalist point that moral trade can be genuinely better than winner-take-all politics: vegans and omnivores trading donations, effective altruists supporting each other’s favorite causes, and so on. I’d also like to avoid the trivial answer that “we should just use the correct morality,” because the whole setup assumes persistent reasonable disagreement about what the correct morality is.

Which premise or inference should I reject? Please identify the exact step where the argument first goes wrong, if it does; explain why that step initially looks compelling; distinguish the relevant senses of Pareto improvement, moral value, coercion, and bargaining power; and say what the major alternative diagnoses would cost. In particular, I want to know whether the problem is cardinal comparison across factions, the move from individual preference trade to inter-moral-system trade, the Board’s neutrality assumption, the baseline against which “improvement” is measured, or something else. Also explain how a good Moral Exchange Board could capture real gains from compromise without becoming a protection racket for anyone strategic enough to develop monstrous values.
