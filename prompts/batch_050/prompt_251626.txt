So I've been moderating (if you can call it that) a running argument between two friends in our little philosophy circle, all year, about whether death is bad for the person who dies. One of them is a committed Epicurean about it: when you're gone there's no you for death to be bad for, fearing it is irrational, and the deprivation intuition is just confusion dressed up as concern. The other is a deprivationist: death is bad for you precisely because it cuts off goods you would otherwise have had, even though there's no moment at which you're around to suffer the loss. They've been talking past each other in the usual ways, and I've finally bullied them into doing an adversarial collaboration instead of another three-hour loop. Your job: draft the joint statement they'd produce.

The thing that keeps blowing up their discussions is this passage from Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus, which the Epicurean friend reads as settling things and the deprivationist friend reads as a disguised concession: "And even as men choose of food not merely and simply the larger portion, but the more pleasant, so the wise seek to enjoy the time which is most pleasant and not merely that which is longest." The deprivationist says: look, even Epicurus admits pleasant time is a good, so a life with more of it is better, so dying early deprives you of something and is therefore bad for you, done. The Epicurean says the passage is about the shape of a good life from the inside, that hedonic goods need a living subject to be good for anyone, and that "longest vs. most pleasant" is a question about how to live, not about whether ceasing to exist is a misfortune. Make sure the statement doesn't just relitigate this but nails down exactly what each side thinks the passage establishes and what it doesn't.

Structure it like this: "Agreed Claims" (numbered, ~200 words, things both would actually sign, e.g. that dying itself isn't an experience, that the badness-if-any isn't located at a time, whatever else survives); "The Passage, Twice Read" (~150 words, each side's reading of the quote above, in terms the other side would accept as a fair statement of that reading); "Where We Still Differ" (~250 words, the residual disagreements stated precisely, not as slogans); "Cruxes and Bets" (~200 words, the sub-questions that would move at least one of them, and for each a case or consideration both agree bears on it — things like the symmetry with prenatal non-existence, whether something can be bad for someone at no time at all, whether comparisons between a shorter and longer pleasant life smuggle in a third-person standpoint — with each side's predicted verdict recorded). Please keep everything in wording both parties could put their names to, no strawmen, and if part of their fight turns out to be verbal, say which part in the disagreements section rather than pretending it's substantive.
