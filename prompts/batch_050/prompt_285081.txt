I am preparing a position statement for a philosophy-of-biology reading group on what Dennett calls free-floating rationales — reasons that exist without any reasoner — and I want a consistency audit before I circulate it. The discussion will center on this passage from Chapter IV of Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859), where natural selection is described in openly personal terms:

"Man can act only on external and visible characters: nature cares nothing for appearances, except in so far as they may be useful to any being. She can act on every internal organ, on every shade of constitutional difference, on the whole machinery of life. Man selects only for his own good; Nature only for that of the being which she tends."

My question is whether the view I have built around this passage hangs together. Here are my commitments:

(a) Darwin's personification of selection — Nature as a she who tends, cares, and selects for a being's good — is philosophically innocent shorthand. Every sentence of the passage can in principle be translated, without explanatory loss, into purely causal vocabulary about heritable variation and differential reproductive success. The translation leaves no residue that the original captures.

(b) Selection explanations answer genuine "why" questions, and the rationales they cite are really there to be discovered rather than projected by us: there is a fact of the matter about what the heart's valves are for, fixed by causal history, which is why biology can get function attributions right or wrong.

(c) Calling something a reason is a normative claim: a reason counts in favor of some arrangement, belief, or action being a certain way, and no set of purely descriptive facts about causal history can by itself entail that anything counts in favor of anything.

(d) The human capacity to recognize, weigh, and offer reasons is itself a product of natural selection in the same sense as the heart's valves: its existence and general profile are explained by what it was selected for.

(e) When an immune system, a thermoregulatory mechanism, or a trained neural network produces an outcome we can rationalize, there is no fact of the matter, independent of our interpretive interests, about whether the system really has a reason for what it does; reason-talk for such systems is a stance we adopt, not a feature we find.

Please go through these and tell me: which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be; for each genuine tension, how severe it is — rank them rather than treating them as equal — and exactly what would have to be given up on each side; and which single revision would restore the most consistency at the lowest cost to the overall view. As part of the audit, tell me whether, on my commitments, Darwin's "she tends" language should be read as harmless shorthand or as tracking something the causal translation cannot say, and what that verdict implies for whether free-floating rationales, as I have characterized them, are genuine reasons or only as-if reasons. Please keep the whole response under 700 words.
