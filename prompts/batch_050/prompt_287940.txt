I'm preparing a seminar session on game theory and film, and I want to use the ferry scene from The Dark Knight as the centerpiece: the Joker rigs two ferries leaving Gotham with explosives, puts the detonator for each boat on the other boat, and announces that if neither ferry blows the other up by midnight, he'll destroy them both. One ferry carries ordinary citizens, the other convicted prisoners. The standard reading, which I've seen everywhere, treats this as a textbook one-shot prisoner's dilemma and treats the outcome — neither side pushes the button, and one prisoner memorably takes the detonator and throws it out the window — as a sentimental refutation of the "rational" move to defect.

I want to push my students past that reading, because I think it's too easy, and I'd like you to do the analysis properly. First, is the ferry scene actually a prisoner's dilemma at all? Lay out the payoff structure carefully: I suspect that once you take seriously that mutual inaction ends with the Joker killing everyone, the game is closer to an assurance or coordination problem with a grim default, and that changes what "defection" even means. Second, whatever the game is, the scene's drama depends on neither boat knowing whether the other can be trusted — so read it against what we know about the evolution of cooperation: is the ferry situation more like Axelrod-style iterated play or like a one-shot anonymous encounter, and what do Axelrod's results (the success of reciprocating strategies, the role of the shadow of the future, the importance of recognizability and reputation) suggest about which structural features are missing from the Joker's setup and which are present? Third, the Joker is clearly running the whole thing as an experiment on human nature, convinced everyone will defect. What does the cooperation literature say his experiment actually tests, and where does his experimental design cheat?

Finally, the part I care most about: what does the film itself get right that the game-theoretic formalism tends to flatten? The prisoner who throws the detonator away doesn't cooperate by playing a strategy — he unilaterally removes himself from the game, which looks less like Tit for Tat and more like refusing the payoff matrix altogether, and the citizen ferry's agonized vote before failing to act dramatizes something about the gap between aggregated preferences and individual willingness. Where does the scene resist a clean game-theoretic reading, and is that resistance a failure of the film's logic or the most philosophically honest thing in it? I'd like an answer I can hand out as a reading, so keep it rigorous but self-contained.
