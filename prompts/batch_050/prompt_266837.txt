Next spring I will be giving the first public defense of my dissertation's central chapter at a political theory colloquium, and I need a preparation sheet for the question period. Here is the thesis I am defending. The familiar justifications of a duty to vote are consequentialist in structure: your vote matters because of its expected effect on outcomes, and in a large electorate that effect is vanishingly small, so the duty is correspondingly weak or absent. I argue instead that the duty to vote is a duty of fair play grounded in the division of civic labor. Democratic authorization is a collective enterprise that requires some threshold of participation from the citizenry; those who abstain while benefiting from the legitimacy, stability, and decision-procedures that others' participation sustains are free-riding on that labor. Crucially, this reframing dissolves several standard puzzles: a vote in a noncompetitive district is not wasted, because its contribution to the enterprise of authorization does not depend on affecting the winner; voting for the lesser of two evils is obligatory rather than distasteful, since what is owed is one's share of the collective work, not a vote expressive of one's deepest convictions; and principled abstention as a form of protest is impermissible precisely because its expressive force is parasitic on the participation of the people it purports to signal against. I also draw a consequence that will be controversial: because the duty is one of contributing labor rather than producing good outcomes, I resist the claim (associated with some libertarian critics of mass democracy) that badly informed citizens have a duty to abstain; on my view, modest epistemic shortcomings do not defeat the fair-play obligation, though gross negligence about the stakes might.

The audience: roughly fifteen people. Most are normative democratic theorists of a deliberative or civic-republican bent, for whom participation is intrinsically valuable and who will bristle at my picture of voting as owed labor rather than self-government. There are two or three rational-choice political scientists who take the paradox of voting and rational ignorance seriously and treat expressive accounts of turnout as the only empirically respectable ones. There is at least one libertarian-leaning political philosopher sympathetic to the view that incompetent voting is a wrong against one's fellow citizens. There may also be a graduate student or two who has read the recent empirical literature on whether abstention actually functions as protest.

Distinguish the objections that target my fair-play grounding from those that target the implications (noncompetitive districts, lesser-evil voting, the anti-abstention-for-incompetence conclusion), since I want to know which parts of the structure are load-bearing.
