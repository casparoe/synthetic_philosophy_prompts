Two colleagues of mine, Mara and Dev, got into an argument I was asked to referee, and I would like a proper adjudication rather than a split decision. Both are strong amateur chess players: same club, ratings within twenty points of each other, and an even score across a dozen tournament games. Last week they spent an hour analyzing a position from a game Mara had played that afternoon. Mara concluded that White is objectively winning with best play; Dev concluded that the position is a draw with accurate defense. Each had checked the other's analysis line by line and found no blunder in it; they simply weighted the positional considerations differently. The dispute that followed was about what rationality now requires of each of them.

Mara's opening move was conciliationist. Given that they are epistemic peers about positions like this -- same training, near-identical ratings, the same board in front of them -- the bare fact that Dev, having examined exactly what she examined, reached the opposite verdict is itself evidence that she has gone wrong somewhere. Rationality, she argued, requires splitting the difference: she should move from "White is winning" to something like "White is perhaps slightly better," and Dev should make the symmetric move toward her. The higher-order fact of peer disagreement, she said, should partially swamp first-order confidence.

Dev's reply had two parts. First, the proposal is self-undermining: among the professional philosophers who have thought hardest about peer disagreement, conciliationism is at best a minority position, so by Mara's own rule she should abandon conciliationism rather than apply it. Second, he accused it of demanding spinelessness. His confidence is not a free-floating opinion but the output of an hour of calculation he can still survey and still find sound; treating Mara's disagreement as counterevidence means discounting his own reasoning in favor of the mere fact that she reasoned otherwise -- and if she reasons symmetrically, they end up deferring to each other and ultimately to nothing.

Mara's rejoinder: Dev was double-counting. His calculation had already received its full weight when it produced his conclusion; citing "but I calculated carefully" as a further reason to discount her disagreement counts the same evidence twice. As for self-defeat, she distinguished: conciliationism is a thesis about how to respond when a peer disagrees about a first-order matter on shared evidence, not a thesis about how to conduct philosophy, and a norm need not survive being applied to itself to be correct.

Dev's final move: even granting that disagreement is evidence, he said, Mara was conflating two norms. Meeting in the middle may be wise policy for managing a joint decision or a shared reputation, but epistemic rationality is answerable to the truth, and the truth about the position is fixed. If White is genuinely winning and both conciliate to "slightly better for White," Mara has moved away from the truth and Dev toward it -- the rule tracks social comfort, not accuracy. He added that they could simply run a strong engine over the position that evening, and asked what work conciliation was doing that the facts would not do in a few hours.

Please go through the four moves in order, rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, and then tell me: (a) whether the disagreement should change either player's credence, and in what direction; (b) whether the self-defeat objection and the double-counting reply could both be good points at once; and (c) what both are missing. I am especially unsure about two things neither addressed: whether their new disagreement about how to respond to disagreement should itself trigger a further round of conciliation, and whether Dev is permitted to downgrade his estimate of Mara's peerhood precisely because she disagrees with him.
