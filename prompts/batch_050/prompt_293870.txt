I have just had a long argument with a friend about whether the intentions and selection procedures surrounding a statistical analysis can change the evidential force of the same numerical result. The argument began with optional stopping: two experimenters observe exactly the same sequence of outcomes and stop at the same final sample size, but one had fixed that sample size in advance, whereas the other had repeatedly checked the results and intended to stop as soon as they became favorable. My friend says the completed data are identical and therefore support the hypothesis to exactly the same degree. I replied that, at least in ordinary significance testing, the stopping plan changes the probability of obtaining a result as extreme as the one observed, so it changes what the result licenses us to conclude.

We then noticed that similar cases appear elsewhere. A treatment difference of the same size may be the one comparison a study was designed to test, or it may be the largest difference found after examining many outcomes, subgroups, covariate adjustments, and endpoints. Again, the final estimate and standard error can be identical, but the process that produced them is not. In a third case, two literatures contain the same five favorable experiments, but in one literature every completed experiment was published, while in the other an unknown number of unfavorable experiments were set aside. The available papers are the same, yet my friend still wants to say that they provide the same evidence.

I am looking for the established term, if there is one, for the general phenomenon here: the final observations remain fixed, while their evidential status changes because of the analyst’s plans, the range of analyses that would have been performed under other outcomes, or the process selecting which results become visible. It does not seem to be exactly selection bias, because in the optional-stopping case no observation is necessarily omitted. It also seems broader than multiple testing, because that term fits the subgroup example better than the publication example. I am unsure whether this is one phenomenon or several related phenomena that should not be grouped together.

Please explain whether there is a standard name for the general pattern, or propose and define a precise name if there is not. Identify the nearest established concepts, explain how the pattern differs from each, and mark its boundary cases. In particular, discuss whether the pattern concerns evidence itself, the calibration of a procedure, or both; whether a Bayesian account is really indifferent to stopping plans and analytic flexibility if the likelihood is unchanged; and whether outcome-dependent publication remains relevant even to a Bayesian who sees only the published results. I am less interested in being given a single label than in getting the conceptual carving right. Please answer in continuous prose only, as in an email or letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
