So I got into it with my brother-in-law over the holidays about that idea where you spray particles into the upper atmosphere to bounce sunlight back and cool the planet down (solar geoengineering I think its called). He thinks its insane hubris, I kept playing devil's advocate, and afterwards I tried to write his side up as a proper argument to see if it actually works. Now I'm worried it works too well. Here it is:

(1) It's wrong to deliberately make a huge number of people's survival depend on a system that would kill them if it ever suddenly stopped working.
(2) Once you start dimming the sun, you can't just stop, because all the warming you've been masking comes roaring back in like a decade instead of being spread out over a century, and thats worse than never starting. (I read this gets called termination shock.)
(3) Anything run by humans stops eventually, one way or another -- wars, budget fights, some country pulling out of the agreement, the tech failing. Nothing humans maintain has a perfect track record over centuries.
(4) So starting geoengineering means creating exactly the kind of dependency (1) says is wrong, which means we should never start it, no matter how bad warming gets.
(5) But wait -- billions of people alive today depend completely on industrial agriculture, and if that suddenly stopped they'd starve. Same with the power grid, supply chains for medicine, all of it. By this argument's logic it was wrong to build any of that.
(6) Nobody actually believes it was immoral to invent farming or electricity, not even my brother-in-law. So either (1) is false or basically all of modern civilization is a moral crime, and I can't swallow either.

The move from (4) to (5) feels too slick to me but when I try to say what makes the geoengineering case special (something about how nobody on earth got a vote? or that the collapse would be faster?) I feel like I'm just grabbing differences after the fact to protect a conclusion I already had. Which step should I actually give up? If I could keep one thing it'd be (1), because it really does seem wrong to build a doomsday dependency on purpose. But I want to know what rejecting each step costs, including the ones that look obvious.
