I am a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and I keep running into the same move in journal clubs and on conference panels: someone presents a hypothesis about the adaptive function of a trait -- say, that a particular pattern of behavioral variation is a bet-hedging strategy, or that some feature of human reasoning evolved because of its role in social coordination -- and a senior member of the audience says, more or less, "that's a just-so story," and the room treats the hypothesis as dealt with. Sometimes this seems exactly right; other times the person presenting has comparative data, a model, and a manipulation experiment in preparation, and the charge still gets made and still lands. So I would like to know what, precisely, someone could be claiming when they call an adaptive explanation a just-so story.

Please do not give me a general survey of the adaptationism debate. What I want is a disambiguation: a menu of the distinct, precise theses that the charge might be expressing, ordered roughly from the weakest and most defensible to the strongest and most radical. For each reading, I want (i) the thesis stated precisely enough that one could tell whether a given piece of research violates it, (ii) a verdict on whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible methodological demand, or something that is probably false, and (iii) an assessment of whether that reading actually licenses dismissing the kind of hypothesis I described above, or whether it only demands more of the research program.

I can think of several candidate readings that I would like treated, though you should add any I am missing: that the hypothesis is not falsifiable at all; that it is falsifiable in principle but currently unsupported by evidence that discriminates it from plausible alternatives, including drift, constraint, and byproduct hypotheses of the sort associated with the spandrels critique; that the evidential bar for adaptive explanations should be asymmetric -- higher than for other historical hypotheses -- because of some special feature of natural selection as an explanatory resource; that the hypothesis confuses a trait's current utility with its origin; or that there is something illicit about reasoning from function to history at all. I am particularly unsure whether the last of these collapses into one of the others or is genuinely a separate claim.

Finally: my suspicion is that the charge is doing different work in different mouths -- that sometimes it is a request for a specific discriminating test, and sometimes it is the expression of a general skepticism that no evidence could satisfy. If that is right, I would like to know which readings correspond to a demand that can in principle be met, and what evidence would meet them, so that I can tell in practice whether the right response to the charge is "here is the test" or "we disagree about something more basic." A list I can bring to the next journal club would be ideal.
