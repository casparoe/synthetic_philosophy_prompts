I co-teach a college intro seminar on Chinese philosophy (with the actual prof, I'm the grad student), and we're doing a week on Wang Yangming. Every year the students read the stuff about the unity of knowledge and action and nod along like it's obviously true, or dismiss it as obviously false, and neither group seems to have actually felt why anyone would insist on it. The claim, as I understand it, is roughly that if you genuinely know something -- like that you ought to honor your parents -- you already act on it, and if you don't act, that shows the so-called knowledge was never the real thing, just secondhand verbal knowledge. Wang's own examples lean on stuff like: you can't know the smell or taste of something without actually smelling or tasting it, and nobody who truly knew filial piety the way they know a bad smell would fail to be filial.

So: design me a 75-minute activity for about 14 students that makes them run into this idea themselves before they ever hear Wang's name or the phrase "unity of knowledge and action." I want something with real structure -- rules, roles, maybe a couple of staged choices with genuinely small stakes (not money; we have like a $20 snack budget and some leftover departmental prizes) -- where students first confidently claim to know things and then get put in positions where their "knowledge" conspicuously fails to issue in action, or where what they thought they knew turns out to be just memorized verbal formulas that collapse on contact with the actual task. The students should generate the gap between knowing-that and knowing-how-to-act themselves, get a bit frustrated by it, and only afterwards have the debrief reveal that a 16th century neo-Confucian built a whole moral psychology on exactly this gap.

In the debrief section of your answer, map specific moments of the activity onto the parts of Wang's view (including how he'd handle the obvious objection "I know I should exercise more, I just don't" -- because that objection is going to come up in the first ninety seconds, guaranteed). Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time we run it -- my guess is students will treat it as a point about procrastination or weakness of will rather than about what knowledge is, but you tell me -- and how to handle it. And be honest about which features of the actual doctrine the activity can't reproduce, because I suspect the full view has a moral-spiritual self-cultivation dimension that no parlor game is going to capture, and I'd rather you flag that than pretend the activity does more than it can.
