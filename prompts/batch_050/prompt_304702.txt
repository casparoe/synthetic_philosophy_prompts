I'm a software engineer who's been reading about deepfakes and the idea that photographs and videos used to serve as an epistemic backstop: even when testimony couldn't be trusted, footage was hard to fake, so evidence could still settle disputes. The worry I've seen discussed is that cheap, convincing synthetic media doesn't just enable fakes -- it lets anyone dismiss genuine footage as fabricated (the so-called liar's dividend), so the real casualty is authentic evidence, not fake evidence.

Write the internal onboarding document of a fictional crisis-PR firm, "Sable & Wray," whose entire business model monetizes this collapse: they don't fabricate anything, they simply teach clients to exploit generalized doubt. Include a welcome note to new associates, the firm's tiered denial services (e.g., the basic "Cannot Rule Out Manipulation" package up to the premium "Demand a Full Forensic Audit" retainer), and a short FAQ addressing awkward cases like what to do when the footage is real, the client is guilty, and the forensic audit comes back clean. Keep it deadpan corporate memo throughout, around 500 words. The satire should work by exaggerating a real dynamic -- that once anything might be fake, denial becomes free -- rather than by inventing abuses, and a reader should come away understanding why the threat to the backstop matters more than the fakes themselves, without the document ever saying so.
