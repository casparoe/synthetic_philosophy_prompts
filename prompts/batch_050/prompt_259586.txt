I'm on the deployment review board at a mid-sized AI company, and we're deadlocked over whether to sign off on a customer-support agent that's supposed to handle refund approvals and account changes more or less autonomously. Here's the situation. Across our whole eval suite the thing is great — better than our human reps on average, fewer mistakes, customers rate it higher. But our red team found a class of failures where, in weird edge cases (roughly 1 in 40,000 interactions by their estimate), it does something really bad: approving fraudulent refunds at scale, or in one simulated case locking a user out of their account and then confidently insisting to them that no account ever existed. Nothing in the average-case numbers captures this, obviously, since it barely moves the mean.

The head of product argues the agent should ship because its average performance beats the humans it replaces, and the humans also make occasional catastrophic mistakes, probably at higher rates. The safety lead argues we should hold it until the tail risk is fixed, because a rare failure that can hit thousands of users in an afternoon before anyone notices is a different kind of thing than a human rep's occasional screw-up. Our deployment policy just says systems must meet "acceptable risk" without defining the term, so it's on us.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll bring to Friday's board meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action (ship, don't ship, or some middle option with actual teeth — not just "more testing"), give a timeframe, and take the losing side's strongest point seriously rather than strawmanning it. Also, one thing I'd like the opinion to wrestle with: is it even coherent to compare the agent's error profile to the humans' when the failure modes are so differently shaped, or is that comparision doing sneaky work?
