You are a political theorist specializing in democratic institutional design. A colleague who edits a policy magazine has asked you to vet the central argument of a draft op-ed. Think carefully, and begin by sketching the argument's structure before evaluating it.

The op-ed's author reasons as follows: we already entrust randomly selected ordinary citizens with the gravest decisions the state makes, since a jury of twelve can send a person to prison for life without anyone demanding that jurors pass exams, campaign for the role, or be vetted for expertise; therefore, if the lottery is good enough for the courtroom, it is good enough for the legislature, and a parliament chosen by lot, with members serving a single non-renewable term, would abolish career politicians at a stroke. As historical warrant, the author quotes Aristotle's Politics (IV.9, 1294b): "it is thought to be democratic for the offices to be assigned by lot, for them to be elected oligarchic" — reading this as proof that the lottery, not the ballot box, is the genuinely popular instrument of rule.

Audit the jury-to-legislature analogy. Give an explicit correspondence table (juror to allotted legislator, verdict to statute, trial to legislative term, and so on), then identify where the mapping breaks: consider at minimum that juries apply settled law to particular facts under a judge's direction while legislatures make general law and set their own agenda; that jury service is short, one-shot, and answerable to no electorate, whereas longer allotted terms create incentives to professionalize; and that single non-renewable terms may eliminate career politicians while merely relocating career power to permanent staff, agencies, and lobbyists. Finally, state which of the author's conclusions survive the audit: (a) that our jury practice already licenses lotteries for high-stakes political decision-making, and (b) that sortition plus strict rotation ends careerist politics rather than displacing it. Does Aristotle's classification of lot as democratic and election as oligarchic rest on the jury analogy at all, or on a different argument — and if different, is it stronger or weaker than the one the op-ed actually makes?
