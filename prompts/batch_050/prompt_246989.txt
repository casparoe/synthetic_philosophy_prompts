I am drafting a paper on the ethics of war and need a thought experiment that runs the debate over the moral equality of combatants in the opposite of its usual direction. On the traditional picture associated with Michael Walzer, soldiers on both sides of a war are moral equals: whatever the justice of their cause, combatants are permitted to kill enemy combatants and are legitimate targets themselves. On the revisionist picture associated with Jeff McMahan, there is no such equality: permissibility tracks liability, and liability tracks responsibility for an objectively unjustified threat, so in the standard example the unjust combatant is liable to be killed while the just combatant is not. Every textbook case I can find illustrates the view in that comfortable direction. I want a single case in which the revisionist view licenses the inversion: an unjust-side combatant is permitted to kill a just-side combatant, and the just-side combatant is not permitted to kill back -- while the traditional view, surveying the same facts, still treats the two as equals who may each kill the other.

Construct the scenario to these specifications. It must be realistic: a fictional present-day conventional war described in ordinary terms, a single engagement, one paragraph, no futuristic technology and no stipulation of the moral verdicts (the facts have to generate the liability, not announce it). Soldier A fights for the side whose war is, by ordinary lights, clearly just, but in this engagement A is liable to defensive killing on the revisionist view -- and I want his liability to arise without culpability on his part, so that the case tests the revisionist's distinctive claim that one can be liable through a fully excused, non-culpable choice. Soldier B fights for the aggressor side and must be a genuine participant in the unjust war effort -- not a medic, not a defector, not someone whose duties happen never to touch the fighting -- yet in this engagement B is not liable to be killed on the revisionist view, because the only person B threatens is A, who is liable. You will probably find, as I have, that the cleanest construction makes B's particular act on this occasion a defensive one: the unjust side's soldier doing, locally and for once, something objectively just.

After the scenario, demonstrate that it works. State the verdict each of the two views delivers about both killings (B killing A, A killing B) and derive each verdict from the view's principles rather than asserting it. Identify the load-bearing feature of the case -- I suspect it is the presence of non-liable third parties endangered by A's mission -- and show what happens if it is removed: confirm that the case then collapses back into the standard pattern, with B liable and A permitted to kill him, since this is what convinces me the scenario isolates the right variable. Say whether A's excused ignorance is essential or whether the case survives if A knows exactly what he is doing. Then give the traditionalist's strongest objection to the case -- for instance, that it licenses the killing of a soldier who did everything his evidence and duties required of him, or that it collapses the distinction between the justice of the war and the justice of conduct within it in a way soldiers could never apply in the field -- and sketch the revisionist's best reply. Close with your own assessment: is the inversion this case produces a reductio of the liability account, or a bullet it should proudly bite?
