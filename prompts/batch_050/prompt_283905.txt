I was appointed chair of the risk and conduct committee of a regulated prediction market six weeks ago, and the first dispute of my tenure has split the committee. Contracts on our exchange pay $1 if the listed outcome occurs. In a market on the next parliamentary election — one contract for each of seven parties, plus a contract that pays out if any other party wins — our in-house market-making algorithm malfunctioned after a software update. For roughly forty seconds, the YES prices it quoted across these eight mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive contracts summed to $1.18. Each individual price was within the day's trading range and unremarkable on its own, but the set was jointly incoherent: anyone selling one unit of every contract would collect $1.18 in premiums against a certain $1 obligation at settlement. An outside firm's automated system detected exactly that and repeated the trade until we halted the market, locking in about $200,000 of riskless profit. Our terms make trades final except in cases of "manifest error," a phrase our rules have never defined, and we have no precedent to work from.

The firm argues that no single price was manifestly erroneous, that the excess was visible only to someone watching all eight order books simultaneously, and that arbitrage of precisely this kind is the service that keeps our prices honest; voiding would punish the party that corrected our market and teach future arbitrageurs that we renege when we lose. Our desk replies that no rational agent would simultaneously stand behind that set of prices, since holding the set guarantees a loss under every possible outcome, and that a machine's momentary incoherence is the paradigm of manifest error even when every component price is defensible. Our counsel says both readings of the clause are legally available, so the merits are ours to decide.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before Friday's vote. It should recommend one of three options — void the trades in full, honor them in full, or adopt a materiality rule voiding only trades executed while the summed price exceeded $1 by a specified margin (the sum in fact drifted from $1.06 to $1.18 over the window) — and it should reason from the logic of Dutch book arguments rather than merely gesturing at them: whether coherence constrains a price set as a whole or each price separately; whether the defect lies in the incoherence itself or only in its actual exploitation, given that the mispricing would likely have corrected itself within the minute had no one traded; and whether voiding trades in the name of coherence is in tension with the Dutch book idea itself, since the sure loss is supposed to be the sanction that gives the norm its force. The memo should state plainly which consideration on the losing side is strongest, and what standard each option would commit us to in future cases.
