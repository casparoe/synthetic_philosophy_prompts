I was discussing George Eliot with a friend and floated a reading that I now want stress-tested: Eliot’s deepest objection to evangelical religion is not that its doctrines are false, nor that religious feeling is intrinsically irrational, but that it can create a morally dangerous form of epistemic insulation. On my reading, evangelical authority becomes especially corrosive when it lets a preacher frame disagreement as infidelity, convert moral complacency into piety, and rehearse objections without ever exposing himself to a real opponent. The problem is therefore institutional and psychological before it is theological: religion becomes a technology for protecting mediocrity, vanity, and cruelty from correction. I realize Eliot does not treat all religion this way, which is why I’m describing this as one strand of her view rather than her whole theory of religion.

The passage prompting this is from Eliot’s essay “Evangelical Teaching: Dr. Cumming”: “But the preacher is completely master of the situation: no one may hiss, no one may depart. Like the writer of imaginary conversations, he may put what imbecilities he pleases into the mouths of his antagonists, and swell with triumph when he has refuted them. He may riot in gratuitous assertions, confident that no man will contradict him; he may exercise perfect free-will in logic, and invent illustrative experience; he may give an evangelical edition of history with the inconvenient facts omitted:—all this he may do with impunity, certain that those of his hearers who are not sympathizing are not listening.”

What would count against my interpretation? Don’t just say that Eliot elsewhere portrays sincere or admirable believers. I want the strongest possible defeaters, ranked from potentially fatal to merely awkward. For each one, explain whether the problem comes from this essay itself, from Eliot’s fiction and other nonfiction, from biographical or historical context, or from my conceptual claim about “epistemic insulation.” In particular, would it undermine the reading if Eliot’s target is Cumming’s personal mediocrity rather than evangelical institutions? If her criticism depends on theological assumptions I’ve missed? If the sympathetic religious characters in her fiction are themselves epistemically insulated? Or if the passage is mainly satire and cannot support a general diagnosis? Also distinguish what a defender of my reading should honestly concede from what looks threatening but can be absorbed without changing the core interpretation.
