Hey — I'm working on a piece for our magazine's ideas desk about recommendation algorithms (think TikTok's For You feed, Spotify autoplay, YouTube's up-next queue) and I keep hitting a framing problem. The standard pushback I get in interviews and comment sections is that these systems leave users perfectly free: nothing is banned, nothing is blocked, you can always just close the app. In other words, the classic negative-liberty defense — no interference, no unfreedom. But a political theorist I spoke with said the better lens is freedom as non-domination: you're unfree when some agent has the standing capacity to push you around on an arbitrary basis, even if it never actually does.

So here's my ask: argue that a well-tuned recommender system can dominate its users in this republican sense even though it never removes a single option from the menu. I want the strongest version of that case — including why quietly steering what people attend to and come to want might be worse, freedom-wise, than openly forbidding things — and then deal with the obvious rejoinder that the system is just code, not a master, so talk of domination is a category error. If you need to fill in plausible details about how these systems work (predictive models, feedback loops, engagement metrics), go ahead, just keep it realistic.
