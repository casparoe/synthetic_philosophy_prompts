I'm running a discussion night for our AI reading group and I want to make one of those "which position on LLM understanding do you secretly hold" sorting quizzes. 10ish questions, each built around a concrete scenario with 3 or 4 answer options, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions.

The positions that need to show up as possible outcomes:
- the hard syntax line: no amount of text-only symbol pushing could ever be understanding, no matter how good the behavior gets
- the grounding view: text-only models can't understand because their words aren't causally hooked up to the world, but a robot or multimodal system with the right perceptual links could be
- the functionalist view: if the internal organization and behavioral competence are rich enough, that just IS understanding, and it doesnt matter what the training data was
- the phenomenal view: understanding requires consciousness, so the whole question turns on whether there's anything it's like to be the model, not on what it can do
- the deflationist view: "understand" is a grab-bag of different capacities, LLMs plainly have some and lack others, and asking yes-or-no is a category mistake

Cases I want used as question material (you can add one or two of your own if they do real work):
- a model that learns chess purely from millions of written transcripts of games, never seeing a board or piece, and ends up beating strong players -- does it know what a pin is?
- an octopus-style setup: a system trained only on intercepted messages between two humans, one stuck on a desert island describing it to the other, that gets so good it can impersonate the islander flawlessly -- where does its "knowledge" of coconuts live, if anywhere?
- a model given a made-up word defined only by its role in one paragraph ("a fleem is whatever you use to...") that then reasons correctly about fleems in novel situations, including counterfactuals nobody spelled out
- a version of the chess model where we open it up and find its internal states track board geometry in a decodable way -- should that change anyone's verdict?

Rules of the quiz: no question or answer option may use the words "semantics," "grounding," "Chinese room," "functionalism," or name any position -- everything has to be answerable from gut reaction to the case, so people get sorted by what they believe rather than by which label sounds smart. Each option should be phrased as what you'd say about the case ("yes, it knows, because..."), not as a theory.

In the scoring key: note any answer combination that none of the five positions endorses, and say whether that combination looks incoherent or just unexplored. And flag any pair of positions the quiz can't reliably tell apart rather than pretending the questions separate them.
