I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on methods in ethics, where we've studied reflective equilibrium as a method of justification -- the back-and-forth adjustment between a person's judgments about particular cases and the general principles that might systematize them, in its narrower and wider versions -- along with the standard objections, including the worry that the method merely launders a person's existing prejudices into a coherent package. I also work as a tutor at my university's writing center, and during essay season a large share of my 45-minute sessions are students in introductory ethics courses drafting papers that defend a moral thesis. These papers almost always contain the same defect: the student's thesis, the principle they invoke, and their own stated judgments about cases do not fit together, and the advice my training gives me -- tell them to make the argument consistent -- does not tell either of us which part should change.

I want to turn reflective equilibrium into a procedure I can actually run in a session, without re-deriving the theory each time and without requiring the student to have read any philosophy beyond their course. Please write the procedure as an ordered sequence of steps a tutor can follow: what to elicit first (the case judgments, the principle, or the thesis, and why that order), what artifact each step should produce on paper, how to distinguish a genuine conflict from a merely apparent one (e.g., a judgment that needs its scope specified rather than revised), a decision rule for what to revise when a conflict is real -- the case judgment, the principle, or the way the case is described -- and a stopping rule that tells me when further adjustment is no longer a coherence repair but a substantive philosophical choice the student has to make for themselves. The procedure should end with what I write in the session notes my supervisor requires.

Two constraints matter and should shape the design. First, our writing center has a non-directiveness policy: I am not supposed to substitute my own moral views for the student's. Please identify which steps of the procedure genuinely let me stay neutral, and which steps secretly cannot, since my sense of which judgments count as settled and which principles count as candidates will leak in somewhere. Second, mark explicitly the points where judgment cannot be eliminated from the procedure -- where following it correctly still requires a discretionary call -- rather than hiding those points behind mechanical language.

Then explain which features of the full philosophical method have to survive this simplification and which can be safely dropped, with a reason for each call. For instance: does the distinction between narrow and wide equilibrium matter in a 45-minute session, or is it an unaffordable luxury? Does the requirement that judgments be considered rather than offhand survive, and if so, what is a tutor-level operational test for it? Can the conservatism objection be addressed procedurally at all, or must I simply flag it in the notes?

Apply the procedure to these three sample papers, showing the steps running:

1. A student argues that eating factory-farmed meat is wrong because it is wrong to cause avoidable suffering to sentient beings, but elsewhere in the same paper says that humane free-range meat is permissible, that lethal animal research is justified when it produces medicines that save human lives, and that killing insects in one's home needs no justification.
2. A student defends a strict consequentialism about punishment and, when the counterexample of framing an innocent person to prevent riots comes up in her own paper, bites the bullet and says framing the innocent would in that case be right. The paper is, as far as I can tell, internally coherent. I find the conclusion abhorrent, and I suspect the honest output of the procedure is that there is nothing for me to fix -- an answer I will not like. Show whether the procedure in fact gives that answer, and what I am permitted to do next.
3. A student writing on obligations to future generations holds judgments and principles that conflict in a way admitting two symmetric repairs, with nothing internal to the paper favoring one over the other. Show where the procedure stops and what I tell the student.

Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: which defects in a paper it will systematically miss, and which of my tutoring failures it would be a mistake to blame on it.

End each worked application with a line beginning "Verdict:" stating in a short phrase what the procedure directs me to do in that session, and end the entire response with a final line beginning "Summary:" giving one sentence on what a session-level reflective equilibrium procedure can and cannot establish.
