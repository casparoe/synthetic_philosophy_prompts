The question I set:

"Epicurus argues that death is not bad for the person who dies. Reconstruct his argument, making explicit the assumption about value that it depends on. Then set out the two main Epicurean challenges to the common-sense view that death is bad for us — the timing problem and the symmetry argument — and assess how well the deprivationist response (death is bad because it deprives the person of goods they would otherwise have had) answers each of them."

The student's answer:

"Epicurus's argument runs as follows. All good and bad consist in experience: we are hedonic creatures for whom pleasure is the only good and pain the only bad. Death is the end of all experience. While we are alive, death has not yet arrived, and once it has arrived, we no longer exist to experience anything at all. So death is nothing to us, neither good nor bad for the one who dies. The argument depends on what we might call the experience requirement: nothing can be good or bad for you unless it affects your experience.

Two problems threaten the ordinary view that death is bad. First, the symmetry argument: the period of nonexistence after death is exactly like the period of nonexistence before birth, and since nobody regards the latter as a misfortune, consistency demands that we stop regarding the former as one. Epicurus himself pressed this point by asking us to treat the stretch of time before our birth and the stretch after our death as equivalent. Second, the timing problem: if death is bad for someone, there must be a time at which it is bad for them, but there is no such time — not before death, since it hasn't happened yet, and not after, since there is no subject left to be harmed.

The deprivationist replies that death is bad because it deprives the person of goods they would otherwise have enjoyed; the badness is comparative, a matter of the actual life being worse than the life that would have been lived. Nagel defended something like this with the example of the man betrayed by his friends behind his back — he suffers a misfortune without ever experiencing it, so the experience requirement must be false. On the symmetry argument, the deprivationist can note that we are biased toward the future: we care about goods in our future in a way we simply don't about the past, so the two periods of nonexistence aren't on a par after all. This asymmetry of concern also dissolves the timing problem, since the comparison locates the harm in the future goods the person would have had.

Overall the deprivationist reply seems to work, though it leaves open whether death is always bad or only usually bad."
