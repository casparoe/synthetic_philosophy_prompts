So I'm drafting a short essay for my blog (and maybe eventually a philosophy of AI reading group I'm in) and I'd love some comments before I keep going. The topic is the whole "tool AI vs agent AI" thing. Everyone keeps saying tools are safe because they just do what you ask, and agents are dangerous because they pursue their own goals. My essay argues this distinction is mostly confused. Here's the core of what I've got so far:

My claim is that being a "tool" or an "agent" isn't a property of the AI system at all -- it's a property of the relationship between the system and its user, and specifically of what I call revocability: who can still take the goal back. A chess engine that suggests moves is a tool because you can ignore every suggestion at zero cost. But now imagine a hiring pipeline where the model screens resumes and the humans have, over three years, quietly stopped reading them. Technically a human still signs off on every rejection. But the sign-off is theater, because the reviewers no longer have the skills or the time to second-guess the screen. I'd argue this system is an agent in every sense that matters, even though the architecture never changed. And the reverse holds too: a super autonomous long-horizon agent that a watchful human can interrupt, redirect, and roll back at any moment is functionally a tool. So on my view the tool/agent line tracks revocability, and revocability has two seperate ingredients: practical (can you actually stop it?) and epistemic (would you even know when to?). The scary failure mode isn't rogue goals, it's what I call "quiet delegation" -- the slow, rational, individually harmless process by which the epistemic ingredient erodes. You can't revoke a goal you've forgotten how to pursue yourself.

Where I want comments: (1) Does this argument actually dissolve the tool/agent distinction, or does it just relocate it -- i.e., am I smuggling agent-ness back in through the word "revocability"? I have a nagging feeling that a critic could say the epistemic ingredient is doing all the work and that it secretly presupposes the very notion of an independent goal-pursuer I claim to have eliminated. (2) Is the practical/epistemic split clean, or do the two collapse into each other on closer inspection? (3) What would you say is the single weakest inferential step in the essay, stated as steelmannedly as you can? Feel free to be brutal, and if you think the whole framing is off, say that instead of line-editing a doomed argument.
