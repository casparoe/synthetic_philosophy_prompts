I am a retired experimental physicist who, over the past two years, has been making my way through the classics of philosophy and social theory, and I now help run a fortnightly reading group at our local adult education institute (about a dozen members, mostly fellow retirees from technical professions, 90 minutes per session). We have just finished Weber's Protestant ethic essay, and I found myself stuck on a problem my training makes it hard to let go of: Weber wants a set of values -- the calling, ascetic self-denial, worldly success as a sign of election -- to do genuine causal work in the rise of capitalism, yet every value one might cite arrives entangled with interests, institutions, and incentives, so how could one ever tell whether the culture caused the conduct or merely decorated it? I would like to design an activity for our group that makes participants feel this identification problem in their own behaviour rather than just hear about it. My current thought is some kind of trading or accumulation game in which subgroups are given different framings or declared norms, after which the group must argue -- from behavioural evidence alone -- whether the norms caused the divergence in outcomes or whether the payoff structure did, with the facilitator having secretly arranged things so that the honest answer is underdetermined. Please design this activity properly: rules and materials, what each subgroup is and is not told, how to stage the post-game inquest so that the causal ambiguity actually bites, and a debrief that connects what happened to Weber's thesis and to the general problem of explaining with values, including one point where the analogy to the historical case breaks down. Present the whole design as a numbered list of exactly five items -- (1) setup and materials, (2) rules, (3) running order with rough timings, (4) the debrief and its mapping onto the thesis, (5) predictable failure modes and the analogy's limits -- with each item consisting of two to four sentences and no introduction or conclusion.
