I am designing cases for a seminar on coherence arguments for expected utility maximization. A colleague classifies agents as rationally criticizable or not, claiming to assess only the structure of their preferences and choices, never the substantive value of what they want. Her verdicts so far are:

1. An agent strictly prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, and will pay a small fee to trade B for A, C for B, and A for C: criticizable.
2. An agent has the same cyclic strict preferences but will not pay anything to make those exchanges; when forced to choose from {A, B, C}, she always selects A: not criticizable.
3. An agent has complete, transitive, and continuous preferences over lotteries but violates the independence axiom in an Allais-style pattern; there is no predictable plan reversal or opportunity to extract a sure loss from her: not criticizable.
4. An agent satisfies the standard static axioms for expected utility, chooses one plan over another, and then, after an event she agrees provides no relevant information, switches to the plan she had rejected; because the reversal is predictable, another person can charge her small fees for arranging both stages: criticizable.
5. An agent chooses an act that yields a worse outcome in every possible state than another available act: criticizable.
6. An agent declines to rank two options, but her choices are stable, non-cyclic, and never generate a loss: not criticizable.

The next case is this. An agent’s preferences at each time are complete, transitive, continuous, independent, and respectful of dominance. Before an uninformative signal is received, she chooses plan X over plan Y. She also predicts that, once the signal arrives, the mere fact that Y is immediately available will lead her to switch to Y, although the signal will provide no information about outcomes and she will not regard her ends as having changed. She does switch. Nobody can charge her for the reversal, no sequence of accepted trades produces a guaranteed loss, and the final outcome is not statewise dominated.

Predict my colleague’s verdict. In doing so, identify at least two general rules that are each compatible with all six earlier verdicts but diverge on this case—for example, a rule requiring protection from sure loss or exploitation and a rule requiring diachronic consistency of plans. State which precedent is closest, which rule best explains the whole track record, and what further case would most decisively distinguish the leading rule from its nearest competitor. Also indicate how confident you are in the prediction, where your reconstruction of her standard is most likely to fail, and what additional verdict or clarification would change your conclusion.
