I'm TAing an intro phil of mind course, about 30 undergrads, and I get one 75-minute session on functionalism. Reading Putnam and a bit of Block hasn't landed -- they nod along to "mental states are functional states" but I don't think they feel what's weird about it, i.e. that the same mind could in principle run on neurons, silicon, or a bunch of people passing notes, and that it's genuinely unclear where "running it" stops and "faking it" starts.

Design me an activity that makes them collide with that. Rough idea I had: split them into groups and give each group the same little "mind" -- some simple spec, like a thing with a few internal states that takes inputs (sees red, hears a loud noise, hasn't eaten) and produces outputs (approaches, flees, says something) according to transition rules. Each group has to implement the same spec in a completely different medium: one group with people playing the internal states and passing tokens, one with paper slips and boxes, one with hand signals or whatever. The rest of the class only sees the inputs and outputs and has to guess which groups are "running the same mind."

The part I actually want is the complications. I want at least one group secretly told to implement the spec with a giant lookup table -- a precomputed list of every possible input history and what to output -- no internal states at all, just consulting the list. And one group whose rules are subtly gerrymandered so that what counts as "one state" for them corresponds to two states in the original spec, so the individuation of states gets messy. When the lookup-table group produces perfect input-output behavior, I want the class to have to actually argue about whether that group had the mind or just simulated its behavior, without me telling them the answer in advance. That's basically Block's China-body/lookup-table worry but I want them to generate it themselves before they hear any names.

So give me: the actual spec for the little mind (simple enough to run live but rich enough that the lookup table is visibly absurd -- like the table gets huge fast), the rules and materials for each implementation, how to structure the guessing part so it's fun, a timeline for the 75 minutes, and the debrief where I map what happened onto multiple realizability, realization vs simulation, and the state-individuation problem. Also tell me what's going to go wrong the first time -- e.g. groups improvising instead of following the rules, the token-passing group losing track of state -- and how to handle it. And be honest about which part of the issue the activity can't reproduce (my guess: anything about whether functional organization is *enough* for experience, as opposed to just behavior).
