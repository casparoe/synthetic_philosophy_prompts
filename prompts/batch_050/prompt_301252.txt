In my metaphysics seminar on causation, a classmate circulated an argument meant to establish that omissions are never causes of anything, and although none of us liked the conclusion, nobody could cleanly locate the failure. Here is the argument as he wrote it. (1) Causation is a relation that holds, when it holds, between actual events — concrete occurrences in the world's history. (2) An omission is not an event: when Priya forgets to water the orchid she promised to tend, nothing happens that could serve as a relatum; there is only the orchid's going unwatered, which is no occurrence at all. (3) So when we say that Priya's failure to water caused the orchid's death, either the claim is false or the real causal relata are actual events — the dry soil, the heat, the orchid's cellular breakdown — and the mention of Priya's omission does some other, non-causal work: registering that she ought to have watered it, or pointing to what would have prevented the death. (4) What goes for Priya goes for every case: wherever we speak of an omission as a cause, the causal work is done entirely by positive events, and the omission-mention merely signals a violated norm or supplies a contrast. (5) Therefore no omission ever causes anything. The reason I cannot simply accept (5) is that the argument does not stop where my classmate wants it to: if (2) is right, then absences generally — the lack of oxygen in the cabin, the missing enzyme, the food shipment that never arrived — are likewise causally inert, since they are no more events than Priya's omission is, and I am not willing to say that a child's starvation causes nothing, or that a parent's failure to feed her is not among the causes of her death.

Tell me which premise or step I should reject, and do the full diagnostic work. Explain why the rejected premise looked plausible in the first place — I suspect part of the appeal of (1) and (2) is a picture of causation as physical production, and I would like that picture articulated rather than caricatured — and then price the alternatives: what it costs to deny (1) by letting negative items such as absences, failures, and non-occurrences into the causal relata; what it costs to deny (3) by holding that omission-talk does genuinely causal work even if omissions are not events; and what it costs to accept (4) while resisting (5) by treating some absences as causes and others not, and on what principled basis (norms, contrasts, counterfactual dependence?) one could draw that line. In particular, address what I take to be the real test of any fix: whether it avoids profligate causation. If Priya's omission caused the orchid's death, did my failure to water it — and a stranger's in São Paulo, and every other non-waterer's — cause it too? I want to know which response to the argument handles this selection problem best, and whether any response handles it without simply helping itself to the very normative facts about what Priya was supposed to do that my classmate will insist are non-causal.

Write this as someone who works on the metaphysics of causation, and think carefully through each horn before recommending one. Before settling on a diagnosis, briefly sketch the strongest version of every option — including the option of accepting (5) outright and explaining away our ordinary omission-judgments as pragmatics — so that I can see the costs side by side rather than receiving only the winning view. Where the state of the debate matters, distinguish what is broadly agreed upon (for instance, that any adequate account must explain why one omission among millions gets singled out as the cause) from what remains genuinely contested (for instance, whether causation by absence is real causation or a useful fiction layered on top of it), and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus.
