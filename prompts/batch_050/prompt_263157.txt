I teach a third-year undergraduate course in reasoning and evidence, 28 students, one 80-minute session, and I want an activity on Simpson's paradox that goes beyond the standard "always disaggregate" moral. Design a game in which students, playing as a hospital advisory board with point-based incentives rather than money, first commit publicly to choosing between two treatments on the basis of aggregate recovery rates you supply, then watch the recommendation reverse when you release the same data disaggregated by patient severity; the rules should make the initial choice feel rational so the reversal stings. Then run a second, structurally parallel round in which disaggregating by a different variable (one that is downstream of the treatment rather than a pre-existing patient characteristic) also reverses the ordering — but this time the aggregate answer was the right one, so that the group discovers empirically that "disaggregate whenever you can" is just as fallible a rule as "trust the totals," and that only the causal role of the grouping variable, not the arithmetic, tells you which table to believe. The paradox must be enacted by the payoff structure and the staged release of information, not explained up front, and the term should not be mentioned until the debrief. Present the design as a numbered list of exactly six items — materials and setup, round-one rules including the full payoff tables with invented but self-consistent numbers, the first reveal and how to handle students who immediately generalize the wrong lesson, round-two rules including its payoff tables, a debrief that maps each stage of play onto the statistical and causal structure, and an honest account of what will predictably go wrong on a first run — each item a sentence or two plus whatever tables it requires, with no introduction and no conclusion.
