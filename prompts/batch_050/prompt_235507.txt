I have spent the past year trying to settle my views on truth, and the classical pragmatists are where I keep ending up. I would like an audit of the position I have arrived at, because I suspect it contains fractures I cannot see from the inside. My anchor text is Lecture VI, "Pragmatism's Conception of Truth," of William James's Pragmatism: A New Name for Some Old Ways of Thinking (1907), where James writes:

"True ideas are those that we can assimilate, validate, corroborate and verify. False ideas are those that we can not. That is the practical difference it makes to us to have true ideas; that, therefore, is the meaning of truth, for it is all that truth is known-as."

My commitments, as best I can state them:

1. I take James at his word in this passage and throughout the lecture: truth is not a standing property an idea possesses in itself but something that accrues to it through processes of verification, and an idea no one could ever verify lacks the only thing truth is known as.

2. With Peirce, I hold that reality is independent of what you, I, or any finite community happen to think, and that the true opinion is the one that inquiry, pursued without limit, would finally converge upon. I take this convergence picture to be continuous with, not rival to, the Jamesian view in (1).

3. I continue to accept the correspondence formula: a true idea is one that agrees with reality, and I read James as fully endorsing this and merely offering an account of what such agreement consists in experientially.

4. I am a realist about the deep past. Whether the number of leaves that fell in a particular Himalayan valley in 10,000 BC was even or odd is, I take it, determinately true or false right now, even though every trace is gone and no process of verification, actual or ideal, present or future, could ever settle it.

5. I deny that utility is ever constitutive of truth. A belief that comforts the believer, organizes her life, or helps her cope can still be simply false, and its usefulness contributes nothing to making it true.

Does this package hang together? Please identify the genuine tensions, ordered by severity, and for each state precisely which commitment would have to be given up or weakened and at what cost to the rest. Distinguish these from any merely apparent tensions that dissolve on a careful reading of the lecture itself, and explain the reading that dissolves them. Finally, describe the cheapest single revision that would restore consistency, and be honest about what that revision commits me to. I am not asking for a survey of pragmatism; I want to know what my own five theses commit me to, and I have no prior preference about which of them survives.
