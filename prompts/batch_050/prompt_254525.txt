Our city council has been arguing about sortition for weeks, and I want to make an April Fools packet that is funny because it takes the idea completely seriously. Write a deadpan municipal FAQ and new-resident guide, around 900 words, for the fictional city of Bellwether, which has abolished council elections and replaced them with a Civic Lottery. Every resident over sixteen is automatically entered; forty-nine assembly members are drawn with stratification by ward, age, and whether they rent or own; they serve staggered eighteen-month paid terms; and anyone selected is legally excused from jury duty because they are already doing enough. Ordinary residents can petition items onto the agenda, but city staff and facilitators control the briefing materials. A proposal has to survive two separate randomly drawn bodies: a drafting panel that hears evidence and a review panel that can send it back, delay it, or pass it. Assembly members cannot campaign, accept gifts, promise future favors, or be reselected for twenty years, and they can be recalled only by yet another citizens panel that judges whether they violated procedure, never whether people dislike their decisions.

Format it like a real city website, with sections such as What if I am selected?, Can I decline?, How do I address my assembly member?, What if my assembly member is clearly incompetent?, and a cheerful sidebar for visitors attending a public session. The comedy should come from bureaucratic calm colliding with genuine democratic problems: political equality versus competence, descriptive representation versus accountability, deliberation versus agenda control by staff, local knowledge versus captured expertise, and the weirdness of being governed by neighbors who cannot be rewarded, threatened, primaried, or voted out. Please don't turn it into a lazy joke about ordinary people being stupid, and don't make sortition magically solve politics either. Include at least one moment where the lottery system handles a conflict more fairly than elections plausibly would, followed by another where its legitimacy starts to feel disturbingly abstract. No citations, theorists, or explicit philosophical labels; the reader should recognize the arguments only because the FAQ keeps tripping over them.
