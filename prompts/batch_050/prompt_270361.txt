I've been reading LessWrong and the Sequences-adjacent literature for years, so my native vocabulary is Bayesian: priors, likelihood ratios, calibration, prediction markets, Dutch books, and the slogan that probability is in the mind rather than in the coin. In a recent forum thread I watched the old question resurface -- can a finite sequence of outcomes itself be random, or is randomness only ever a property of the process or distribution that generated it? People kept invoking Kolmogorov complexity and Martin-Löf randomness, pointing out that a string of a million alternating heads and tails feels nonrandom in a way a typical jumbled string doesn't, even though both have the same probability under fair-coin assumptions. I have a rough grasp of the incompressibility idea, but I keep tripping over the fact that the whole apparatus seems to describe objects (strings) whereas everything I trust myself to reason with describes bets and beliefs.

So here's what I want: restate the algorithmic-randomness position -- roughly, that a finite sequence can be random in itself, to a degree, via incompressibility or the failure of effective statistical tests -- entirely in the vocabulary I actually use. Translate each piece: randomness-as-incompressibility into something about compression as a forecasting task or a betting scheme; the statistical-test characterization into claims about whether any forecaster in some class could systematically win against the sequence; the dependence on a choice of universal machine into whatever it becomes when the translator is an agent with a prior. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the position survive the translation cleanly, which parts you had to distort or quietly strengthen to fit (I'm suspicious that the move from "no computable bettor profits" to "this string is random" smuggles in something my framework doesn't license), and which parts have no expression at all in betting-and-belief talk.

Finally, tell me what to make of the residue. If something in the algorithmic-randomness picture can't be said in the language of priors and payoffs, is that a genuine limitation of the Bayesian vocabulary -- evidence that there are mind-independent facts about strings that no amount of coherence constraints on credences will capture -- or is it a sign that the in-itself talk was never fully coherent and the only real content was the forecasting claim all along? I'd rather get an honest verdict than an ecumenical one, so if you think one framework is just missing something the other has, say so.
