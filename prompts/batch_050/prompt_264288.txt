My grandmother has moderate dementia and it turns out she signed an advance directive years ago, while she was still sharp, saying she wouldn't want antibiotics for pneumonia once she stopped recognizing family. Now she's at that point and she's honestly pretty content day to day, which has reopened the whole fight. Whenever this comes up -- in our family, and in the stuff my mom's hospital ethics person sends her -- someone reaches for the Ulysses story: she tied herself to the mast in advance, we have to respect her earlier self over her current self, case closed. Other people push back with a different comparison, that the directive is more like a will, and wills govern property, not people.

I want an audit of how far the Ulysses analogy actually holds here, because I suspect it does less work than people think. Like: what maps onto what? In the story, Ulysses knows exactly what the sirens will do to his judgment and stays fully himself the whole time -- is a dementia patient in that position, or is the later self a different person in a way Ulysses isn't? Ulysses wanted to hear the singing and just not act on it; does that match what grandma wanted, or does it change the analogy? And where does it break down entirely -- past-me isn't the one living with the consequences, the way Ulysses still was? Then the will comparison too: what does each analogy quietly smuggle in about whether the earlier self has authority and why?

If either analogy genuinely supports overriding her current contentment, say so, don't just conclude the comparison is imperfect so it fails. And please be clear about what's basically settled among philosophers who work on advance directives versus what's still genuinely contested -- I don't want a contested view presented to me as the consensus, because I'll be repeating this at a family meeting and I don't want to overstate it.
