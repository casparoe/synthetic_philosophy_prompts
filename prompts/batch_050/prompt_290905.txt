As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, I've been struck by an uncomfortable resemblance between one of the community's most celebrated practices — tracking your own calibration and updating your self-trust on that basis — and what epistemologists call bootstrapping, a procedure philosophers like Stewart Cohen and Jonathan Vogel argue cannot generate knowledge. Vogel's well-known worry is that you cannot establish that an instrument is reliable by relying on that instrument's own readings alone, and Cohen extends the point to any attempt to leverage a belief-forming process into knowledge of its own reliability. Consider this case, which I think sharpens the tension: Ada, a superforecaster, keeps a meticulous prediction log for ten years. Her only means of recording outcomes is her own judgment, and her only evidence that her judgment is reliable is the log itself — yet the log shows her 70% predictions coming true almost exactly 70% of the time, across thousands of varied, seemingly independent events, with no streaks or suspicious clustering. Argue that Ada's bootstrapped inference genuinely constitutes knowledge that her judgment is reliable, despite the circularity, and diagnose precisely where the standard anti-bootstrapping arguments go wrong in her case. Present your argument as a numbered list of exactly five items, each consisting of a sentence or two of argumentative prose, with no introduction and no conclusion.
