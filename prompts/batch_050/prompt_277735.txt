I teach a second-year undergraduate course in philosophy of science, and I have reached Kuhn week with a familiar problem: my students can define incommensurability on an exam, but they all privately believe it reduces to something tame — either a translation difficulty that patience and a good glossary would solve, or an excuse for relativism that a sufficiently careful appeal to the evidence would dissolve. I was complaining about this to a colleague in the history of science, and her suggestion was that they will never feel the bite of the idea from a lecture, because the bite consists precisely in discovering, from inside a practice, that the standards you would use to settle the dispute are themselves part of what is disputed. The course has about 24 students and I have two 75-minute sessions available. I would like you to design a structured classroom activity that makes participants run into incommensurability themselves before the term is ever uttered.

Here is a seed I have been toying with, which you should feel free to adopt, modify, or replace if you can do better. I want to split the class into groups, give every group the same collection of ambiguous raw material — I had imagined a deck of cards bearing things like cloud photographs, fossil images, or short clinical case descriptions — but give each group a different set of seed concepts, sorting instructions, and criteria for what counts as a successful classification. Each group would then develop its own scheme with its own internal standards, score its own progress, and produce findings it regards as well-supported. Then comes the phase I care most about: the groups must communicate their results to one another, agree on a neutral body of evidence, and adjudicate whose scheme is better — and I want the design to be rigged, fairly and invisibly, so that this adjudication predictably collapses. Ideally there is also a later phase where new cards arrive that fit no group's scheme, so that something like an anomaly crisis and a defection can occur organically rather than by my announcement. Please give me the complete runnable design: materials, group assignments and roles, the exact rules and any incentives or scoring (nothing requiring money), a minute-by-minute timeline across the two sessions, and clear instructions for what I as facilitator should and should not say at each stage.

I also need the debrief planned out rather than improvised: a mapping from specific moments in the activity (the point where two groups use the same word for different categories, the point where evidence that is decisive under one scheme is simply inert under another, the collapse of the neutral adjudication) onto the Kuhnian notions they are meant to embody, including theory-ladenness, exemplars, anomaly, and the incommensurability of standards as distinct from mere translation failure. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I suspect at least one group will treat the whole thing as a contest to be gamed, and another will converge prematurely on a shared vocabulary — and how to handle each failure mode. Finally, be candid about which features of actual paradigm shifts the activity cannot reproduce: my worry is that a ninety-minute game cannot generate genuine entrenchment, or the way a practitioner's perceptual habits and sense of what is even salient are supposed to transform, and I would rather have that limitation flagged in the design than have my students leave thinking they have undergone a revolution.
