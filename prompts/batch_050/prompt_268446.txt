I'm TA-ing a decision theory seminar this term and my Allais lecture went badly again. I wheel out the standard gambles -- 1A a guaranteed million versus 1B an 89% shot at a million plus 10% at five million plus 1% at nothing, then 2A an 11% shot at a million versus 2B a 10% shot at five million -- everyone picks 1A and 2B, I announce they've all violated the independence axiom, and some student says "so much the worse for the axiom" and I never have a good comeback ready. So I want to rehearse. You play a committed defender of Allais-style choices: you genuinely think preferring 1A over 1B and 2B over 2A is perfectly rational, that the independence axiom is a theorem someone smuggled into the definition of rationality rather than a constraint on it, and that the pull of certainty is a legitimate attitude toward risk rather than a bias to be trained out of people. Stay in character until I say stop. Keep replies to a paragraph or so, concede small points when they're actually forced but never the main one, and please don't give me the dorm-room version of this view where you just say "imagine how stupid I'd feel walking away with nothing" -- engage with the actual argument, including the numbers.

Here's my opening move, and you have to deal with the specifics of it, not just huff at it. Take your 2B preference and run the second pair as a two-stage lottery with balls numbered 1 to 100 in an urn. Stage one: you learn only whether the drawn ball is in 1-11 or 12-100. If it's in 12-100, both options pay nothing and your choice is moot. If it's in 1-11, then 2A is now a guaranteed million and 2B is a 10/11 shot at five million -- which is just the first pair over again, the one where you took the sure thing. So ex ante you strictly prefer the 2B plan, but you can predict right now that if stage one comes up live you'll want to switch to the 2A plan. That gives me two ways to make money off you: either I sell you the 2B ticket cheap and then charge you a fee to swap it for the 2A ticket once stage one resolves (a tidy little pump), or you have to precommit, handcuff yourself to an earlier self whose judgment your later self regards as mistaken, and eat the cost of that. So which horn are you taking -- exploitable, or a slave to your past self -- and why isn't this decisive for the axiom?
