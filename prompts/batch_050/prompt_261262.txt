Below is the position statement I intend to circulate in advance of a graduate workshop on moral responsibility. Before I send it, I want a consistency audit of the five theses on which it rests:

(1) Galen Strawson's Basic Argument is sound: no one is ever ultimately morally responsible for what they do, because responsibility for an action requires responsibility for the character from which it flows, and securing that would launch an impossible regress of self-formation.

(2) Frankfurt-style cases succeed: moral responsibility does not require the ability to have done otherwise.

(3) Our ordinary practices of blame, resentment, and moral anger are entirely in order just as they stand; they presuppose nothing false, and accepting (1) gives us no reason to reform them.

(4) Desert has no justificatory force anywhere: in particular, criminal punishment is legitimate only where it carries a forward-looking rationale.

(5) Reflective adults who deliberately reshape their own dispositions — say, someone who methodically trains herself out of a cowardly temperament or into an honest one — can thereby come to be genuinely responsible for their characters.

Tell me which pairs (or triples) of these genuinely conflict and which only appear to. For each genuine conflict, say how severe it is, trace exactly which premise of which thesis does the damage, and state what revision would dissolve it and what that revision costs the overall view. If the set cannot be held as stated, identify the single change that restores consistency most cheaply. Please rank the problems by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad.
