I teach an upper-level seminar in philosophy of cognitive science (14 students, one 75-minute session in a regular classroom with movable furniture), and we are spending three weeks on enactivism and embodied cognition against classical representationalism. Design an in-class activity that makes students feel the force of the enactivist claim that skilled action can proceed without building and consulting an internal model — that continuous perceptual coupling with the environment can do work the textbook picture assumes requires stored representations.

The experience I want them to have: a strategy that encodes the environment in advance should visibly fail the moment the environment changes mid-task, while a strategy that stays coupled to the world should adapt without anything like re-planning. They should run into this themselves — I would rather they not hear the word enactivism until the debrief, and there should be no lecture up front. Give me rules and roles, materials I can assemble for under twenty dollars, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a scoring or stakes scheme that keeps both strategies honestly attempted rather than letting one team throw the comparison.

Then give me a debrief plan that maps what happened onto the actual debate: sensorimotor contingency accounts of perception, the outfielder problem as a case study, and the standard representationalist reply that some problems are representation-hungry (absent states of affairs, planning, abstraction). Include the strongest objection a sharp representationalist in the room will raise against the activity itself and what I should say to it. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and be frank about which enactivist claims the activity can illustrate and which it cannot touch.
