I am a retired experimental physicist — forty years of low-temperature work — who came to philosophy only after retiring, and I have lately been reading about the foundations of inductive inference: why we are entitled to project past regularities into the future, and why, among the infinitely many hypotheses compatible with any finite body of data, we prefer the simple ones. I keep encountering the suggestion that Solomonoff induction settles both questions at once: enumerate the computable hypotheses, weight each by two to the negative of its Kolmogorov complexity relative to some universal reference machine, and predict by the resulting mixture. The advertised virtues are that the simplicity preference falls out as a theorem rather than being postulated, and that the method converges to the truth at least as fast as any alternative in the limit.

I am not yet in a position to judge whether this is a discovery or a sales pitch, so before I invest more time I want a specification to hold it and its rivals against. Write the desiderata that a theory of inductive inference must meet to count as an adequate account of scientific prediction, with the consideration or case behind each. Please do the following.

1. Present the desiderata as a table with exactly these columns: Desideratum (one sentence, precisely stated); Ground (the case, puzzle, or argument that motivates it — for instance Goodman's grue example, the demand for guidance at finite sample sizes rather than only asymptotically, or the need to say what happens when the data-generating process lies outside the hypothesis class entirely); Standing (one of: non-negotiable; negotiable but costly to drop; preference of a particular camp, and if so name the camp); Tensions (which other rows this one strains against, if any); Confidence (high, medium, or low that this belongs on the list at all, with a half-line reason). Make sure the following appear somewhere, though you may reframe, merge, or subdivide them: deriving the simplicity preference rather than assuming it; independence from, or a principled way to live with, the arbitrary choice of reference machine or language; convergence to the truth as data accumulate; something useful to say at finite sample sizes, which is where science is actually conducted; and implementability in principle, not merely in the limit of unbounded computation. At the foot of the table, state which single desideratum you judge does the most work in excluding candidate theories.

2. Then a second table, with one row per genuine conflict you identified, and columns: Rows in conflict; Nature of the conflict (theorem, well-supported conjecture, or suspicion); Who bears the cost (which camp must give something up, and what). I am particularly interested in whether the desiderata are jointly satisfiable at all: I gather that the universal prior is uncomputable, that the invariance theorem bounds the arbitrariness of machine choice only by an additive constant, and that no-free-lunch results suggest no method dominates across all possible worlds. If some subset of the desiderata cannot be jointly met, that fact is itself the result I most want, so state it plainly and say which requirement you would relax first.

3. Briefly — a few sentences each, no more — say how Solomonoff induction, subjective Bayesianism with a defensible prior, minimum-description-length methods, and classical frequentist prediction fare against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scoring; I can run the scoring myself once I trust the table.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the specification as a whole, and single out the one entry — a desideratum, a claimed tension, or a standing judgment — that you are least sure of, explaining why. If what you are least sure of is whether some entry is a genuine requirement or merely the prejudice of whichever camp I happened to read first, say so directly.
