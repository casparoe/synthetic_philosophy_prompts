I spent forty years as an experimental physicist (condensed matter, nothing cosmic) and since retiring I've fallen into a philosophy reading group with some neighbours, mostly lawyers and a retired GP. It's my turn to lead a session and I picked the rotation stuff -- Newton's bucket, whether space is a thing in itself or just a web of relations between bodies. Partly because I thought my background would help, partly I now suspect it'll hurt.

So don't explain the debate to me, I've read my way into it. What I want is a plan for how to run the explanation for this group. In what order do the pieces have to come so that the bucket actually lands as a puzzle -- my worry is that if I start with the bucket they'll just nod and say "centrifugal force, next question," which is exactly the reaction I need to dissolve, so what has to be in place before the bucket can do its work? What's the one example or variation I should build the whole evening around -- I'm tempted by the rotating-globes-in-empty-space case but maybe that's too abstract for non-scientists -- and what does that example need to include and, just as importantly, leave out? Which wrong turns should I expect, in roughly the order they'll occur: I'm guessing (a) this is just about fictitious forces and engineering frames, (b) didn't Einstein settle all this, and (c) an instinctive "obviously motion is only relative" that turns out to be harder to defend than it sounds -- where in the sequence do I head each off, and which of them should I actually let run for a bit because being wrong there is instructive? And give me one question I can pose near the end that would show whether they've grasped what's at stake rather than just picked a team.

Also tell me what I should openly bracket at this level -- quantum gravity, the formal guts of general relativity, whatever -- so I can say "that's beyond us tonight" without feeling like I'm dodging. And one more thing: I'm a physicist, so my instincts are suspect here. If my whole framing of the session is off -- if I'm posing the bucket as a puzzle about physics when the interesting bit is metaphysics, or smuggling in some assumption a philosopher would spot instantly -- say so plainly rather than politely working around it. I'd rather rebuild the evening than deliver a confident wrong version.
