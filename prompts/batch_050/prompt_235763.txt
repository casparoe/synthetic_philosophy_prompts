So here's an episode everyone sort of knows the outline of: the Newfoundland cod collapse. For centuries the Grand Banks off Newfoundland were supposedly so thick with cod you could barely row through them, then industrial factory trawlers showed up after WWII, Canada extended its 200-mile limit in 1977 and took over managing the stock, set quotas based on stock assessments that later turned out to be wildly optimistic, foreign fleets kept hammering the fish just outside the line on the "nose and tail" of the Banks, and in 1992 the government declared a moratorium that threw something like 30,000+ people out of work basically overnight. The cod still haven't really come back as a commercial stock.

Here's what I want: analyze this through Hardin's tragedy of the commons, but take seriously the awkward fact that it doesn't cleanly fit. Hardin's parable is about open access with no rules at all, but the northern cod fishery was one of the most heavily *managed* fisheries in the world -- quotas, licenses, a whole government department. So is this actually a tragedy of the commons, or a failure of management, or does the distinction collapse when you look closer? Map the players properly: inshore fishers, offshore trawler companies, the scientists doing the assessments, the politicians setting quotas above what the science said, and the foreign fleets. For each, say what their incentive structure actually was and whether they were playing the "add one more sheep" move Hardin describes, or something else -- e.g., were the regulators themselves trapped in a commons-style dynamic with elections and fishing towns instead of fish?

Then run it the other direction. Does the case vindicate Hardin (the logic wins no matter how much regulation you pile on if the incentives don't change), or does it vindicate Ostrom instead -- i.e., look at which of her design principles for commons governance were missing (monitoring by locals, graduated sanctions, fishers having a say in the rules, clear boundaries) and whether their absence is the real explanation. And one thing I keep wondering: is there a case that bad management was *worse* than open access, because the official quotas made everyone feel like someone had it under control while the fish disappeared? Say where you land.
