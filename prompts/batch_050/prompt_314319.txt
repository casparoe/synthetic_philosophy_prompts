I'm a TA for an upper-level philosphy of math course and I've got a stack of problem sets to get through this weekend. The topic this week was indispensability arguments and mathematical explanation in science (we did the usual periodical cicada case, a bit on the honeycomb example, Field-style nominalism, etc.). Could you grade one student's answer for me? I want a grade out of 25 plus the specific comments justifying it — so go through the answer point by point, say what earns marks and what loses marks and why, and flag anything that's subtly wrong or conflated even if it sounds fine on a quick read, because that is exactly the stuff I tend to miss when I'm grading at 11pm. Also don't be swayed by the confident prose — this student writes well, but I care about whether the philosophy is actually right.

The question (25 points): (a) State the Quine–Putnam indispensability argument for mathematical platonism with its premises made explicit, including the notion of ontological commitment it relies on. (b) Explain how Baker's "enhanced" indispensability argument based on periodical cicadas is supposed to differ from the original argument, and what dialectical work the appeal to explanation is doing. (c) Give one objection to either argument and briefly assess it.

The student's answer:

"(a) The argument runs as follows. (P1) Naturalism: we should look to our best scientific theories, not to armchair metaphysics, to tell us what exists; in particular, we ought to be ontologically committed to all and only the entities those theories refer to. (P2) Confirmational holism: since theories are confirmed as wholes rather than sentence by sentence, the empirical evidence that confirms our best physics also confirms its mathematical components. (P3) Mathematics is indispensable to our best scientific theories — there is no way to do modern physics without quantifying over numbers, functions, sets, and so on. (C) Therefore we ought to believe in the existence of mathematical objects: platonism is true.

(b) Baker's enhanced argument starts from the periodical cicadas, which have life cycles of 13 or 17 years — both prime numbers. The biological explanation of this fact essentially involves number theory: if a predator has a periodical life cycle of its own, then whenever the predator's period is coprime with the cicada's, predator and cicada co-emerge only once every product-of-the-two-periods years; since a prime is coprime to every shorter period, prime life cycles maximize the interval between disastrous co-emergences and so were selected for. Baker's point is that the mathematics here is not merely representing a biological fact but explaining it — primality is part of the explanans. The enhanced argument thus differs from the original Quine–Putnam argument only in restricting attention to theories with explanatory rather than merely predictive power; otherwise it rests on the same two premises, naturalism and confirmational holism. Just as we believe in electrons because they are indispensable to our best explanations of observed phenomena, we should believe in numbers for the same reason.

(c) Objection: the nominalist (Leng, Bangu, Saatsi) replies that in the cicada case the mathematics merely represents the underlying biological facts — predator satiation, co-emergence intervals — and does no explanatory work of its own, so no commitment to numbers follows. Assessment: this reply has real force, because whether a mathematical posit plays an explanatory or merely representational role is precisely what is in dispute, and the defender of the enhanced argument owes us a criterion for telling the difference rather than simply asserting that primality explains. Still, the nominalist faces the burden of showing that the biological story can even be stated without the number theory, which is not obviously possible."
