I host a long-form philosophy podcast, and next week I am recording an interview with a metaphysician whose new book defends an uncompromising version of the conceivability-to-possibility inference. Her position, in brief: what is ideally conceivable — what survives sustained reflection without contradiction, not merely what seems possible at first glance — is metaphysically possible; and since a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a human being lacking all conscious experience is ideally conceivable, zombies are possible and physicalism about consciousness is false. She is technically careful: she distinguishes prima facie from ideal conceivability and negative from positive conceivability, she knows the Kripkean apparatus of a posteriori necessities cold, and in the book's final chapter she claims to have answered the objection that conceivability tracks our concepts rather than the world. Standard warm-up questions will get me the book's table of contents read aloud.

Write me the sequence of interview questions I should put to her, designed so that each question presupposes she has given her best answer to the previous one. The sequence must do the following work, in this order: force her to state exactly what epistemic state "ideal conceivability" is and how a finite, fallible reasoner could ever know she has achieved it rather than merely failed to notice an incoherence; press on whether the zombie intuition is positive conceivability at all, or simply the failure to attend to consciousness while imagining a body — and on what test could distinguish the two from the inside; confront her with the symmetry problem, namely that a convinced physicalist reports finding the impossibility of zombies equally conceivable, and ask what conceivability evidence is worth when informed parties diverge; and push her on the mathematical case, where both a conjecture and its negation can seem conceivable to competent reasoners, at most one of which is possible.

Structure your answer in four named sections, in this order: Opening Question (one question, phrased as I would ask it aloud, that gets her strongest version on the table without giving her a prepared sound bite; at most 60 words); The Escalation (the four questions above, numbered, each stated as I would ask it, followed by a two-to-three sentence note naming the reply I should expect from her and the follow-up I need to have ready); The Closing Question (a final question that does not ask what would change her mind, but instead asks her to identify a case where her own method delivers a verdict she finds unwelcome, and to explain why she trusts the method anyway); and Moderator Notes (a short paragraph flagging the two points in the sequence where she is most likely to redefine a term mid-answer, so I can pin it down before proceeding). Total length roughly 700 words.
