I am drafting the position statement for a graduate seminar on philosophy of religion, in which I plan to defend a particular reconstruction of the karma doctrine. Before I submit it, I would like a consistency audit of the commitments I intend to defend. They are as follows:

(1) The most defensible interpretation of karma is as a theory of moral desert: the distribution of suffering and flourishing within a life is, on the whole, deserved in virtue of actions performed in earlier lives, so that much apparently unmerited suffering is in fact merited.

(2) The Buddhist analysis of persons is correct: there is no enduring self that persists even through a single lifetime, let alone across many. What exists is a causal continuum of momentary psychophysical states, and rebirth is the continuation of such a continuum, not the migration of a subject from one body to another.

(3) Karma operates impersonally. There is no cosmic judge or administrator who weighs deeds and allocates outcomes; moral causation is simply part of how the world works, no more administered than gravitation.

(4) The chief philosophical attraction of the doctrine is explanatory: unlike rival responses to the problem of evil, it accounts for why suffering is distributed so unevenly among people who are, by any ordinary standard, innocent.

(5) Universal compassion is non-negotiable: everyone who suffers merits compassion, and no fact about the origins of their suffering could render compassion toward them inappropriate.

Please audit this set rather than evaluate the truth of its members. Which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, and which pairs merely appear to be and dissolve on inspection? Order the genuine tensions by severity, and for each one state the cheapest available revision and what it would cost the overall position. If more than one tension is genuine, conclude by telling me which single revision would restore the most coherence to the set as a whole.
