So I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I keep running into this finding that's been nagging at me: in countries with the most gender egalitarian policies and attitudes — the Nordics are the usual example — the gender split in certain careers is actually *bigger* than in less egalitarian countries. Fewer women in engineering in Norway than in, say, Algeria or the UAE, roughly speaking. People call it the gender-equality paradox and everyone I interview seems to have a different take on what it means.

Here's the actual prompt of my question though. These same countries run big policy programs to push the numbers back the other way: scholarships and campaigns to get girls into STEM, targets for gender balance in university departments, recruitment quotas in tech, the works. I'm trying to figure out what a practice like that secretly commits you to. Because on one reading, the paradox suggests that when people are free and secure, women just choose people-oriented fields more often — in which case the programs look like they're overriding free choices in the name of a 50/50 outcome nobody actually picked. On another reading, the choices themselves are still shaped by culture (or by a rational response to workplace sexism), so the programs are counterweights rather than distortions. What does the policy package presuppose about the nature/culture question, and about what justice even requires here — equal outcomes, equal opportunities, or equal *conditions of choice*? Does the practice only make sense if preferences formed under culture don't fully count as someone's own? And is the practice actually tied to one of these views, or could several different theories license it? Also curious what the policy would have to look like if you fully accepted the "free choice under affluence" explanation — would any intervention still be justified, and what kind?
