I am a PhD candidate in philosophy of technology, and next term I will be leading two 75-minute discussion sections (roughly 16 undergraduates each, mostly non-philosophy majors) attached to a lecture course on technology and society. The students will have read Langdon Winner's "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" for the session, but past experience with this course tells me that most will come in treating the thesis as obviously false. Their default position is that technologies are neutral instruments: politics, on their view, resides in the intentions of designers and the choices of users, never in the artifact itself. Simply re-arguing the point at them has not worked in previous years.

Please design an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of the stronger version of the thesis before we debate it explicitly — the claim that a design decision its makers would defend as purely technical, aesthetic, or economic can come to distribute power, access, and status in ways that function like legislation, while being considerably harder to identify, contest, or repeal than legislation.

Requirements:

- No lecture or framing up front. Participants should have to make design decisions themselves, under constraints, and only afterwards discover the distributive consequences of what they built. The idea should emerge from what they did, not from what I tell them.
- The Moses overpasses example is off-limits; it is overworked, and several students will already know it from other courses. Construct an original scenario (a campus system, a scheduling tool, a shared space, a piece of software — your choice, but it should feel mundane rather than sinister).
- Include a phase in which participants who are disadvantaged by the artifact must try to get it changed. The difficulty of this appeal — the absence of any obvious addressee or procedure — should itself be one of the things the activity teaches. If your design includes roles or hidden information, explain how they produce this effect without the facilitator steering it.
- Provide: complete rules and materials (nothing requiring more than paper, sticky notes, and a shared document); a minute-by-minute timeline; and a payoff or evaluation scheme that uses only trivial stakes (e.g., points, priority, small privileges) while still making the disadvantaged outcome genuinely sting.
- Provide a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the activity onto the components of the thesis: the "technical" rationale, the emergent pattern of advantage, the artifact's resistance to revision. The debrief should end by eliciting from the students the strongest objection — that the politics was in the designers' choices all along, not in the thing — and the activity must have generated material that makes this objection feel both natural and somehow incomplete. Tell me how to use that material without simply lecturing.
- Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (e.g., participants gaming the rules, treating it as a design competition, or converging too quickly on the intended moral) and how to handle each failure.
- Finally, be honest about which feature of the thesis the activity cannot reproduce — for instance, the distinction between arrangements that merely happen to have political effects and arrangements that require or are strongly compatible with particular forms of social order — and advise me on how to supply that missing piece in the remaining discussion time.
