For my philosophy seminar on self-locating belief and anthropic reasoning, I'm writing a paper on the tension between the self-sampling assumption (SSA) and the self-indication assumption (SIA), and I need a thought experiment built to order. Please construct a scenario meeting all of the following specifications:

1. Everyday materials only: no cosmology or multiverses, no clones or duplicates, no amnesia drugs, no infinite populations -- just ordinary uncertainty a real person could face (lotteries, hospitals, administrative mix-ups, and the like are fine).

2. SSA and SIA deliver sharply divergent credences about some proposition in the case -- differing by at least an order of magnitude -- and you show both calculations explicitly, including how each principle handles the protagonist's evidence.

3. The uncertainty driving the divergence is uncertainty about how many observers there are in the relevant class, so that the case has the structure of the presumptuous philosopher problem transposed into daily life, rather than the structure of Sleeping Beauty (nobody loses memories or wakes up twice).

4. One of the two verdicts should strike an uncommitted reader as clearly wrong, and you should say honestly which principle you take the case to embarrass.

After presenting the scenario: identify the single structural feature that forces the two principles apart; describe what happens to the divergence if that feature is removed; and sketch how a defender of the embarrassed principle would attack the case's setup -- its reference class, its informational structure, or whatever else -- rather than simply accept the verdict. The point of my paper is to test whether the SSA/SIA dispute genuinely arises outside anthropic cosmology, so don't stipulate the verdicts: derive them.
