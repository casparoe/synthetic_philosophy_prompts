I was at a reading group last week where someone defended dualism-adjacent views of consciousness with an analogy I've also seen in a couple of popular books: consciousness is like the wetness of water. No single H2O molecule is wet, wetness only shows up when you get enough molecules together, and it's perfectly real and does real causal work (it dissolves things, it drowns people). Likewise, the argument goes, no neuron is conscious, consciousness emerges at the level of the whole brain, it's real and causally efficacious in its own right, and it's a category error to demand a reductive explanation of it in neural terms -- you might as well demand the wetness of one molecule. My friend concluded that physicialism about consciousness is therefore in trouble, or at least that the "explanatory gap" is no more mysterious than the gap between one molecule and a wet puddle.

I have a nagging feeling the analogy proves less than he wants, but I can't pin down where. So: audit it properly for me. Give the explicit mapping (molecule, collection, wetness, molecular interactions -> what on the consciousness side?), then the disanalogies -- and separate the ones that actually matter for the anti-reductionist conclusion from the pedantic ones. Some things I suspect matter: we can in fact derive and predict liquidity from statistical mechanics and intermolecular forces, so is wetness really a case of the strong, irreducible emergence my friend needs, or only of the harmless "weak" kind where the higher level is unexpected-ish but fully accountable from below? If it's only the weak kind, does the analogy actually support the opposite of his conclusion? Also: wetness seems to be a functional/behavioral profile (flows, wets, takes the shape of the container), and the hard problem is precisely that consciousness doesn't seem to be one of those -- does that break the mapping at the load-bearing point? And is there an equivocation between "real and causally efficacious" in the water case vs. what's needed for an emergent mind to do causal work without either overdetermining the neurons or being epiphenomenal?

If the analogy mostly carries weak emergence, tell me what a better analogy for strong emergence would be -- or is the deeper problem that every stock example (traffic jams, flocks, temperature, life itself) is also weak emergence, so the analogy cupboard is bare and that itself is evidence about something?
