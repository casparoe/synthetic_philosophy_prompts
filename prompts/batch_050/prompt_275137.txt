I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I keep running into the same move at work. In sprint planning last week I argued we should keep refining a deployment pipeline — better rollback handling, cleaner failure modes — and a senior engineer shut it down with "perfect is the enemy of good." He clearly meant: stop working on this, it's good enough, ship it. But when I pressed him on whether that was a claim about diminishing returns, about the opportunity cost of my time, or about my supposed inability to stop tinkering, the conversation went nowhere. I suspect the slogan is doing several different jobs at once and that only some of them would actually justify his conclusion.

Please act as a philosopher of practical rationality who is also comfortable with the economics of search and deliberation. Think carefully and, before writing your final answer, briefly brainstorm candidate readings so you don't collapse distinct ideas into one.

Then give me a menu of the precise claims "perfect is the enemy of good" could be making — for example (without limiting you to these): a diminishing-returns claim about the marginal value of further improvements; a bounded-rationality claim that deliberating and searching cost resources, so maximizing is itself irrational and one should satisfice against an aspiration level; a psychological claim that perfectionists freeze or never ship; an opportunity-cost claim about what else the time could buy; and a claim about aspiration levels ratcheting upward so "good" never feels final. For each reading, state the claim precisely enough that I could tell what evidence would count against it, assess whether it's a platitude, substantive and defensible, or actually false, and — most importantly — say whether that reading would actually support my colleague's use of the slogan to end the argument, or whether it would instead require further premises (e.g., about where the aspiration level is set, or who bears the cost of the pipeline's failure modes). If some readings support stopping in some situations but not others, say what the situational variable is.

Format: a numbered list of readings, each with the three verdicts (plausibility, supports-his-move?, needed premises), followed by a short bottom line on which reading a maximally charitable version of my colleague is most likely relying on and what question I could ask him to test that. Hard limit: 750 words total.
