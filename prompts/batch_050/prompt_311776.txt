A few friends and I have been working our way through the American version of The Office, and our viewing nights have turned into a running argument I can't settle. One friend insists that laughing at Michael Scott is basically cruel: we're enjoying a lonely, desperate man's humiliations, and the show only gets away with it because he's fictional. Another friend says the humour is just incongruity -- a boss who acts like a needy child, corporate speak colliding with genuine emotion -- and that talk of cruelty is moralizing nonsense. A third, who has read a little psychology, keeps invoking something called benign violation theory: comedy happens when something seems wrong or threatening but is simultaneously revealed to be okay, and the mockumentary format is what keeps us safe. I find all three of these plausible in different scenes and none of them adequate to the whole show, so I want a careful reading, not a verdict delivered from an armchair.

Read The Office through the classical theories of humour -- superiority (Hobbes's sudden glory at another's expense), incongruity, relief, and benign violation -- using them as competing lenses rather than a checklist. Take seriously the episodes everyone knows: the cringe of the Dinner Party, the oblivious cruelty and pathos of moments like Scott's Tots, the way the documentary crew's presence makes us complicit witnesses rather than detached observers. Where does each theory genuinely explain what's funny, and where does it merely redescribe it? In particular: is our laughter at Michael superiority-based, and if so, does the show's eventual tenderness toward him -- his redemption with Holly, the finale's warmth -- retroactively convict our earlier laughter, or does it show that superiority laughter was never what we were doing?

I also want to know where the show resists the theories. Cringe comedy seems like a problem case for benign violation, since the violation often doesn't feel benign at all -- many viewers, including me, genuinely have to pause or look away. Does the talking-head interview format, where characters confide in us, turn us into Michael's friends rather than his judges, and does that change which theory applies? Finally, say what you think the show adds to the philosophical debate that the theories alone don't capture: is there something about sustained, serial acquaintance with a ridiculous person -- laughing at someone for nine seasons -- that one-shot jokes and laboratory experiments can't reveal about how humour and affection interact?
