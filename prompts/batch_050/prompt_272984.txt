I pushed back that this cant be the whole story, since predicates like "rich" cause sorites trouble even when everyone knows the exact bank balances involved. My friend conceded that but said "rich" isn't observational — with genuinely perceptual predicates, she insists, indiscriminability is doing all the work, and if you handed the subject exact measurements of every stimulus the paradox would just evaporate. I want to settle this bet.

Construct a thought experiment that tests my friend's claim: a sorites series built on a perceptual, response-dependent predicate (something like "looks red," "feels warm," "sounds louder") in which the subject has access to exact numerical measurements of every stimulus in the series — and in which the paradox still bites anyway. Constraints: the setting has to be realistic (a paint-mixing station, an audiology booth, a color-vision test — no sci-fi, no demons, no magically enhanced perceivers); the scenario itself should be one paragraph; and you can't cheat by making the measuring device imprecise, hiding the readouts from the subject, or making the steps between items fuzzy in some unspecified way — the numbers are exact, known, and each step is measurably different from its neighbors.

After the scenario, give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no wrap-up: (1) lay out the series, the predicate, and the measurements, and run the paradox explicitly so it's clear it still goes through despite the numbers; (2) say what happens to the indiscriminability diagnosis — whether my friend's claim survives, or whether the diagnosis has to be reformulated, and how; (3) state what an epistemicist and a supervaluationist each say about some item in the mushy middle of the series, and whether the exact measurements do either of them any good; (4) name the single feature of the case that's doing the work, and show that the paradox collapses if you remove it (for instance, if the predicate were defined directly on the measurements rather than on how things strike the subject); (5) give the strongest attack on the case — roughly, how someone would argue that the predicate stopped being genuinely perceptual the moment instruments entered the story, and whether that move works.
