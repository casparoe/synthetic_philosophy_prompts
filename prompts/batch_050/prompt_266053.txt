I've been thinking on my own about whether a finite sequence of outcomes can genuinely be called random, and I'd like your help. Please answer as an expert in the philosophy of probability and algorithmic information theory, and take the time to reason carefully rather than giving me a survey of views.

Here are my gut verdicts, and I want to know what principle, if any, lies behind them. A coin that comes up HTTHHTTHTH feels random; one that comes up HHHHHHHHHH does not, even though I know both sequences are exactly equally probable. A sequence that alternates perfectly, HTHTHTHTHT, feels non-random no matter how long it runs. The first thousand decimal digits of pi look as scattered as any coin record, yet they feel non-random to me because a short rule generates them all. A shuffled deck's exact order feels no more random than the same deck in perfect suit order, even though my coin verdicts should apparently commit me to saying it is. And a sequence produced by a computer's pseudorandom generator, whose seed I later learn was very short, feels like it gets demoted from random to merely random-looking the moment I learn that fact.

Is there one account that delivers all of these verdicts, or at least most of them? Please state the principle (or two or three candidates) precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it saves and which it forces me to give up, and say what it predicts for a case I haven't considered: a coin record that is statistically unremarkable but happens to encode, in binary, the text of a short novel. Finally, I want honesty about the epistemic status of your answer: which of my verdicts is your proposed principle most hostage to, and what consideration or counterexample would lead you to revise the principle or conclude that no single account can capture these intuitions?
