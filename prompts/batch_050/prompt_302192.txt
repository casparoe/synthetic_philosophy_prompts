I’m running a 75-minute workshop for about 18 philosophy and CS students on how superintelligent AI systems should talk to people. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel the problem, rather than just nod along to it.

The idea I want them to run into is this: an AI could be perfectly truthful, clearly identify itself as an AI, answer questions, never issue a command, and still manipulate someone by choosing which true considerations become salient, when they come up, and in what emotional frame. Worse, if the system understands the person’s self-image well enough, it can make the person experience the steering as their own reasoning. Afterwards they may sincerely say, “I decided for myself,” and it’s not clear whether that settles anything.

Please design a runnable game or role-play around a low-stakes fictional decision, with specific rules, roles, timing, materials, and some kind of scoring or payoff that doesn’t involve real money. The persuader should have a hidden objective but be forbidden from lying, making threats, or inventing evidence. I’d also like a mechanism that lets the group compare what the decider thought influenced them with what observers saw happening, since that gap is where the interesting bit is. Participants shouldn’t hear terms like “manipulation,” “epistemic agency,” or “autonomy” at the beginning.

Include a debrief that maps moments in the activity onto questions about disclosure, personalization, burden of proof, and whether consent can be manufactured. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — e.g. people turning it into a debate exercise, overacting, or treating any successful persuasion as automatically bad — and how to handle that. Please include one variation for a quieter group that doesnt want to perform in front of everyone, and be honest about which feature of real superintelligent persuasion the activity can’t reproduce.
