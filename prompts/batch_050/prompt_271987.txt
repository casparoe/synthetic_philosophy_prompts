I am trying to formulate a useful frame-problem rule for an autonomous household robot: after acting, which propositions in its world model should persist, which should be revised, and which should become uncertain? My tentative principle is that an action changes its direct target, anything physically attached to or supported by that target, and any causally required side effect, while every other proposition persists. I suspect that this principle is too crude.

Here are my verdicts on a set of cases:

1. The robot moves a book lying next to a mug. The book’s location changes; the mug’s location remains unchanged.
2. The robot slides a tray on which a mug and phone are resting. The tray, mug, and phone all change location, even though the action directly targets only the tray.
3. The robot moves the top block in a stack sideways. Only that block moves. If it moves the bottom block sideways, the blocks supported by it move as well.
4. The robot paints the north wall blue. The south wall remains white, but the brush becomes wet and the paint supply decreases, even though these were not goals of the action.
5. The robot opens a window. The curtains may move and the room will probably become cooler after some time, but the phone’s battery charge remains unchanged.
6. The robot turns off the lamp in a dark room. The keys remain where they were, but the robot should no longer rely on its visual key detector; its informational capacities and plan may need to change.
7. A trusted organizer tells the robot that Tuesday’s meeting has moved from Room 204 to Room 306. The robot should update the meeting’s location while retaining its time. If the organizer later says the meeting has been canceled, the robot should no longer treat the meeting as taking place in Room 306, although it can retain the historical fact that it had been scheduled there.
8. The robot tells a human that the meeting has moved. This changes what the human has been told and probably what the human believes, but it does not by itself move the meeting.
9. The robot unplugs a freezer. The freezer immediately stops receiving power and will eventually become warmer, but the ice cream inside should not yet be represented as melted.
10. The robot pulls a suitcase with a coat draped over its handle. The coat will probably move with the suitcase, but it might fall. The robot should represent both possibilities rather than confidently preserve or confidently revise the coat’s location.
11. The robot drops a vase and it shatters. The model should not merely change the vase’s location while preserving its previous identity and integrity; it should replace the intact-vase representation with a representation of fragments.
12. The robot removes a battery from another robot. The second robot loses the ability to act, its position remains unchanged, and any stored goal may persist in memory even though it can no longer be pursued.

I would like you to identify the most precise principle, or at most two competing principles, that captures these verdicts. Please do not merely say that the robot should update causally relevant facts: explain what makes a fact relevant, and distinguish at least direct effects, support and containment, unintended side effects, delayed effects, probabilistic effects, changes in informational capacity, changes produced by communication, and changes in object identity. If no simple principle saves every verdict, prefer a principled revision of one or more verdicts over an unstructured list of exceptions. Also give two new cases not listed above and say what your proposed principle predicts about them.
