I've spent the better part of a decade reading the rationalist blogs, and one habit that stuck is asking, before I commit to a view, what it would actually take to pry me off it. The view I want stress-tested here is the transparency thesis about photography, most associated with Kendall Walton: that photographs are transparent in roughly the way telescopes, mirrors, and periscopes are, such that when I look at a well-made photograph of my grandmother I literally see her, at a remove, rather than merely seeing a representation of her. Seeing, on this view, requires the right kind of counterfactual dependence between the scene and my visual experience -- had the scene been different, the image and hence my experience would have been different in systematically corresponding ways -- and photographs satisfy that condition mechanically, whereas paintings and drawings satisfy it only through the filter of a fallible artist's judgments. Hence photographs put us in perceptual contact with the past, and this supposedly explains their evidentiary force in courtrooms and journalism.

I find the view compelling, and I want a serious map of its exposure. What would count against it? Please address, at minimum:

1. The intuition-pump defeaters. If the security guard watching a live CCTV feed sees the intruder but the detective reviewing the recording a year later does not, what exactly distinguishes the cases, and does that distinction do independent work or merely restate the thesis? Likewise the contrast between a mirror and a photograph of the same scene. Which of these cases would a transparency theorist concede as genuinely uncomfortable, as opposed to dismissing as loaded uses of the success-verb "see"?

2. The structural arguments. Is the absence of egocentric spatial information in photographs -- I cannot learn where things are relative to me by looking at one -- a defeater, or does it just show photographic seeing is an impoverished mode of seeing, like seeing in dim light? Does the thesis predict anything its rivals (photographs as traces, as representations, as testimony-like records) do not, such that the debate is about predictions at all rather than about which description we prefer?

3. The technological stress tests. Do AI-generated images that are photorealistic but counterfactually disconnected from any scene break the view, vindicate it (by showing that what matters is precisely the dependence they lack), or leave it untouched? Does the thesis survive contact with prosthetic vision, mediated perception generally, and the question of where seeing stops and inference begins?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones the view's own defenders treat as real costs, and close with an honest verdict: is the transparency thesis a falsifiable position with identifiable breaking points, or has it been qualified into something that no possible case could dislodge -- and if the latter, is that actually a defect for a thesis of this kind, given how slippery a success-term "see" is?
