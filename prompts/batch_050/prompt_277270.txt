I run a small ethics reading group (we meet at my place, there is always too much hummus) and two of my members have been going at it all semester about what adult children owe their parents. One defends Jane English's friendship model from her paper on grown children -- basically that once the parenting is done, any remaining duties flow from the ongoing loving relationship, not from repayment, and that favors done for you don't put you in debt the way loans do. The other thinks this lets kids off way too easy and defends a gratitude/repayment view: our parents gave us enormous, partly unasked-for benefits, and receiving a benefit you accept (even if you didn't request it) generates a genuine obligation that doesn't evaporate just because the relationship sours. They finally agreed to try writing a joint statement instead of arguing, and got stuck immediatly. Draft the statement for them, as if it were the output of a real adversarial collaboration both could sign.

One wrinkle: they BOTH cite the same chunk of Aristotle at each other, from the end of Book VIII of the Nicomachean Ethics (chapter 14, here in Rackham's translation): "For friendship exacts what is possible, not what is due; requital in accordance with desert is in fact sometimes impossible, for instance in honoring the gods, or one's parents: no one could ever render them the honor they deserve, and a man is deemed virtuous if he pays them all the regard that he can. Hence it would appear that a son never ought to disown his father, although a father may disown his son; for a debtor ought to pay what he owes, but nothing that a son can do comes up to the benefits he has received, so that a son is always in his father's debt. But a creditor may discharge his debtor, and therefore a father may disown his son." The gratitude person quotes "a son is always in his father's debt" and stops; the friendship person quotes the opening "friendship exacts what is possible, not what is due" and stops. So the statement needs a shared interpretation section: a gloss of the whole passage that both would sign, and then a short note from each side on what they think the other side is missing in it -- e.g. whether Aristotle's debt talk is literal moral bookkeeping or a metaphor inside a theory of friendship, and what he means by saying requital to parents is impossible but you pay "all that you can." Neither of them is allowed to just cherry-pick their half of the quote anymore.

Format-wise, here's what I want: first a numbered list of claims both parties can genuinely sign (I suspect it includes stuff like: parents typically do make very large sacrifices; grown children normally do have special duties toward their parents that they don't have toward strangers; whatever those duties are, they don't include obedience or open-ended self-sacrifice; and the "you owe me, pay up" framing is at least sometimes morally gross -- but figure out what actually belongs and phrase it so neither side is smuggling in their conclusion). Then a disagreement section where each remaining dispute is stated in neutral wording that each party would accept as a fair statement of their own position. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case both agree bears on it and record each side's prediction about it -- some candidates I'd push on: whether an unrequested benefit you couldn't decline grounds obligation (case: the parent who paid for college unasked and now invokes it to demand weekly visits); whether what you owe tracks the current relationship (case: two siblings whose parents sacrificed equally, but one stayed close and one drifted); and whether the duty survives mistreatment or abandonment (case: the father who left when she was six but paid child support on time every month). Finally, if some part of their fight turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why -- my suspicion is they mean different things by "owe," but I want to see the argument, not just the assertion.
