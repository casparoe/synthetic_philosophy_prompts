I'm in a seminar on ethics and social change and we just did Appiah on moral revolutions — his claim that things like the slave trade, dueling, and footbinding didn't end because people were finally persuaded by moral arguments (those had been sitting around for centuries) but because the practice became dishonorable in the eyes of the people whose good opinion its practitioners actually cared about. Williams has a related thought about how you can't really argue people out of a whole form of life. For a creative assignment I have to write a satire of the theory, so: write me a deadpan advice column, around 600 words, called something like "The Honor Consultant," in which a naive reformer writes in asking how to abolish some cruel but perfectly legal and prestigious practice, and the columnist replies with total straight-faced practicality — pamphlets of reasons are a waste of good ink, no philosopher has ever ended anything, what you actually need is to make the practice slightly ridiculous, get the right sort of people to stop inviting its practitioners to dinner, and perhaps arrange for its most respectable defenders to be seen doing it somewhere embarrassing. The letter writer keeps earnestly proposing arguments and public debate and the consultant keeps patiently correcting him, citing the dueling and slave-trade cases as proof of method. The practice in the letter can be historical or invented-modern, whichever makes it funnier. The humor should come from the consultant being completely right according to the theory, and it should stay deadpan the whole time, no winking at the reader. Also no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — continuous prose only, like an actual letter someone would recieve in the post.
