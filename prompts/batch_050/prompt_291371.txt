So here's the situation. I run an informal ethics reading group with some friends who work in tech policy, and we keep hitting the same wall: everyone agrees lying is bad, but the second someone misleads instead -- says the literally true thing that they know will create a false belief -- half the room relaxes and the other half gets *more* annoyed. The classic example that keeps coming up: a chatbot that's been instructed to "never lie" responds to a user asking "will my data be sold?" with "we comply fully with all applicable data protection laws," which is technically true and everyone in the room except the lawyers reads as "no."

I want to move past the vibes, so: act as a philosopher who specializes in the ethics of communication (lying, deception, assertion, Grice and all that). First, brainstorm freely for a few sentences about what the *possible* positions even are on the lying/misleading question, before you commit to anything. Then build me a decision tree that sorts a member of my group into a named position based only on how they react to concrete cases -- NOT on how they describe their own view, since that's exactly what's failing us.

Requirements, and I'm picky about these:

1. The tree should be built from the logical space, not from a list of names. Start from the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions (equivalence views, lying-is-worse views, misleading-is-worse views, "it's all context/convention" views, etc.) in that space. If some combination of answers is unoccupied, note it and say briefly whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

2. Each branch node must be a concrete scenario or claim someone can react to without knowing the literature -- like the chatbot case above, or a doctor giving a technically-true-but-evasive answer, or an attempted mislead where the hearer fails to draw the inference. Ideally at least one node should turn on a case where the stakes of lying vs. misleading are held exactly equal.

3. 5-6 questions max, at least 5 distinct leaves. My group will riot if it's longer.

4. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't genuinely separate, rather than pretending the cases distinguish them.

5. Wherever a node or a leaf bears on AI assistants (since that's what my group actually cares about), say so explicitly -- e.g., does the position change if the speaker has no beliefs or intentions in the human sense, or if the misleading output was nobody's intent at all?

Think carefully about which distinctions actually do work in the tree and which only feel like they do.
