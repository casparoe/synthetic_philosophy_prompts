I TA for an undergrad philosophy of logic seminar (about 14 students, one 75-minute session) and we're doing a week on dialetheism and paraconsistent logic. The problem: every year the students nod along when we say "explosion is bad," but I don't think they actually feel why. To them, "if a contradiction is true then everything follows" sounds like a technicality you can just define away.

So: design an in-class game that makes them feel the difference between inconsistency and triviality. My rough idea is that the game's rulebook should genuinely contradict itself — not a misprint, but a real conflict between two rules that both clearly apply to a recurring situation (e.g. one rule says all trades are binding once made, another says any player can veto a trade involving a card that was once theirs). Disputes are guaranteed, and scoring should be set up so that someone will inevitably try the move "well if the rules say both P and not-P, then the rules say I win, hand over all the points." The rest of the group then has to figure out how to keep playing rather than let the game collapse into triviality.

Give me: the full rules and the exact contradictory clauses, materials, a scoring scheme that doesn't use real money, how disputes actually get resolved mid-game, and a debrief that maps what happened onto explosion and paraconsistent consequence. No lecture up front — they shouldn't hear the words "dialetheism" or "explosion" until afterwards. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I suspect one group will just vote to strike one of the rules, which kind of misses the point), and be honest about what the game can't reproduce — presumably real dialetheists think some contradictions are actually true, not just that a rulebook is broken, and a game can't make that feel plausible.
