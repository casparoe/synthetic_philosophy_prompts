I'm a PhD student in epistemology TAing an upper-level undergrad seminar this semester, about 14 students, and I get one 80-minute meeting to run on pragmatic encroachment vs. contextualism. Design me an in-class activity for it. The core thing I want them to feel in their gut is the stakes-sensitivity of knowledge talk: that whether it seems right to say "you know the pharmacy is still open" shifts when what rides on it goes from a minor errand to someone's medication. But I also want the activity to tease apart the two views structurally, not just as labels: have some participants be the deciders whose own stakes vary round to round, and others be judges who only decide whether the decider "knew" -- and ideally set it up so the judges' own context can be manipulated independently of the decider's stakes, so the class can see whose practical situation is actually doing the work.

Give me the full setup: rules for however many rounds you think fits the time, a point/payoff scheme that makes checking your belief cost something but doesn't need real money, and how to keep the judges' verdicts from being contaminated once outcomes get revealed (outcome bias is gonna be a problem I think). Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto subject-sensitive invariantism, contextualism, and the intellectualist who says the game only shows that acting on a belief is stakes-sensitive, not knowledge itself. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- e.g. students gaming the point system or treating escalating stakes as escalation of required certainty rather than stakes. And be honest about which part of the debate a game with payoffs simply can't settle, since no behavior in a classroom distinguishes knowledge from justified reliance.
