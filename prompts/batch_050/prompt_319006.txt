I'm preparing an undergraduate seminar on personal identity for next spring, and for the unit on whether a self can be distributed across brains — the line of thought Hofstadter develops in I Am a Strange Loop, where a person's self persists, partially and to varying degrees, in the minds of people who knew them well — I need a classroom thought experiment that isn't the usual fare. My students have already worked through teletransportation, brain transplants, and fission cases, and I find that those scenarios smuggle their verdicts in by stipulation: once you grant perfect copying, the theories just read off their conclusions. I'd like you to construct a new case to specifications, one that makes the disagreement between theories do the work.

Construct a thought experiment, set in a plausible near-future medical or technological context (no teleporters, no perfect whole-brain emulation, no Cartesian souls), in which whether an 'I' is distributed across two brains turns on a single continuously variable parameter — something like the bandwidth or latency of information exchange between the two brains — such that at one extreme of the parameter the case is uncontroversially one person with an unusual cognitive setup, at the other extreme it's uncontroversially two separate people, and the middle region is genuinely contested. Then take three positions — (a) a Parfit-style psychological-continuity view, (b) animalism, on which we are biological organisms and identity is a matter of biological continuity, and (c) a Hofstadter-style pattern view, on which a self is a coarse-grained pattern that can exist partially and by degrees in other brains — and show what verdict each gives at three points along the parameter (low, middle, high) and why. Constraints: the scenario itself should be one paragraph; the technology should be a recognizable extension of things that already exist (think split-brain research, cochlear implants, or brain-machine interfaces) rather than magic; and the verdicts must be generated from the theories, not written into the setup.

After the scenario, give me the engineering report. Identify which feature of the case does the work in making the three theories diverge, and tell me what happens if that feature is removed — does the case collapse back into a standard fission or split-brain case? Then play defense for each side: how would the animalist argue that your parameter is metaphysically irrelevant (that no amount of information flow between two organisms makes them one), and how would the Hofstadterian respond to the charge that talk of 'partial survival in others' confuses causal influence and memory with literal selfhood? Close by telling me which of the three positions your case embarrasses most, and whether you think it embarrasses it fairly.
