I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and last week over drinks a friend of mine who works on decision theory walked me into an argument I can't find my way out of. Some background he gave me: there's an idea floating around that in a big enough universe, your own choices count as evidence about the choices of huge numbers of agents similar to you, since similar minds facing the same problem tend to land on the same answer. My friend thinks this idea refutes itself, and his argument goes like this:

(1) The rational choice is whichever has the best expected outcome given everything you know — including what your own choice tells you about the world. (2) Your decisions correlate with the decisions of agents relevantly similar to you, so when you choose, you learn something about what your "decision twins" are choosing too. (3) The universe is enormous, plausibly infinite, so there are staggering numbers of agents similar enough to you facing similar enough problems — on distant planets, in other Everett branches, wherever. (4) Even a modest correlation, multiplied across that many agents, means one choice of yours carries enormous evidential weight: cooperating is evidence of a cosmos tilting toward cooperation, defecting of a cosmos tilting toward defection. (5) So in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with a stranger, cooperating is rational even if there's zero chance they can ever retaliate or reciprocate. (6) And notice nothing in (1)–(4) requires the other agents to be able to affect you, or even to know you exist. So the same reasoning says you should sometimes eat real costs to "cooperate with" causally isolated beings — aliens beyond the observable universe, say — who will never find out what you did and couldn't do anything for you even if they did. (7) But that's absurd. You can't play a prisoner's dilemma with someone who doesn't know you're at the table and has nothing at stake in you. So at least one of (1)–(4) has to go, and my friend's money is on (1): treating your own actions as evidence is just dressed-up superstition, and rationality should only care about what your actions cause.

Here's my problem: I have a nagging feeling something shifts between (5) and (6), but when I pressed him he said correlation doesn't care whether the other guy can see you, and I didn't have a comeback. I'm also weirdly reluctant to abandon (2), since it feels like the only thing justifying one-boxing in Newcomb's problem, which I'm attached to. So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? And if the argument is actually sound all the way through, tell me which premise is cheapest to give up. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the step you'd throw out — then give the reasoning, no preamble and definately no restating the argument back at me.
