I never went to university, but I have spent the better part of fifteen years making markets -- first informally, taking small bets among colleagues, and later running a modest online book on obscure sporting events. My entire working vocabulary is prices. A belief, for me, is a pair of quotes I am willing to honor on both sides; my margin of safety is the width of my bid-ask spread; and my recurring nightmare is the arbitrageur who trades against me in a circle and walks away with a guaranteed profit. I taught myself whatever probability I know in order to stay solvent, and I think in odds the way other people think in words.

Recently, through a podcast and some late-night reading, I stumbled onto a dispute among philosophers of probability about whether rational degrees of belief have to be single numbers. As far as I can tell, one side -- the Bayesians -- holds that a rational agent's confidence in any proposition can always be captured by one precise probability. The other side defends so-called imprecise probabilities: in conditions of genuine ignorance, they say, your attitude is better represented by a whole range of probabilities, and it would be irrational to feign a precision you do not possess. There are puzzle cases involving urns whose composition is known versus unknown, and arguments about whether a range of credences collapses back into a single number the moment you are forced to act on it. I found myself oddly at home in this literature, and also oddly unable to tell whether it was saying anything I did not already know from quoting prices.

What I would like is a faithful restatement of this debate in the vocabulary I actually think in -- quotes, spreads, arbitrage, adverse selection, bankroll -- followed by an honest report on what does not survive the translation. Which claims translate cleanly? My suspicion is that a bid-ask spread is exactly what these philosophers mean by an imprecise credence, and that their coherence requirement is nothing more exotic than my no-arbitrage condition, but I want that checked rather than assumed. Which claims translate only with distortion? When I widen my spread, for instance, I might be reporting my ignorance, or protecting myself against better-informed counterparties, or simply charging for my trouble -- and I cannot tell whether the philosophers' notion of an imprecise belief distinguishes these or conflates them. And which claims have no expression at all in the language of prices? Where the translation fails, I want a verdict: does the residue reveal something my market vocabulary is missing, or does it show that the untranslatable claim was less substantive than it sounded? I ask because, after fifteen years of quoting two-way prices on everything from elections to second-division football, I am genuinely unsure whether I possess beliefs in the philosophers' sense at all -- or only a trading book.
