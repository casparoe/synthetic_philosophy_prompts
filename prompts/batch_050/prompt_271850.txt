I’m a high school junior and my history club somehow ended up arguing about causation instead of doing actual homework. Draft the joint statement that these two camps could both sign after an adversarial collaboration:

- The “but-for” camp: an event in history counts as a cause of some outcome only if, holding the other actual circumstances fixed, the outcome would not have happened without it.
- The “actual-path” camp: something can be a cause if it was part of the process that actually produced the outcome, even if another sufficient cause was waiting in the wings.

Use this imaginary but realistic case as the shared test. In a constitutional monarchy, parliament passes a no-confidence vote against the prime minister by three separate blocs. Bloc A defects because of a famine, Bloc B because of an unpopular war, and Bloc C because of a corruption scandal. Each bloc has enough votes by itself to make the motion pass, all three blocs actually vote for it, and none of them knows beforehand that the other two will. The motion passes by a huge margin. Removing any one bloc’s votes, while keeping the others fixed, would not change whether the motion passes, although it might change the margin, the later coalition, or which explanation newspapers print the next day.

The statement should have:

1. a numbered list of claims both camps can honestly accept, including at least some claims about historical evidence and not just the toy case;
2. their remaining disagreements, phrased neutrally enough that neither side is made to look dumb;
3. a disagreements table with the exact question, the but-for answer, the actual-path answer, and why it matters for writing history;
4. the cruxes that would make one side revise its view;
5. any cases or kinds of evidence both sides agree are relevant to those cruxes, with each side’s prediction stated in advance.

Please also have the document distinguish overdetermination from cases where one cause merely preempts another, and from cases where several causes are each necessary parts of one bigger mechanism. Don’t just declare one camp the winner at the end. If part of the disagreement turns out to be about what “explain” means rather than about causation itself, say exactly which part.
