I'm a high school junior writing a paper for my civics class, and I've gotten stuck on a question I can't resolve with the textbooks I have. It's about who gets to be part of "the people" in a democracy in the first place, before any voting even happens.

Here's what's puzzling me. The rules my country (the US, but this seems true of most democracies) uses to decide who counts as a member seem to rest on different and maybe conflicting ideas, depending on which rule you look at:

- A baby born on US soil to tourists is a citizen for life, even if she leaves the next day and never returns. A child brought here at age two who grows up here, speaks only English, and knows no other home can be shut out entirely unless she naturalizes.
- A permanent resident can live here for forty years, pay every tax, serve on juries' pool lists, and be subject to every law, but never casts a ballot. Meanwhile a citizen who has lived abroad for thirty years, pays no US taxes, and may never come back keeps her vote forever.
- A 17-year-old can hold a job and have income tax withheld from her paycheck, but has no say over the government taxing her. In some states, a person convicted of a felony loses the vote even after serving his sentence, sometimes for life.
- Nobody ever asked any of us to consent to membership. It was assigned at birth, based on either where we happened to be born or who our parents are.

So here's my question. What theory of political membership does this patchwork presuppose? I've seen three candidate principles mentioned in my reading: (1) everyone whose interests are affected by a decision should get a say in it; (2) everyone subjected to a government's coercive laws should get a say over that government; (3) membership is a kind of inherited or birthright status, like membership in a family, that doesn't need justification by either of the first two. Does our actual practice presuppose one of these? Does it presuppose some mix, and if so, is the mix coherent or is it just historically accumulated? For each rule I listed, tell me which principle best explains it and which principle it flatly contradicts. Also distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to be compatible with — I suspect some rules could be licensed by several different theories, and I want to know which parts of the system are philosophically underdetermined versus which parts force a commitment.

Finally: if we took principle (1) or principle (2) fully seriously as the basis for membership, which of the rules above would have to change, and what would they change into?

(yes/no, with a few words why) | Would it survive under principle (1)? | Would it survive under principle (2)?
