Write a satirical op-ed of roughly 600 words, pitched at a major newspaper's opinion page, in the voice of a retired presidential spokesman who is a proud lifelong bullshitter in Frankfurt's technical sense: his entire career rested on serene indifference to whether his claims were true, and he has always insisted on the dignity of that craft as against both lying and honest error. His subject is the AI industry's habit of describing language models' confident fabrications as "hallucinations." His grievance: the word grants machines an innocence he himself never enjoyed, since a hallucination is an involuntary perceptual mishap — honest error in exotic dress — whereas he, as a professional, can recognize indifference to truth at fifty paces, and this is it. The comedy should run on real features of the territory rather than invented ones: the liar must track the truth in order to conceal it, the honest reporter aims at it and misses, the bullshitter alone treats it as beside the point; and on the genuine puzzle of whether a system with no attitudes toward truth at all can even be indifferent to it, or whether the spokesman is committing the same anthropomorphism he mocks in the industry. Keep him deadpan and internally consistent — he must never once slip into valuing truth; the nearest he comes to sincerity is professional pride. The ending should land somewhere, ideally letting his closing flourish quietly commit him on the question the op-ed never asks outright: whether bullshit is a diagnosis of attitudes or of behavior, and so whether the industry's euphemism is a category error, an evasion of responsibility, or accidentally correct.
