Last weekend I spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend who is a convinced relationist about space — Leibniz on her shelf, Mach in her back pocket. I walked away still holding the substantivalist side, and I want to stress-test that commitment rather than merely re-litigate the argument.

Here is the passage our dispute kept circling. In the Scholium to the Definitions at the start of the Principia (I read it in the Motte translation), after presenting the bucket experiment, Newton makes a striking concession: discovering and effectually distinguishing the true motions of particular bodies from the merely apparent ones is, he says, a matter of great difficulty, because the parts of that immovable space in which true motions take place do not in any way come under the observation of our senses. He then adds that the situation is not altogether desperate, since we have some arguments to guide us — partly from the apparent motions, which are the differences of true motions, and partly from the forces, which are the causes and effects of true motions.

My position is roughly this: Newton is right that absolute space exists as something over and above the material bodies in it, and the inertial effects he points to (the concave surface of the spinning water, the tension in the cord joining two revolving globes) are genuine evidence for it, because they are effects that no inventory of relations among bodies alone can explain. My friend thinks the passage I've just described gives the game away: if the parts of absolute space do not come under the observation of our senses even in principle, then positing absolute space is metaphysics untethered from physics, and Newton's own appeal to forces and apparent motions shows that everything we actually have access to is relational anyway.

So: what would count against substantivalism as I've stated it? I'd like a map of the view's exposure, not just a recital of the standard objections. In particular:

First, on the passage itself — is my friend reading it correctly? Is Newton's concession merely epistemic (we cannot directly observe absolute space, though we can infer it from its effects), in which case it is no more damaging than the unobservability of electrons, or is it doing more work than that, amounting to an admission that absolute space is idle in his own mechanics? If there is a defensible interpretation on which the epistemic humility and the ontological commitment come apart, I want to know what would have to be true of inference-to-unobservable-entities generally for that defense to hold.

Second, which developments and arguments actually bear on the view, and how decisive is each? I presume the list includes Mach's suggested relational redescription (and whether it can in fact reproduce inertial effects without remainder), the general theory of relativity and whether its geometrization of gravity vindicates or dissolves absolute space, and whatever follows from the diffeomorphism invariance of the theory — but I want each item graded: fatal, serious but answerable, or merely awkward. If the honest verdict is that classical substantivalism is dead but some successor view (a structured spacetime with its metric as a genuine entity, say) inherits everything the substantivalist wanted, then say so plainly and tell me whether that is a retreat, a refinement, or a vindication.

Third, what would a substantivalist of good faith concede as counting against the view, as opposed to what critics allege? If the view's defenders can absorb every result by weakening the claim — absolute space, then absolute spacetime, then the metric field, then just the geometric structure — I want an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into something unfalsifiable, and whether that matters for a metaphysical view of this kind. What observation, experiment, or formal result would leave the substantivalist with nothing left to say?

Finally, tell me which defeater you judge strongest, and whether my friend's reading of the Scholium passage — that Newton himself supplied the ammunition — is historically and philosophically fair, or an overreading.
