So I co-run our university's undergrad philosophy-and-cog-sci reading group, and in two weeks it's my turn to lead the session. I picked "sphexishness" as the theme: the idea that behavior which looks flexible and intelligent can actually be a rigid loop, and the uncomfortable follow-up question of how much of our own behavior is like that. My anchor for the evening is Fabre's famous digger-wasp observations (the ones retold in Wooldridge's The Machinery of the Brain and made famous in philosophy by Dennett and Hofstadter, who coined the word "sphexish"): the wasp leaves her paralyzed cricket at the burrow entrance, pops inside to inspect, and if an experimenter nudges the cricket a few inches away while she's in there, she comes out, drags it back to the entrance... and goes inside to inspect all over again, loop after loop, never just pulling it straight in. I'm planning to read a passage from Fabre's The Hunting Wasps aloud at some point in the session. The group is mostly first- and second-years, a mix of philosophy and biology kids, no prior exposure to any of this, and we have about 75 minutes.

Pretend you're a veteran teacher of philosophy of mind who's run exactly this session a dozen times and knows every way it goes wrong. Think carefully and, if it helps, brainstorm a bit before settling on a plan. What I want from you is NOT the explanation itself but a battle plan for the session: the order the pieces have to come in and why (e.g. what do they need to already buy about the difference between flexible and merely complicated behavior before the wasp story lands as anything more than a fun bug fact?); exactly where in the arc I should read the Fabre passage aloud so it does maximum damage, and what has to be primed beforehand so they hear the repetition as eerie rather than cute; which misconceptions they'll predictably walk in with or form on the spot -- I'm guessing at least "this is just instinct, and instinct is a bug thing, not a us thing," "sphexish just means stupid," and someone will try to turn it into a cheap argument that determinism means we're all wasps -- and the precise moment in the sequence to head each one off, before it hardens. I also vaguely know there are ethologists who think the wasp story is overhyped and that later experiments show some flexibility, so tell me whether to bring that up, and if so at what point, without it deflating the whole evening.

Finally: give me the one human example you'd use as the bridge from wasps to us (and tell me what it must have and must lack -- I want them squirming, not nodding along), one end-of-session question I can pose that tests whether they actually got the concept rather than just enjoyed the anecdote, and the stuff I should openly refuse to get into at this level (I'm assuming the full-blown compatibilism wars, but you tell me).
