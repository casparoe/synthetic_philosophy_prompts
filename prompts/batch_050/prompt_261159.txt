So I'm in this reading group on classical Chinese thought and last week we hit the Zhuangzi skill stories (Cook Ding, the cicada catcher, the swimmer at the Lüliang falls) and one guy, Dave, put together an argument that he says shows the whole wu-wei thing is incoherent, and honestly I've been chewing on it for days and can't find the crack. Here's his argument, cleaned up a bit:

(1) Wu-wei just means acting without deliberate effort or striving -- flowing with things rather than pushing -- and the Daoist texts hold it up as the ideal way to act and live.
(2) If you deliberately try to get into some state, then whatever you're doing in that moment is an act of trying, so it isn't wu-wei.
(3) Practicing something is deliberately trying to get better at it.
(4) So nobody can practice wu-wei, or practice anything *in order to become* wu-wei: the practicing cancels out the thing it's aimed at.
(5) But Cook Ding spent something like nineteen years getting to the point where his blade moves on its own, and the other skill stories also describe people who reached effortlessness through long, deliberate training. These stories are the text's own illustrations of the ideal.
(6) So the ideal is presented as reachable only through practice, while (4) shows it's unreachable through practice.
(7) Worse, nothing in steps (1)-(4) is specific to Daoism, so the same argument shows you can't practice being relaxed, spontaneous, or natural either -- which would make advice like "just be yourself" or a jazz musician practicing improvisation equally incoherent.
(8) Since that's absurd, and since (5)-(6) make the Daoist texts contradict themselves either way, wu-wei is a concept nobody could ever coherently aim at, and the Daoists were confused.

Dave says the only way out is to admit the Daoist ideal is self-undermining, like trying to fall asleep by concentrating on falling asleep, forever. I really want to keep (1) and (5) -- the skill stories are clearly doing *something* important in the text -- so I'd rather sacrifice something in (2)-(4), but I can't say which one without hand-waving. Which premise or step should actually be rejected, why did it look so plausible, and what does rejecting it cost (e.g., does it force some reading where wu-wei was never about effortlessness in the first place)? Also, when you answer, please be clear about what's more or less settled among people who work on this stuff versus what's genuinely contested -- like how wu-wei should even be translated, whether Laozi and Zhuangzi mean the same thing by it, or whether the skill stories are about wu-wei at all. Don't present one scholarly reading as the consensus if there isn't one.
