Next month I will present a paper to a political theory colloquium defending a modified version of the moral bioenhancement program associated with Ingmar Persson and Julian Savulescu's Unfit for the Future. Where they argue for compulsory, population-wide moral bioenhancement as a response to civilizational risks (environmental catastrophe, advanced weapons in the hands of bad actors), my paper defends what I call the fiducial version: enhancement should be concentrated on, and made a condition of holding office for, the narrow class of decision-makers whose failures could produce catastrophic harm — heads of government, senior military commanders, intelligence chiefs, central bank governors, executives at firms developing potentially dangerous technologies. My core argument is that the fiducial version captures most of the risk reduction Persson and Savulescu want while escaping the strongest liberal objections to their universal version, since we already accept intrusive psychological vetting, medical disclosure, and behavioral constraints as conditions of holding precisely these roles. I also argue that enhancing a small, identifiable population is more politically feasible and easier to monitor for unintended effects than treating everyone.

The audience will be about a dozen people, none of whom works on enhancement ethics specifically: roughly eight political theorists in the Rawlsian and liberal-egalitarian tradition; two critical theorists influenced by Foucault who will hear "condition of office" as a biopolitical sorting mechanism; one philosopher of biology who doubts that "moral dispositions" name anything a biomedical intervention could cleanly target, as opposed to a heterogeneous bundle of context-sensitive capacities; and one visiting economist who thinks institutional incentive design makes agent-level disposition enhancement otiose.

Write my Q&A prep sheet. I want the objections in roughly the order they are likely to come, each phrased in the idiom of the audience member who would raise it — the Rawlsians, the Foucauldians, the philosopher of biology, and the economist should not sound alike. For each objection: state whether it targets the shared Persson–Savulescu framework or my fiducial modification specifically; give me a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds; indicate what I should concede up front rather than defend; and note where my reply is weakest. Where my fiducial modification creates problems the universal version does not have — for example, around how the enhanced would be selected, who controls the criteria, and what happens to the legitimacy of a political order whose rulers are pharmacologically altered — say so plainly rather than letting me discover it in the room. Order the objections by expected damage, not by the order in which they occur to you. Finally, give me the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and explain why I have no good answer to it.
