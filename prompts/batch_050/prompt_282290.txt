We're on the machinery chapter of Capital vol 1 in my history of econ thought seminar, and I keep getting stuck on how Marx would actually read the Luddites. Can you analyze the Luddite revolts (England, roughly 1811-1816, weavers and croppers smashing power looms and shearing frames) through Marx's theory of machinery? Not just "would he support them" but the actual mechanics:

On Marx's account, were the Luddites right about what was hurting them but wrong about the target -- i.e. his distinction between the machine itself and the capitalist use of the machine? Does that distinction hold up, or is it a bit too convenient, since for the worker on the ground the machine IS the wage cut?

Second, Marx argues machinery under capitalism tends to deskill workers, drag women and children into the workforce, and lengthen the working day rather than shorten it. How well does that map onto what happened to the framework knitters and croppers specifically, given that these were skilled artisans losing a craft identity, not just factory hands?

Third, where does the episode push back on the theory? As I understand it the Luddites often targeted specific employers who broke craft norms (like using unskilled labor or making shoddy "cut-ups"), and some were fine with machinery as such. Does that complicate Marx's implied story that worker resistance starts aimed at machines and matures into being aimed at the system -- like maybe the Luddites were never confused about the target in the first place and were running a kind of bargaining strategy?

Finally, the aftermath: industrialization kept rolling anyway, real wages stagnated for decades before eventually rising. Does Marx's theory predict that smashing the machines was doomed, and does the long-run outcome vindicate him or embarass him? Feel free to say where the theory just doesn't have anything to say rather than forcing it.
