I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade, and one thread that never quite dies is how a reasonable person should respond upon discovering that smart, well-informed people disagree with them. A small reading group I'm in has asked each of us to write down our settled view on peer disagreement so the others can stress-test it. Here is roughly where I've landed, and I'd like to know whether the set hangs together.

(1) Equal weight. When I learn that someone I judge to be my epistemic peer on a question disagrees with me about it, I should move my credence roughly halfway toward theirs.

(2) Independence. In judging whether someone counts as my peer on a question, I have to bracket the disputed reasoning itself. I cannot demote someone from peer status on the grounds that they reached a different conclusion about the very matter under dispute; peerhood has to be established on independent grounds, such as track record on other questions.

(3) Meta-level exemption. Conciliationism is itself controversial among epistemologists, most of whom are presumably my peers on the topic, so applying (1) to it would be self-defeating. I therefore stay steadfast about conciliationism as a rule for first-order questions, and about the reliability of my own basic reasoning faculties, and I treat both as falling outside (1)'s scope.

(4) Aumann. Rational agents with common priors and common knowledge of one another's posteriors cannot agree to disagree. So when friends of mine who are as smart and as informed as I am persist in disagreeing with me, either someone's reasoning is failing somewhere or the common-prior and common-knowledge conditions don't actually hold.

(5) Inside view. In practice, when I disagree with people I respect — about AI timelines, nutrition, monetary policy, whatever — I usually stay fairly close to my original position, because I have access to my own reasoning, my own reading history, and my sense of which objections I've already considered and survived, in a way they cannot. I take this to be legitimate private evidence rather than bias.

What I want is an audit, not a survey of the literature. Which pairs of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, and which only appear to? For each real tension, say how severe it is and what the cheapest fix would cost; and if several of the problems could be repaired by a single revision, tell me which revision cleans up the most. I've deliberately not signaled which commitment I'd give up, because I honestly don't know.

Two requests about how you answer. First, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this — for instance, what the agreement theorem actually proves versus whether its conditions ever obtain among real humans, or what the Equal Weight View says versus whether it's true — and don't present a contested position as though it were the consensus. Second, no formatting: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Continuous prose only, the way you'd write a letter.
