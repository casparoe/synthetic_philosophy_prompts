I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm drafting a longform piece on self-deception. Write the essay at the heart of it for me. The essay should engage the standard paradox — deception seems to require a deceiver who knows the truth, so self-deception looks as impossible as barricading a door from both sides — against the deflationary view that what we call self-deception is only motivated reasoning, strategic avoidance of evidence, or wishful belief, with no genuine deception present. Adjudicate using three test cases: a smoker who never looks up the survival statistics; an executive who accepted reassuring accounts of irregularities she could easily have investigated; a spouse who fails to notice an affair that everyone else can see. To sharpen the question, the essay should propose and apply its own criterion for when motivated belief slides into self-deception — for instance, whether the person is managing their own future beliefs as an object to be engineered, rather than merely erring. It must reach a verdict: self-deception is a real, distinct state, or it isn't and the cases dissolve without remainder. Begin with the essay itself, no preface, opening with its thesis in the first sentence.
