In Chapter IV of A Treatise on Probability (1921), the chapter on the principle of indifference, J. M. Keynes confronts the fact that the principle yields contradictory answers when the same unknown quantity can be redescribed in different but equally natural ways — his discussion includes Bertrand-style geometric examples and cases where one parametrizes a problem by a quantity itself or by some monotone function of it (for instance, by a substance's specific volume rather than its specific density), so that a uniform distribution over one description is non-uniform over the other. Keynes's response, in essence, is not to abandon the principle but to restrict it: he holds that indifference may be invoked only among alternatives that are, relative to the evidence actually in hand, ultimate and incapable of further subdivision — and that the paradoxes arise precisely because reasoners apply the principle to alternatives that are not ultimate, where a hidden further distinction would break the symmetry the principle requires.

Keynes died in 1946, two years before Shannon's founding paper on information theory and decades before E. T. Jaynes argued (from the 1950s through the 1970s) that problems like Bertrand's random-chord paradox are not ill-posed at all: once the physical randomization procedure is taken seriously, the requirement that the solution be invariant under the problem's natural transformation groups (translation, rotation, scale) uniquely determines the probability distribution — in the chord case, Jaynes claimed, the answer 1/2 — and that maximum-entropy inference generalizes the principle of indifference to continuous sample spaces in a way that resolves, rather than merely restricts, the classical contradictions.
