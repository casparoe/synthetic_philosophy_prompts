I'm in Year 12 (well, the American equivalent — I'm a high school junior) and our school runs an end-of-year philosophy symposium where students defend a thesis in front of a panel. For my slot I've chosen to defend the following position: that in a Rawls-style original position, where the parties have no basis for assigning probabilities to which person in society they will turn out to be, choosing principles of justice by maximin — selecting the arrangement whose worst possible outcome is the best available worst outcome — is more rational than the Harsanyi-style alternative of assuming an equal chance of being anyone and maximizing average expected utility. My supporting claim is that the situation is one of genuine ignorance rather than risk, so the equiprobability assumption that expected-utility reasoning needs is itself the thing that has to be argued for, and that minimax regret reasoning points in roughly the same direction as maximin here rather than rescuing the utilitarian conclusion. I should admit my sympathy for Rawls mostly comes from one chapter of a book we read in class, so my grasp of the decision theory is shakier than I'd like the panel to notice.

The panel will consist of three people. First, our economics teacher, who taught us expected utility theory last term and mentions Harsanyi approvingly at every opportunity; I expect him to press the point that maximin ignores likelihoods altogether and therefore licenses choices any sensible person would call irrational, like refusing a gamble with a tiny chance of a slightly bad outcome no matter how good the upside. Second, a history teacher who likes Rawls but thinks of the difference principle as a piece of egalitarian intuition and will ask why I need the decision-theoretic machinery at all if the conclusion is just that we should care about the worst off. Third, a senior student who did a project on social choice and enjoys asking technical questions; I'm told she's preparing something about whether maximin violates basic consistency requirements for rational choice, possibly involving dominance or independence. The rest of the audience will be classmates who have read a short handout on the original position and will follow the teachers' lead.

Please produce a prep sheet I can revise from: the objections in roughly the order I should expect them, phrased the way each of these three people would actually put them, each with a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, an honest note on where each reply is weakest, and what I should concede gracefully rather than fight. Include the objection I'm hoping nobody raises — I suspect it concerns whether maximin secretly builds in an implausibly extreme aversion to risk, or whether my claim about ignorance versus risk just relocates the problem rather than solving it — and tell me which single point in my own thesis is the softest. Please write the whole answer in British English.
