I am a high school student writing an essay for my philosophy class on whether clinical delusions count as genuine beliefs. Take the Capgras delusion, in which a person insists that a close family member has been replaced by an identical-looking impostor, or the Cotard delusion, in which a person claims to be dead or not to exist. There is a debate between the doxastic view, which holds that delusions really are beliefs (unusual and irrational ones, but beliefs all the same), and views holding that they are some other kind of mental state entirely, such as imaginings, acceptances, or something sui generis. My essay argues against the doxastic view, and I want to structure it around four objections, but so far I only have three that I am happy with and a fourth that I suspect overlaps too much with the others.

The objections I already have are these. First, the action objection: beliefs are supposed to guide behavior, yet patients rarely act on their delusions with the consistency you would expect; a Capgras patient typically does not file a missing-person report for the "real" spouse or treat the alleged impostor the way one would treat an actual impostor, and clinicians sometimes describe this as the patient living in two separate worlds at once. Second, the evidence objection: beliefs are supposed to be responsive to evidence, but delusions are held fixed in the face of overwhelming counterevidence, including the testimony of everyone the patient trusts. Third, the integration objection: ordinary beliefs are woven into a person's whole web of belief and support obvious inferences, but patients often fail to draw even trivial consequences from their delusions and seem to wall them off from everything else they think and do.

Here is what I need. Give me one more objection to the doxastic view that is not on my list and is not merely a rewording, a special case, or a combination of the three objections above. Ideally it should be an objection that would still have force even if the two-factor theory of delusion formation is correct -- that is, even if delusions arise from an anomalous experience plus a separate deficit that prevents the patient from rejecting the belief the experience suggests. After stating the objection, tell me honestly whether you think it succeeds, and say which of my three existing objections it is most likely to stand or fall with, so that I do not end up with two sections of my essay collapsing into one.
