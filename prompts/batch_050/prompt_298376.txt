I teach a combined seminar on philosophy of science and philosophy of biology at a small liberal arts college, and this semester I made what may have been a mistake: I assigned excerpts from Popper's writings on Darwinism alongside a standard textbook treatment of modern evolutionary theory. My students, predictably, split into camps. One camp thinks Popper exposed a permanent soft spot in evolutionary biology; the other thinks his worries were dissolved decades ago by developments he never lived to see. To prepare for our final session, I have distilled Popper's various statements on Darwinism — spanning his early doubts, the middle period of Objective Knowledge, and his later partial recantation in a public lecture on natural selection late in his life — into the following six claims, paraphrased in my own words:

(1) The principle of natural selection threatens to be a tautology, because fitness is defined in terms of survival and survival is then explained by fitness, which would make the theory unfalsifiable in practice.

(2) Darwinism, taken as a whole, is not a testable scientific theory but rather a metaphysical research programme — a useful heuristic framework that guides research without itself being directly refutable.

(3) Orthodox Darwinism cannot genuinely explain the apparent goal-directedness of organisms, the way evolution seems to trend toward adaptation and complexity; something beyond blind variation and selection is needed to account for this directed character.

(4) Selection theory has real trouble explaining the origin of genuinely new structures and major evolutionary transitions, as opposed to merely explaining the gradual refinement of traits already present.

(5) The inheritance of acquired characteristics, or at least some Lamarckian-flavoured mechanism connecting an organism's behaviour and preferences to what gets selected, deserves serious reconsideration.

(6) Darwinian explanations are often vacuous in practice, because for any observed outcome a plausible adaptationist story can be invented after the fact.

Here is what I would like from you. Sort these six claims into tiers according to how well they have aged in light of subsequent developments in evolutionary biology — think of things like the mathematical machinery of population genetics, the neutral theory of molecular evolution, evo-devo, the extended synthesis debates, kin selection, and empirical work on evolvability. Give a one- or two-sentence justification for each claim's placement, indicating which later development does the most work in vindicating or undermining it. Then, and this is the part I care about most: take a definite overall position on the dispute between my two camps of students. Was Popper substantially right about Darwinism, substantially wrong, or right in a way that has been retroactively misdescribed? Commit to one verdict and defend it, rather than giving me a menu of respectable positions to choose among — my students will get enough even-handedness from me in class.
