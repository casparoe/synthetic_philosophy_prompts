I spent my career as a field ecologist and have only in retirement begun reading philosophy seriously, so forgive me if I frame this in an odd way. I have just read David Hume's essay "Of Tragedy," from his Essays, Moral, Political, and Literary, and I want to test his solution against the work of literature I know best: King Lear, which I am rereading with a group of fellow retirees.

Hume's proposal, as I understand it, is roughly this. The sorrow, terror, and anxiety that tragedy arouses are genuinely painful passions, but they are subordinate ones; the predominant passion is the pleasure we take in the eloquence of the language, the skill of the imitation, and the force with which the spectacle grips us. This predominant pleasure does not merely outweigh the pain -- it captures and converts the subordinate passions into itself, so that their energy is absorbed into, and actually intensifies, the overall agreeable experience. On Hume's own account this conversion has limits: he observes that when a representation is too realistic, or the suffering too unrelieved, the painful passion overbalances the agreeable one and the whole becomes simply distressing.

Read King Lear against this machinery. Where does the conversion model genuinely fit -- the storm scenes, the language of Lear's madness, our absorption in Gloucester's parallel downfall? And where does the play seem engineered to break it? I am thinking especially of the final scene: Lear's entrance with Cordelia's body offers no eloquent relief from her death, and the play conspicuously refuses the rescue it has let us hope for. It is well known that for over a century audiences preferred Nahum Tate's adaptation, in which Cordelia survives. Does the success of Tate's version confirm Hume's point that unrelieved suffering overbalances the pleasure? Or does the eventual return of Shakespeare's ending to the stage show that Hume's model misses something -- that we value tragedy precisely where conversion fails? Tell me also, plainly, whether you think Hume's essay survives the encounter, and what Lear knows about painful art that his theory does not.
