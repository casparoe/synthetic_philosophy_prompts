I'm a grad student in a bio department where two senior people have been re-fighting the units of selection war for years: one is a died-in-the-wool gene's-eye view type (replicators, vehicles, the organism as the gene's survival machine), the other is a multilevel selection person who thinks group selection is real, empirically important, and got unfairly buried after the 60s. They've finally agreed to co-write a joint statement for a workshop, and they asked me to produce the first draft, which I now realize was a trap.

Draft that document for me. Concretely I need:

1. A numbered list of claims both of them can actually sign -- about what natural selection requires to operate, about what a "vehicle" or "interactor" even is, about cases like eusocial insects and cancer (i.e. selection running at the level of cell lineages inside a body, against the organism). Don't pad this with vacuous stuff like "selection is important"; I want agreement that's at least mildly load-bearing.
2. A disagreements table where each side's position is worded so that side would accept the wording of their own view. The tricky one I keep seeing: my gene's-eye colleague says multilevel selection is just inclusive fitness bookkeeping in a different notation, mathematically interconvertible, so there's nothing to disagree about; my MLS colleague says that equivalence move only works by defining fitness in a way that drains "the group was selected for" of any causal meaning. I need this stated so it doesn't come out slanted.
3. The cruxes -- the sub-questions where, if you settled them, at least one of them would have to move. For each crux, a concrete case or finding both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Run at least one hypothetical case I'll supply: a population of clonal trees in a forest, where trees in the same grove share a root system, and groves differ in traits that affect grove-level survival through fire. Who counts as the "individual" here, and does either framework struggle?
4. A short section at the end saying which parts of their dispute you think are genuinely about the world and which are about bookkeeping conventions or words, and why.

Also, since I'm the one whose name will be on the draft, tell me where it's most likely to have accidentally favored one side -- which formulations you were least sure were neutral -- and what argument or consideration would make you rewrite one of the disagreements as verbal (or vice versa, definately flag it if something I listed as verbal is actually substantive). Don't just say "reasonable people disagree"; tell me which specific sentence is the riskiest.
