You are a philosopher specializing in action theory, with side expertise in the behavioral-economics literature on intertemporal choice. Please think carefully, and begin by briefly brainstorming the nearest established concepts before committing to an answer; I will not be satisfied with a bare label.

I want to know whether the following pattern has an established name, and if not, how it should be carved. Two instances:

First, a dieter who every morning sincerely judges that, all things considered, she ought to skip dessert that evening; every evening, faced with the menu, she comes to judge that tonight is a justified exception, orders the dessert, and by the next morning judges that she made a mistake. At no point does she act against her own concurrent best judgment.

Second, a researcher who plans to finish a paper by Friday keeps re-deliberating each day, and each day's deliberation concludes, on apparently fresh grounds, that one more day of delay is warranted. He never once does what he at that moment takes to be wrong, yet he reliably does what every past and future self condemns.

This is close to akrasia, but textbook akrasia is usually defined as acting against one's better judgment, and here there is no such act: the judgment moves so that the action never has to. It is also close to hyperbolic discounting, but that literature typically models a stable preference ordering whose weighting shifts with delay — a change in what the agent wants — whereas what strikes me in these cases is a change in what the agent sincerely believes to be best, with the beliefs apparently recruited by the occasion rather than arriving independently of it.

Three questions. (a) Does this configuration — unfailing compliance with one's synchronic better judgment, combined with systematic, temptation-favorable instability of that judgment across time — have a name of its own in the literatures on weakness of will, self-deception, or rationalization, and if so, under whose treatment? (b) How exactly does it relate to akrasia proper, to motivated reasoning, and to preference-reversal models: which, if any, does it reduce to, and from which does it remain genuinely distinct? (c) Is the difference between "the judgment shifts to meet the desire" and "the desire shifts while the judgment holds" even detectable from choice behavior alone, or only from the agent's deliberation — and does that bear on whether the phenomenon is one thing or two? What I want is the carving: what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are.
