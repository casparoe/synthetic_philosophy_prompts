I'm designing a seminar session that pairs Book V of Boethius's Consolation of Philosophy with Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five, and I'd like a reading of the novel through Boethius's account of divine eternity. The Tralfamadorians perceive all moments of time at once, as a completed whole, and Billy Pilgrim -- having come unstuck in time -- adopts their attitude: he already knows how and when he will die, treats every event as fixed, and copes with the bombing of Dresden by directing his attention to the pleasant moments, since nothing could have gone otherwise. Boethius, by contrast, argues that God's eternal knowledge of events is like an observer seeing everything in a single present, and that such knowledge does not impose necessity on the choices foreseen. My question is whether the novel dramatizes the Boethian position or only a fatalist caricature of it. Does Billy's passivity follow from merely knowing the future, as Boethius's own analysis would deny, or does the novel quietly assume that foreknowledge entails fixity -- and if so, where exactly does it part ways with the Consolation: in the metaphysics, or in the observer's relation to what is seen? Second, both texts offer eternity as consolation for suffering, but Boethius insists his picture leaves moral responsibility intact, while Vonnegut's refrain of resigned acceptance seems to preclude blame even for atrocity. Does the novel expose a genuine tension in using timelessness as comfort -- that consolation and condemnation may pull apart -- or does it simply evade the problem Boethius confronted directly? Please point to the scenes that do the real work (Billy's foreseen death, the Tralfamadorian advice, Dresden), say where the novel resists the Boethian reading rather than illustrating it, and end with what fiction adds here that Book V's arguments alone do not.
