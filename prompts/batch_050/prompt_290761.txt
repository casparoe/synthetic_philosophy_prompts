Write a short story (somewhere around 800-1200 words) built around the medieval philosopher Peter Abelard's idea about sin: the sin isn't in the deed itself or in what comes of it, it's in the consent of the will. Fully meaning to do the wrong thing makes you just as guilty as doing it, even if you never lift a finger. And doing something terrible by genuine accident, or in honest ignorance, is no sin at all. Even a flash of temptation that you feel and then refuse isn't sin — only the consent is.

Here's the setup I'd like: a small town, whatever era you want. Two characters whose stories mirror each other:

- one privately consents, wholeheartedly, to do something genuinly awful (say, killing a rival who's about to ruin them) and takes real steps toward it, but through sheer accident the deed never happens — or better, the opposite of what they wanted happens, and the town ends up celebrating them as some kind of hero
- another character does an outwardly worse-looking thing with a totally clean conscience — they honestly believe they're helping, through no carelessness of their own it goes horribly wrong, and the town condemns them for it

The story should end with the town's verdict landing exactly backwards from Abelard's verdict, and I want the reader to feel the pull of both sides — don't strawman the townspeople, their way of judging has to seem natural and decent too. No narrator pausing to lecture about medieval ethics; the idea has to come through the events, and maybe through one character who quietly sees the mismatch and can't get anyone to care.

Optional wrinkle if you can fit it: a third character who never does anything at all, outwardly blameless, but consents in their heart — and what carrying that costs them.

Keep it grounded and human, not a tidy parable. Medieval setting is fine if you want it, but modern day works too — a pharmacist and a mixed-up bottle would do the job nicely, for instance.
