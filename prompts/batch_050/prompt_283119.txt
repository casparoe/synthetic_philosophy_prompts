A friend in my reading group put together an argument that seems to show rational people can almost never deliberate, and none of us can find the hole even though the conclusion is obviously nuts. Here it is:

(1) Deliberating about whether to do A is only rational if, at the time, the question of what you'll do is still open for you. (Nobody deliberates about which horse to bet on after watching the replay.)

(2) So if your evidence lets you confidently predict that you'll do A, deliberation is pointless -- for you, the question is already settled.

(3) Rational people believe what their evidence supports.

(4) But we all have overwhelming evidence about our own future behavior in everyday matters: I know I'm not going to quit my job tomorrow on a whim, I know I'll order the usual at lunch, I know I'll pick the kids up from school. Predicting myself is the easiest prediction I ever make.

(5) So a rational person is in a position to confidently predict the outcome of nearly all of her own everyday choices.

(6) So deliberation is pointless in nearly all everyday choices; it's only rational in the rare cases where you're genuinely opaque to yourself.

(7) But obviously we deliberate constantly about exactly these mundane choices, and it doesn't seem irrational.

Something has to give, but honestly (1) through (4) all look innocent to me. If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) than (4) -- I really do seem to know what I'll do. Which step actually goes wrong, why does it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? Bonus points if you can say something about cases where the prediction feels like it really does kill deliberation (like when I catch myself predicting I'll cave and eat the cake) versus cases where it doesn't.
