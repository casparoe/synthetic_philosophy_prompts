For a graduate philosophy of mathematics seminar, I need the specification against which any account of mathematical truth should be evaluated, before we get to scoring the usual contenders (platonism, fictionalism, structuralism, formalism, intuitionism, and so on). Write the requirements list itself: what such an account must be able to do to count as adequate, with the case or consideration motivating each requirement, and a note on whether each is a genuine desideratum or merely a preference of one camp.

The list should cover, at minimum, the standard pressure points -- the apparent objectivity and mind-independence of mathematical truth, our epistemic access to whatever mathematics is about, the applicability of mathematics in empirical science, the possibility of mathematical error and of long-standing open conjectures, and the surface grammar of mathematical existence claims. But I also want the list tested against three less standard probes, so please address these explicitly:

1. A community could be systematically wrong about a mathematical question for centuries (as arguably happened with the parallel postulate's independence). Must an adequate theory explain what makes such extended, rational, communal error possible, or is explaining individual error enough?
2. Consider hypothetical beings whose physics contains no discrete objects and no stable one-to-one correspondences -- would they develop arithmetic, and does an adequate theory of mathematical truth have to say something about whether "their" mathematics could differ from ours, or is that question illegitimate?
3. Mathematicians widely report that certain conjectures feel inevitable or forced in a way that aesthetic judgments do not. Must a theory account for the phenomenology of mathematical compulsion, or is phenomenology no constraint on metaphysics at all?

For each requirement, state the consideration behind it and whether dropping it would be a real cost or merely an ideological one. Then tell me whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable -- I suspect there is a standing tension between giving mathematics a face-value truth-conditional semantics and giving a naturalistically acceptable epistemology of it, and if that tension is real and general rather than an artifact of particular theories, say so explicitly, because that is the part of the specification I most want to see. Finally, identify the single requirement that excludes the most popular candidates.

Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; do not score the contenders, we will do that in class. End with a last line in the form \answer{...} containing a short label for the single most exclusionary requirement you identified.
