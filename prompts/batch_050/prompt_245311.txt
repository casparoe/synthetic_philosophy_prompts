I lead design reviews at a company that makes professional equipment -- a mix of physical tools and the software that comes with them. Every sprint someone proposes either stripping friction out ("make it seamless, the tool should disappear") or putting friction in ("the user has to notice this"), and the arguments just go in circles because we have no shared standard. I've been reading about Heidegger's distinction between ready-to-hand and present-at-hand -- equipment you use transparently without thinking about it vs. equipment that becomes an object of your attention -- and the idea that tools mostly become visible to us through breakdown. I want you to turn this into a procedure my team can actually run in about 20 minutes during a review: an ordered set of questions to ask about any proposed feature or change, tests for whether a given element currently functions as ready-to-hand or has slipped into presence (and whether that slip is a failure or something we did on purpose), and criteria for when forcing the user to attend to the tool is the right call versus when it's just a malfunction we've rationalized. If the three breakdown modes (conspicuousness, obtrusiveness, obstinacy) do real work in the procedure, build them in; if not, drop them and say why. Then run the procedure on three cases from our backlog: (1) a confirmation dialog before deleting a whole project, (2) a status dashboard on a machine that operators have learned to ignore and basically never look at anymore, (3) a proposal to hide all manual controls behind an automatic mode that works 95% of the time. At least one of these should come out with an answer a product manager will hate. And mark the steps where the procedure has to hand off to a human judgment call because Heidegger simply has nothing to say about it.
