So there's a guy in my lab, Tomasz, who does RL but also sits in on the animal minds reading group, and we've developed this running game where someone brings a system and he gives a snap verdict on whether it can suffer. I want to predict his verdict on next week's case, because I have money riding on it (a coffee, but still). His track record so far: a thermostat -- obviously not. A sea slug's gill-withdrawal reflex, even with sensitization after shocks -- no. A lab rat doing conditioned freezing in a fear-conditioning setup -- yes, definitely. A tabular Q-learning agent in a gridworld getting big negative rewards -- no, "it's just a lookup table." A fancier version of the gridworld agent where the negative reward is unavoidable and the converged policy still occasionally wanders into the punished cells -- still no. A hard-coded robot with no learning that shrieks and backs away from heat sources -- no, which annoyed the behaviorist in the group. Then the interesting one: an RL agent with an internal variable that accumulates negative reward into something like a persistent stress level, which globally shifts its exploration and risk-aversion even in parts of the environment unrelated to where the punishments happened -- he said yes, that one genuinely worried him. But when asked about the same stress-variable agent with its policy frozen, no more learning, the variable still accumulating and still biasing behavior, he hesitated for a while and landed on "probably no, or at least way less."

Next week's case, which I'm bringing: a model-based agent, Dreamer-ish in style, that does most of its learning on imagined rollouts from its own learned world model. Its negative rewards now come entirely from the model's simulations -- it hasn't encountered an actual punishing stimulus from the environment in a long time -- but internally it treats the simulated negative rewards exactly like real ones, including feeding them into that same persistent stress variable Tomasz found worrying, which measurably changes its behavior across the whole environment. Nothing out there is threatening it; the "bad stuff" exists only inside its own model of what could happen. Predict his verdict and walk me through your reasoning: what are the two or three principles his earlier verdicts are jointly consistent with (e.g. something about plasticity, something about lingering internal states with global effects, something about the signal needing to track genuine stakes for the system rather than fictive ones), which principle does this new case split them on, and what's your best guess for where he lands given the hesitation he showed on the frozen-policy case? Also: what case would you bring the week after to settle which principle he's actually running?
