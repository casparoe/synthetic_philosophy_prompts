**Bad Faith in the Age of Authenticity**

Sartre's concept of bad faith is usually introduced through his celebrated vignettes: the café waiter who performs his role a little too eagerly, as though being a waiter exhausted what he is, and the woman on a date who leaves her hand resting in her companion's hand while talking of elevated things, neither withdrawing it nor acknowledging what is happening. What unites these cases is a flight from the dual structure of human reality. Consciousness, for Sartre, is at once facticity — the given situation, body, past and social position we did not choose — and transcendence — the perpetual surpassing of that given towards possibilities. Bad faith exploits this duality: it consists in treating one's facticity as though it were destiny, or one's transcendence as though one could float free of every fact, whichever best avoids the anguish of freedom.

The standard examples of bad faith are excuses: I had no choice, I was made this way, circumstances compelled me. On this reading, the antidote to bad faith would appear to be a recovery of authorship over one's life — the discovery and expression of who one truly is. Contemporary culture has enthusiastically supplied exactly this antidote. We are urged to find ourselves, to live authentically, to be true to ourselves, and we are offered an entire industry of practices devoted to excavating the real self buried beneath social expectation.

My thesis is that this culture of authenticity is not the cure for bad faith but one of its most sophisticated vehicles. Consider what happens when someone announces that they have at last discovered who they really are. The discovered self is treated as an inner object: something fixed, present within, awaiting disclosure. Once unearthed, it does the work that fate and circumstance did for Sartre's excuse-makers. Choices are no longer made but read off: I could not take that job, it would not be me; I must leave this relationship, I have to honour my truth. This is structurally identical to the waiter's performance, with one refinement — the role that exhausts the person is now interior and, apparently, self-chosen. The waiter at least had his costume handed to him; the authenticity-seeker tailors his own and is thereby all the more imprisoned.

A complication must be acknowledged. What I am describing looks very close to what Sartre calls the spirit of seriousness: the attitude that takes values as given things, inscribed in the world rather than sustained by free choice. But I want to insist on a distinction. The serious person locates fixed value outside himself — in God, in duty, in the done thing. The authenticity-seeker manufactures the idol internally and worships a self of his own making. Whether this distinction survives scrutiny is, I admit, unclear: the manufactured self functions exactly as given values do, as a screen against anguish, and perhaps the interiority of the idol makes no philosophical difference at all.

There is a further, distinctively modern twist. Because the authentic self must be expressed to count as lived, it is increasingly constituted as an object for others: curated, displayed and reflected back through the look of an audience. Sartre's account of being-for-others suggests a danger here — one comes to apprehend oneself through the Other's gaze and mistakes the displayed image for the discovered self, adding a second layer of self-objectification to the first.

One might object that none of this is bad faith at all, but mere error: since Sartre denies that any fixed self exists to be discovered, the authenticity-seeker is simply mistaken about the metaphysics. I think this underestimates the phenomenon. The surrounding culture may supply the belief, but the belief must be actively sustained against the anguish that continually betrays our freedom to us, and that sustaining — knowing, in some manner, and refusing to know — is precisely the paradoxical structure Sartre attributes to bad faith.

A final caveat: Sartre himself sometimes speaks of authenticity in a positive register, particularly in later and occasional writings, as the lucid assumption of one's freedom and situation. My target is therefore not authenticity as Sartre occasionally conceives it, but the contemporary imperative to discover a true self — an imperative whose metaphysics Sartre's early work, I argue, gives us the tools to dismantle.
