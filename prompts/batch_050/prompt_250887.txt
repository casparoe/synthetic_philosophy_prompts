You are a philosopher of economics with deep expertise in the socialist calculation debate of the 1930s and 1940s. Think carefully and rigorously about the question below; before composing your final answer, spend some time brainstorming the strongest versions of each side's position rather than reaching for the standard textbook summary of the debate.

Consider the following passage from F. A. Hayek's essay "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (American Economic Review, 1945):

"All economic activity is in this sense planning; and in any society in which many people collaborate, this planning, whoever does it, will in some measure have to be based on knowledge which, in the first instance, is not given to the planner but to somebody else, which somehow will have to be conveyed to the planner."

Now consider a widespread contemporary practice. Large retail chains and e-commerce firms coordinate inventories numbering in the millions of items through centralized algorithmic systems: a single forecasting and optimization engine decides how much of each product is ordered, where it is warehoused, how it is routed, and often what price it carries in each location. Crucially, these systems routinely override the on-the-ground judgments of local store and warehouse managers -- the employee who knows that a local festival is coming, that a road closure will delay deliveries, or that a product is about to be featured by a regional influencer. The corporate norm is that the central algorithm's output wins, and local discretion is treated as noise to be disciplined rather than information to be exploited. Some commentators, noting that a single such firm coordinates more distinct goods than a mid-century national planning bureau ever did, conclude that the calculation debate is over: computers have solved the knowledge problem, and Lange's side (planning aided by computation, with prices set by iteration rather than haggling) has been vindicated over Hayek's and Mises's.

My question is about what this practice presupposes. First, reconstruct the implicit theory of economic knowledge built into the norm of algorithmic override: what must be true about the nature, location, and transferability of economically relevant knowledge for it to be rational to systematically subordinate local judgment to central computation? Read the Hayek passage carefully -- does the practice refute it, comply with it (by finding a new mechanism by which knowledge "will have to be conveyed to the planner"), or simply sidestep it by discarding whatever knowledge cannot be conveyed? Second, determine what the practice does *not* presuppose: a firm's internal allocation runs against a background of market prices for its inputs, its capital, and its labor, and its own scale is disciplined by competition. Does the algorithm's success inside a market therefore leave Hayek's argument entirely intact, and does Lange's own model arguably depend on the same background prices in the same way? Third, identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined -- which features of it a Hayekian, a Langean, and a plain cost-minimizing operations researcher would each describe in their own vocabulary without any empirical disagreement. Finally, tell me concretely what would have to change about the institution -- its scale, its insulation from market prices, its treatment of local knowledge -- before it would genuinely count as a test of the calculation debate rather than a routine case of planning within a price system.

Write a well-organized response of roughly 900-1200 words. The quoted passage does not count toward this length.
