**Vagueness Without Checkable Facts: A Deflationary Proposal**

The sorites paradox forces a choice about vague predicates such as "heap," "tall," and "red." Two responses dominate the literature. Epistemicism holds that every vague predicate has a sharp boundary — some exact number of grains separates the heaps from the non-heaps — but that this boundary is unknowable, typically because knowledge requires a margin for error that borderline cases cannot supply. Supervaluationism holds instead that vague language admits many equally admissible precisifications; a sentence is true simpliciter only if true on all of them, so borderline claims are neither true nor false, while classical tautologies remain true even when no disjunct is.

My thesis is that this dispute, as standardly conducted, rests on a mistake about where the disagreement lives.

Begin with an observation. Both theories make exactly the same predictions about linguistic practice. Consider everything we could in principle observe: competent speakers' graded application dispositions, their reluctance to assert or deny in borderline cases, their use of hedges like "sort of," their convergence under forced choice, their judgments about what would improve precision. The epistemicist predicts all of this via ignorance: speakers cannot know where the cutoff lies, and their behavior rationally reflects that ignorance. The supervaluationist predicts the very same pattern via indeterminacy: speakers' dispositions fail to privilege any one sharpening, and borderline reticence is a fitting response to genuine unsettledness. Same data, two explanations.

Standard arguments on both sides fail to engage this equivalence. Appeals to the intuition that there is "no fact of the matter" in borderline cases simply report the linguistic phenomena both theories predict. Appeals to the unintelligibility of an unknowable cutoff confuse our inability to find the boundary with its absence — precisely the conflation the margin-for-error principle was designed to block. Neither side's favorite consideration is evidence against the other, because neither consideration discriminates between the hypotheses.

One might object that the theories diverge logically: supervaluationism preserves excluded middle without any true disjunct, and it treats "definitely" as a non-epistemic operator, while the epistemicist reads determinacy talk epistemically. I grant these are real differences in consequence. But none of them is checkable against usage. No corpus, no experiment, no pattern of assent and dissent can tell us whether excluded middle holds because some disjunct is secretly true or because truth is supertruth.

What follows? Not that the dispute is empty, but that it has been misfiled. If two semantic theories are empirically equivalent with respect to all linguistic evidence, the choice between them is not a choice within semantics at all. It belongs to metasemantics — to the theory of what determines content in the first place. The real question is whether determinacy of meaning requires that the determinants of meaning (usage, eligibility, naturalness, or whatever one's metasemantics favors) privilege a single cutoff. The epistemicist says the determinants do their work below the threshold of our access; the supervaluationist says the determinants simply run out. That is a substantive disagreement — but it is a disagreement about content-determination, and the battery of sorites-driven intuition pumps that fills the vagueness literature is powerless to settle it. Defending either position therefore requires doing metasemantics directly, not semantics.
