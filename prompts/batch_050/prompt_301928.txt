I'd like you to act as a philosopher of moral psychology with a strong command of twentieth-century history, and to think carefully before answering, laying out your reasoning step by step before delivering any verdict. Here's the situation. My roommate Daniel and our friend Sofia, both of whom audited a course on the Holocaust with me last semester, recently got into a long argument at dinner that has since sprawled across a dozen text messages, and they've deputized me to settle it. I can't, so I'm outsourcing to you. The argument concerns the old dispute between Christopher Browning's "ordinary men" reading and Daniel Goldhagen's "willing executioners" reading of why rank-and-file perpetrators -- say, the middle-aged reservists of Police Battalion 101 -- participated in mass killing. Daniel's position is that the evidence overwhelmingly favors situation over disposition: these were mostly conscripted family men, not fanatics; almost nobody who asked to be excused from the shootings was punished for it, yet almost nobody asked, which he says shows the crushing power of conformity, deference to authority, and not wanting to let down one's comrades. His general claim is that decent people will do monstrous things when the situation is arranged to elicit them, and that the lesson is humility -- any of us would likely have done the same.

Sofia pushed back in stages, and this is where it got interesting. First she said Daniel's own facts undermine him: precisely because refusal was tolerated, the situational-pressure story can't carry the weight he puts on it -- these men had an exit and didn't take it, and many volunteered for killing duties or killed with a zeal the situation didn't require, which points to something in them (a culture of antisemitism, or at least a prior willingness) rather than something merely around them. Daniel answered that this confuses what the exit felt like from the inside with what it cost from the outside: opting out meant visibly breaking with your unit in front of everyone, and "you won't be shot for refusing" is compatible with "refusal is socially unbearable for most people," so the low refusal rate still indicts the situation, not the men. Sofia then shifted ground: she granted the conformity mechanism but argued it's doing less explanatory work than advertised, because conformity itself presupposes something to conform to -- if the men hadn't already regarded Jews as outsiders whose deaths didn't much count, peer pressure to participate in massacring them wouldn't have functioned the way peer pressure to, say, falsify a report would. So culture is upstream of situation, and Browning's account is at best incomplete rather than an alternative to Goldhagen's. Daniel's final move was to accuse Sofia of a subtle character-based story in disguise: once you say the men killed because of what they believed and valued, you've abandoned the radical "it could be anyone" lesson and you're back to explaining evil by the evil (or the bigotry) of particular people, which he thinks is both psychologically naive and morally comforting in a way the truth rarely is. Sofia replied that "it could be anyone" is itself the comforting thesis, because it lets everyone off the hook as victims of circumstance, and that the honest position is that it could be anyone raised into and marinated in the wrong beliefs -- which is still a warning, just a different one.
