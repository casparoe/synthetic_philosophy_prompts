In the debate over whether lying is morally worse than merely misleading — asserting a falsehood with intent to deceive versus asserting a truth calculated to induce a false belief — I have, so far as I can tell, assembled the considerations that exhaust the standard literature. On the side of a genuine difference, with lying the worse offense:

1. The norm-of-assertion argument: by asserting, a speaker undertakes a commitment to the truth of what is said and licenses the hearer to rely on it. A lie breaches that commitment, whereas an implicature carries no comparable warrant, so the liar violates a norm that the misleader leaves intact.
2. The erosion-of-testimony argument: the practice of testimony, on which an enormous share of our knowledge depends, rests on the reliability of what is said rather than of what is merely suggested. Lies corrode that practice in a way misleading implicatures do not, because hearers are entitled to trust assertions outright but can rely on implicatures only defeasibly.
3. The shared-authorship argument: a victim of misleading must draw the inference that produces the false belief and is therefore a partial author of their own deception, which divides culpability. The victim of a lie is deceived through no epistemic contribution of their own, so the liar bears it alone.

On the side of symmetry — or of misleading being equally bad or worse:

4. The parity-of-intention-and-outcome argument: liar and misleader aim at the same false belief through the same cooperative use of language, and the victim ends in the same epistemic state. Morality should not track the syntactic channel by which a deception happens to be delivered.
5. The communication argument: implicatures are part of what a speaker communicates, not an appendage to it. Hearers process them as content and act on them accordingly, so the said/implicated boundary marks nothing the deceived party could register as a difference.
6. The aggravation argument: misleading is often worse. It secures deniability, allowing the deceiver to evade accountability afterward; it recruits the victim into generating the false belief personally, and self-generated beliefs tend to be more durable and more resistant to correction than beliefs one has simply been handed; and it adds an insult by preserving the technical defense that one never actually said the falsehood.

What further consideration bears on this question that is not on my list? It must be genuinely new — not a rewording, a special case, or a combination of the six above — and it must bear on the moral status of lying relative to misleading in particular, rather than on the wrongness of deception in general. It may take any form: a structural feature of one practice, an analogy to a neighboring domain such as promising, omissions, perjury, or nonverbal communication, or an empirical premise with normative weight. Then assess your proposal: does it support difference or symmetry, how much weight does it carry relative to the considerations already listed, and where is it most vulnerable to objection?
