QUESTION: State the absent-minded driver problem, including a payoff structure that generates it. What is the ex ante optimal strategy, and why does the driver's reasoning at the exit seem to push against it? What, if anything, does the puzzle show about decision-making under imperfect recall?

STUDENT ANSWER: The absent-minded driver, due to Aumann, Hart and Perry, is driving home at night on a highway with two exits. Getting off at the first exit is terrible (payoff 0), getting off at the second gets him home (payoff 4), and missing both means sleeping in the car (payoff 1). The catch is that the exits look identical and he's absent-minded: when he reaches an exit he can't remember whether he's already passed one.

Ex ante, the best he can do is a mixed strategy: continue with probability 2/3 at each exit. This maximizes 4p(1-p) + p², giving an expected payoff of 4/3. Exiting deterministically at the second exit isn't an option, since he can't tell which exit he's at.

The puzzle arises once he's sitting at an exit. He assigns probability 1/2 to being at each, so getting off has expected payoff (1/2)(0) + (1/2)(4) = 2, which beats the 4/3 he'd get from sticking to the plan. So at the exit he reasons himself off the highway even though continuing was the plan he'd have chosen before setting out. His uncertainty here is self-locating, a bit like in the Sleeping Beauty case.

The cleanest resolution is precommitment: since he can foresee the temptation to exit, he should bind himself in advance to the ex ante optimal plan, say with a device that implements the 2/3 strategy. With commitment the time inconsistency disappears, so the paradox really just shows that imperfect recall makes precommitment devices more valuable, not that rational choice is genuinely conflicted across time.
