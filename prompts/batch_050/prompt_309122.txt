I am a PhD candidate in classics, working on a dissertation about moral exemplarity in ancient literature, and I am drafting a chapter that uses Plutarch's Lives to argue that moral learning from biography depends on attending to a subject's flaws and inconsistencies rather than idealizing them. While surveying secondary material, I came across a characterization of Plutarch's project in a recent handbook chapter on ancient biography, and it left me uneasy, because if it is accurate, my chapter's central premise is in trouble. The passage reads as follows: 'Plutarch explains in the preface to his life of Alexander that he is writing biography rather than history: since his aim is to reveal character, he is entitled to pass over the famous battles and great deeds and attend instead to the small saying, the jest, the telling gesture. Because the moral lesson always comes first, he does not hesitate to reshape his material accordingly, smoothing contradictions, omitting discreditable episodes, and selecting anecdotes for their edifying power, which is why the Lives are best read as a pagan counterpart to saints' lives: portraits of virtue offered to the reader for imitation. The famous pairing of a Greek life with a Roman one, capped by a formal comparison, is essentially a rhetorical frame that lets him deliver the same lesson to both halves of his world, and precisely because of this moralizing agenda the Lives are of limited use to the modern historian, except as evidence for the ethical preoccupations of Plutarch's own age.'

My worry is that the first part, about the preface to the Alexander, sounds right to me and matches what I remember, but the conclusions the author draws from it feel too strong: I have a fairly clear memory of Plutarch dwelling at length on the disgraceful sides of figures like Antony and Alcibiades, and even saying somewhere that he includes worse characters so readers can learn what to avoid, which seems flatly at odds with the saints' lives picture. I also suspect the treatment of the pairings undersells them, though I could not say precisely how, and the final judgment about the Lives' uselessness to historians strikes me as a controversial verdict presented as settled fact. But the handbook is reputable and the author is a proper specialist, so I do not want to rely on my memory against it.

Then tell me whether the handbook's practical implication for someone in my position, namely that one should not read the Lives as studying flawed exemplars but as offering polished models for imitation, survives the corrections or has to be given up.
