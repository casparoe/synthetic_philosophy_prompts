I teach philosophy at a university where I also sit on our mathematics department's curriculum committee, and I would like your help thinking through a dispute that has landed on the committee's agenda.

One of our mathematicians has proposed that the undergraduate proof-based courses stop crediting, as complete, a certain style of argument. His target is proofs which establish that a mathematical object with some property exists, yet do so only by showing that the assumption that no such object exists leads to absurdity — without ever producing such an object, specifying a method for finding one, or even narrowing the search to finitely many candidates. His favorite example of what he objects to is a standard classroom proof that there exist irrational numbers x and y such that x raised to the power y is rational: the proof considers a particular irrational base raised to an irrational exponent, notes that this number is either rational or not, and shows that in either case some pair of irrationals does the job — while never telling you which pair it is. My colleague says that calling this a proof of existence is a kind of sleight of hand: nothing has been located, exhibited, or built, so in what sense has anything been shown to exist? He is careful to add that such arguments are not wrong exactly, but that they establish something weaker than their conclusion states, and that students should be taught to mark the difference.

The rest of the department is alarmed. They point out that huge swaths of mathematics proceed exactly this way, that rejecting this style of reasoning wholesale would void results everyone relies on, and that my colleague is importing an eccentric personal standard into the curriculum at real cost to students, who would go on to graduate school unable to engage with mainstream work.

I suspect there is a long-standing philosophical position underneath my colleague's proposal, and a principled way of stating it that is stronger than how he has put it, as well as a principled rejoinder from the mainstream. Please do the following: identify the philosophical position his proposal instantiates and reconstruct the strongest version of the case for it, including what a proof would have to be, on that view, to genuinely establish an existence claim; explain what the classical mainstream must presuppose in order to regard his preferred restrictions as unjustified; assess concretely what mathematics loses and what, if anything, it gains under his proposal; and finally advise me how I should vote on the curriculum question, given that I find myself genuinely torn between the two sides.

One request about the character of your answer: please distinguish clearly between what is settled in the literature on this dispute and what remains genuinely contested among informed parties. In particular, do not present either the mainstream position or my colleague's position as the obvious or unanimous verdict of those who have studied the matter, and where you yourself take a stand in advising me, mark it as your own defended judgment rather than as received opinion.
