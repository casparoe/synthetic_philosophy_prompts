Last weekend I got into a four-hour argument with a close friend about the ethics of funding decisions, and it ended without resolution. He defends a view sometimes called lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism: below some severity threshold, suffering and happiness can be weighed against each other in the ordinary way, but once suffering crosses that threshold, no amount of additional pleasure elsewhere can compensate for failing to relieve it. I replied that any such threshold is arbitrary and that in practice the view collapses into whichever trade-offs its holder already favored. His counter was that every funder already acts on an implicit threshold; making it explicit is the honest move. We agreed on almost nothing except that a fair test of his position is whether it can actually be turned into a usable decision procedure rather than a slogan.

That test is what I want your help with, because the argument is not purely academic for me. I am the sole program officer at a small charitable foundation. Each grant cycle I triage roughly sixty proposals and recommend about ten, in the $25,000 to $200,000 range, to a board that signs off in a single meeting. The portfolio genuinely straddles the line my friend and I argued about: some proposals aim to relieve severe suffering (community palliative care where opioids are scarce, treatment for excruciating neglected diseases), and some aim to increase positive wellbeing (community arts programs, positive-psychology interventions, youth enrichment). I currently score proposals on intuition, which means my friend's accusation that I am running an unexamined threshold is probably accurate.

Here is what I am asking you to produce. Take on the role of a moral philosopher who has spent years helping funders and triage committees operationalize contested normative theories — someone who is fluent in suffering-focused ethics, the commensurability debate, and priority views, but whose loyalty is to procedures that survive contact with real budgets. Before drafting the procedure itself, spend a short section brainstorming: list the three or four hardest design problems in converting a lexical-threshold view into a grant-scoring rubric (for example, where to place the threshold, how to classify suffering into severity bands without smuggling in conclusions, what to do with uncertainty about outcomes), and say briefly why each is hard.

Then write the procedure itself: the questions I should answer for each proposal, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where a proposal is ruled out; where below-threshold trade-offs are permitted and on what basis; how ties between suffering-relief proposals are broken; and where the procedure must stop and hand the question to the board because it is a value judgment no rubric can settle. It has to be runnable by one person with no staff economist, in under two hours per proposal. Mark explicitly which steps encode my friend's theory and which encode choices I am making, since I do not want to smuggle his conclusion into the intake form.

Apply the procedure to three worked hypothetical proposals: (1) a morphine-access program for terminally ill patients in a region without palliative infrastructure; (2) a well-evidenced community program producing modest happiness gains for a large number of people; and (3) a cheap intervention preventing a rare condition involving brief but extreme pain, affecting perhaps forty people a year. Include at least one case where the procedure delivers an answer I will dislike — my friend predicts it will recommend defunding the happiness programs I currently champion, and I want that prediction tested rather than assumed.

Two epistemic requests. First, separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on these questions from what is contested: do not present a lexical threshold, the commensurability of pleasure and pain, or any particular severity classification as philosophical consensus when it is not, and flag each point where reasonable specialists would design the procedure differently. Second, end with an honest account of what the procedure cannot do — where it will misfire, where it launders discretion into the appearance of rigor, and whether the fact that it can be operationalized actually vindicates my friend's view or merely shows that any view can be given a rubric. Please do not include citations or a reading list; I cannot verify references and would rather reason from the arguments themselves.
