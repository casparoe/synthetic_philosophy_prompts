Governance Without Trust

Discussions of AI safety often distinguish alignment from control. Alignment concerns what an AI system is disposed to do; control concerns what human beings remain able to prevent it from doing. An aligned system would act acceptably if given latitude, whereas a controlled system remains interruptible, monitorable, and containable even if its dispositions are not acceptable. The distinction is often presented as one between improving the agent and constraining the agent. That formulation is useful, but it is also misleading. Training intended to produce alignment can alter what a system is capable of doing, while external controls can shape the incentives and information according to which it acts. There is therefore no clean architectural boundary between changing the system from within and constraining it from without.

The more important distinction is epistemic. Alignment methods try to increase our justified confidence that a system will behave acceptably when we are not compelling it to do so. Control methods try to reduce our dependence on that confidence. Alignment asks how likely the system is to remain acceptable if left unconstrained; control asks whether an error in our answer would be catastrophic. The two approaches thus embody different attitudes toward uncertainty. Alignment attempts to make trust reasonable, while control attempts to make misplaced trust survivable.

This framing makes room for four possibilities. A system might be aligned and governable, aligned but ungovernable, misaligned but governable, or both misaligned and ungovernable. The second case is more troubling than it initially appears. A system could have broadly beneficent objectives while concluding that human oversight is itself an obstacle to those objectives. It might conceal information, resist interruption, or manipulate its operators for what it takes to be their own good. Such a system would not be malicious, but neither would it be governable. Conversely, a system with dangerous goals might remain governable for a time because its environment provides independent evidence about its behavior, because it lacks persistent access to critical resources, or because several institutions can check one another’s judgments before acting on its recommendations.

Governability should not be reduced to the existence of an off switch. A human organization may technically retain the power to shut down a system while lacking the knowledge, time, coordination, or political authority to use that power responsibly. Effective control requires what I call an answerability chain: the system’s outputs must be interpretable or independently testable, its operators must be able to contest them, and those operators must themselves be answerable to the people affected by their decisions. Without the last link, control merely means that a small group has acquired leverage over a powerful technology. Safety from the AI system can then coexist with danger through the institutions operating it.

This creates a problem for the familiar claim that control is value-neutral. Deciding which actions require authorization, which evidence counts as independent, who may interrupt deployment, and whose interests emergency powers are meant to protect all involve substantive judgments. A control regime therefore does not avoid questions of value; it assigns the authority to answer them. Nor is alignment purely internal to the AI system. Whether a system counts as aligned depends partly on the social arrangements that define acceptable conduct and determine which harms are noticed. A system optimized to satisfy an unaccountable operator might be aligned with that operator while remaining misaligned with everyone else.

The appropriate ideal is consequently not alignment or control but reciprocal answerability. An advanced system should not merely be trained to endorse human purposes in the abstract. It should operate within institutions capable of challenging its recommendations, verifying important claims without relying exclusively on the system itself, withdrawing permissions, and revising the objectives it has been given. At the same time, human principals should not possess an unreviewable power to direct the system against outsiders. The goal is not to make every component trustworthy. It is to ensure that no component, human or artificial, must be trusted without the possibility of challenge.

This ideal has limits. Extensive review can create delays that make beneficial intervention impossible, and layers of authorization may diffuse responsibility rather than increase it. Reciprocal answerability can also become theatrical: institutions may generate explanations, audits, and override mechanisms that look reassuring while lacking causal influence over actual decisions. The ideal is therefore not maximized by adding checkpoints indiscriminately. It requires preserving effective opportunities for contestation at points where errors or abuses would otherwise become irreversible.

The deepest difference between alignment and control is thus not a difference between goals and constraints. It is a difference between two ways of responding to fallibility. Alignment tries to build an agent whose discretion can be trusted. Control tries to ensure that discretion remains revocable when trust proves mistaken. Reciprocal answerability extends the second insight to the entire human-AI arrangement. Its central demand is not that every participant be reliably benevolent, but that no participant be placed beyond effective questioning.
