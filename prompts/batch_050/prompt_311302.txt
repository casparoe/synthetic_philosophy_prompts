A newspaper column on electoral reform that I read recently characterises Arrow's impossibility theorem as follows:

"Kenneth Arrow's celebrated theorem proves that no voting system can ever be fair. Arrow assumed three uncontroversial requirements any decent voting rule should meet: first, that the rule should work for every possible profile of voter preferences, however odd those preferences might be; second, that if every voter prefers X to Y, the rule should rank X above Y; and third, that society's ranking of X against Y should depend only on how voters rank X against Y, not on how they feel about some third option Z. He then proved that the only rule satisfying these requirements is one that simply copies the preferences of a single voter -- a dictator. The lesson usually drawn is that majority voting is inherently incoherent, since Arrow showed that majority rule can produce cycles, and that the dream of aggregating individual preferences into a rational social preference is therefore dead."

Something in this seems overconfident to me, and I suspect the column has blurred together at least two distinct results, but I cannot put my finger on exactly where. Could you audit the characterisation?
