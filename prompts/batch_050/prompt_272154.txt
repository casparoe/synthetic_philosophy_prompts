My roommate is a math major and I'm a philosophy minor, and we keep having the same dumb fight. Last night I said that the continuum hypothesis probably doesn't have a real answer -- since the standard axioms of set theory famously don't settle it, mathematicians basically get to choose whether to work in universes where it's true or false, so at least that part of math is invented, like picking the rules of a game. He hit me with "mathematics is discovered, not invented" like that ended the argument, and said even the continuum hypothesis is true or false "out there," we just haven't found the right axioms to see which yet.

Here's my problem: I've heard this slogan a million times, about primes, about pi, about imaginary numbers, and I honestly can't tell what it's even claiming half the time. Sometimes it seems to just mean "math answers aren't up to us" -- fine, obviously you cant just decide that 7+5=13. But my roommate clearly meant something much stronger, something that settles a metaphysical question about infinite sets. And I suspect those are very different claims, and that the boring one doesn't actually get him what he wants.

Can you break the slogan down into the distinct precise theses it could be expressing -- from the most deflationary reading (something about proofs being constrained, maybe) up through full-blown Platonism about abstract objects -- and for each reading say: is it plausible, and does it actually support using the slogan against my continuum hypothesis point? Lay it out as a list I can bring back to him, not a general lecture on Platonism. And if it turns out that every defensible reading leaves our actual argument untouched, and only the indefensible ones settle it, I want to hear that too.
