My roommate trades crypto and has been reading a bunch of stuff from the ergodicity-economics corner of the internet — the basic pitch being that expected value is a scam invented for ensembles, and what actually matters for you is the time-average growth rate of your wealth, since you only get to live one trajectory rather than the average over a thousand parallel copies of yourself. Fine, I buy that this reframing does real work (it seems to explain insurance and the Kelly criterion in one shot, which is neat). But last night he walked me through an argument that he says proves no rational person should ever accept any risk of ruin whatsoever, and it basicly feels like it proves way too much. Here it is, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it:

(1) You live one life in sequence, so the quantity that governs how well you actually do is the time-average growth rate of your wealth along your single trajectory, not the ensemble average (expected value) across hypothetical copies of you.
(2) For multiplicative dynamics — you repeatedly stake some fraction of what you have — the time-average growth rate equals E[log of the growth factor], the probability-weighted average of log(wealth after / wealth before). So rational choice just is maximizing E[log wealth].
(3) Any gamble with a nonzero probability of wiping you out makes E[log wealth] equal to minus infinity, since log(0) is minus infinity and any nonzero weight on minus infinity sinks the whole expectation.
(4) Therefore no gamble carrying any nonzero risk of ruin can be rational, no matter how tiny the risk or how enormous the upside. (He added that if you repeat such gambles, hitting ruin eventually is a probability-one event anyway, since zero is an absorbing state.)
(5) But essentially every choice in real life carries some nonzero risk of ruin: crossing the street to get lunch, starting a company, keeping savings in any single bank, getting on a plane to a wedding.
(6) So rationality forbids doing basically anything. Actually it's worse than forbidding — sitting still and doing nothing also has risks, so the view doesn't even leave you a permitted option.

The conclusion is obviously nuts, so something in the chain has to give, but each individual step looks locally fine to me, which is exactly what bothers me. Which step do you reject — and if it's one of the early ones, what does rejecting it cost? I'd really like to hold on to the core ergodicity insight if possible, i.e. I don't want the fix to just be "expected value was right all along," because that throws out the nice explanations of insurance and Kelly sizing. Also: once you've located the flaw (or told me which premise is cheapest to drop), say what the repaired view actually implies for a concrete case — like a one-in-ten-million chance of losing everything in exchange for a near-certain doubling of your wealth every year for a decade. Does the repaired view still warn against it, and if so, on what grounds that don't also forbid crossing the street?

At the very end of your answer, give me a machine-readable line in the form \answer{...} containing the number of the first step you reject (for example \answer{step 3}), or \answer{sound} if you think the argument is actually valid and I should just bite the bullet on one of the premises.
