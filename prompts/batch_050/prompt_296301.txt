For the past year or so I've been turning over a thought experiment I came across by accident, about a scientist named Mary who knows every physical fact there is to know about color vision — the wavelengths, the optics of the eye, the firing patterns in the brain — but who has spent her entire life in a black-and-white room and has never actually seen color. The question is whether she learns anything new the first time she steps outside and sees something red. I've thought about it long enough that I've come to a definite conclusion: I believe she does learn something, something no accumulation of physical facts could have handed her in advance. I've never been able to say why in a way that convinces my friends, so I'd like to try another route.

Write me a sestina about Mary. For clarity, I mean six stanzas of six lines each, plus a three-line envoi, where the same six end-words rotate through the stanzas in the fixed pattern and all six appear in the envoi. One of the six end-words should be red. Choose the other five yourself, but choose them so that their constant return does real work: I want the recurrence of the words to enact the way this debate keeps circling back to the same spot, with the same terms meaning something slightly different each time around, until the envoi finally breaks the circle and delivers a verdict.

The poem must take a side — mine. Don't weigh the positions and leave the question open; commit to the claim that Mary learns something new, and make the poem earn that claim rather than simply announce it. Along the way it should confront the strongest objection I know of, which is that Mary doesn't acquire new knowledge at all, only a new ability: recognizing red, imagining it, remembering it — something more like learning to ride a bicycle than learning a fact. Answer that objection within the poem itself, not by dismissing it.

After the poem, add a short prose note — a paragraph is enough — stating where you took liberties with the thought experiment or leaned on a reading of it that reasonable people contest, so that I know which parts of the poem I'd have to defend if I shared it.
