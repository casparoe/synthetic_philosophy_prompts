The triviality results associated with David Lewis show that, given a few natural assumptions about how indicative conditionals embed and how their probabilities behave, the thesis that the probability of an indicative conditional equals the conditional probability of its consequent given its antecedent forces probability functions toward triviality. The literature has since branched in several directions: deny that indicatives express ordinary propositions at all (suppositional and expressivist accounts); treat "if" as restricting a modal or probability operator rather than as a two-place connective; adopt dynamic semantics on which conditionals lack stable, classically evaluable content; restrict the equation to limited contexts or assertion conditions; or revise the background logic and probability theory. Each response preserves some intuitions and sacrifices others, and each generates its own open problems: how conditionals behave under negation, under other conditionals, and under quantifiers; what to say about nested and compounded conditionals; how indicative and subjunctive forms relate; and how any of this interacts with decision theory, where conditional probabilities are doing real work rather than merely serving as test cases.
