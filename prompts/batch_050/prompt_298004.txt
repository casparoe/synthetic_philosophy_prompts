I have no academic background in philosophy, but for years I have been turning over a question on my own that I cannot settle, and I would like help thinking through what follows from it.

The question is whether self-deception is even possible. By self-deception I mean cases like this: a man whose marriage is failing, who on some level plainly knows it — the evidence is in front of him every day — yet sincerely insists, to himself and to others, that everything is fine. The puzzle, as I have come to understand it, is twofold. First, deceiving someone seems to require that the deceiver knows the truth while the deceived does not; if the deceiver and the deceived are the same person, that person must apparently both believe and not believe the same thing at the same time, which looks like a contradiction. Second, deception seems to require an intention — the deceiver deliberately misleads — but if I deliberately set out to mislead myself, surely I would see through my own plan, the way you cannot surprise yourself by hiding your own birthday present.

My own attempted solution is that self-deception is not a matter of believing a falsehood but of managing one's attention: the uncomfortable belief never quite gets formed because you keep yourself from dwelling on the evidence — changing the subject inside your own head, seeking out one-sided information, never putting the suspicion into words. But I am unsure whether this dissolves the paradox or merely relocates it, since managing your attention in that targeted way seems to require knowing, at some level, exactly what you must not look at.

Here is my actual question: what are the most important practical implications of this puzzle, and of the main strategies for resolving it, for how I should conduct my own inner life? For instance: if genuine self-deception turns out to be impossible, does that mean I can trust my sincere avowals more than people generally assume, or does it instead mean that what I have been calling self-deception is a different, subtler failing that requires different defenses? If it is possible but only through something like attention-management or a compartmentalized mind, which everyday practices would that diagnosis actually predict to help — journaling, saying my reasons out loud, asking others to challenge me, committing myself in advance to predictions? And what does the analysis imply about responsibility: is a self-deceived person more like a liar or more like the victim of a liar, and should the answer change how I judge, say, a relative who seems to be deceiving herself about her drinking?

I would like the implications to be genuinely consequential — conclusions that would alter a habit, a policy of self-scrutiny, or a judgment I make about others — rather than a survey of positions, and I would like you to say plainly where the disagreement runs deep enough that no confident practical conclusion follows.
