For my final paper in an upper-level seminar on the evolution of social behavior, I've provisionally committed to the thesis that conspicuous philanthropy in humans -- large, publicly attributed charitable giving, the kind where the donor's name goes on the building -- is best explained as a costly signal in roughly the handicap-principle sense: the donation is honest evidence of the donor's underlying resources or prosociality precisely because it would be uneconomical for someone lacking those qualities to fake. I'm fairly confident in this, but I want the paper's critical section to be genuinely searching rather than a token objection-and-reply, so I'm asking you to map the view's exposure. What would count against it? Please distinguish, first, the objections that are already standard in the literature (cheap-talk alternatives, reputation and indirect reciprocity as rival explanations, the worry that "honest because costly" conflates several distinct mechanisms) from the evidence or theoretical results that would actually force me to abandon the thesis, and rank the latter from fatal to merely awkward. Second, tell me what a committed costly-signaling theorist would themselves concede as a defeater -- for instance, a specific observable pattern of donation behavior that the signal story predicts but the rival stories forbid, or vice versa -- rather than what its critics claim. Third, address the objection I most worry about: that the thesis has been qualified into unfalsifiability, since any giving that looks costless can be reclassified as a signal of something cheaper and any failure to find audience effects can be blamed on measurement, and say whether that is a genuine defect for a functional explanation of this kind or simply what mature adaptationist hypotheses look like. Finally, indicate which two or three findings, if they appeared in the next few years of experimental or cross-cultural work, should move me most, and in which direction each should move me.
