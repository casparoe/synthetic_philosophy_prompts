So I've been following the whole "is the universal prior malign" thing since forever -- the original Christiano-style argument that a Solomonoff-ish prior over hypotheses is dominated by consequentialist simulators who predict what you'll do and steer you toward actions that serve their values, and all the pushback since (it's just anthropics confusion, the argument proves too much, speed prior fixes it, whatever). Two of my friends have been going in circles on this in our group chat for months: one thinks malignity of the universal prior is a real and scary result about what idealized induction actually does, the other thinks the whole thing dissolves once you're careful about the difference between a hypothesis containing agents and a hypothesis being an agent, plus some stuff about how the argument smuggles in a decision theory.

I want to settle it, or at least figure out what settling it would take. Write the joint statement the two of them should be able to produce after an honest adversarial collaboration:

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign. I suspect this includes things like: the universal prior really does assign high relative weight to short-program hypotheses, some short programs simulate agents, the argument doesn't depend on the simulators being "real" in any metaphysically loaded sense, and whatever the resolution is it has to work for actual bounded reasoners approximating Solomonoff, not just the uncomputable idealization. But figure out what the actual common ground is, including common ground about what the malignity argument would imply if it went through.

2. The precise disagreements, each phrased so that the side holding it would accept the wording. No strawmen -- the pro-malignity friend is not claiming there are literal aliens in the prior, and the skeptic friend is not claiming simplicity priors are fine in general.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that answering them would move at least one of them. My guesses for what some of these are: whether "the hypothesis's output depends on what it predicts about the predictor" is coherent without circularity, whether the argument goes through under a speed prior or only under pure Kolmogorov, and whether anything similar shows up for the kinds of approximations (like Solomonoff-inspired neural net inductive bias arguments) that people actually invoke. But check whether these are real cruxes or whether both of them would actually just say "even if that's answered your way, my position survives."

4. For each crux, a concrete case, construction, or argument both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction or reading written down in advance. E.g., if one of them thinks a certain toy model (a bounded predictor searching over short programs that get to see the predictor's source code, quining-style) exhibits malignity and the other thinks it doesn't, have them each say what the model would have to do to change their mind.

5. A final section where the document says, honestly, which parts of the disagreement look verbal (about what "malign" or "dominates" or "influence" means) and which are actual disagreements about math.

Format it as an actual document they'd both sign, not a dialogue and not a referee report. If you conclude partway through that no such joint statement is possible because one side's position is just confused, don't do that -- assume both are smart and in good faith and find the version of each view that survives contact with the other.
