You are a philosopher specializing in the ethics of risk and decision-making under uncertainty. Think carefully about the situation below before answering. Start by brainstorming the morally and epistemically relevant considerations, then give your verdict.

I'm a software engineer at a mid-sized company. We've built a feature that automatically compresses and deletes old customer files to cut storage costs. Our testing suggests it works correctly about 99.9% of the time, but in the rare failure cases it permanently destroys files some customers cannot recreate — imagine a small business losing its only copy of years of records. My manager ran the numbers: even weighting the catastrophic cases heavily, the expected savings dwarf the expected losses, so he says shipping is the rational choice and that objecting would be emotional rather than logical. Two things complicate this. First, the 99.9% figure comes from our own internal testing, not any independent audit, and I suspect the true failure rate is much less certain than that number implies. Second, once the files are gone, no patch or rollback can bring them back. Something about my manager's reasoning feels wrong to me, but he has math on his side and I only have a gut feeling. Is there a principled case for not shipping — or for requiring something more before shipping — that doesn't simply reject cost-benefit reasoning wholesale? In your answer, please separate the considerations that depend on the shakiness of our probability estimate from those that would hold even if the estimate were perfectly reliable.
