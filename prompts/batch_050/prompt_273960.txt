I'm a high school student, and I've noticed a pattern in how people my age (and, honestly, most adults I know) form their picture of the political "other side," and I'd like to know whether it has an established name.

Here is the pattern. Most of what I "know" about what the other side believes doesn't come from hearing them make their own case. It comes from my side's commentary about them: group chats passing around screenshots of the dumbest post anyone found that week, videos where a commentator I agree with dismantles some random person's terrible argument, a parent or teacher summarizing the other side's position in one sentence before explaining why it's wrong. The end result is that I can recite, in detail, the worst reasons anyone has ever given for the positions I disagree with, because those are the examples that get selected, shared, and laughed at. But if you asked me to state the strongest case for those same positions, I'd struggle. My picture of the other side has been assembled entirely from their worst representatives, pre-selected by people who already oppose them.

I've tried to match this to concepts I already know, and none of them fit exactly. It's not a straw man, because a straw man is something you do within an argument — misrepresenting a specific opponent's position — whereas I'm describing something that happens before any argument takes place, in what a person reads and watches. It's not quite an echo chamber either: the standard picture of an echo chamber is that you never encounter the other side at all, but here you encounter them constantly, only filtered through hostile selection. And it's not exactly the "weak man" I've seen people discuss, since that's a move a speaker makes — deliberately rebutting the weakest opposing arguments — while I'm asking about the situation of the audience member whose entire understanding ends up built out of those weak examples, often without any single person intending that outcome.

So my questions are these. Does this configuration have an established name? If it does, how does it differ from the three concepts above, and which of them is it most easily confused with? If it doesn't have a name, could you coin one, define it precisely, and mark out its edges — for example, does it still count when the hostile selection is done by a recommendation algorithm rather than by people, and does it count when the worst examples being circulated are, as it happens, genuinely representative of the other side? I should say that I won't be satisfied with a bare label; what I really want is the carving — what exactly this phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its boundaries lie.

One more request: please write your answer in British English.
