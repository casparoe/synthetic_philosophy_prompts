I'm a third-year PhD student putting together a prospectus for my dissertation committee, due in about six weeks, and I'd like help drafting it. My topic is the identity problem for mathematical structuralism: the familiar point, going back to Benacerraf's observation that the natural numbers can be modeled equally well by the von Neumann ordinals (where 2 is literally an element of 4) or the Zermelo numerals (where it isn't), seems to show that numbers can't be sets, and structuralists conclude that arithmetic is only about positions in a pattern, not objects with intrinsic identities. But here's my half-formed idea that I'd like the prospectus to be built around: practicing mathematicians make cross-structural identifications all the time without embarrassment. The integers are treated as literally contained in the rationals, the rationals in the reals, the reals in the complexes, even though each stage can be constructed in incompatible ways from the last; meanwhile 'is 2 an element of 4?' is dismissed as a non-question. So there must be some operative distinction between legitimate and illegitimate identifications, and I don't think the standard structuralist literature states it precisely. My hunch is that the distinction tracks whether the identification preserves the structure that the embedding theorems certify, rather than tracking anything about the objects themselves, but I haven't worked out whether that's a criterion, a stipulation, or just a description of sociological fact.

Draft a two-page prospectus that turns this into a fundable, defensible project. It should state the central question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; explain why the main existing positions (ante rem structuralism, in re or eliminative structuralism, and the 'multiple reductions' quietism that just declines the question) leave it open rather than answering it; lay out what I would actually do across the dissertation's chapters, including which mathematical cases I'd examine as data (the number-system embeddings, competing constructions of the reals, and at least one case from outside arithmetic, such as the rival definitions of ordered pair or of ordered tuples of varying length); and say what the possible conclusions are and what considerations would decide between them. I also want an honest section on what would count as the project failing — for instance, if it turns out the distinction is purely conventional and there is nothing philosophical to say beyond that — and a realistic note on scope, since I have about two and a half years and a committee that will balk at anything requiring serious new technical results. Please don't include citations or a bibliography; I'll supply those myself, but do name the positions and arguments so I know what to cite.
