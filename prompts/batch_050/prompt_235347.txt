Two friends of mine, a biology graduate student and a philosopher of science, fell into an argument after a seminar on evolution that they still haven't resolved, and I'd like a referee. The biology student, Dana, started by rehearsing the old tautology objection: "survival of the fittest" is empty, she said, because the fittest are just the ones who survive, so the theory that the fittest survive explains nothing and cannot be wrong. Her friend Marcus replied that this objection was retired decades ago by the propensity interpretation: fitness is not actual reproductive success but a probabilistic disposition — an organism's expected reproductive contribution given its traits and its environment — the way a die's bias is a disposition to land on six. The theory's claim, then, is that differences in fitness probabilistically explain differences in actual success, and that is no more circular than explaining a glass's breaking by its fragility.

Dana pushed back on two fronts. First, she said, if fitness is a propensity, then the fitter type can be outreproduced by sheer bad luck — a lightning strike, a flood — so "the fittest tend to survive" is only a statistical tendency, and the theory dodges falsification by retreating to talk of expected values that are never directly observed. Second, and she thought this was the deeper problem, expected success is always relative to an environment and a time horizon, and in practice biologists decide which environment and horizon count by looking at what actually happened: the trait that won is declared fitter in "the relevant environment," which just reinstates the circularity through the back door. Marcus answered that statistical explanations are still explanations — nobody thinks a fair coin's probability of heads is vacuous because individual flips surprise us — and that identifying a disposition partly through its manifestations is normal science: we learn a glass is fragile by seeing glasses break, yet fragility still explains breaking because it is grounded in molecular structure that can be independently characterized. Dana's reply was that fitness conspicuously lacks any such independently characterizable ground: there is no "molecular structure of fitness," since the very same trait is fitness-enhancing in one environment and ruinous in another, so the fragility analogy fails exactly where it needs to hold. Marcus's last move was to concede that fitness is environment-relative but insist that relational properties are still real properties — "to the left of" is relational and perfectly explanatory — and that the theory's genuine content is the claim that trait frequencies change as a function of heritable variation in fitness, which is testable in the lab.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move. For each of the main moves above, say whether it succeeds, partly succeeds, or fails, and why — including whether Dana's "back door circularity" charge actually lands, whether the fragility analogy does the work Marcus needs, and whether his final retreat to lab testability saves the theory's content or concedes Dana's real point. Finally, say who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
