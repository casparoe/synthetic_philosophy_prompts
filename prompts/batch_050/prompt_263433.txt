I'm preparing to teach a unit on consent in a graduate seminar on practical ethics, and I've noticed that my students -- and, frankly, most of the literature -- evaluate theories of valid consent by checking them against whichever cases their author happened to care about. Before we read anything else, I want a specification. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of what makes consent valid must satisfy: the cases it must classify correctly, the distinctions it must be able to draw, the roles it must be able to serve, and the commitments from neighboring areas (law, moral psychology, political philosophy) it cannot contradict. Give each desideratum together with the case or consideration that motivates it, stated concretely -- for instance, the person who agrees to sex because their landlord has threatened eviction, the patient who signs for surgery without being told of a cheaper alternative their insurer prefers not to mention, the enthusiastic but quite drunk person at a party, the person deceived not about what will happen but about who their partner really is, the person who undertakes something (parenthood, an irreversible medical procedure, emigration) whose experiential character cannot be known in advance, and the person who clicks "I agree" on terms nobody reads.

Within that list, I want you to make definite choices rather than surveying options. Mark each desideratum as non-negotiable or as a camp-specific preference, and commit to that classification with reasons: for example, take a stand on whether a theory that cannot distinguish having terrible options from being coerced is thereby refuted or merely unfashionable, and on whether consent is fundamentally a mental state, a performative act, or some hybrid, framed as a requirement the theory must encode rather than as a question to leave open. I especially want you to state the purpose-relative desiderata explicitly: a theory of consent has to function both as a moral permission-transformer between individuals and as a workable standard for criminal law and clinical practice, and some things demanded of it in one role may be impossible in the other. If some subset of the desiderata you've listed is jointly unsatisfiable -- say, matching our intuitions about deception cases while also giving law a bright-line test, or treating background injustice as consent-undermining without invalidating most economic agreements -- say so plainly and identify which desideratum you would sacrifice; that is the part I most want to see argued for. Finally, tell me which single requirement, if enforced, excludes the largest share of positions my students will otherwise encounter, and defend your pick. Keep the weight on the criteria themselves; I'll do the scoring of particular authors in class.
