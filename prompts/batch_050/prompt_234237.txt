I run a fifty-minute seminar for second-year philosophy undergraduates, and next week the topic is whether conceivability is a guide to possibility. I've set them this passage from Hume's A Treatise of Human Nature (Book I, Part II, Section II):

"WHEREVER ideas are adequate representations of objects, the relations, contradictions and agreements of the ideas are all applicable to the objects; and this we may in general observe to be the foundation of all human knowledge... The plain consequence is, that whatever appears impossible and contradictory upon the comparison of these ideas, must be really impossible and contradictory, without any farther excuse or evasion."

Don't explain the passage to me; plan how I should build the session around it as the thesis under test. Specify the order in which the ideas must be introduced: I assume students need the distinction between imagining and conceiving, and between logical, metaphysical, and natural possibility, in place before Hume's inference can be assessed rather than nodded at — but tell me whether the dependency really runs that way, and where Hume's adequacy condition ("adequate representations") belongs in the sequence, given that it's the load-bearing premise nobody notices. Choose the one example that will carry the session — I'd rather avoid the standard philosophical zombie; a case that seems clearly conceivable but is arguably impossible would do more work — and say what the example must contain and must lack to put pressure on the passage in the right direction. Map the misconceptions I should expect: I'm anticipating "conceivable means picturable," "possible means physically possible," and the reading of Hume's claim as one about the limits of our imagination rather than about the fit between ideas and objects; indicate at exactly which point in the sequence each should be headed off, and whether the Kripke-style challenge (necessary truths we seem able to conceive as false) should be raised by me or engineered so a student raises it. Finally, give me one closing question that would show the class can evaluate Hume's principle rather than recite it, and name what I should deliberately refuse to cover at this level.
