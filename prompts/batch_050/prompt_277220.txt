I run a second-year undergraduate political theory seminar (16 students, one 90-minute session) and we are mid-way through Rousseau's Social Contract. The students have read the famous passage in which whoever refuses to obey the general will shall be compelled to do so, and this will mean nothing other than that he will be forced to be free. They dutifully flag the phrase as sinister or confused and move on. I want an activity that makes the idea land before it gets named: I want them to experience, in their own small choices, the gap between what a person wills in the moment and what a person wills as a member of a lawgiving body -- and to feel both the plausibility and the creepiness of someone telling them that being constrained is their real freedom.

Design me a runnable activity for this session. Requirements:

1. The experience must come first and the vocabulary second. Participants should enact a collective decision that later binds some of them against their occurrent preference, and the moment of enforcement should genuinely happen in the room, not be described hypothetically. Only in the debrief should the phrases general will, particular will, and forced to be free be introduced.

2. Give me concrete mechanics: the setup and stakes (no real money; points, privileges, or commitments are fine), the rules of the decision procedure, who enforces, a minute-by-minute timeline, and exactly what I as facilitator say and don't say at each stage. Include at least one design feature that lets a dissenter comply while denying that the law is theirs -- the activity should not stack the deck toward Rousseau's conclusion, or the debrief will be hollow.

3. The debrief: map specific predictable moments in the activity onto the conceptual distinctions Rousseau needs (will of all vs. general will, citizen vs. subject, law as self-given), and state plainly which feature of the doctrine the activity cannot reproduce -- I suspect the claim that the general will, properly formed, cannot err is beyond any classroom simulation, but tell me if you think otherwise and what to do about it.

4. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this -- students gaming the payoff structure, a faction forming that breaks the fiction, someone genuinely upset at being coerced -- and how to handle each.

Finally, for each major design choice (the stakes, the enforcement mechanism, the moment of naming), say how confident you are that it will produce the intended effect rather than a different one, and single out the single element of the design you are least sure will work as intended.
