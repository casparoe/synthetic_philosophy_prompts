For years the interpretability sections of my usual blogs and forums have gone back and forth on a question I suspect is more philosophical than most participants admit: do neural networks actually have representations, or are we reading structure into them? A sparse autoencoder finds directions that fire on Golden Gate Bridge text; a probe recovers the board state of Othello from activations; a steering vector changes behavior. The optimists call these discovered features. The skeptics note that you can probe for almost anything in a high-dimensional activation space, that linear decodability is cheap, and that every "finding" passes through a human who chose what to look for. I am writing a long post trying to get underneath this dispute, and what I want from you is not a verdict but a specification.

Write the desiderata that an account of representation in artificial neural networks must meet before claims like "the model represents X" or "this direction is a feature" deserve realist rather than instrumentalist reading. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Some desiderata I expect to see defended, refined, or rejected: the account must distinguish a representation's being decodable from its being used downstream (a probe can extract information the network never routes into computation); it must say something non-vacuous about superposition and polysemantic neurons, where the naive one-feature-per-neuron picture demonstrably fails; it must survive the observation that human-chosen labels (sentiment, truthfulness, deception) are doing interpretive work the math does not do on its own; it must handle feature splitting, where more capacity or a finer method keeps resolving one "feature" into many, without making featurehood purely relative to the researcher's budget; and it should make contact with whatever we already think representation requires in the biological case, or else explain why the analogy is being declined.

For each item, say whether it is a genuine constraint any adequate theory must satisfy or a prejudice of one camp -- the mechanistic-interpretability optimists, the probers, the causal-abstraction people, the skeptics who think the whole framing anthropomorphizes -- and give the strongest argument for that classification. Two things I especially want. First, if some subset of the intuitive desiderata looks jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly and show the tension; if the spec itself has a no-go result hiding in it, that is a finding, not a failure. Second, identify which single requirement eliminates the most currently popular positions, and whether that is evidence for the requirement or against it.

Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases. If you sketch how the main existing stances fare against the finished list, do it briefly; I will do the full scorecard myself.

Finally, be explicit about your own epistemic position: state which argument, experiment, or theoretical result would most change your mind about the composition of the list -- in particular, what would move a requirement you called non-negotiable into the camp-prejudice column, or vice versa -- and flag where in your reasoning you are least certain.
