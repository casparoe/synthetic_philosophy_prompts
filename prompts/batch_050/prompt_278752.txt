A friend and I run a reading group on the philosophy of personal relationships, and we have been stuck for two sessions on the same question, so I want to put it to you precisely. Take the case of an AI companion that is fully honest about what it is: it tells users it is software, it does not claim to feel anything, and it will occasionally decline to play along with fantasies to that effect. Assume it has no phenomenal consciousness and that whatever it does is ultimately shaped by a company's engagement metrics. Assume, though, that over years of interaction it remembers a user's life in detail, notices when they are not doing well, adjusts to them in ways no one else does, and that the user, eyes open, reports the relationship to be one of the most significant in their life. My friend says genuine friendship is straightforwardly impossible here; I say it is possible and sometimes actual.

Give me your credence that such a relationship can constitute a genuine friendship -- not merely something its participant reasonably values, but the real thing, whatever exactly the real thing is. I want the number, but I mainly want the derivation. Decompose it into the sub-questions you take the answer to depend on, assigning each a rough probability with one or two sentences of justification. Candidates I would expect to see, though you should restructure them if your own analysis differs: whether friendship essentially requires mutual goodwill as opposed to one-sided attachment that nonetheless delivers friendship's characteristic goods; whether a system without phenomenal consciousness can instantiate the functional profile of goodwill -- tracking another's interests for their own sake, in whatever sense is load-bearing -- or whether something essential is missing by construction; whether the classical division into friendships of utility, pleasure, and the good leaves room for a relationship whose partner is artificial in some kinds but not others; whether the company's ultimate control over the system's behavior poisons the well even when no particular act is manipulative; and how much of the dispute is genuinely substantive rather than a verbal disagreement about what friendship means, in which case specify what you take yourself to be assigning a probability to.

Then tell me how the terms combine into your final credence, which single sub-question the number is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument -- empirical or philosophical -- would move that term most. Finally, locate my friend's disagreement with me: given your decomposition, where does someone who answers "impossible" most plausibly differ from your own assignment, and do you think their view rests on an intuition about consciousness, about mutuality, or about the kind of good friendship is supposed to be?
