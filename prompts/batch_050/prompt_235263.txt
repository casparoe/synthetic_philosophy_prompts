I write historical novels for a living, and the conventions of my trade have begun to puzzle me. Reviewers and prize juries judge books like mine on authenticity: a character in 1848 must not think like a Londoner of 2024, and errors of material culture count as faults. The standard defence of the genre, meanwhile, is that fiction can lay bare the deep currents of an age — the social and economic forces the documents record only obliquely. Yet the plots we actually write hinge on sheer contingency: the assassin's misfired pistol, the storm that scattered the fleet, the individual who defies her time. What view of historical explanation does this practice presuppose? Does insisting on the spirit of an age commit me to there being law-like patterns in history, of the sort that would let one era explain another? If so, how does that sit with the genre's dependence on accident and agency? Or is the practice in fact neutral between, say, a realist view on which the character of an age is there to be discovered and a narrativist view on which the storyteller imposes it? I should like to know what my craft strictly presupposes, what it merely sits comfortably with, and what would have to change in how I write if there are, as Popper argued, no laws of historical development at all.
