I am a second-year undergraduate taking a module on bioethics and health economics, and we have just finished the unit on triage, QALYs, and the value of a statistical life. For a seminar exercise I would like you to write a piece of sustained satire, and I would like it to be good enough that my tutor cannot fault the underlying theory.

The piece: a deadpan patient-information leaflet, in the style of the folded pamphlets you pick up in an NHS waiting room, from a fictional hospital trust that has decided to run every decision — admissions, theatre lists, dialysis slots, the lot — on pure cost-per-QALY maximisation, with no other considerations permitted. About 600 to 700 words. Give it a title and the usual leaflet furniture (a "Welcome" section, perhaps "What to expect during your stay", "Frequently asked questions", "Our commitment to you"). The voice should be warm, reassuring, and procedurally cheerful throughout: the horror must come entirely from the content, never from the tone, and there must be no winking at the reader.

The humour should arise from exaggerating real features of the framework rather than invented ones, so that someone who has actually studied this material recognises the targets. Among the things the leaflet should quietly take for granted: that quality-of-life weights can be elicited from the general public and applied to other people's conditions; that future benefits can be discounted; that an unidentified future beneficiary and an identified patient in front of you count the same; that the claims of many small beneficiaries can aggregate past the claim of one badly-off person; and that whatever cost-per-QALY threshold the commissioners have set is simply a fact about the world, like the visiting hours.

At the end, outside the leaflet itself and clearly separated from it, add a brief note (200 words or so) doing two things. First, say where the piece takes liberties or leans on a contested reading — for instance, where it treats as settled what defenders of QALY-based allocation would say is a caricature, or vice versa. Second, separate what is broadly agreed on all sides (that these are genuine and unresolved difficulties for the framework) from what is genuinely contested among people who work in this area, and do not present a contested verdict as though it were the consensus.

Please write the whole response in British English.
