I'm a distributed systems engineer by trade — consensus protocols, failover, replication, control planes, the usual. I recently read Dennett's brain-transplant story, in which his brain is removed and kept alive in a vat, radio-linked to his ambulatory body, and he cycles through candidate answers to the question of where he is: where the body is, where the brain is, wherever the control signals run, and finally the suggestion that the question may have no determinate answer because his "point of view" was never the kind of thing that has a location. I have the strong sense that this entire dilemma is one my field solved in practice years ago, and I want to test that sense rigorously.

Restate each of Dennett's successive candidate answers, one by one, in the vocabulary of distributed systems: primary node and failover, active-passive versus active-active replication, control plane versus data plane, split-brain scenarios, migration of a service between hosts, and the question of what constitutes the identity of a running system across such migration. Where my vocabulary lacks a term you need, coin one explicitly rather than borrowing Dennett's. For each candidate answer, then give a residue report: which of its claims translate cleanly, which translate only by smuggling in an assumption (and which assumption), and which parts of the philosophical worry find no expression in systems vocabulary at all.

Then the harder part. My working hypothesis is that Dennett's final move — dissolving the question rather than answering it — corresponds to the standard engineering position that "where is the service?" is ill-posed once control is distributed, and that the systems translation therefore vindicates him. But I suspect something essential is being lost in translation: a system, unlike a person, arguably has no point of view that the question of location could be about in the first place. Tell me whether this residue is a defect of the engineering framework (it simply cannot represent the thing Dennett is asking about), or whether it exposes the question as one that only seems substantive because ordinary embodiment hides its distributed character. If you conclude the latter, say what feature of ordinary brains makes the illusion of a located self so much more compelling than the illusion of a located service.
