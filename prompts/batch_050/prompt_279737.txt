I’m a novelist revising a book where the main characters keep facing choices between their own deepest good and what seems best from an impartial point of view. I’ve been reading around Sidgwick’s dualism of practical reason, and I want to turn it into a reusable “scene audit” I can apply whenever a plot turn is supposed to be morally agonizing. I don’t want the usual crude conflict between selfish desire and selfless duty: Sidgwick’s problem is more interesting because both self-interest and impartial concern seem to make a rational claim, and there may be no higher principle that settles which should win. At the same time, I don’t want to turn my drafts into philosophy seminars, so the procedure has to be usable by someone thinking in terms of motives, stakes, consequences, and narrative symmetry.

Please write a step-by-step procedure I can run on a scene in maybe fifteen minutes: what to identify first, which distinctions absolutely have to survive the simplification, what questions to ask about uncertainty and foreseeable consequences, when a supposed dilemma is actually just appetite versus conscience, when one side rationally dominates, and when the dualism is genuinely left unresolved. Include a few diagnostic rewrites for making a weak dilemma stronger without making the characters implausibly angelic or monstrous. Also mark the points where the procedure cannot decide the scene for me and where an aesthetic judgment has to take over. Then apply it to three situations: a doctor who can save either her estranged daughter or two strangers; a civil servant who can expose a harmful policy at the cost of destroying her career and family stability; and an aging artist who can either preserve his reputation by suppressing a younger rival’s better work or step aside and be forgotten. In at least one application, let the procedure give a verdict I probably won’t want, even if that means saying my planned “tragic dilemma” is actually morally one-sided.

Please be candid about how confident you are in each part of the interpretation, especially where later readers disagree about what Sidgwick’s dualism really implies. At the end, single out the one point you are least sure of and explain what would change your mind.
