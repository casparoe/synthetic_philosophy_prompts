After a long argument with a friend about whether cooperation is ever rational without contracts, I tried to reconstruct the rule behind her verdicts rather than relitigate the conclusions. Use the standard prisoner’s dilemma payoffs: temptation 5, reward 3, punishment 1, sucker 0. By cooperation has a foothold she means that, without external enforcement, binding promises, or altruism added by stipulation, cooperation is either choice-worthy for the relevant agents or robustly favored by the process described; by defection wins she means the opposite. Her verdicts were:

- Anonymous one-shot strangers, no information beyond the matrix: defection wins.
- Indefinitely repeated play with the same partner, high continuation probability, perfect monitoring, and memory: cooperation has a foothold through conditional strategies.
- Repeated play with a commonly known final round, even if the horizon is long: defection wins by unraveling.
- Indefinitely repeated play with high continuation probability but implementation trembles, so intended cooperation sometimes arrives as defection: cooperation has a foothold only for forgiving or error-correcting conditional rules, not for brittle grim trigger; if the environment makes forgiveness too easy to exploit, defection wins.
- One-shot play between perfect copies or agents with common knowledge of identical source code: cooperation has a foothold.
- Clonal microbes playing a one-shot public-goods prisoner’s dilemma under high relatedness and limited dispersal: cooperation has a foothold; random mixing removes it.
- One-shot encounters embedded in a reliable reputation system where third parties shun defectors: cooperation has a foothold even if the current pair never meet again.

Now consider the new case we argued about. Two unrelated, non-identical agents face a one-shot prisoner’s dilemma. It is true, and common knowledge, that a predictor models both agents and produces this acausal correlation: if an agent cooperates, there is an 85 percent chance the other cooperates and a 15 percent chance the other defects; if an agent defects, there is an 85 percent chance the other defects and a 15 percent chance the other cooperates. Their choices are causally independent; there are no repeats, no kinship, no reputation effects, no enforcement, and no later selection.

Predict my friend’s verdict, but do not just guess. State the rule she seems to be running if there is one; give at least two other rules that fit all the earlier verdicts about as well; say what each candidate rule predicts for the new case and why; then give your best prediction with reasons, including how much weight should go to numerical expected payoff, robustness against exploitation, known-end effects, and the difference between rational choice and evolutionary stability. Finally, name one further variant that would most efficiently discriminate among the leading rules, and explain what answer to that variant would establish.
