So I spent most of my career as a physical chemist, retired about four years ago, and I've been spending my free time getting into philosophy of language (my wife thinks I've lost it, but whatever). I recently read about Frege's distinction between sense and reference -- the idea that the meaning of a term isn't just the thing it picks out, since "the morning star" and "the evening star" pick out the same object but mean something different. As someone who spent decades thinking about molecules, this immediately clicked for me.

But then I came across Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment, and now I'm confused about what Frege would even say about it. The setup, as I understand it: imagine a planet just like ours where everything is the same, except the stuff they call "water" isn't H2O but some other molecule XYZ that looks, tastes, and behaves identically. When I say "water" and my twin on Twin Earth says "water," do we mean the same thing? Putnam says no -- my twin's word refers to XYZ, not H2O, even though everything going on in our heads is identical. So meaning "ain't in the head," or however he puts it.

Here's my question: if Frege were alive in 1973 when Putnam published this, what do you think his reaction would have been? Would he have accepted that reference is fixed by your environment in a way that escapes anything in your psychology, or would he have dug in and said the sense of "water" is the same for me and my twin, and Putnam is just equivocating on what "meaning" means? I keep going back and forth on this. On one hand Frege seems to want sense to be something shareable and objective, not private mental imagery, so maybe externalism wouldn't scandalize him as much as people assume. On the other hand the whole point of sense seems to be explaining how someone can understand a term without knowing everything about its referent, and Twin Earth seems to make that impossible -- my twin and I understand our words exactly the same way but the references differ.

Please reason through it seriously, not just "it's hard to say." Give me your best reconstruction of what his actual position would have been, based on his commitments, and note where his views would have been put under the most pressure. Also tell me where you think Frege would have been right and where Putnam would have been right, because honestly I'm a chemist and the idea that the identity of a substance is fixed by its molecular structure, not by how it appears to anyone, seems completely obvious to me -- but I'm told philosophers find this controversial.
