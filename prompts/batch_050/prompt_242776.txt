ok, context first since it matters: I teach high school civics and after the referendum last year (the one where basicly every economist said the proposal would wreck the budget and it passed anyway on the back of some viral garbage) the district is rolling out a mandatory "information literacy" unit and I've been voluntold to help design it. Which has forced me to actually write down what I believe about democracy and knowledge, and I genuinely can't tell if it coheres. Roughly:

1. Every adult citizen gets exactly one vote, and this isn't a means to good outcomes, it's grounded in equal moral standing. Even if weighting votes by knowledge produced better policy, I'd still think it was wrong.
2. That said, plenty of policy questions really do have right answers (whether rent control shrinks housing supply, how viruses spread) and mass voter ignorance causes serious, objective harm. I'm not a relativist about any of this.
3. Competence tests or plural voting are completely off the table. Any institution that sorts voters by knowledge gets captured and just launders the ruling class's preferences into "expertise."
4. Mandatory civics and information-literacy education in public schools is good and necessary. It's the legitimate alternative to exclusion.
5. The state should never be in the business of adjudicating which political claims are true. That power is more dangerous than misinformation itself.
6. For genuinely technical questions I'd rather hand binding decisions to a randomly selected citizens' assembly briefed by experts than leave them to the general electorate.

So: does this hang together? My brother-in-law keeps insisting 1 and 6 flat-out contradict each other, which... maybe? I honestly don't see it. Tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like it until you think them through seperately, put the real ones in order of severity, and for the worst one tell me what the cheapest repair is and what it costs me.
