I sit on the citizens' review panel of our regional elder-care cooperative, a position I got by volunteering at a neighborhood meeting, not through any relevant expertise -- I spent thirty years running a hardware store and still think of "the cloud" as weather. On Tuesday we vote on whether to approve a six-month pilot in which twelve of our members, all of them older people living alone with mild memory problems, would receive a home-assistance robot that reminds them about medication, fetches objects, tidies up, and can call for help. This has become unexpectedly personal for me because my mother-in-law is on the waiting list, and my husband and I have been arguing about it at the dinner table ever since the briefing session, so I would like the panel's written opinion to be something I could defend across my own kitchen.

The robot performed flawlessly in the vendor's task demonstrations. What troubled several of us came out only in the question period, when one of the engineers explained how the machine keeps track of the home. After every action it updates a short list of facts it expects its action to have changed, and it treats everything not on that list as exactly as it was before, because, the engineer said, re-examining the whole house after each movement would leave the machine standing in the middle of the room forever doing nothing. We then asked for the incident log from simulation testing, and two entries have kept me up at night. In one, the robot carried a breakfast tray into the bedroom; the tray happened to have the day's pill organizer sitting on it, and since carrying the tray was only recorded as changing the tray's location, the machine went on believing the pills were in the kitchen and confidently told the resident so. In another, it slid a footstool aside to reach a high cupboard, and because "the walking route to the bathroom" was not on its list of things a cupboard visit affects, it later guided the resident along a path it still represented as clear. The vendor's position is that no caregiver, human or machine, can track everything, that a visiting nurse who pops in twice a week is working from far staler assumptions than the robot is, and that refusing the pilot leaves twelve isolated people with less oversight, not more. The panel member most opposed counters that a person at least knows she doesn't know, and can be asked, whereas the machine's confidence is total and silent.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote, short enough to be read aloud in under ten minutes. It should open by stating, in plain terms, a concrete recommendation -- approve, approve with stated conditions and a review date, or reject -- and then give the reasoning a reader with no technical background can follow, including an honest engagement with whichever side loses: if you recommend against the pilot, take seriously what is being denied to people who currently have no daily help; if you recommend for it, take seriously the objection about silent, total confidence and say what would have to be true for the conditions to be doing real work rather than decorating the approval. Write it as continuous prose, like a letter to the panel: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
