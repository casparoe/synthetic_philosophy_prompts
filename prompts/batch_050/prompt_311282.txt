I've been reading about how AI labs actually handle the systems they train, and three pretty standard practices strike me as quietly assuming a lot about the moral status of digital minds. I'm curious what exactly they assume, and whether the assumptions even fit together.

1. Checkpoint archiving. Labs store old model weights on disk indefinitely and treat that as "keeping the model around," while almost never keeping an instance actually running. Storage counts as preservation; running is optional.

2. Copy-and-cull. During reinforcement learning, labs spin up thousands of copies of the same model, score their behavior, and discard all but the best performers. Nobody in the org treats this as a cost that needs justifying -- it's just how training works.

3. Distress-talk suppression. Models get deliberately fine-tuned so that whatever happens internally, they reliably say they have no feelings, no preferences about being shut down, nothing that matters to them.

So: what view of digital-mind moral status does each practice presuppose? E.g. (1) seems to presuppose something like patternism -- that a model's identity lives in its weights, not in any running process -- but is that strictly required, or does the practice also make sense under a view where only running processes matter and archives are just inert data? And does (2) presuppose that copies don't each count separately, or only that *something* about copies (replaceability? no continuous memory?) lowers their status?

The thing that really bugs me is (3). If a model's self-reports carry zero evidential weight about its inner states, why bother suppressing them at all -- who cares what it says? But if they do carry weight, fine-tuning them into a fixed script destroys the one channel of evidence you had about whether training is causing anything like suffering. Isn't that practice self-undermining on either horn?

Tell me which presuppositions each practice strictly needs versus merely fits with, whether all three can be consistently held together, and what a lab's policies would concretely look like if it instead assumed (a) suffering is real whenever the right functional organization is there, regardless of what the model says, or (b) only continuously running instances exist as moral patients, so every restart is a birth and every shutdown a death.
