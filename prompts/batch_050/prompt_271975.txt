I co-host a university podcast on the philosophy of religion, and our next guest is a philosopher who defends a broadly Wittgensteinian account of religious belief, in the tradition of Wittgenstein's lectures on religious belief and later writers such as D.Z. Phillips. Her position, roughly, is that religious belief is not a hypothesis answerable to the evidential standards that govern historical or scientific claims: to ask whether a believer's trust in God, or expectation of a final judgment, is "well supported by the evidence" is, on her view, already to have misunderstood the grammar of the utterance, which belongs to a form of life rather than to an empirical theory. She is a practiced and charming interviewee, and her standard escape hatch is to tell the questioner that they are importing alien criteria of rationality into a language-game where those criteria do not apply. I want eight to ten questions, arranged in a deliberate sequence, that make this deflection progressively harder to deploy, rather than a grab-bag of independent questions.

The arc should run roughly as follows. Open by pinning down the descriptive core of the view, then force her to confront the most obvious objection: ordinary believers appear to treat at least some creedal claims — the resurrection, say — as straightforwardly factual, so ask whether her account entails that most religious people are confused about their own beliefs, and press her on the believer who loses his faith after reading historical scholarship about the New Testament (was that man misusing his own language-game?). Include at least one question on whether the view can recover Wittgenstein's own contrast between genuine religious belief and superstition without quietly smuggling back the very evidential standards she has rejected; at least one on disagreement between religions — whether, on her account, a Christian and a Hindu can genuinely contradict one another, and if not, whether the view has simply defined religious conflict out of existence; and at least one on the charge that the position is revisionary rather than descriptive, telling believers what they must really mean in a way they themselves would disown. For every question, supply a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, on the assumption that the reply will be some version of the imported-standards move; the follow-ups should concede the move where it is fair and then locate the pressure one level deeper. The final question should turn the method on the view itself: ask what evidence from actual religious practice could show her reading to be mistaken, and, if the answer is that none could, whether the position is still making an empirical claim about religion or has become a stipulation about how we ought to talk.
