my friend sam died last month, pretty much out of the blue, and his family asked if id read something at the memorial next friday. we met in a college seminar on classical chinese philosophy and basically became friends over the zhuangzi, the death material especially, so i want to write the poem myself instead of pulling some generic grief poem off the internet.

Youre a poet who also actually knows classical chinese philosophy -- i mean familiar with the zhuangzi itself, not a wikipedia summary. before you write anything, spend a bit brainstorming which six end-words to build the sestina around and why, and think carefully about what the form itself can do for this material. then write the poem.

The form: a sestina. six stanzas of six lines, three-line envoi, end-words cycling in the standard permutation. heres the core idea i want executed: zhuangzi's line on death is that nothing ends, it just turns into the next thing. when his wife dies and huizi shows up to find him drumming on a basin and singing, his defense is that the sequence runs from nothing, to breath, to form, to life, and now to death, like the seasons turning. when master lai is dying, his friend shoos the weeping family away from the bedside and wonders aloud what the creator will turn him into next -- a rat's liver, an insect's arm. the butterfly dream refuses to say which side of the transformation hes really on. a sestina is that, formally: six words passed through a fixed cycle until, after six stanzas, the pattern has come back around to its starting order. so the end-words should be six things you could become -- drawn from or at least in the spirit of the text's own examples (butterfly, arm, liver, drum, seasons...) -- and each rotation should feel like a transformation, not a repetition. by the envoi all six should sit side by side like theyve reached a truce.

Constraints, and these matter:

1. dont flatten it into "death is just change, so cheer up." in zhuangzi's own reply to huizi he admits he grieved like anyone else when she first died, and only then thought his way through to the seasons stuff. the grief comes first and the perspective does not cancel it. if the poem lands on serene acceptance it will be false to the text and useless to me. start the first stanza in the actual room, at the actual memorial, so the philosophy arrives as something remembered, not decoration.

2. the skull passage is off limits -- the one where he dreams of a skull, offers to have it restored to flesh and life, and the skull declines. i take it seriously as part of the view but there is no version of "the dead have it better" i can read to sam's parents. the poem may concede that we cant know what death is like from inside; it may not claim its preferable.

3. accuracy. our old proffesor will be in that room, and if the poem just name-drops zhuangzi and a butterfly over generic stanzas about letting go, she will know, and i will know she knows.

4. performable. lines short enough to say aloud without running out of breath, no end-word so awkward that i trip on it mid-stanza, and id like to make it at least to stanza three before i start crying, so dont front-load the heaviest material.

after the poem, give me three or four sentences on what you did with the end-words and anywhere you bent the form, so i know what im signing off on.
