So my friend group has this running fight between two people about coherent extrapolated volition and idealized-preference theories generally. One thinks extrapolation is the only serious way to get a principled alignment target out of actual human preferences, which are a mess. The other thinks idealization is basically a laundering operation -- you wash preferences until they're "coherent" and what comes out reflects the idealizer's choices, not the person. They've agreed to try an adversarial collaboration and I want you to draft the joint statement they should come out with.

Here's the setup to use. Person A defends the CEV-style move: take a person's actual dispositions, idealize them (better informed, more coherent, more time to reflect, the parts of themselves they'd want to be more like), and treat the output as what they really want -- with the claim that this is principled because the idealization procedure is fixed in advance and keyed to facts about the person. Person B's objection is that there's no fact of the matter about what the extrapolated version of someone wants, because each step of the idealization (how much information, in what order, which inconsistencies get resolved which way, which "parts of themselves" count as the authentic ones) is a choice point where the designer's values do the work, so "what you'd want if you knew more" is indefinitely many different things.

The document should have:

1. A numbered section of claims both can actually sign. I'd expect things like: raw revealed preferences are inconsistent, easily manipulated, and sensitive to framing, so no serious view can just read values off behavior; some transformation of actual preferences is unavoidable; extrapolation outcomes genuinely depend on details of the procedure, so "just idealize" is not a complete theory. Keep pushing until you have claims that are neither vacuous nor slanted toward one side.

2. A disagreements table where each side's position is phrased in words that side would accept. I suspect the real fault lines are: whether indeterminacy in the extrapolation output is a bug or just honest underdetermination we'd have anyway; whether there's a meaningful difference between "discovering" and "constructing" someone's idealized preferences; and whether convergence across people under extrapolation would be evidence for the view or an artifact of the procedure. But figure out the actual cruxes rather than just using mine.

3. For each crux, something both sides agree would bear on it, with predictions written down in advance. E.g., if you ran extrapolations from many slightly different starting conditions on real people's stated values and they converged, the CEV person predicts convergence, the skeptic predicts the convergence tracks the idealization choices not the person -- or whatever they actually predict, you write it. Thought experiments count as tests here (the genie who shows you your extrapolated self and you hate it; the person whose extrapolated volition diverges wildly depending on which self-doubt you amplify), but say what each side has to concede if the case goes the other way.

4. A final short section on which parts of their disagreement they should admit are verbal. My guess is "what you really want" is doing a lot of unearned work for both of them, but you decide.

Don't adjudicate who wins -- the point is a document both could sign. Casual tone is fine, it's for a group chat.
