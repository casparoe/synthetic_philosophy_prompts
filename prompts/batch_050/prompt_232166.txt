In six weeks I will be the invited speaker at a public meeting convened by our city council, which is deciding whether to rezone a low-rise, mostly owner-occupied district for mid-rise apartment buildings. I am a political philosopher who works on housing, and I have been asked to give a twenty-minute presentation defending a position I hold, followed by an open question period. My position has two parts. The philosophical thesis: long-term residents have a genuine moral claim on how their neighborhood changes, rooted in the plans, relationships, and financial security they have built there, but this claim is a claim to compensation and to a real say in the process, not to a veto; and the people who would live in the district if housing were built but currently cannot afford to — and who therefore are not in the room — have standing equal to that of current residents. The policy mechanism I attach to it: when a block is upzoned, the newly created development rights are issued to existing owners as tradable certificates rather than accruing to whoever assembles the lots; owners can sell them to developers, hold them, or band together to retire them and thereby buy the density back down at a published price; and a fixed fraction of the land-value uplift is captured to fund below-market units in the same district.

The audience: about sixty members of the district's neighborhood association, predominantly homeowners with a median tenure of twenty-five years, many of whom are counting on their home equity for retirement; two council members who need the association not to revolt; and one organizer from the citywide tenants' union who has already told a reporter that my scheme is a payout to the already-rich.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the ten or so objections this particular audience will actually raise, phrased in the vocabulary they will use — property values, parking, shadows, the character of the neighborhood, we weren't asked, who profits — rather than in mine, ordered by how likely each is to come up and how much damage it does if I handle it badly. For each: a two-minute reply in plain language, what I should concede up front, what I must not concede, and where my reply is weakest. Where the stated objection is a proxy for a different, unspoken concern, tell me so and tell me how to address the concern underneath. I especially need help with the philosophically serious objections rather than the merely loud ones: whether decades of residence and investment create something like a property right in a neighborhood's character; whether extending standing to people who do not yet live in the district is coherent or whether it proves too much, giving everyone a claim on every neighborhood; and whether my compensation scheme directs resources to incumbent owners rather than to those the housing shortage actually harms. Finally, identify the question I should be hoping nobody asks, tell me why it is the most dangerous one available to this audience, and give me the best answer I can honestly offer if someone asks it.
