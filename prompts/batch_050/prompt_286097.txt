I'm TAing an undergrad social/political philosophy course and need a second opinion on an exam answer before I finalize grades (the professor curves off my marks, so I want to be fair but not soft). Below is the question and one student's answer. Give me a letter grade and then the specific comments you'd write on the paper to justify it -- point by point, not just an overall vibe. I especially want to know if anything in the answer is wrong in a way that's easy to miss on a quick read.

EXAM QUESTION: Explain the discursive dilemma using an example of a three-person committee making a decision. Why does the dilemma arise even when every individual member is perfectly consistent? Describe the premise-based and conclusion-based procedures as possible responses, and give one reason to think each is inadequate. Finally: does the dilemma show that groups cannot be rational agents?

STUDENT'S ANSWER:

Take a tenure committee with three members, Alice, Bob, and Carol. They have to decide whether a candidate gets tenure, and they've agreed that tenure should be granted iff the candidate's research is strong. So there are three propositions in play: (p) the research is strong; (p→q) if the research is strong, the candidate should get tenure; and (q) the candidate should get tenure. Suppose they vote as follows. Alice thinks the research is strong and votes yes on all three. Bob thinks the research is strong but rejects the conditional -- he thinks research isn't the only relevant criterion and in this case teaching is too weak -- so he votes yes on p, no on p→q, and no on q. Carol accepts the conditional but thinks the research is weak, so she votes no on p, yes on p→q, and no on q. Every member is perfectly consistent: each one's vote on q follows from their votes on p and p→q. But if we aggregate each proposition separately by majority vote, the committee accepts p (Alice and Bob), accepts p→q (Alice and Carol), and rejects q (Bob and Carol). The group's collective judgment set is inconsistent: it has committed to p, to p→q, and to ¬q. So individual rationality plus majority rule gives you collective irrationality.

This is essentially the Condorcet voting paradox applied to propositions instead of candidates. One fix is the premise-based procedure: vote only on the premises p and p→q, and let logic determine the conclusion. Here the committee accepts both premises, so it must grant tenure, and the outcome is consistent. But this is inadequate because the committee ends up granting tenure even though a majority of its members voted against it, and whoever frames the agenda -- decides what counts as a "premise" -- gets enormous power over the result. The other fix is the conclusion-based procedure: vote only on the conclusion q. That gives a consistent verdict (no tenure), but it's inadequate because the group never settles its reasons, so it can't justify the decision to the candidate or treat the next case consistently -- a different majority could accept the premises in the next case and the committee would have no stable view about what follows.

Since the problem comes from majority aggregation itself, the dilemma only arises under simple majority rule -- unanimity or supermajority requirements would prevent the inconsistency, because they'd make it harder to assemble the conflicting majorities.

Does it show groups can't be rational agents? Yes. If the most natural way of combining consistent individual judgments produces an inconsistent group judgment, then talk of "what the committee believes" is just a fiction; there's no agent there, only individuals.

That's the whole thing. Grade it like you were me.
