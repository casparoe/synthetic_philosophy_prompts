When I try to extend ordinary "more good is better" reasoning to outcomes involving infinitely many people, I keep hitting the same wall, and I want to know whether it has a name. Two instances.

First: one world contains infinitely many people, half at a low level of well-being and half at a high level, alternating down the generations; another contains infinitely many people, all at the level exactly midway between those two. The two worlds seem, if anything, equally good on average. But when I compare them by pairing people off and asking which world wins pair by pair, I find I can arrange the pairing so the middling world wins every single pair, and equally arrange it so the middling world loses every single pair, while the "natural" pairing — first person with first person, second with second — gives no verdict at all. Nothing about the worlds changes; only my bookkeeping does.

Second: two policies each shape an endless future. Each produces infinitely many years in which it does better than the other and infinitely many in which it does worse; the better years, taken alone, add up beyond any bound, and so do the worse years. I find that depending on the order in which I count the years off against one another, the running comparison can be made to favor either policy by any margin I like.

In both cases the verdict depends entirely on a choice that seems morally irrelevant: how I line things up, or in what order I add. This is not the problem of both options being infinitely good and therefore tied (infinitarian paralysis), and it is not a single gamble whose expected value refuses to settle (the St. Petersburg paradox); here each comparison is between perfectly determinate outcomes, and the trouble is that the comparison itself has no stable answer. Is there an established name and literature for exactly this phenomenon? If it has been handled only under headings that don't quite fit, explain how the thing I describe differs from each and which comes closest; if nothing fits, coin a term, define it, and mark its boundary cases — for instance, does it still arise when only one side is infinite, or when a privileged matching exists (same time, same place, same person)? A bare label won't satisfy me; I want the carving. Finally, state how confident you are in your identification, and single out the specific claim in your answer that you are least sure of.
