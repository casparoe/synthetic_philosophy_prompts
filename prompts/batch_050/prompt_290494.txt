I belong to a small mineral-collecting club -- none of us has any academic training, but we argue a lot over dinner. At our last meeting one member, who enjoys old philosophical puzzles, claimed he could prove that what our specimens show depends on which words we happen to have invented. He defined a word: call a sapphire "blarm" if it is blue and first examined before January 1, 2030, or if it is green and not first examined before then. Every sapphire anyone in the world has examined so far is, trivially, blarm. Then he walked us through this:

(1) Every sapphire examined to date has been blue, and therefore also blarm. The examination reports support "All sapphires are blue" and "All sapphires are blarm" to exactly the same degree, since they are the very same reports.
(2) Confirming a generalization is a matter of finding instances: a claim of the form "All F are G" is supported by each examined F that turns out to be G, whatever predicates F and G may be.
(3) So the two hypotheses are supported equally by everything we have seen.
(4) Neither vocabulary is more basic than the other. Blarm is definable from blue, green, and the date; but blue is equally definable from blarm and a matching word "greem" (green and first examined before the date, or blue and not examined before then) using that same date. Two vocabularies that can translate each other completely stand or fall together -- neither is the "real" color language.
(5) So there is no neutral, language-independent fact about which hypothesis the stones support. Whether your evidence favors "all sapphires are blue" or "all sapphires are blarm" is fixed by which descriptive vocabulary you adopt, not by anything in the stones.
(6) Every empirical science rests on confirmations of this same shape, so scientific support generally is relative to a choice of vocabulary, which is a matter of convention.
(7) Therefore the idea that evidence tells us about the world independently of how we choose to describe it is an illusion.

I refuse to accept (7), yet each step sounded harmless while he was saying it. Two things nag at me. First, I suspect something slips between (4) and (5): from "each language can fully translate the other" he moves to "the two languages are equally good, and neither carves the world more accurately," and I cannot tell whether those are the same claim or two different ones. Second, when another member suggested the obvious repair -- simply ban hypotheses whose predicates mention particular times, places, or objects -- our club secretary objected that "every sapphire in our display cabinet is blue" mentions a particular object too, and yet examining cabinet sapphires one by one plainly does seem to support it, just as a geologist's claims about the rocks of one particular formation are supported by samples from that formation. If the repair cannot tell the cabinet case apart from the blarm case, it throws out honest local generalizations along with the trick word, and it is no repair at all. I will note, since he brushed past it, that whether a stone is blarm at all seems to depend partly on when we happen to look at it, which strikes me as an odd feature for a property of stones to have -- but he insisted the definitions are clear and that is all that matters.

So: which step should I reject, why did it look acceptable, and what does rejecting it cost? If I am forced to choose, I would rather give up the instance principle in (2) than concede that evidence speaks only to our vocabulary and not to the world. But I would like the price of each candidate repair laid out plainly -- banning all reference to particular times and objects; holding that some predicates genuinely track natural groupings better than others, whatever our language says; or holding that what matters is which predicates have actually been used in successful past inferences -- and I want to know whether any of them can convict blarm while acquitting the display cabinet.
