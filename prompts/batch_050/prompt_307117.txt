My roommate got really into Dennett's four-creature picture from Kinds of Minds last semester (Darwinian creatures that only evolve by selection, Skinnerian ones that learn by trial and error in their own lifetime, Popperian ones that test hypotheses in some inner simulator so their ideas can die instead of them, and Gregorian ones that import mind-tools like language from their culture) and now she classifies literally everything, which is fun but I suspect she's winging it. Her verdicts so far: gut bacteria, Darwinian, obviously. Her dog, who learned to roll over for treats and keeps offering it whenever he wants anything, Skinnerian. A chimp in a documentary that sat and stared at a banana and some boxes for a long time before stacking them, Popperian, because the trial-and-error happened in its head. Her nephew, who talks himself through puzzles out loud, Gregorian. She also said an octopus that figured out a screw-top jar after a bunch of fumbling was Skinnerian, and that a slime mold solving a maze is Darwinian "even though no neurons," which I found interesting.

Here's the thing: I think at least three different rules fit all of those verdicts. There's the official Dennett one, but there's also something like how much of the learning happens within one lifetime versus across generations, and something like whether the creature gets its strategies from other creatures (the dog and nephew both learn from us, the slime mold and bacteria don't), and maybe a plain language rule too. So I want to test her. Next time she brings it up I'm going to ask about two cases: (1) orcas, whose pods have distinct hunting techniques -- like the wave-washing trick for knocking seals off ice floes -- that youngsters apparently pick up from the pod over years, but who as far as anyone knows don't have anything like language; and (2) a current chatbot, which was shaped by a training process involving astronomical amounts of trial and error, but which can now reason step by step in words and even talk itself through a problem like her nephew does.

Predict what she'll say about each case and tell me which rule she seems to actually be running. If her existing verdicts genuinely underdetermine it, walk me through what the main candidate rules predict for the orca and the chatbot, give your best guess with reasons, and name the further case (real or hypothetical) that would settle which rule she's on. I'm less interested in the "right" taxonomy than in whether her instinct is tracking Dennett's actual distinction or something adjacent to it.
