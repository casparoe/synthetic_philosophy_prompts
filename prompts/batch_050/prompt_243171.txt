I'm in a phil seminar on transformative experience (we read L.A. Paul, and also William James's conversion lectures for background) and I keep arguing with my roommate about whether big life decisions can be rational at all. In Lecture X of *The Varieties of Religious Experience*, James introduces his conversion cases by saying: "One must know concrete instances first; for, as Professor Agassiz used to say, one can see no farther into a generalization than just so far as one's previous acquaintance with particulars enables one to take it in." And a bit earlier he describes instantaneous conversions as ones where "a complete division is established in the twinkling of an eye between the old life and the new." My roommate's take is that if the Agassiz point is right, you basically can't rationally choose any life-changing thing, since you can't grasp the "particulars" of the life on the other side before you're in it. But when I actually consult my gut on cases, I don't get a clean pattern like that, and I want to know what principle I'm actually running on.

So here are my verdicts. Having a kid: you can't know what it's like beforehand, but I still think it can be a rational decision, people do it reasonably all the time. Moving to a country where I don't speak the language for a job: clearly rationally decidable, even though it will change who I become. A pill that permanently makes you content with whatever life you happen to have, so you'd stop wanting the things you currently care about: I have a strong intuition that you can't rationally take it, even if you'd be happier. Converting to a demanding religion, James-style, where the whole structure of your values flips: I don't think you can decide this by weighing reasons from your current standpoint, it sort of has to happen to you (which I guess is why James emphasizes the surrender aspect). Getting a cochlear implant as a deaf adult whose social world is built around Deaf culture: I actually think this one *is* decidable by reasons, even though I've read that some people in that community experience it as identity-threatening. And the one I flip-flop on: a treatment that would cure someone's severe depression but, as a side effect, noticeably flatten their emotional range, so the art and relationships they currently love would matter less to them afterwards.

Is there one principle that captures all of these, or do I have to give one up? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- either state the principle you think best fits my verdicts or say flat out that no single principle does -- and then give your reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question back to me. In the reasoning, tell me which case motivates each part of the principle, be honest about which of my verdicts it can't save (I suspect the cochlear implant and the conversion verdicts are in tension, since both involve losing access to a whole way of valuing things), and say whether it matters if the transformation is gradual versus James's "twinkling of an eye" kind. Also tell me what the principle predicts for a case I haven't listed: a 70-year-old deciding whether to undergo an experimental memory treatment that would erase most of her episodic memories of her late husband but leave her skills, friendships, and personality intact.
