I got thinking about this after an argument at a dinner party the other week, where someone dredged up the Brexit referendum again. The franchise for that vote was genuinely odd: my old flatmate, a Portuguese nurse who'd lived and paid taxes in London for a decade, couldn't vote. An Irish friend who'd arrived six months earlier could, because of the old reciprocal arrangements with Ireland. So could Commonwealth citizens resident in the UK -- a Canadian grad student in my cohort had a ballot while she didn't. British citizens who'd emigrated to Spain more than fifteen years earlier were shut out, while those gone only ten years were in. Sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds had just voted in the Scottish independence referendum two years before, but not this time. Everyone at the table agreed the question of who gets to decide a country's future was exactly the thing the referendum couldn't itself decide, and then we got stuck.

So here's what I want: analyze the Brexit franchise through the lens of the two main answers to the democratic boundary problem -- the all-affected-interests principle and the all-coerced (or all-subjected) principle. For each one, map it carefully onto the actual case: which of the groups above (long-settled EU nationals, Irish residents, Commonwealth residents, teenage citizens, expats, and for that matter the other EU member states whose economies were plainly affected) does each principle enfranchise, and on what reasoning? Then tell me where each principle breaks or gives an answer that feels wrong -- e.g., does all-affected really mean French farmers get a say in British fisheries policy forever, and does all-coerced really exclude the expat in Málaga who still holds only a British passport?

Two harder follow-ups. First: it's widely thought that enfranchising resident EU citizens would have flipped the result. Does the fact that the franchise question is outcome-determinative show that the boundary problem can't be solved neutrally, or is judging a franchise by what it would have produced just a category error -- legitimacy doesn't run backwards from results? Second, I want you to stress-test a principle I came up with after too much wine: "exposure-weighted membership" -- your claim to a vote scales with your expected years of future subjection to the outcome, so the 70-year-old pensioner counts for less than the 19-year-old, and the emigrant counts for almost nothing unless she's likely to return. Does this actually fix anything the other two get wrong, or does it just smuggle in a technocratic gerrymander dressed up as democratic theory? Be honest about where the standard theories are simply silent rather than forcing them to pronounce.
