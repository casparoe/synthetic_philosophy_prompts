Now that I have retired from experimental chemistry, I have been reading philosophy of history and keep encountering the claim that Hitler was merely the match that ignited the dry tinder of postwar Germany: defeat, the Treaty of Versailles, economic instability, antisemitism, anticommunism, and weak democratic institutions supplied the fuel. Some writers use this picture to argue that Nazi Germany, or something very close to it, was structurally likely even without Hitler; others reply that matches are not interchangeable and that Hitler's particular ambitions, political gifts, and decisions were the crucial ignition source. Please audit this combustion analogy rather than simply repeating the familiar “person versus circumstances” debate. Set out what in the historical case is supposed to correspond to fuel, oxygen, heat, activation energy, spark, and chain reaction; identify the respects in which intentional agents, institutions, and counterfactual possibilities differ from chemical systems; and distinguish which disanalogies matter for the analogy's intended conclusion from those that are merely incidental. In particular, assess separately the claims that without Hitler there would still have been an authoritarian nationalist Germany, a Nazi movement, a Nazi government, a major European war, and a Holocaust. Once the analogy's limits are explicit, does it support either side, or should it be replaced by a better model of historical causation?
