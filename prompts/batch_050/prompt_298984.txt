After a long argument with a friend, I would like help clarifying a conceptual point about mechanistic explanation in biology, particularly the “new mechanist” picture associated with Machamer, Darden, and Craver.

Imagine that researchers are studying how liver cells regulate glycogen production over the course of the day. They discover that small pulses of a hormone reliably precede changes in the cells’ production of glycogen. Blocking the hormone’s receptor inside the liver cells eliminates those daily changes, while experimentally inducing the pulses changes them in predictable ways. My friend concludes that the hormone pulses must therefore be components of the glycogen-regulating mechanism. I object that they might instead be an upstream cause, an enabling condition, or a temporal input that the mechanism uses without being part of it.

Please explain how a mechanist should distinguish among these possibilities. In particular, address the following points:

- Why does successful experimental intervention provide evidence that something is mechanistically relevant without settling whether it is constitutive of the mechanism?
- How should we understand the difference between one mechanism causing another and one entity or activity being a component within a mechanism?
- Does the fact that the hormone and the liver cell exist at different spatial or organizational levels settle whether the hormone belongs to the mechanism?
- Could the answer change if the hormone were produced inside the same cell rather than delivered from elsewhere, or if the receptor and hormone formed a recurrent feedback loop with the rest of the system?
- What principled boundaries, if any, can prevent a mechanistic explanation from expanding indefinitely to include every causal influence on the phenomenon?

I am less interested in a verdict about the imaginary case than in a clear account of the conceptual distinction at stake and of where the mechanist view is or is not committed to sharp boundaries between a mechanism and its environment.
