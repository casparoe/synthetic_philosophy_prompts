Two people in my political theory reading group, call them Ana and Ben, have been going in circles about the boundary problem for a whole semester and I've finally convinced them to do an adversarial collab instead of another shouting match. Ana's an all-affected-interests person: she thinks everyone whose interests are significantly affected by a decision should get a say in it, so in principle the demos for a given decision might spill way beyond any state's borders. Ben's an all-subjected person: he thinks what matters is being coercively subjected to a law or an authority, so the demos is roughly whoever the state can actually enforce against, and mere affectedness without subjection buys you nothing. Your job is to write the joint statement they should come out with, the document both of them could sign.

Here's the concrete situation I want the statement to engage with, because it's the case they always fight about: the country of Valdheim wants to build a giant hydroelectric dam. It will flood farmland across the border in its neighbour Kastrel, raise electricity prices in a third country that imports Valdheim's power, and the reservoir will take forty years to fill, so the biggest losers are people who haven't been born yet. Meanwhile about ten thousand Kastrel citizens commute into Valdheim daily and are subject to Valdheim's labour laws while they're there, but can't vote in Valdheim and aren't affected by the dam at all. Ana and Ben disagree about basically everyone in this story.

The statement needs four things. First, the claims they can both actually sign, which I suspect includes things like: some version of the inclusion question can't be settled by democratic vote itself on pain of circularity; both principles give counterintuitive answers in at least some cases; and the dam case shows that affectedness and subjection genuinely come apart. Second, the exact points of disagreement, each phrased carefully enough that the person whose view it is would accept the wording as a fair statement of their own position, not a strawman. Third, the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions where resolving it would force at least one of them to move. My guess is one crux is whether the principle of inclusion is supposed to track who owes justification or who is owed protection from interference, and another is whether future people and the unborn are a problem for Ben's view specifically or a problem for everyone. Fourth, for each crux, a case or argument they'd both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction about it written down in advance, so neither can move the goalposts later.

One thing to be careful about: part of their fight might be verbal. Ana sometimes talks as if the boundary principle is about who has a claim to influence, Ben as if it's about what makes coercion legitimate, and I'm not sure those are the same question. If it turns out they're answering different questions under one label, the statement should say so and split it, rather than papering it over.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, like a long letter, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I know that makes the cruxes-and-predictions part harder to scan, but I want to see if the disagreements can be made exact in running text rather than hidden inside formatting.
