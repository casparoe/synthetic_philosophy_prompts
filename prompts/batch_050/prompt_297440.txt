Background, so you know where I'm coming from: I'm a retired physical chemist, spent forty years doing spectroscopy, and since retiring I've been working my way through the philosophy of mathematics. I've gotten stuck on Skolem's paradox and, more precisely, on the fact that the discussion about it seems to go around in circles, and I'd like your help diagnosing why.

Here's the setup as I understand it (correct me if I've got it wrong). First-order ZFC, if it has a model at all, has a countable one, by the downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem. But ZFC itself proves there are uncountable sets. The standard resolution I keep reading is that "uncountable" is relative to the model: a set can be uncountable from the inside, because the model contains no bijection with omega, while being countable from the outside, because we outsiders can see such a bijection. Fine. I can follow the formalism, it's just model theory.

But here's where the argument in the literature, and in my reading group, stalls. One side says: that's the whole story, the paradox is dissolved, "uncountable" was never an absolute notion to begin with, and anyone still bothered by it is confusing what's true in a structure with what's true simpliciter. The other side says: hold on, that resolution already leans on an "outside" perspective where the words "countable," "set," and "bijection" have a fixed intended meaning -- otherwise the claim that the model is countable from the outside is itself just something true in yet another model, and you've pushed the problem up one level rather than solved it. So the second camp thinks the paradox isn't a contradiction but a symptom of something real: that formalization can't pin down its own subject matter. The first camp answers that demanding a perspective "outside all models" is incoherent, or at least not a demand mathematics has to meet. Round and round it goes. I've also seen a third move, that full second-order semantics restores categoricity and thus absoluteness, and the counter-move that this just relocates the determinacy question into the metalanguage.

You are a professional philosopher of mathematics and logic, and I want you to take this seriously and think carefully, not give me a textbook survey. Please start by brainstorming three or four competing diagnoses of what kind of disagreement this actually is, and then argue for the one you find most convincing. Some hypotheses I've been toying with, offered as hypotheses only: maybe it's a purely verbal dispute about who gets to use the word "absolute"; maybe there's a hidden substantive disagreement about whether informal mathematical language has a determinate interpretation prior to any formalization; maybe the two camps are answering different questions (one about what's provable, one about what fixes reference) under one name; or maybe the whole thing turns on an empirical-ish question about how mathematicians actually acquire and use the concept of countability, which nobody in the debate seems to investigate.

Given your diagnosis, tell me concretely what would count as progress: a distinction that needs drawing, a theorem that would settle part of it, a concession one side should just make, or an argument that the impasse is permanant because the question is ill-posed. If you think both camps are missing something -- for instance, that a working scientist's instinct that our concepts are anchored by successful application has any bearing here -- say that too. Be honest about where the genuine philosophical weight lies rather than declaring a winner by fiat.
