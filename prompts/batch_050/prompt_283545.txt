I've been thinking about a puzzle I stumbled into on my own. If value just means "what there is reason to care about," then suppose a powerful and vindictive demon credibly threatens to ruin my life unless I admire him: now I plainly have a strong reason to admire him, but he is no more admirable for it. This is what I gather people call the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, and I want to evaluate proposed solutions without first learning the standard list of complaints.

Write the specification an adequate solution must meet: a list of desiderata with the case or consideration behind each. Please include, at minimum, which actual cases the theory must classify correctly (and which verdict counts as correct), which distinctions it cannot collapse (I suspect there is a real difference between reasons directed at the object and reasons directed at my own state of mind, but I want this on the list as something to be justified or dissolved, not assumed), whether the solution must generalize to reasons for belief rather than being allowed to work only for admiration and the like, and what would count as explaining why a reason is of the wrong kind as opposed to merely labeling it so. For each desideratum, say whether it is a genuine constraint any solution must respect or a prejudice of one school of thought, and flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied.

Keep the emphasis on the criteria rather than on scoring named theories, but close by telling me which single desideratum does the most work in ruling out the obvious candidates. End with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing a short name for that most decisive desideratum.
