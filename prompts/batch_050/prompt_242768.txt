so my cousin said "what goes around comes around" after our landlord, who had stiffed her on the deposit, got audited by the tax office about a year later. Fine. But then she used it again in an argument where she told me not to bother warning the new tenants about him, because his behavior would catch up with him anyway. That second use felt off to me -- like the saying was doing way more work there than the first time -- but I couldn't pin down exactly what she was even committed to.

Can you separate out the different precise claims this saying could be making? I'm guessing the menu includes something like: a social-feedback reading (people who mistreat others burn their reputations and networks, so on average they do worse); a psychological reading (acting badly warps your own character in ways that cost you); some kind of impersonal moral bookkeeping built into the universes structure; a version that only works if you assume rebirth or a cosmic judge; and maybe the weakest possible one, where it just means actions have consequences at all. For each reading: is it a platitude, an empirical bet, or a metaphysical claim? Which ones are actually defensible and which are just comforting -- and do the empirical ones even survive the obvious selection effect, i.e. that we only ever notice and retell the cases where it "came true" and forget the landlords who retired rich?

And the part I actually care about: which of these readings, if any, would support her conclusion that warning the new tenants was pointless? Because it seems to me the most plausible reading (the social-feedback one) gives you a reason TO warn people, since reputations only punish people if information gets around, and the saying only gets her the quietism by quietly switching to the least plausible reading. Tell me if I'm right about that. Lay the readings out as a list with verdicts, not one long essay picking a favorite.
