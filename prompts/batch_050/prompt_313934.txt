In my former life as a physicist, I learned to treat forces as vectors: each contributes its own magnitude and direction to the resultant, and each keeps that character no matter what other forces are acting. I keep meeting the same picture in the ethics literature -- a moral reason is described as like a force, carrying a fixed weight and polarity into every situation, with moral principles playing the role of the laws that tell us which features exert which pull. Particularists, as I understand them, reject this outright and reach instead for culinary or aesthetic analogies: a reason is like an ingredient whose contribution depends on the whole dish, so that a feature which speaks in favor of an action in one context may speak against it, or say nothing at all, in another. I would like both analogies audited. Please lay out the correspondences each proposes -- what plays the role of the vector, the resultant, the superposition principle, on the one side, and of the ingredient, the recipe, the finished dish, on the other -- then identify where each mapping breaks down and what each quietly presupposes about the context-dependence of reasons. Most importantly: once the disanalogies are on the table, does either side's substantive conclusion -- that ethics requires general principles, or that it can do without them -- actually follow, or was the analogy doing the arguing?
