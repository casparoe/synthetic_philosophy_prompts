Analyze the March 2023 collapse of Silicon Valley Bank as a case study in information cascades and rational herding. For purposes of the analysis, take the following facts as stipulated: the bank had an unusually concentrated base of technology startups and venture-capital-backed firms; rising interest rates had reduced the value of its long-term bond holdings; on March 8 it announced both a loss from selling some of those bonds and a plan to raise new capital; warnings and withdrawal recommendations then spread rapidly through venture-capital networks, founder group chats, and social media; customers could move funds electronically; and regulators closed the bank on March 10.

Begin by mapping the episode carefully onto an information-cascade model. Identify each depositor’s private signal, publicly observable action, uncertainty, and payoff. Explain what subsequent depositors could rationally infer from seeing or hearing that others were withdrawing, and at what point further private information might cease to affect behavior. Do not simply call every withdrawal a cascade: distinguish at least four mechanisms that may have been operating simultaneously:

1. an informational cascade, in which withdrawals are interpreted as evidence about the bank’s underlying condition;
2. a strategic bank-run equilibrium, in which each depositor withdraws because others are expected to withdraw, regardless of what they believe about long-run asset value;
3. reputational or fiduciary herding among venture capitalists, in which advisers fear being the one who failed to warn portfolio companies; and
4. a communication cascade, in which warnings become simpler, more urgent, or more certain as they are relayed through a network.

For each mechanism, state what evidence from the stipulated facts would count for or against treating it as primary, and explain how the mechanisms could reinforce one another.

Then use three analytical concepts defined here for this exercise. The cascade substrate is the network through which actions and warnings become visible. The compression ratio is the amount of imitation and systemic damage generated per unit of time after the initial signal. Epistemic debt is the extent to which later actors’ confidence rests not on independently examined evidence but on the presumed information of earlier actors. Apply these concepts to the case: how did the bank’s concentrated clientele affect the substrate, how did electronic banking and synchronized professional networks affect the compression ratio, and how might epistemic debt have grown even though no individual warning was obviously irrational?

Next, consider the actors’ options. What should a depositor, a venture capitalist advising many startups, the bank’s management, and a regulator each have done under the four mechanisms above? Address whether fuller disclosure by the bank would have reduced mistaken inference or merely accelerated a run; whether emergency liquidity support addresses insolvency concerns, coordination failure, or both; and whether withdrawal restrictions could ever be epistemically justified rather than merely prudentially necessary.

Finally, let the case test the theory. Which features of the collapse does standard cascade reasoning explain well, and which does it misdescribe or leave unexplained—for example, the role of strategic complementarities, clustered social ties, real-time communication, and actions whose very occurrence worsens the underlying fact being inferred? Does the episode show that individually rational herding can destroy an institution that would otherwise have survived, that it can efficiently expose an already fragile institution, or that the case cannot settle that question? End with a disciplined verdict identifying where cascade theory illuminates the collapse, where bank-run theory is indispensable, and where neither framework is sufficient on its own.
