In the free will debate, skeptics like Caruso (and Pereboom before him) defend what they call the quarantine model: even if no one deserves punishment in the basic-desert sense, we can still incapacitate dangerous people the way we quarantine carriers of a contagious disease. We don't blame the person with measles, we don't think they merit confinement, and we keep the confinement as mild as possible, but we still restrict their liberty to protect everyone else. So, the argument goes, criminal justice can be just as justified without any appeal to desert. Dennett-type critics push back that this is too quick.

How far does the analogy actually hold? Give me the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the pathogen, the carrier, the exposure risk, the public health authority -- and then audit it. Specifically I want you to get at: (1) quarantine is justified because the carrier isn't an agent whose behavior is sensitive to incentives, but criminals (even on a determinist picture) are -- does that difference break the analogy or just complicate it? (2) quarantine doesn't send any normative signal to third parties, whereas punishment arguably partly works by expressing and maintaining norms -- is that a feature the analogy quietly drops, and does the skeptic have anything to replace it with? (3) in medicine we'd find quarantining asymptomatic carriers of something harmless monstrous, so what in the analogy tracks the gravity of the crime -- or does the model collapse the distinction between dangerousness and wrongdoing entirely? Finally: does the skeptic's conclusion (no desert, but incapacitation is fine) survive the disanalogies, or was the analogy carrying more weight than it can bear? And if you think there's a better analogy for what the skeptic wants -- insurance, nuisance abatement, something else -- say what it is.
