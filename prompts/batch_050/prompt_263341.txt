After a semester seminar on democratic theory and a lot of reading on my own, I've become a cautious convert to sortition, and I've written up the position I've arrived at for my reading group to attack. Before I hand it over, I'd like an outside audit of whether my commitments actually cohere. Here they are, roughly in the order I'd defend them:

(1) One chamber of the national legislature should be replaced by an allotted assembly: about 400 citizens chosen by stratified random sampling, serving staggered one-year terms, holding full binding legislative power rather than an advisory role. (2) The fundamental justification for democracy is political equality: every competent adult has an equal claim to participate in ruling, and elections are at bottom an aristocratic institution that launders privilege into legitimacy. (3) I trust deliberation: ordinary citizens, given time, balanced materials, and skilled facilitation, reach better judgments than either mass electorates or committees of experts, because on most political questions cognitive diversity matters more than individual expertise. (4) All that said, the lottery should exclude people unable to participate meaningfully, and I'd tentatively favor a minimal civic-literacy screen as well, along the lines of what we'd expect of jurors or naturalization candidates. (5) I believe accountability is non-negotiable: anyone who wields coercive political power must be answerable to the public and, in the last resort, removable by it. (6) I still think referendums are the right instrument for constitutional-level changes. (7) Advisory minipublics are a fig leaf — if the assembly's decisions can simply be ignored by elected politicians, the whole exercise is decorative.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture on sortition. Go through the set and tell me which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, ordered by severity, and for each one say exactly what would have to give and what the cheapest repair costs me. Please also distinguish real tensions from merely apparent ones — I suspect at least one objection my reading group will raise dissolves on inspection, and I'd like to be able to say why. Finally, if you think one single revision would resolve the most trouble, name it, but don't assume you know which commitment I'm least attached to; I'm genuinely unsure myself.
