A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether DNA really is a code, and the structure of the argument bothers me enough that I want a diagnosis rather than another round. My position, roughly the one associated with Maynard Smith, is that the genome is a code in the strictest sense going: the mapping from codons to amino acids is arbitrary in something like the way the mapping from words to meanings is arbitrary — a different set of tRNAs could in principle link the same codons to different amino acids — and evolution has produced a system whose causal organization turns on sequence rather than on the chemistry of the individual bases. Hers is that code and information talk in molecular biology is a borrowed idiom that does no real work: everything a molecular biologist can state in informational vocabulary can be redescribed without residue in terms of causal specificity, templating, and catalysis, and the semantic gloss is decoration added for textbooks.

What struck me afterward is that neither of us disputed a single fact. We both know about wobble pairing, about the variant codes in mitochondria and certain ciliates, about the redundancy of the standard code, about what aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases do. We agreed that Crick's adaptor hypothesis turned out to be roughly correct and that the code is not chemically frozen in every detail. The disagreement survived all of it. And the literature on this question — teleosemantic accounts of genetic information, purely causal-information accounts, skeptics who hold the whole idiom to be metaphor — has been recycling the same moves for roughly three decades without, as far as I can tell, converting anybody.

So my question concerns the shape of the impasse rather than its verdict. Is this a verbal disagreement about what the words code and information must mean before they may be applied — with me importing a semantic notion and my friend a merely causal one — or is there a substantive question underneath, and if so, what is it? Three candidate diagnoses occurred to me, and I would like them assessed or replaced by a better one. First, that the two sides are answering different questions under one label: whether informational vocabulary is dispensable in biological explanation, versus whether DNA instantiates a genuinely semantic relation — and these need not stand or fall together. Second, that a hidden empirical disagreement is doing the work, about how arbitrary the code actually is, given evidence for stereochemical affinities between codons and amino acids — a question neither side states cleanly because neither wants the dispute to turn out empirical. Third, that the real disagreement is about what kind of fact would ever vindicate semantic vocabulary anywhere, so that biology is merely the battlefield and the war is about the naturalization of content in general.

Give me your diagnosis, grounded in what each side actually argues rather than in caricatures of it, and then address the harder part: what would count as progress. A sharper trichotomy among Shannon information, causal specificity, and semantic content? An empirical result about the code's origins? A worked example of an explanation in molecular biology that demonstrably cannot be given without semantic vocabulary? Or should we expect none of these to settle the matter — and if so, what does that imply about whether the question was well-posed to begin with?
