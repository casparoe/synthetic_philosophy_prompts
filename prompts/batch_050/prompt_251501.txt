So I'm a novelist -- literary fiction, a couple of midlist books, nothing you'd have heard of -- and I've been circling an idea for a novel about a man who keeps a dream diary and slowly comes to suspect the diary is inventing the dreams rather than recording them. That sent me down a rabbit hole and I discovered philosophers have an actual fight about this. As I understand it, Norman Malcolm argued in his book on dreaming that dreams aren't experiences unfolding in us while we sleep at all, that the dream is essentally constituted by the report we give on waking, so asking what really happened at 4 a.m. is somehow misconceived. And Dennett, coming from a totally different place, defends his own version where the "dream" might be composed in the very instant of waking, like a prefab tape slotted into memory -- and neither of them buys the common-sense picture where a little movie plays in your head at night and you just happen to remember fragments of it in the morning. I find this whole dispute weirdly close to my novel, and I want to understand it properly.

Here's what I'd like from you. I'm fluent in the vocabulary of fiction -- drafts and revisions, point of view, the narrator versus the implied author, free indirect style, the reader's experience versus the text on the page, the difference between what happened in the story and what the telling makes of it. Restate the Malcolm-Dennett dispute in that vocabulary, as precisely as you can: the received view, Malcolm's conceptual claim, Dennett's composition-on-waking claim, what each of them is actually denying, and what could even count as evidence between them. For what it's worth, my own suspicion going in is that the waking-report stuff maps perfectly onto narration and the disagreement is really about whether there's anything beyond the telling, which novelists would call an unanswerable question about stories nobody reads -- so if you think that suspicion is wrong or lazy, say so.
