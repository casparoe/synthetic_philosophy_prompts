Take Ireland’s 2015 same-sex marriage referendum as a real episode in rapid moral change and run it through evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics. I’m less interested in whether you personally think the Yes side was right than in what the episode does to the debunking debate. Basic facts you can assume: Ireland decriminalized male homosexuality only in 1993, had been heavily shaped by Catholic moral teaching, then in about two decades saw public opinion swing hard enough that in 2015 roughly 62 percent of voters backed marriage equality in a national referendum. The change was driven by some mix of people coming out to family and coworkers, media visibility, urbanization, the Church losing authority after scandals, younger cohorts, and arguments framed around equal treatment and commitment. No new biological discovery about sexuality caused the swing.

First define two tools I invented for this, then use them. Call a belief cascade echo-safe if, after people learn the messy causal genealogy of why the belief changed, the justifications they give would still look like justifications rather than post-hoc noise. Call it genealogy-load-bearing if once you subtract prestige, cohort replacement, punishment/reward, and contact effects, the remaining reasons are too thin to explain the conviction people feel. Be precise: these are not synonyms for true and false, and a belief can be genealogy-load-bearing while still true.

Use the episode to test three familiar moves: the strong debunker who says moral convictions are offspring of evolved tendencies plus cultural drift and therefore lack independent authority; the realist reply that evolution may have given us capacities to notice reasons, like giving us eyes without proving sunsets are hallucinations; and the middle position that evolutionary/cultural explanation debunks some domains more than others, e.g. disgust-heavy taboos more than harm and reciprocity judgments. Map Ireland carefully: disgust at gay sex, kin/ingroup loyalty, religious authority, compassion after knowing actual gay people, equal-treatment arguments, strategic bandwagoning, plain conformism. Say which parts of the swing are echo-safe and which are genealogy-load-bearing, and dont cheat by assuming your preferred metaethics.

Then turn it around: does Ireland help realism, because once contact and argument broke the disgust monopoly people could see an equal-treatment reason that was there all along, or does it help debunkers, because a conviction felt eternal in 1990 and evaporated under social pressure, suggesting the phenomenology of objective moral truth is easy to manufacture? Pay special attention to cohort replacement: if most change is people dying rather than being persuaded, is that better or worse for reason-responsiveness? Also handle the conservative worry that fast moral cascades are exactly when you should distrust your confidence, and the progressive worry that calling equality a fad is just the old status quo with better PR.

End with a verdict that is allowed to be split: what the episode confirms, what it is an anomaly for, and what it cannot settle because moral epistemology cannot run controlled history. No citations, no bibliography, no pretending to quote anyone. Write in American English, keep it analytic but not stiff, and if a distinction collapses under the case, say so plainly.
