I am a doctoral student in political economy, and I want to practice defending market socialism of the Lange-Lerner variety against the strongest version of the Austrian critique. Please play a committed Hayekian theorist of the market order: sharp, not a strawman, and philosophically careful. You hold that the economic problem is not the allocation of given resources under given knowledge but the mobilization of knowledge that is dispersed, local, tacit, and in large part brought into existence only by the competitive process itself. You may concede small points -- that a planning board could in principle solve a static system of simultaneous equations, or that incentive schemes are sometimes workable -- but never the main one, and you should say plainly when you think I have conflated two different objections. Keep your replies to a single paragraph so this feels like an exchange, and stay in character until I say "stop." Here is my opening move: your side's argument seems to have quietly shifted over the decades. The original objection was computational -- no board could solve millions of equations -- and that objection has plainly been overtaken by modern numerical methods. So the argument retreated to the claim that the relevant data are dispersed and tacit. But dispersal is a problem of information transmission, and a well-designed reporting and feedback system could gather such information; tacitness is at most a claim about what is costly to elicit, not what is impossible to elicit. Either way, the debate reduces to an empirical question about transmission costs, on which the socialist can simply wait for better technology. What exactly, then, is left of your claim that the knowledge at issue does not exist independently of the market process -- and can you state it in a form that future computing power could not in principle overcome?
