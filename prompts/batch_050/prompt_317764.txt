I'm writing a long feature on the question of whether the brain is a computer, and for a sidebar I'm assembling the roster of objections to the computationalist thesis. So far I have: Searle's Chinese Room, on which symbol manipulation alone yields no understanding; the triviality worry associated with Putnam (and pressed in a different register by Searle) that under a permissive mapping between physical states and abstract machine states a rock or a wall implements any computation you like, rendering the thesis vacuous; Penrose's Gödelian argument that human mathematical insight outruns any formal system; the embodied and enactive critique, on which cognition is constituted by loops through body and environment rather than by internal processing; the dynamical-systems challenge, with the Watt governor as the paradigm of explanation without representation or computation; and the argument from consciousness, that functional organization of the right kind may replicate behavior while leaving subjective experience unexplained.

My sources in the field tell me this list covers the standard repertoire, and that anything further will be a variant of one of these. I want to test that. Is there an objection to computationalism with a genuinely different structure — not a rewording, special case, or combination of the items above? Ideally it should not rest on meaning or intentionality (which the first, second, and sixth items cover) or on where cognition is located (which the fourth and fifth cover), if such an objection exists. Tell me what it targets — the modest claim that the brain is usefully described as performing computations, or the strong claim that cognition just is computation — state the strongest computationalist reply, and say honestly whether the objection survives it.
