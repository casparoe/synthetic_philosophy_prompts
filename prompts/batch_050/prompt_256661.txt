I teach a 75-minute upper-level ethics seminar with 16 students, and I want a classroom activity called The Museum Budget that makes them feel the tension between comparing atrocities and treating such rankings as morally obscene. Act as both a professional philosopher and an experienced classroom game designer. Think carefully, and internally brainstorm a few mechanics before choosing the strongest one.

Here’s the setup I want: the class is supposedly advising a small memorial museum with enough permanent exhibition space for only four of six cases of mass violence. Use six fictional but historically plausible case cards, each described in 80–120 words. The cases should vary along awkward dimensions: number killed, proportion of a group destroyed, duration, intentionality, cruelty of methods, destruction of culture or future generations, quality of evidence, and how forgotten the event has become. Don’t use thinly disguised versions of one famous atrocity, and don’t ask students to role-play victims or perpetrators.

Design rules that force comparison without letting it become a simple body-count exercise. Maybe include a limited number of “incomparable” tokens, dissent cards, stakeholder constraints, or a second phase in which the class learns that two apparently different cases are actually the same event described at different scales—but improve or replace these ideas if you have something better. There should be real choices, a point-based payoff scheme without money, and incentives that make refusing to rank genuinely costly rather than letting students safely say everything is incomparable.

Please give me:

1. a concrete 75-minute timeline;
2. exact materials and preparation;
3. clear rules for each round, including how individual judgment turns into a collective decision;
4. the six case-card profiles, with enough variation to generate disagreement but no gratuitous detail;
5. a payoff or scoring scheme that rewards practical reason-giving while penalizing fake precision;
6. a twist or constraint that reveals how rankings change when the practical purpose changes—memorialization, reparations, prosecution, prevention, or public education;
7. a 15-minute debrief connecting specific moments in the game to commensurability, cardinal versus ordinal ranking, morally relevant versus voyeuristic detail, and the difference between saying two evils can be compared for a purpose and constructing a league table of suffering;
8. what will predictably go wrong the firts time I run it, including students turning it into trivia, hiding behind “everything is incomparable,” or competing over who can sound most morally serious;
9. an explicit note about what the activity cannot reproduce—especially lived loss—and what I as facilitator must say or supply instead.

The key is that students should run into the problem through the rules before I name it. Keep it respectful, runnable, and not preachy.
