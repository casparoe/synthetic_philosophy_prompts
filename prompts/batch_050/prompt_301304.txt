I'm putting together a handout for an applied ethics course I'm teaching next term, and the unit covers moral bioenhancement: the view, defended by a prominent school in bioethics, that technological progress has placed catastrophic destructive power within reach of ordinary individuals while human moral psychology remains calibrated for life in small hunter-gatherer bands, so that we have a duty to develop biomedical means — pharmaceuticals, genetic selection, neural interventions — of strengthening moral dispositions such as altruism and the sense of justice, with compulsion potentially justified on public-safety grounds if voluntary uptake proves insufficient. My students tend to read the position as either obviously correct or obviously monstrous, and neither reaction forces them to articulate what exactly makes them uneasy. I want a piece of satire that does that work for them.

Write a parody patient information leaflet — the folded sheet that comes inside a medicine box, with the standard headings (what the medicine is and what it is used for; what you need to know before you take it; how to take it; possible side effects, complete with the usual frequency bands; how to store it; contents of the pack and other information) — for EMPATHIN, a fictional compulsory moral-enhancement drug administered to all citizens in a society that has implemented the program in full. The leaflet must be written throughout in the flat, bureaucratically soothing register of a real leaflet, and the humor must never break that register or wink at the reader.

The satire should land the standard objections to the program without ever stating them as objections: the circularity of letting a calibration committee define and dose the target moral traits (refusal to take the drug might be listed as itself diagnostic of the condition the drug treats); the way a public-safety rationale quietly dissolves informed consent; the worry that amplifying or blunting particular moral emotions may disable capacities a functioning morality needs, such as righteous anger at injustice or the willingness to perform distasteful duties like triage; the suspicion that compulsory benevolence makes people more governable rather than more moral; and the security argument taken at full strength — that a single unenhanced individual with the right laboratory equipment poses an existential risk, so universal dosing is simple species self-defense. A specialist in the debate should recognize that the author understands the position being satirized and judges the satire to be roughly fair; a general reader should find it funny anyway. Around 700 words.
