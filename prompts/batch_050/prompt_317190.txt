I spent forty years as a materials scientist, mostly ceramics and alloys, and since retiring I've been reading philosophy for fun. I keep coming back to this question about when a thing is identical to the stuff it's made of, and I noticed my own gut answers don't seem to hang together. So here are my verdicts, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, is behind them. Case one: a sculptor makes a statue out of a lump of clay, then on a bad day squashes it flat. I say the lump survives but the statue does not -- so they can't have been one and the same thing even while they coincided, since one has a property the other lacks (surviving squashing). Case two: same sculptor, but instead she carefully reshapes the statue into a bowl over a week, bit by bit, never destroying anything abruptly. Here I want to say the statue stops existing at some point even though nothing dramatic happens, but I can't say when, and that bothers me. Case three: a bronze statue is melted down and the same bronze is cast into a new statue of someone else. I say the original statue is simply gone, full stop -- the bronze persists but not the statue. Case four: my old corvette has had its engine, gearbox and body panels replaced piecemeal over twenty years, and I still think of it as my car, the same one I bought in 1983. And here's the pair I can't reconcile with case one: in case one I denied statue = lump because they have different persistence conditions, but in case four I'm happy to say the car persists through gradual total replacement of its parts, which seems to give 'car' and 'collection of parts' different persistence conditions in exactly the same way. Case five, the one I keep flip-flopping on: the statue is accidentally dropped and loses its nose, and a conservator sculpts a new nose from fresh clay of the same type. Part of me says the original statue is back with a repair, part of me says there's a new object that shares most of its matter with the old one. Is there a single principle about material constitution that delivers verdicts one through four and settles five, or do I have to give one up -- and if so, which is the weakest? Please keep the whole answer under 600 words, I'm not looking for a literature review, just the best candidate principle stated precisely and an honest note about which of my verdicts it can't save.
