For decades, social scientists and commentators have argued about whether "generations" -- Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z -- name anything real, and the argument seems no closer to resolution. On one side, critics argue that generation labels are arbitrary binning: cohort effects are statistically inseparable from age and period effects (linearly, the three are interdependent), boundaries are chosen for narrative convenience, and within-cohort variation dwarfs between-cohort variation. On the other side, defenders invoke something like Mannheim's notion that a generation is constituted not by shared birth years but by shared exposure to formative events in late adolescence -- a "generational location" that gives the cohort a distinct orientation. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are talking past each other because "real" is doing double duty: the critics deny that generations are sharply bounded natural kinds, while the defenders only need generations to be causally operative social kinds, which can be fuzzy, unevenly distributed across a cohort, and partly produced by the labeling itself. Diagnose the disagreement: is it verbal, empirical, or about the ontology of social kinds? Identify any hidden question -- statistical, causal, or metaphysical -- that is actually doing the work, and say what would count as progress: a distinction worth drawing, a finding that would change one side's mind, or an argument that the impasse is permanent.
