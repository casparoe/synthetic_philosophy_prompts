I'm a high school junior, and for my philosophy club I'm designing a one-page handout on whether machines could ever genuinely mean something, as opposed to shuffling symbols that only count as meaningful because we built and read them that way. Please write a decision tree that sorts the main positions on original versus derived intentionality.

Two positions should anchor the tree. First, Searle's: he argues that brains have original intentionality in virtue of their biological-causal makeup, while artifacts like computers have at most derived intentionality borrowed from their designers and users, and his Chinese Room case is meant to show that executing a program never adds up to understanding. Second, Dennett's: he thinks the original/derived distinction collapses, since our own intentionality is no more intrinsic than a machine's. His thought experiment involves a coin-accepting device built to take US quarters; shipped to a country whose different coin happens to match a quarter in size and weight, it would accept those coins just as readily, and nothing inside the device settles which it "really" detects — meaning gets fixed by design history and by how useful it is to interpret the device, not by any intrinsic inner fact.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names:

- Each branch node should be a question phrased so a club member could answer it without having read anything — for instance, what they want to say about the coin device after the move, about the Chinese Room, about a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person that pops into existence by accident, or about a parrot trained to produce sensible-sounding sentences.
- Leaves should include Searle-style biological naturalism, Dennett-style interpretivism, teleosemantic views that ground meaning in selection or learning history, causal-informational theories, and an eliminativist or deflationary leaf if the space supports one.
- Each leaf gets one sentence stating its most awkward consequence — what it is forced to say about today's chatbots, or about the accidental duplicate.
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, and note any coherent-looking combination of answers that no one seems to occupy, with a guess at why.

Write the response in American English, present the tree as an indented outline, and keep the whole thing under 550 words.
