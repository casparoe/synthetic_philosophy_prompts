I'm in a reading group that meets every other week to discuss a chapter of a popular book on arguments in the philosophy of religion, and last time we covered the Euthyphro dilemma. One member of the group, who tends to be confident about these things, gave us his summary of what the dilemma shows, and I wrote it down because I suspected it was too neat. His version ran roughly as follows: Socrates asks Euthyphro whether the pious is loved by the gods because it is pious, or pious because the gods love it; transposed to monotheism, this becomes the question of whether good things are good because God commands them, or whether God commands them because they are good. On the first horn, morality is the product of God's will, so had God commanded cruelty, cruelty would be good, which no one can accept — hence this horn makes morality arbitrary and God's goodness an empty compliment. On the second horn, moral truths hold independently of God's commands, which means morality doesn't depend on God at all, rendering God unnecessary for ethics. He concluded, with the book's apparent blessing, that the dilemma therefore refutes divine command theory and establishes that morality can stand on its own without any theological foundation, which is why Socrates — history's first great secular moralist — posed it in the first place.

I'm inclined to find the dilemma genuinely powerful, so I don't want to dismiss his summary out of reflex; but a few things nag at me. The leap from "morality doesn't depend on God's commands" to "morality doesn't depend on God" struck me as possibly sliding past something a theist would care about, and I wasn't sure whether the arbitrary-cruelty point against the first horn is an argument or just an appeal to what we find uncomfortable. I also have a vague memory that the actual dialogue doesn't conclude anything so tidy.

Could you audit his summary for me? Which parts are a faithful statement of the dilemma and its force, and which parts go beyond it? In particular: does the second horn really show that God is unnecessary for morality, or only something narrower? Is the arbitrariness charge against the first horn as decisive as he makes it sound, and are there moves available to a careful divine command theorist that the summary ignores? Is the attribution of a secularizing agenda to Socrates accurate to what the dialogue actually does and how it ends? And finally, once the summary is corrected to what it should have said, how much of his conclusion — that the dilemma refutes divine command theory outright — survives?
