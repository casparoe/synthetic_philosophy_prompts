I sit on the research ethics board of a university medical center, and I need a short written opinion I can circulate before our meeting next Thursday. Here's the case. About ten years ago a researcher here (fired at the time, now deceased) ran a dosing study on cognitively impaired patients without valid consent from anyone — the subjects couldn't consent themselves and no proper surrogate consent was obtained. The data are, embarrasingly, still the only usable data anywhere on this particular dosing question for this population. A new team, completely unconnected to the original guy, wants to reanalyze and publish them. Their argument: a national guideline panel meets in about 8 months to set the dosing standard; if our data are in the record, the question gets settled for good, and that matters because right now, with the question technically open, hospitals around the country keep running small sloppy ad hoc trials on exactly this patient population — maybe 15 a year, a few dozen patients each, with consent procedures barely better than the original study's. So the pitch is basically: one act of using tainted data now, versus an indefinite stream of fresh taint.

The problem is our charter. After the scandal we adopted a clause saying the institution shall not derive scientific benefit from research conducted in violation of its ethical standards. Some board members read this as a bright-line, agent-relative thing: WE do not profit from exploitation, whatever the downstream consequences. Others say the clause was always about deterrence, and deterrence is moot here because the researcher is dead and consent rules have since been tightened anyway. Families of the original subjects are split: one family has written to demand the data be destroyed, another says destroying them makes their father's suffering pointless.

Please write the opinion. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a timeline (including what, if anything, we communicate to the guideline panel, and what conditions we'd attach to any permitted use), and it has to take seriously the strongest version of the losing side, whichever side that turns out to be. In particular, I want you to grapple with the weird structure of the case: if the point of the no-benefit clause is respect for exploited subjects, it's strange that honoring it seems to guarantee that more subjects get exploited. Does that make the clause self-defeating, or is that exactly the kind of consequentialist reasoning that agent-relative duties are supposed to block? I honestly don't know, and that's the part of the memo I most need you to get right.
