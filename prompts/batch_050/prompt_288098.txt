I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on the foundations of expected utility theory, and I need a test case for the money-pump argument for transitivity. Construct a decision scenario to the following specifications.

The scenario must feature an agent with a strict preference cycle over three ordinary options: she prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, and in each case prefers the latter enough that she would pay a small sum to swap the worse for the better. Yet no money pump actually succeeds against her, even though she faces the offers freely, repeatedly, and with full information, unlimited time, and no memory or attention failures. Constraints: the options must be concrete, non-exotic goods (no lotteries over options whose structure secretly encodes the trades, no options defined as "the opportunity to trade later"); her immunity must flow from a psychologically realistic policy or disposition, not from the stipulation that she simply refuses; and the case must not exploit preference change mid-sequence, since a pump against an agent whose tastes shift is no argument against stable intransitivity.

For the scenario you construct, deliver the following. First, trace the attempted pump step by step and show exactly where and why it stalls. Second, identify the auxiliary assumption the money-pump argument needs beyond intransitivity itself — candidates include that one-shot choice dispositions reveal preferences, that agents evaluate each trade myopically rather than anticipating the sequence, or that declining a swap betokens indifference rather than incomparability — and demonstrate that your case violates precisely that assumption and no other. Third, state what happens if the assumption is reinstated: does the pump then run to completion, and does the agent's immunity collapse into either transitivity or exploitable irrationality? Fourth, give the strongest reply available to a defender of the money-pump argument — for instance, that the agent's sequenc-anticipating policy itself constitutes a higher-order preference that renders her effectively acyclic over plans, so the argument's target was never raw pairwise preference in the first place — and assess whether this reply saves the argument or merely redescribes the agent as transitive at a different level of description. Fifth and finally, draw the moral: does your case show that money-pump arguments establish a genuine synchronic constraint on preference, or only a conditional claim about agents who choose in a particular way?
