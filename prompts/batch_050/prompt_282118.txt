For the past year or so I've been keeping a notebook of public arguments that end in a way that feels strangely unsatisfying, and I'd like to know whether the ending I keep seeing has an established name, or whether I've just built a private filing system for something nobody else has bothered to mark out. The pattern is easiest to convey through cases. When astronomers adopted a sharpened definition of "planet" that excluded Pluto, the argument about Pluto's status didn't so much get resolved as evaporate: the sentence the Pluto loyalists had been defending was quietly rendered false by stipulation, and any replacement sentence they could offer — that Pluto deserves the title, that the new boundary is badly drawn — now meant something different from what they had originally cared about. I've noticed the same shape elsewhere. When a body like a medical association revises the criteria for some condition counting as a disease, the people who lose out lose not only the verdict but the very vocabulary in which to register their residual objection, because the words they would have used now officially mean something else. In each case a term gets repaired or tightened, the old dispute dissolves, and yet the underlying practical question — who gets the status, the sympathy, the funding, the seat at the table — sits there exactly as before, only now there is no shared language left in which to press it.

I've tried to fit this under labels I already know, and none quite works. It isn't simply a case of two people talking past each other, because before the repair both sides genuinely meant the same thing by the contested word. It isn't quite the trick of winning an argument by redefining your opponent's terms mid-debate either, because the repair is often perfectly defensible on its own merits — the sharper term may be more useful for science or administration — and the people doing the redefining aren't usually trying to silence anyone. And it isn't ordinary progress, where an old question gets answered and everyone moves on, because nothing has been answered; the question has been made unaskable in the old words, and whatever new words exist pick out a slightly different question.

So my question is whether this configuration — a concept is improved, the old dispute evaporates, but the practical stakes remain and the losers are left without the linguistic means to restate their concern — has a recognized name or literature. If it does, please tell me what it's called and, more importantly, how the established account carves the thing: what counts as an instance, what the nearest look-alikes are that don't count, and where the boundary cases fall. If it doesn't have a name, I'd like you to say so plainly and then coin one yourself, with a definition precise enough that I could use my notebook entries as test cases — for instance, would it still be the phenomenon if the redefinition were clearly motivated by the desire to end the dispute? What if the old question later gets re-asked successfully in the new vocabulary — does that retroactively show it was never really this phenomenon, or just that the damage was eventually repaired? I'm less interested in the label than in the carving, so please don't stop at a name.
