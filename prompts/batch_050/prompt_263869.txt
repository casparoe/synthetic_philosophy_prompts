So I'm midway through a novel and I've got a minor character who's started to take over the book, as they do. He runs a consultancy called Inevitable & Partners, and what they sell is retrospective certainty: after any big event (an election upset, a bank collapse, a war, a royal divorce, a surprise hit film) they produce a report for clients explaining why it was, in fact, obviously going to happen all along, and quietly binning their own pre-event forecasts. I invented him as a joke about columnists and now he's my favourite person in the thing.

Rather than describe the firm in narration, I want to drop in one of their actual documents, and I'd like you to write it: the firm's promotional brochure, roughly 550-650 words, in that glossy, soothing consultancy register. Deadpan throughout -- the narrator of the brochure believes every word of it.

The satire needs to work by exaggerating real features of hindsight bias and the narrative-fallacy habit, not invented ones. Things I want smuggled in, though never named as such:

- Their flagship service: the same underlying causes (low interest rates, elite complacency, public mood, whatever) can explain the event happening OR it not happening, and they offer both reports, and clients can buy the one that matches what occurred. This should be presented as a selling point, not a confession.
- A testimonial section where satisfied clients marvel that the signs were there all along -- even though several of them signed off on decisions that show they manifestly did not see the signs.
- A pricing tier where they retroactively rewrite your old predictions, memos and board minutes so that you turn out to have predicted the thing quite well actually, with a premium option where you predicted it better than your rivals did.
- A page on their methodology, which is essentially: start with what happened, pick the facts that lead to it, arrange them into a story with a moral, and discard everything that was also true but leads nowhere. Presented as rigorous.
- Maybe a sidebar about their graduate recruitment: they only hire people who are completely unsurprised by everything, and there's a note of quiet pride that no event in the firm's history has ever surprised a single employee, including the incident that bankrupted their previous office.

The humour should come from the reader slowly realising that the firm's entire product is the thing historians and pundits actually do for free, and that the reports would be unfalsifiable even in principle. No winking from the narrator, no moment where the brochure admits the trick. If you want a couple of invented client names and job titles for the testimonials, go for it, but keep any real public figures out of it.

Also, British spelling please, to match the rest of the manuscript.
