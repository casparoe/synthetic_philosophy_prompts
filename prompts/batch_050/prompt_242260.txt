I am a PhD student in political philosophy, and my dissertation chapter concerns the ethics of public commemoration — statues, street and building names, and the like. I have been collecting my own considered verdicts on cases before engaging the literature, and I would like to know whether any coherent principle unifies them, or whether I must abandon some. Here are the verdicts, stated as honestly as I can:

1. A statue of a slave trader, erected decades after his death during a period of racial backlash and standing today in the central square of a city whose population descends in part from enslaved people: removing it is fine, and relocating it to a museum with explanatory text is my preferred outcome.

2. Melting that same statue down and reusing the metal for a new memorial commissioned by the local community: also fine, though slightly harder for me than relocation. Permanent destruction does not bother me here the way I feel it should bother a pure preservationist.

3. Destroying an ancient statue of a cruel Roman emperor held in a museum: clearly not fine, even if the emperor's deeds were as bad as the slave trader's. The sheer historical distance and the artifact status seem to do the work, but I cannot say precisely how.

4. Renaming a street honoring a nineteenth-century scientist whose scientific work was genuine and whose personal papers, published after his death, show he privately endorsed eugenic views he never acted on or publicly promoted: not fine to rename. I feel the commemoration attaches to the achievement, not the whole person.

5. Removing a living billionaire's name from a hospital wing because the fortune came from a business found to have caused mass harm: fine, and I do not feel the same hesitation as in case 4. Something about the gift being ongoing, or the name functioning as current advertising, seems to matter.

6. Keeping a statue of a minor local figure — a much-loved nineteenth-century schoolteacher memorialized by her pupils — after research reveals she held the racist views typical of her time and place but did nothing specifically wrong: keep it, and adding a "contextualization plaque" strikes me as absurd here, though I support plaques in other cases.

7. A university founded with the fortune of a man who profited from the slave trade, whose name the university itself bears: renaming the institution feels wrong to me — the name has accumulated its own meaning — but removing his portrait from the great hall and renaming the main library feels fine. I cannot currently articulate why the institution's name is different.

8. A mob pulling down a statue that I think should come down anyway: I object to the manner — I want orderly, democratically authorized removal — but I cannot convince myself this is more than a procedural quibble, and I notice I would feel less troubled if the statue were of someone much worse.

Here is the case I keep reversing on: renaming a small town named after a colonial governor who ordered a massacre, where the town's current inhabitants, including descendants of the victims, are roughly split, and where the name is genuinely all most residents know about him.

What I would like: (i) a principle, or two or three candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I can see which verdict each clause is doing work for — I suspect something about what a commemoration currently expresses versus how it originated versus what removal would itself communicate, but I want this made rigorous rather than gestured at; (ii) an honest statement of which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate, and whether the tension between cases 4, 5, and 7 in particular forces me to drop one or reveals a real distinction I have not yet named; (iii) what the best candidate predicts about cases I have not considered — say, a mountain range named for a discredited explorer, or a post-colonial state systematically renaming cities; and (iv) a verdict, with reasoning, on my wobble case.
