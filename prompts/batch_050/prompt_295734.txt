Stayed up way too late last night arguing with a friend about the dogmatism paradox and I'm still kind of stewing about it. His line: if you genuinely know that p, then you already know that any future evidence against p must be misleading evidence, since it points at something false -- so when counterevidence shows up, the rational move is to just dismiss it, full stop. That can't be right, but I couldn't pin down exactly where it breaks, and honestly he sort of had me cornered by 2am.

I've got a second-year epistemology class coming up, 18 students, one 75-minute session, and I'd rather they feel this than hear me lecture it. Design an in-class game or staged activity where the students come to know some fact pretty securely, and then get hit with a stream of counterevidence they have antecedent reason to think is misleading -- so they feel the pull toward just waving it away, and the queasiness of actually doing it. Include the setup and rules, materials, some payoff scheme that doesn't use real money, the sequencing (they should stumble into the problem themselves before anyone names it), and a debrief that maps what happened onto the paradox and lays out the live options -- dismiss the evidence, update anyway, deny they ever really knew, deny closure -- with what each one costs. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which feature of the paradox a game like this just can't reproduce, because my friend is definately going to ask.
