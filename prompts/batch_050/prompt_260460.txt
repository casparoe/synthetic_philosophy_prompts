I'm grading undergraduate answers to an exam question in my normative ethics seminar and want a second opinion on one of them. Assign a letter grade with brief point-by-point comments justifying it: say what the answer gets right, flag anything inaccurate, misattributed, or overstated, and note any steps where the reasoning doesn't follow even if the conclusion is fine. I'm not asking how you would have answered the question -- just assess this student's work as submitted.

The exam question was:

In his 1977 paper Should the Numbers Count?, John Taurek argues that when you can save either one stranger or five strangers, but not all six, you are not morally required to save the five. Set out Taurek's reasoning for this conclusion, then explain how Scanlon's contractualism responds to it. Does the response succeed? Defend a verdict.

The student's answer:

Taurek considers a case where you have a drug that can save five people on one island or one person, David, on another, but not both. The standard view says you must save the five because the loss to five people is five times as bad as the loss to one. Taurek's central move is to invoke the separateness of persons: there is no one who undergoes the aggregated loss of the five, so it is a mistake to sum losses across distinct individuals and conclude that the outcome where five die is impersonally worse. Since each person's claim to be saved is equal and no one's claim can be outweighed by pooling it with others', Taurek concludes you should flip a fair coin, giving each of the six an equal chance, rather than automatically saving the greater number.

Taurek takes himself to have shown that numbers can never be morally relevant in rescue choices. If we could aggregate claims, he thinks, we would be treating the six people as a single mass of welfare rather than as individuals, and any distribution that maximized total benefit could be justified regardless of whom it burdens. The coin flip is therefore mandatory in any rescue dilemma, since it is the only procedure that respects each person's equal moral standing.

Scanlon replies from within contractualism. He argues that the right way to assess such choices is to compare what each affected individual could reasonably reject. Scanlon defends aggregating the complaints of the five: because the total of their complaints is greater than David's single complaint, a principle instructing you to save the greater number is one no one could reasonably reject. On Scanlon's view, then, contractualism vindicates the standard practice of saving the many by adding up individual claims, and this shows Taurek was wrong to insist on the coin flip.

The response succeeds. Scanlon shows that Taurek's anti-aggregation premise, taken seriously, would also condemn ordinary triage and public-health decisions that allocate resources where they benefit more people, and Scanlon's summing of complaints preserves those practices while still treating people as individuals. Taurek's coin flip should therefore be rejected in favor of saving the greater number in all such cases.
