The question I set was: "State the Condorcet jury theorem, including its assumptions. Then explain, in a few sentences, what the theorem does and does not show about when majority voting is a reliable way of getting at the truth."

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

The Condorcet jury theorem says that if a group is deciding between two alternatives where one is correct, and each voter is more likely than not to pick the correct one (probability p > 0.5), then the probability that a simple majority gets it right increases with group size, approaching certainty as the group grows. So a large enough jury is effectively infallible. The assumptions are: (1) each voter's competence p is above 0.5; (2) voters judge independently, i.e. they vote by secret ballot and don't deliberate or talk to each other beforehand; (3) the question is binary and has an actual right answer.

As an illustration: with three jurors each at p = 0.6, the majority is correct with probability about 0.65, which is already better than any individual juror.

What the theorem shows: majority voting is a reliable truth-tracking procedure, which provides an epistemic justification for democracy and for trial by jury -- the crowd is wiser than its members. What it doesn't show: it says nothing about choices among more than two options, nothing about whether real voters are actually better than chance on hard political questions, and nothing about protecting minorities, since it concerns correctness rather than fairness.
