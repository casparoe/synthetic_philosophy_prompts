I am provisionally persuaded that the decision-theoretic program for Everettian quantum mechanics — the line running from Deutsch's original argument through Wallace's later axiomatizations — dissolves the Born rule problem: roughly, that a rational agent who knows the universal wavefunction and accepts a short list of constraints on rational preference must apportion credence across branches according to squared amplitude, so that probability is no worse off in many-worlds than in any physical theory that posits objective chances. I want you to treat this position as the object of scrutiny and map its exposure. What would have to be true for it to be false or badly incomplete? Work through the places where pressure has actually been applied: the status of the individual rationality axioms (which ones are doing the work, and what would the world, or rational agency, have to look like for each to fail); the objection that the argument presupposes rather than derives that amplitude-squared weighting is the rationally required measure over successors; the objection that even a successful derivation leaves the incoherence problem — what probability could mean at all when all outcomes occur — untouched; and whether the derivation survives once you refuse to idealize the agent as knowing the branch structure. For each pressure point, distinguish what would be decisive (a result or argument that collapses the program) from what would merely be awkward (a consequence a defender can absorb by qualification), and say whether the qualification in question is principled or ad hoc. Where the program's own defenders have conceded ground — for instance, by retreating to weaker axioms or by allowing that the constraints are not purely constraints of consistency — identify the concession explicitly and assess what it costs. Finally, compare the position's exposure to that of its in-house rivals, the self-locating-uncertainty account and the envariance-based derivation: does the decision-theoretic program predict or commit to anything they do not, such that some future argument could refute it while leaving them standing, or has it been insulated to the point where no conceivable development could touch it — and if the latter, is that a virtue or a defect? Give me an honest verdict on how well-defended the position actually is, in no more than 900 words of prose, with no citations.
