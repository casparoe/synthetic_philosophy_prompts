I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I sit on the five-person editorial review board of a general-interest magazine. A well-regarded staff writer has pitched a long feature on an independent researcher who argues that roughly three centuries of the early medieval period were invented: the archaeological record for those years is thin, the argument runs, because the years never occurred, and later chroniclers backfilled genealogies, annals, and calendars to make the timeline add up. The writer wants to cover this as a live, unsettled controversy — presenting the conventional chronology and the revisionist one as rival hypotheses a lay reader cannot adjudicate — on the grounds that science journalism debunks too smugly and that consensus has been catastrophically wrong before. Two board members want to kill the pitch as a platform for pseudohistory; two want it commissioned; I hold the deciding vote and must write the board's opinion. Draft that opinion: recommend killing the pitch, commissioning it, or commissioning it with stated conditions, and supply criteria for distinguishing fringe-but-legitimate revisionism from pseudohistory that we could apply to future pitches without simply deferring to expert consensus. Take seriously the strongest version of the writer's worry about reflexive deference to orthodoxy, and state what would have to be true of this particular pitch for even a skeptic of the thesis to regard the coverage as legitimate. Lead with the recommendation in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the assignment.
