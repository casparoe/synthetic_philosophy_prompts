I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I am trying to specify an ethical problem the way I would specify a safety-critical system. The setting is a liberal state considering a class of proposed interventions called “civic disposition modulation”: drugs, gene edits, neural stimulation, and immersive behavioural programmes intended not to cure any recognised illness, but to make people less aggressive, more impartial, more cooperative, or more willing to make small sacrifices for distant strangers and future generations. Some interventions are reversible and temporary; others work by altering personality during childhood. The state is considering three possible policies: offering them voluntarily, subsidising them for people in high-impact occupations, and requiring them where experts judge the aggregate risk of ordinary human partiality to be catastrophic.

Write the specification an adequate theory of permissible moral bioenhancement would have to meet before it could adjudicate those policies. I want the requirements themselves, not a survey of authors. For each requirement, give the distinction, case, or engineering-style failure mode that motivates it. The specification should cover at least:

- how to distinguish moral enhancement from ordinary education, civic training, psychotherapy, and the design of coercive institutions;
- how to avoid treating a statistically common human disposition as a defect merely because it creates political inconvenience;
- how to weigh consent, childhood intervention, authenticity, and the possibility that a person endorsed neither the disposition being changed nor the values used to select the replacement;
- how to handle uncertainty, irreversible effects, unequal access, misuse by an incumbent government, and pressures that make a formally voluntary option practically unavoidable;
- how to decide whether improving compliance with currently accepted morality can count as moral improvement when the prevailing morality may itself be defective;
- how the theory should compare changing persons with changing incentives, laws, technologies, and institutions.

Distinguish requirements that any adequate theory must satisfy from requirements that belong only to a consequentialist, Kantian, liberal, or virtue-oriented approach. Where two plausible requirements cannot be satisfied jointly—for example, robust respect for autonomy and sufficiently rapid risk reduction—state the tension explicitly rather than smoothing it over. I am especially interested in which single requirement excludes the greatest number of superficially attractive proposals, and in whether the strongest objections concern the moral aim of the intervention, the biological means, or the state’s authority to impose it. A concise requirements table is welcome, but each entry still needs its underlying rationale.
