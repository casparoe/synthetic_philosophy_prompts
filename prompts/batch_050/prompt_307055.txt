For the past several years I have defended the view that explanation in biology is constitutively mechanistic: to explain a phenomenon is to show how it is produced by an organized arrangement of entities and activities, where components perform operations whose orchestration, at the relevant level of organization, brings about the explanandum. On this view, models that merely redescribe regularities, derive them from abstract constraints, or fit curves to data without specifying the underlying entities and activities are at best incomplete explanations, whatever their predictive power.

Tell me what would count against this position. Don't just repeat the standard complaint that fundamental physics isn't mechanistic; I want to know what, within biology itself, the view cannot accommodate. In particular, assess at least these candidates, and feel free to add better ones of your own: (i) explanatory uses of topological network models, where the explanatory work seems done by connectivity patterns rather than by the identity and operations of any particular entities; (ii) Hodgkin–Huxley-style models, which predict the action potential from equations fitted to experimental data and were explanatorily celebrated decades before the ion-channel mechanisms were identified; (iii) constraint- or geometry-based explanations, where a biological regularity appears to follow from mathematical necessities holding for any system with certain boundary conditions, whatever its parts are made of; and (iv) evolutionary explanations that cite selection pressures and population structure without decomposing anything into entities and activities at all.

For each candidate, say whether a committed mechanist would concede it as counting against the view or dismiss it as a misunderstanding of what the view claims, and be specific about which dismissal moves are available and whether they are legitimate or merely defensive. Distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would merely be awkward, and rank the defeaters from fatal to mildly embarrassing. I would personally find a counterexample most persuasive if it came from mainstream, successful biological practice rather than from a fringe case.

Then address the insulation question head-on: if every apparent counterexample can be absorbed by stretching the notions of mechanism, entity, activity, or level, does that show the view is robust, or that it no longer risks anything and so explains nothing about why mechanistic research succeeds? Conclude with a verdict on whether the position is well insulated, and whether that is a virtue or a vice.

Begin your response with the bottom line in the first sentence — whether the mechanistic view is genuinely at risk from anything in biology, and if so, from what — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of this prompt.
