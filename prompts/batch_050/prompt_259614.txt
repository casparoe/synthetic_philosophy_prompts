I facilitate a monthly philosophy discussion group for adults with no formal philosophy background -- lawyers, nurses, a retired plumber -- and next month the topic is the question of why promises bind: what, if anything, makes a bare promise morally binding when no law, contract, or third party enforces it. I don't want a lecture on what philosophers have said; I want a plan for how to get the group to the question itself and through it in ninety minutes. Specify: the order in which the pieces must be introduced and what depends on what (for instance, whether the distinction between being bound by law and being bound morally must come first, and why); the one case the evening should be built around -- I'm considering a promise made to someone who then dies before it can be kept, with no witnesses and nothing at stake -- and what that case must contain and must exclude to isolate the bindingness itself rather than guilt, reputation, or consequences; the three or four confusions I should expect from this group (I'm anticipating that promises bind because breaking them has bad effects, and that a promise no one knows about binds no one), the order in which they'll surface, and where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact; the question I can pose near the end that would show the group has grasped the puzzle rather than merely adopted a position; and what I should openly refuse to adjudicate at this level. Lead with the single-sentence core of the plan -- the one move everything else hangs on -- and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
