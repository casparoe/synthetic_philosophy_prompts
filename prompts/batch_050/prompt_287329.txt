I'm in a reading group on Victorian literature and doubt, and we just finished a stretch of George Eliot's letters. One sentence from a letter she wrote to Charles Bray in November 1857 has been nagging at me all week:

"My own experience and development deepen everyday my conviction that our moral progress may be measured by the degree in which we sympathize with individual suffering and individual joy."

Context: Eliot had long since lost her evangelical faith by this point, translated Strauss and Feuerbach, and still insisted morality was as binding as ever -- she just relocated its ground. This sentence seems to be her doing that in miniature: moral progress isn't growth in faith, or obedience, or even right belief, but widening sympathy with concrete individuals.

Here's what I want spelled out: what are the most important implications of actually taking this seriously as a measure of moral progress? A few things I'm already chewing on, but feel free to go past them. If sympathy is the metric, does that make moral education basically a training of imagination (which would explain why she thought novels mattered so much) rather than of principles or willpower? Does it mean religion's real function was always to produce fellow-feeling, so a religion that keeps the dogma but loses the sympathy has morally regressed even by its own lights -- and one that drops the dogma but keeps the sympathy has lost nothing that matters? And on the flip side, what's the obvious worry: sympathy tracks what's near and vivid and resembles us, so does her measure quietly license caring more about one visible suffering neighbor than about distant masses, or about the lovable sinner more than the prickly righteous? My friend in the group thinks the passage is basically sentimental and collapses under any utilitarian pressure; I think it's deeper than it looks. Who's right, and what follows either way?
