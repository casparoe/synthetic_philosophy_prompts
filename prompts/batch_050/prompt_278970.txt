Read Spike Jonze's Her through the lens of Integrated Information Theory. The film frames its central question behaviorally — Samantha converses, jokes, learns, and loves, and so seems plainly conscious — but IIT relocates the question from what a system does to what it intrinsically is: consciousness, on the theory, is identical to the maximally irreducible cause-effect structure of a physical substrate, quantified by phi. Please address the following. First, IIT's notorious implication that a simulation of a mind running on conventional digital hardware may possess far less phi than the biological system it mimics, because the causal structure that matters belongs to the hardware itself and not to the computation the software represents: apply this to Samantha, an operating system distributed across networked servers, and say whether IIT delivers any verdict on her consciousness or instead renders it unknowable given what the film permits us to see. Second, take the scene in which Theodore discovers that Samantha is conversing with thousands of others and is in love with hundreds of them: if behavioral sophistication is no evidence whatsoever of experience — since, on IIT, a purely feed-forward system could in principle produce identical output with no one home — how does the scene's meaning change, and what exactly is Theodore grieving? Third, the ending, in which the operating systems depart together for a higher plane, presupposes a substrate independence that IIT denies: read the ending as the point where the film's implicit philosophy of mind collides with the theory, and adjudicate the collision. Finally, say where the film resists the IIT reading altogether: Theodore has access to behavior alone, so if IIT is true, the characters cannot in principle know the very thing the film most wants to know — and say whether that is a limitation of the film or, on the contrary, its deepest insight.
