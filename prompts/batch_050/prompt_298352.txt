So I finally finished Death's End last month (late to the party, I know) and I can't stop thinking about the dark forest idea. For anyone unfamiliar: the universe is full of civilisations hiding from each other, because the safest move when you spot another one is to destroy it before it destroys you. It gets derived from two axioms — survival is every civilisation's primary need, and civilisations keep growing while the total matter in the universe stays finite — plus the chain of suspicion (you can't know my intentions, I can't know yours, and light-speed lag means we can never talk it out fast enough to fix that) and technological explosion (a weak civilisation can leapfrog a strong one in cosmically short time, so even a harmless-looking one is a threat eventually).

Anyway, somewhere in book three it hit me that this is basicly Hobbes. State of nature, no common power overhead, everyone's diffident, pre-emption is the rational play. I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background — I've read the famous bits of Leviathan and watched one YouTube summary of the rest, so I might be completly wrong here. But I'd genuinely like to know: what would Hobbes himself have made of the dark forest hypothesis?

Some angles I'm chewing on, ignore or extend as you like:

- Hobbes's state of nature argument leans on rough equality — even the weakest person can kill the strongest, so nobody is safe. Dark forest civilisations are massively unequal in tech, but technological explosion sort of re-creates equality over time. Would he accept that as the same thing, or does his argument need equality right now?
- Hobbes thinks the way out is everyone covenanting to hand power to a sovereign. Between star systems you can't enforce anything — messages take years, fleets take centuries. Would he say the dark forest is just the state of nature with the exit permanently bricked up? Or would he expect some civilisation to become a cosmic Leviathan and impose peace by force — and does dark forest logic itself make that impossible?
- His laws of nature say to seek peace when you can get it and keep your right to everything when you can't. The chain of suspicion means you can never verify peace is actually on offer. Would he treat that as "peace is never available, so anything goes", or does his stuff about covenants being void under reasonable suspicion leave room for some kind of signalling?
- Would he actually endorse shooting first? I remember there's a bit in Leviathan about anticipation being the most reasonable way to secure yourself, which sounds a lot like the strike-first logic. But he also paints the state of nature as dreadful, something rational agents should be desperate to escape — and dark forest civilisations can't escape. So does he call the strikers rational, or call the whole thing a tragedy, or somehow both?

Please reason from his actual framework (human nature, equality, fear, covenants, sovereignty) rather than generic "old philosopher would be amazed by space" filler, and flag where you're extrapolating versus where he more or less addressed the question directly.

Format roughly like this:

- The short answer (~100 words): would he recognise the dark forest as his own idea scaled up, plus his bottom-line verdict
- What he'd nod along to (~250 words): which parts of the dark forest logic follow almost straight from his premises
- Where he'd push back (~250 words): where the analogy breaks — equality, the laws of nature, whatever you find
- The Leviathan problem (~200 words): whether there can be a sovereign between the stars, and what he says if there can't
- Best-guess verdict (~100 words): his final position on whether pre-emptive strikes are rational, right, or both
