I keep finding myself using, and reacting differently to, explanations that cite reasons nobody ever entertained, and I'd like the principle behind my verdicts. "The heart beats in order to circulate blood": fine, though no one ever intended this. "Polar bears are white because camouflage made hunting easier for their ancestors": fine. "Evolution wanted polar bears to be white": not fine — too much agency smuggled in, even though it seems to describe exactly the same history as the previous sentence. "The asteroid struck so that mammals could inherit the earth": clearly not fine. "The cuckoo lays its eggs in other birds' nests in order to deceive the hosts": fine, though deception seems to imply a mind. "The thermostat thinks the room is too cold": somehow worse than the heart case, even though the thermostat, unlike the heart, traces back to a designer who genuinely had a reason in mind. "The river flows downhill because it seeks the sea": obviously not fine. What principle, if any, separates the legitimate cases from the illegitimate ones — what does a process have to be doing for there to be a reason without a reasoner? If no single principle captures all my verdicts, tell me which verdict you'd surrender, and test the repaired principle against two cases I didn't list: a trained rat pressing a lever "because it expects food," and a species going extinct "because it failed to adapt."
