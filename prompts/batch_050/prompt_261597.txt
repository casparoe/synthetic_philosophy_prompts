I'm putting together a syllabus unit on the causal exclusion problem and I want a slide of "live options" for my students to attack. Here's what I already have, so don't repeat any of these:

1. Just deny causal closure -- some version of emergentism or interactive dualism where the mental genuinely injects causal oomph into the physical world.
2. Type-identity theory -- the mental cause just is the physical cause under another name, so there's no competition and nothing to exclude.
3. Token-identity (Davidson-style anomalous monism) -- the same event instantiates both the mental and physical properties, and causation is a relation between events, not properties.
4. Honest epiphenomenalism -- bite the bullet, the mental doesn't cause anything, learn to live with it.
5. Genuine overdetermination -- like the two bullets killing the victim simultaneously, the mental and physical causes both fully suffice.
6. Counterfactual/dependence accounts -- distinguish being a cause from being causally relevant, and argue mental properties earn their keep because effects counterfactually depend on them.
7. Yablo's proportionality move -- determinables and determinates (red/scarlet, pain/C-fiber firing) don't compete, and the mental property is the proportionate cause for the effect.
8. Interventionism -- Woodward-style, mental-to-physical claims fail the conditions for a clean intervention so the exclusion principle doesn't get traction.

What I want is a ninth option that isn't on this list and isn't a rewording, combo, or special case of one of them -- e.g. not just "another way of being a physicalist about the events" and not "overdetermination but only sometimes." It should have a genuinely different structural idea about where Kim's argument goes wrong (or about what survives it). Explain how it works, say which premise of the exclusion argument it touches, and then be straight with me about whether it's actually defensible or just sounds clever.
