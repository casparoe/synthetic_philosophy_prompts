I'm working on a paper about AI moral status and a friend hit me with an argument that my whole approach is dead on arrival, and I can't find the hole. (1) If a system is sentient, it's sentient in virtue of implementing some computation — some pattern of functional organization — since that's basically the only thing a biological brain and a silicon system could have in common that would matter. (2) A physical system implements a computation iff there's a mapping from its physical states onto the computation's states such that the transitions line up. (3) Given a complex enough system and enough freedom in choosing the mapping, you can find such a mapping between pretty much anything and any computation you like — the molecular jiggling of a wall over an hour, say, can be mapped state-by-state onto whatever your brain did this morning. (4) So the wall implements whatever computation makes you sentient. (5) The wall is not sentient. (6) So (1) is false: there's no purely computational criterion of sentience, and my project of figuring out which AI systems deserve moral consideration by examining their functional organization collapses into either panpsychism or drawing the line by gut feeling.

I want to keep (1) if at all possible. My instinct is that something's off in how (2) and (3) interact — the mapping feels like it's doing no real work — but every restriction on "legitimate" mappings I've tried either smuggles in an observer's choice (making sentience relative to who's describing the system, which seems just as bad) or amounts to defining sentience twice. So: which step do I actually reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me for the AI case specifically? For instance, does the fix also handle a giant lookup table that just plays back recordings of a sentient system's behavior — does it rule that out too, or does it leave that one standing, and is that a problem?
