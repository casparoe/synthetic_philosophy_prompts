Next month I'm giving a talk in the colloquium series at my old physics department -- I'm retired now and have gotten into philosophy late in life, so be gentle. My thesis: in a one-shot symmetric game like the prisoner's dilemma played between people who reason alike, the rational move is to cooperate. Not because of any spooky causation, but because whatever I end up deciding is strong evidence about what the others decide, and I should prefer the news my own decision delivers. I'm drawing on those old magazine experiments where readers mailed in postcards saying whether they'd cooperate, and on the lottery where sending in more entries raises your chance of winning but shrinks the prize if too many people enter. The audience will be mostly physicists and applied mathematicians plus a couple of economists, none of whom have read the decision theory liturature, and there's at least one guy who'll insist that if you can't causally influence the other players, cooperating is just irrational, full stop. Give me a prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this crowd, phrased the way they'd phrase them, roughly ordered by how much damage they'd do, each with a short reply and a note on where the reply is soft. And tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
