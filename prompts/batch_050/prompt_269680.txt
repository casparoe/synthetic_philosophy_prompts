I'm putting together an application for a summer research grant at my department (about 8 weeks of funding, final deliverable is a working paper of maybe 9-10k words) and I need a proposal. The topic I keep coming back to is what people call commitment races. The rough background, as I understand it: in games like chicken, being able to visibly bind yourself ("I've thrown the steering wheel out the window") is an advantage, and Schelling-style work on strategy showed how rational agents can benefit from making themselves less flexible. And in Newcomb-type situations, agents known to follow a commitment-following policy do better than agents who decide case by case. So far so good for commitment. But here's what bugs me: if everyone knows that committing early and rigidly is advantagous, you get pressure on everyone to commit earlier, more visibly, and more irrevocably than everyone else, and the end result seems to be either mutual destruction (two steering wheels on the road) or a race so fast that nobody actually gets to deliberate about what they're committing to. The supposed rational advantage eats itself.

My half-formed idea for the project: there should be a principled answer to the question of when the capacity to commit helps an agent and when it hurts, stated in terms of the structure of the game and what other agents can observe, rather than just case-by-case intuition. I want to test the hypothesis that commitment power is only advantageous when it's scarce or asymmetric, and becomes neutral or actively harmful once it's symmetric and transparent to all parties, because then the only "equilibria" left are ones where agents are forced into increasingly costly commitments. But I'm not wedded to this hypothesis, and I'd want the proposal to seriously consider that it fails, e.g. if it turns out that symmetric commitment ability instead produces coordination benefits (both sides precommitting to fair bargains).

Write me the proposal: about two pages. State the research question precisely enough that it could actually have an answer in 8 weeks, explain why the existing discussions (the strategic-commitment literature's optimism about binding yourself, and the decision-theoretic literature's optimism about resolute choice) don't already settle it, and lay out what I'd concretely do week by week. Be honest about method: this is conceptual/game-theoretic work, not experiments, so say something about how a formal-model-plus-cases approach can make real progress here rather than just relabeling intuitions. Also include what a positive result would look like, what would count as the project failing (including the possibility that the question dissolves because "commitment" turns out to mean several different things), and one paragraph on scope risks.
