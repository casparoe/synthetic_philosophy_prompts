Here is the question I set (students had ~700 words):

"A city government proposes to deploy an AI planning assistant that will recommend zoning, transit, and budgeting decisions. The designers propose to align the system by aggregating the preferences and judgments of the city's residents. Critically assess the following three worries: (1) Arrow's impossibility theorem shows there is no non-arbitrary way to aggregate residents' preferences, so the project is doomed from the start; (2) Rawlsian political liberalism requires the state, and hence any AI it deploys, to remain neutral between citizens' conceptions of the good, which rules out aligning the system to citizens' values at all; (3) any aggregation must first decide WHOSE values count -- residents only, affected non-residents, future people -- and this boundary problem is itself a value-laden choice that cannot be settled by aggregation. For each worry, say whether it succeeds, and what (if anything) it implies for how the system should be designed."

And here is the student's answer, unedited:

"Arrow's impossibility theorem shows that any procedure aggregating individual preference orderings into a social ordering that satisfies unrestricted domain, the weak Pareto principle, and independence of irrelevant alternatives must be dictatorial. On the face of it this is fatal to the designers' plan: there is no non-arbitrary way to combine residents' rankings of outcomes into a single ranking the AI could pursue. Rawlsian political liberalism gives essentially the same worry from a different direction. Because of the burdens of judgment, reasonable citizens will always disagree about the good, so the state -- and by extension a state-deployed AI -- must remain neutral between conceptions of the good and restrict itself to securing justice. An overlapping consensus is possible, but only on a political conception of justice, not on values generally, so the AI cannot simply read its values off the consensus.

Both worries, however, can be sidestepped. First, Arrow only bites if we insist on purely ordinal information. If the system elicits cardinal utilities -- say via stated willingness to pay -- Harsanyi's aggregation theorem shows that under the expected utility axioms the only coherent social welfare function is the sum of individual utilities. Since that sum is uniquely determined, the impossibility disappears and the designers have a principled target. Second, instead of aggregating preferences the AI could aggregate judgments about what is right or permissible. Judgment aggregation escapes Arrow because it operates on propositions rather than rankings: citizens vote on each relevant proposition and majority rule yields the social judgment. Condorcet's jury theorem further shows that, provided citizens are more likely than not to be right, the majority judgment tracks the truth with increasing reliability as the population grows, so the aggregated judgments will tend to be correct.

The Rawlsian worry is also weaker than it appears. Neutrality of aim requires only that the AI not be justified to citizens by appeal to any particular comprehensive doctrine; it does not require neutral effects. So an assistant that reliably helps residents achieve their own conceptions of the good -- better transit for commuters, quieter streets for families -- is permissible so long as its design rationale is neutral. And where the system must act for everyone at once, as in budgeting, the overlapping consensus already supplies the needed agreement: citizens of a well-ordered society agree on the substantive principles of justice, so the AI can simply implement those principles and need not aggregate values at all.

Conclusion: Arrow blocks ordinal preference aggregation, but cardinal summation and majority judgment aggregation both offer viable escape routes, and Rawlsian neutrality constrains justification rather than outcomes, so the city's project survives all three objections."
