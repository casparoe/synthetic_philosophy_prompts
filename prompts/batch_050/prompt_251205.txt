I'm a novelist — mostly literary fiction, though this new project has me reading more philosophy than I bargained for. The book follows a character, June, who's a kind of connoisseur of pleasures: she keeps notebooks ranking them, and at one point she tells her sister that the pleasure of finally understanding a difficult proof and the pleasure of hot soup when you've got a cold are "the same thing wearing different clothes." Her sister thinks that's obviously nonsense. I realized I didn't actually know who's right, so I fell down the rabbit hole of what philosophers call the heterogeneity problem for hedonism — the worry that pleasures are so wildly various that nothing unifies them as pleasures at all.

From what I can gather there are two big families of answers. One says all pleasures share some common felt quality, a hedonic tone or glow that's present in the soup and the proof alike, differing only in intensity and in what it happens to be attached to. The other says pleasures have nothing in common as feelings whatsoever; what makes something a pleasure is our stance toward it — that we intrinsically want it, want it to continue, would savor it. Could you compare these two properly? I don't want a survey of who said what, I want to see where each one actually cracks under pressure. For instance: how does the felt-quality view handle the fact that I can't locate any single feeling common to the pleasure of scratching a mosquito bite and the pleasure of hearing that a rival's book got savaged in review? And how does the attitudinal view deal with cases where you seem to enjoy something you never wanted and wouldn't choose, or with "false pleasures" — being pleased that something happened when it didn't; what is the attitude even directed at then? Or pleasures with no object at all, like the diffuse glow of a painkiller kicking in?

The practical stakes for me: June has to be philosophically defensible, at least defensible enough that a clever critic character in the book can't flatten her in one line. So tell me which view a smart, slightly obsessive person would most plausibly hold, and what the single strongest objection her sister could throw at it would be. And if you think my whole framing of the debate is off, say so — I'd rather fix the philosophy now than have some reviewer do it for me.
