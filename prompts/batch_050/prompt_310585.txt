I'm a software engineer and I've been reading about money-pump arguments on my own. I put together the argument below and I'm stuck, because the conclusion seems obviously false but I can't locate the bad step. Some context on where I'm coming from: in my world, changing your mind is normal and often rational — we deprecate APIs, migrate databases, reverse architectural decisions when requirements shift. So an argument that concludes that changing your preferences is irrational feels to me like it must prove too much. Here's the argument:

(1) If there exists a sequence of trades, each of which you would freely accept at the moment it's offered, which leaves you strictly worse off than you started (same goods, less money), then your pattern of choices is irrational. This is the standard money-pump diagnosis.

(2) Suppose your preferences change over time in the ordinary way: today you prefer A to B, and you know that next year you'll prefer B to A. (Concretely: right now I'd rather have the motorcycle than the piano; I fully expect that in ten years, being older, I'd rather have the piano than the motorcycle.)

(3) Given (2), a broker can pump you. Today, while you prefer A to B, he sells you A in exchange for B plus a small fee — a trade you gladly make. Next year, once you prefer B to A, he sells you B back in exchange for A plus another small fee — again a trade you gladly make. You're now back where you started, minus two fees. And since your future preference change was predictable, he can do this as often as your preferences cycle.

(4) So by (1), having preferences that change over time — at least when the change is foreseeable — is irrational. A fully rational agent must hold its preferences fixed, or at least must refuse ever to act on a preference it expects to outgrow.

(5) But that's absurd. Acquiring tastes, maturing, revising what you value in light of experience — these aren't just unavoidable, they often seem like paradigms of rational growth. The person who comes to prefer quiet evenings to nightlife, or who develops a taste for coffee, or who stops caring about status goods, isn't thereby exploitable in any way that should worry them, and a theory that says otherwise has lost the plot.

(6) Therefore either the money-pump diagnosis in (1) is wrong, or there's some difference between the classic intransitivity pump (preferring A to B, B to C, C to A at a single time) and my pump in (3) that blocks the inference to (4).

My own guess is that something goes wrong around (3) — it seems like the pump there requires smuggling in an extra assumption that the classic pump doesn't need — but I can't articulate what the assumption is, and (3) does appear to satisfy (1)'s description of a money pump: every trade is freely accepted at the time, and the net result is strictly worse. If I have to give something up, I'd most like to keep (1) in some form and I'd least like to be told that foreseeably changing your mind is actually irrational. Which step do you reject, why does it look compelling anyway, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular: does whatever fix you offer still let the money-pump argument do its usual work against intransitive preferences, or does the fix prove too much in the other direction by defusing the classic pump too?
