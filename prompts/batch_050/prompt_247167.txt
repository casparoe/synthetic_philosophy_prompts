Someone in my reading group on scalable oversight tried to convince us this week that the whole process-based vs outcome-based supervision distinction is illusory, and his argument bugs me because I can't find the hole. He put it like this. (1) The only reason to care about a model's reasoning process at all is that better processes more reliably yield good outcomes; a process that didn't would be worthless however pretty it looked. (2) So our standards for what counts as a good process are justified entirely by outcomes, i.e. by their track record of leading to correct answers. (3) A standard whose justification comes entirely from outcomes is, at bottom, an outcome standard, just worn as a process standard. (4) And checking a proxy is at best an indirect, noisier way of checking the thing it's a proxy for, so applying such a standard step-by-step can't give you anything you wouldn't in principle get from checking the final answer. (5) Conclusion: process supervision is outcome supervision with extra steps. Where you can verify the outcome, it's strictly worse; where you can't verify the outcome, you also can't have validated your process standard, so it can't be trusted either. But if (5) is right, then the empirical reports that step-level feedback catches failures that answer-level feedback misses must be confused or a temporary artifact, which seems to prove too much. I'd really like to keep (1), since it just seems like the basic motivation for caring about reasoning at all. So which step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me?
