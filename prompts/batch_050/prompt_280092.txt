I serve on the board of a small philanthropic fund that supports legal scholarship, and this year we've earmarked money for roughly eight two-year projects on the question of legal personhood for AI systems. At our last meeting the board kept circling the corporate-person analogy: some members treated it as obviously the right template (we made persons of corporations, so we can make persons of models), while others argued the analogy is doing more harm than good, since corporations are personified largely to serve as contracting and liability nodes for identifiable groups of humans, and a deployed model has no members, no stable identity across copies and fine-tunes, and no assets of its own unless we grant them. I've been asked to propose a prioritized list of the open conceptual questions in this area, so that we can solicit proposals against it rather than funding whatever happens to arrive.

What I'd like from you is a defended agenda, not a survey. Candidates on the table include, but are not limited to: the entity-identification problem (what would the person even be, given that a model can be copied, merged, quantized, and continuously updated, so there is no persistent bearer for rights or duties); the bundle-versus-status question (whether the practical goods people want from AI personhood -- the ability to hold assets, sue and be sued, enter contracts, carry insurance -- can be had piecemeal without any freestanding personhood status, and what if anything the status adds); the liability-shield worry (whether personhood would mainly function to absorb responsibility that should rest with developers and deployers, creating an accountability gap rather than filling one); the expressive dimension (what granting or refusing personhood would signal about how we regard these systems, and whether that signal matters independently of legal mechanics); and the question of which real precedents -- corporations, rivers, ships, religious idols, animals in some jurisdictions -- are actually load-bearing analogies versus decorative ones. For each question you prioritize, explain why it is upstream of the others (which later questions cannot be sensibly answered before it), how tractable it is for legal theorists working without new empirical results, and how decision-relevant it is for legislation likely to be drafted in the next five years. Equally, tell me which questions on my list you would deprioritize or dissolve -- which ones, on inspection, rest on a confusion or would not change anything downstream -- since part of the value for the fund is knowing what not to fund.

Finally, please state the criteria your ranking uses and make the trade-offs explicit: if you weight upstream-ness over tractability, or near-term policy relevance over philosophical depth, say so and say why, and note what developments over the next few years -- a particular kind of technical capability, a court decision, a regulatory move in some jurisdiction -- would cause you to reorder the list. The deliverable I bring to the board should read as an argument for an ordering, with the reasoning inspectable enough that a member who disagrees with one of your premises can see exactly where the ranking would change.
