I am preparing a seminar session on the morphology of history, and I would like a poem to anchor it. Please write a sestina on Polybius's cycle of constitutional change (his anacyclosis) and its modern descendants, using the sestina's machinery to do real argumentative work rather than merely decorate the theme.

Specifically:

1. The six end-words should be the six regime-types of the Polybian sequence — kingship, tyranny, aristocracy, oligarchy, democracy, ochlocracy — so that the sestina's fixed permutation of end-words across stanzas enacts the wheel of anacyclosis itself: a recurrence that is strict in form but never a mere repetition, since each word returns in a new position and a new sentence. Take some care with the order; a reader who knows Polybius should be able to trace the canonical degeneration (each good form decaying into its corrupt twin, mob rule collapsing back into one-man rule) in the movement from stanza to stanza.

2. Let one voice within the poem speak for the long lineage of cyclical thought — Ibn Khaldun's account of group solidarity dissolving as dynasties soften into luxury, Spengler's claim that cultures pass through seasons from spring into the winter he calls civilization — without quoting any of them and without reducing any of them to a caricature.

3. The envoi (the closing three lines, in which all six end-words must appear) is where the poem should turn against its own form: it should raise the linear or spiral rejoinder — the suggestion that recurrence is real but that each return happens at a different altitude, that the wheel may be a screw — and it must do so using the six regime-words themselves, so that the very material of the cycle is made to voice the objection to it. Leave the contest unresolved; the seminar's question is whether the envoi's counter-move is a genuine escape from the cycle or merely the cycle's seventh, unwritten stage.

The poem should be accurate enough that a historian of political thought would nod rather than wince, and it should work as a poem for a reader who has never heard of Polybius.

After the poem, include a JSON object summarizing your own construction, with exactly these fields:
- "end_words": the six end-words in the order they appear in the first stanza
- "stage_progression": a brief account of how the Polybian sequence is distributed across the six stanzas
- "cyclical_voices": which of the named thinkers (Polybius, Ibn Khaldun, Spengler) speaks where, in one line each
