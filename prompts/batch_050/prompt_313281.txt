Last night I had a long argument with a friend about a decision I am facing at work, and I would like a careful, philosophically informed second opinion. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher specializing in twentieth-century political thought. Think through the problem step by step: brainstorm the relevant distinctions first, weigh them, and only then advise me.

Here is the situation. I handle communications for a mayoral campaign, and we need to respond to a damaging new report about the city's schools. We have two options. The first is to keep citing a statistic we now know to be false -- the candidate would knowingly assert something untrue, in exactly the way everyone already expects politicians to do. The second is to stop talking about measurable facts altogether and instead tell a vivid, emotionally compelling story about what the people of this city have always known -- a story crafted so it cannot be checked against any facts at all, and one our own team could plausibly come to believe themselves over time.

My friend argued that the first option is clearly worse, because it is the only genuine lie: under the second option, no one would be asserting anything they know to be false, so strictly speaking no one is lying at all. I found myself arguing the opposite -- that the second option feels like the deeper poison -- but I struggled to say precisely why. It seems to have something to do with the difference between concealing particular facts and dissolving the whole practice of checking claims against facts, and perhaps with what each option does to the liar, not just to the audience.

Who is right? And independently of who wins the argument, which option, if either, should I actually take?
