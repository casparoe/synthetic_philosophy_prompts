Everyone says deepfakes undermine the "epistemic backstop" of video evidence, but I want the actual mechanism spelled out, because the naive version of the claim seems too weak. Photographs have been fakeable since the 19th century -- staged shots, darkroom compositing, selective cropping -- and yet courts and newspapers still managed to treat them as evidence. So what exactly is supposed to be new? Please explain the difference between the first-order problem (people believing fake footage) and the second-order problem (people disbelieving real footage, the so-called liar's dividend), and why the second-order one might do most of the damage even if detection tools get really good. Relatedly: in the old regime, was our trust in photos resting on the fact that faking was hard and expensive, or on institutions vouching for provenance? Because I suspect the answer changes what a fix would even have to look like -- better detectors vs. something like cryptographic signing at the camera -- so please explain which diagnosis supports which remedy.
