Two friends of mine got into a proper argument last week and I've been stewing on it since, because I'm about to face something similar myself. Maya is on a hiring panel. She interviewed a candidate, reviewed all the materials carefully, and concluded the candidate is excellent -- maybe 80% confident. Then she learned that Priya, a colleague she regards as a *better* judge of candidates than herself, independently concluded the candidate is poor. Here's their exchange:

Maya: I should roughly split the difference and drop to around 50%. If I kept my 80% while admitting the evidence suggests I shouldn't trust my own judgement, I'd be epistemically akratic -- believing something while believing I shouldn't. That's just irrational, like a smoker who says smoking is bad while lighting up.

Priya: But here's the thing. Your 80% reflects your actual engagement with the interview and the file; my disagreement is just one more piece of data. If you blindly defer to me every time we disagree, you're not reasoning anymore -- you're averaging. Call it "confidence outsourcing". Also, notice the asymmetry: I interviewed the candidate too, and by your own logic *I* should now move towards *you*, which means we both end up somewhere neither of us actually believes. That's clearly daft.

Maya: Fine, but that cuts both ways. Suppose you keep your view and I keep mine. Each of us then believes "my judgement on this candidate is more reliable than the other's" -- but the only evidence we have about whose judgement is better here is symmetric. So you're committed to what I'd call first-order favouritism: trusting your own assessment for no reason beyond the fact that it's yours. Isn't that just bias dressed up as level-splitting?

Priya: No, because there's a real asymmetry you're hiding: I have access to *why* I believe what I believe, and I only have access to the bare fact of your disagreement. Treating those as equal evidence is like saying a witness who saw the event and a witness who heard a rumour cancel out. And anyway, your "akratic smoker" analogy is rigged -- belief isn't voluntary like smoking is, so you can't just decide to be at 50% because the meta-evidence says so.

Maya: Granted, but then give me a principled stopping rule. How much disagreement from me would it take before you budged? If the answer is "none", your position is unfalsifiable and I'm right. If the answer is "some", then the only question is where the line is, and my position is the honest version of yours.

Can you adjudicate this move by move -- saying who's right at each step and where each of them is smuggling in a bad assumption -- and then give an overall verdict on what Maya should actually do? Please also say whether the disagreement is really about epistemology or about something more practical that neither of them has articulated. Answer in British English, please.
