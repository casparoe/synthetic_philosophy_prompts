I'm taking an undergraduate ethics seminar this term, and after last week's session on utilitarianism and its critics, two classmates of mine got into a long disagreement in the hallway that neither of us could settle. I'd like a careful adjudication of their exchange.

The topic was the 'separateness of persons' objection to utilitarianism. Elena opened by stating the objection in what she called its strongest form: utilitarianism treats society as if it were a single person, summing all gains and losses into one ledger, and this is what licenses its worst implications -- for example, that seriously harming one person could be justified by tiny benefits to a very large number of others. Within a single life, she granted, trade-offs make sense, because the same individual who bears the cost also enjoys the benefit; between lives, there is no such individual, so the arithmetic has no subject.

Marcus replied that the objection proves too much. Every non-absolutist moral view, he said, permits some people to be burdened for the sake of others: progressive taxation, wartime conscription, quarantine rules, even the triage protocols Elena herself had defended in an earlier seminar. If the separateness of persons rules out weighing one person's loss against others' gains, then it condemns all plausible political morality, not just utilitarianism -- so it cannot be a distinctive problem for that theory. He added that the utilitarian can perfectly well acknowledge separateness: each person's welfare is assessed individually before anything is summed, so the theory hardly denies that persons are distinct.

Elena answered that this reply misses the level at which the objection operates. Her claim was not that utilitarians fail to notice that there are many people, but that the theory's justificatory structure lets one person's uncompensated loss be cancelled by gains to strangers, whereas justice requires that a burden imposed on someone be answerable to that person. Tracking each welfare level separately before aggregating, she said, changes nothing about what does the moral work at the end.

Marcus then turned the intra-personal analogy against her. When a patient endures a painful operation for the sake of future health, the earlier suffering self is not literally compensated by the later one -- what justifies the trade-off is simply that both stages belong to one life. So on her own reasoning, the permissibility of intra-personal trade-offs cannot rest on compensation either; it must rest on the unity of the person. And the unity of a person over time, he argued, is just a matter of psychological connectedness, which comes in degrees -- meaning the boundaries between persons are not obviously the deep moral joints her objection needs.

Elena's final move was to insist that even if personal identity over time is less metaphysically robust than common sense assumes, there remains a morally crucial asymmetry: the patient can later be glad she had the operation, while the person sacrificed on the utility calculus is never glad, and no aggregate fact changes that. She conceded she wasn't sure this asymmetry survives Marcus's reductionism about identity, but said the burden of proof was his.

Could you adjudicate their exchange as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion? Points one through four should each assess one move in the order made above (Elena's opening objection, Marcus's proves-too-much reply, Elena's structural restatement, and the closing exchange about compensation and identity), saying who is right about that specific point and why; point five should state who is more right overall and what, if anything, both parties are missing. Please answer in American English.
