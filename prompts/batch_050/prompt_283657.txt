I'm a PhD student working on comparative political theory, and I keep circling back to Han Feizi. Last week in our reading group on early Chinese thought I tried to defend him against the standard Confucian objection (that you can't run a state on punishments and rewards alone, you need moral cultivation), and while doing that I accidentally built an argument that seems to show his whole system is self-undermining. My advisor says the argument is too cute and must go wrong somewhere, but she wouldn't tell me where, and now it's bugging me because I can't find the leak myself.

Here's the argument. (1) Han Feizi's core claim is that rulers should never rely on people being good, since the vast majority of people act from self-interest and respond predictably to clear rewards and punishments; institutions must be designed so that order doesn't depend on anyone's virtue. (2) Laws and standards (fa) only produce order if they're actually enforced, and enforcement is done by officials, who are themselves people and so, by (1), act only when the incentive structure makes enforcement worth their while. (3) So the ruler has to monitor and incentivize the enforcers. But the monitors are also people, so they too only monitor when incentivized, which requires monitors of the monitors, and so on. (4) A regress like this can only terminate in some final agent whose compliance doesn't depend on external incentives -- someone who enforces the law because they're committed to it, or to the ruler, or to the Way, i.e., someone whose conduct flows from character rather than from the reward/punishment structure. (5) Therefore any workable legalist state has at least one node where order depends on virtue, which means rule by incentives never actually replaces rule by virtue -- it just pushes the reliance on virtue up to a single point, usually the ruler himself. (6) But if the whole edifice rests on the ruler's character, then legalism is just Confucianism with extra steps and a worse public image. That conclusion is clearly too strong -- it would mean Han Feizi spent a book attacking a position he secretly needed. But each step looks fine to me when I stare at it.

So: which step do you reject? Fair warning, I really want to keep (1) -- I think it's a fair statement of his view and I can defend the attribution with textual evidence if pushed. I'm most suspicious of (3) and (4) but for different reasons and I can't articulate either suspicion cleanly. When you tell me where it breaks, also tell me what Han Feizi himself could say at that step -- I know he has the notions of shi (positional power) and shu (techniques of control), plus stuff about mutual surveillance and making informants of everyone, and I have a feeling the fix lives somewhere in that machinery, but I don't want to just gesture at those terms. And if it turns out the argument is actually valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to give up and what Han Feizi's system looks like after you pay.
