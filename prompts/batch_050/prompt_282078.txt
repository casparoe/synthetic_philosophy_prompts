Take the fight over Confederate monuments in the US -- the removals after Charlottesville and the 2020 wave, but also the whole backlash, the heritage not hate people, the lawsuits, the state laws passed to block cities from taking statues down. Analyze it through Nietzsche's three modes of history from the Uses and Disadvantages essay. The tempting mapping is: defenders are doing antiquarian history, removalists are doing critical history, done. But I think it's messier -- the statues themselves were mostly erected decades after the war, which looks more like monumental history (a usable great past, manufactured on purpose) than antiquarian reverence, and the heritage side now treats those monuments as antiquarian objects. So work through: which mode each side is actually practicing, whether the removalists' critical history is the life-serving kind Nietzsche wanted (he was pretty clear critical history is dangerous and needs to be dosed), and whether monumental history shows up anywhere on the pro-removal side too. Then turn it around: Nietzsche wrote as if there's one community with one life that history serves or sickens. The monument fight is precisely two groups with incompatible pasts sharing one polity. Does the framework survive that, or does it just assume away the hard part?
