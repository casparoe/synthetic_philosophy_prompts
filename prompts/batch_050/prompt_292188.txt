I'm a clinical nurse specialist who's been handed the job of rewriting our hospital's pain-assessment protocol, and I've read enough philosophy of pain (and enough rationalist-adjacent arguments about hedonic tone and the experience machine) to suspect our current protocol rests on an assumption the literature doesn't support: that pain is bad simply because of how the sensation feels, full stop. The protocol gets applied dozens of times per shift, mostly in under five minutes at the bedside, with treatment decisions countersigned by an attending, and the cases that break the current version are these: patients on morphine who report the pain is unchanged but no longer bothers them; the rare asymbolia patient who can localize and describe a pain while sincerely denying that it's unpleasant; and patients — athletes, the masochistic, the stoically religious — who appear to welcome or value a pain they'd still call pain. Take the best going accounts of what makes pain bad (hedonic-tone views, motivational views, preference-based views), translate them into a single ordered bedside procedure — a sequence of questions and checks that outputs one of three recommendations: treat aggressively, treat at the patient's option, monitor only — while preserving only the distinctions that actually change what a nurse should do and cutting the rest. The procedure must say what to do when the patient can't self-report (advanced dementia with distress behaviors), must flag which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, must give worked applications to the three cases above including at least one whose output the ward's instincts will resist, and must state plainly what no such procedure can settle.
