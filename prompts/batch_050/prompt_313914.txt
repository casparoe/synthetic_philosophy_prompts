Last night a friend and I argued about the metaphysics of time until well past two in the morning, and neither of us conceded an inch. She is a committed presentist: only what exists now is real, the past is gone without remainder, and the future is nothing yet. I kept pressing the two objections that always seem to come up. First, the relativity of simultaneity: whether two distant events happen "at the same time" depends on one's frame of reference, so there is no observer-independent now for her present to be, and a presentism that takes the physics seriously seems to make reality itself frame-relative. Second, the truthmaker problem: if the past does not exist in any sense whatsoever, what makes it true that the Battle of Hastings was fought in 1066? Her reply was the standard eternalist one -- all times exist equally, and "now" functions like "here," picking out a location rather than marking a metaphysical spotlight -- but she never satisfied me on why time should seem to flow if every moment simply sits there in the block, tenselessly.

As a peace offering I would like to send her a poem that gives both sides their due rather than declaring a winner, and I want the form itself to carry part of the argument. Write a poem of exactly five quatrains, with no commentary before or after. Give the first stanza to her presentist conviction, the second to the simultaneity objection, the third to the eternalist's "now is like here" reply, and the fourth to the truthmaker worry pressing back the other way; the fifth should leave the dispute genuinely open, ideally by returning to the two of us at the kitchen table rather than to the abstractions. For the structural work: let the rhymes reach across stanzas instead of closing neatly within them -- a word in the first quatrain finding its rhyme only in the fourth, for instance -- so that temporally distant lines turn out to depend on one another the way the eternalist says distant times do, even as the reader can only move through the poem line by line, the way the presentist insists we live. It should be accurate enough that she recognizes her own position in it and cannot accuse me of caricature, and a good enough poem that she forgives me for the argument.
