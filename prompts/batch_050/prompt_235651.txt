So me and a couple guys I work with got into this argument at lunch about when it's ok for countries to bomb other countries to stop a massacre, and it made me realize I have a bunch of opinions I've never checked against each other. I've read a bunch about Kosovo, Iraq, and Libya over the years (not in a class or anything, just wikipedia rabbit holes and the news at the time), and here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Kosovo in 1999 was the right call even without UN approval. Milosevic was ethnically cleansing Albanians, and waiting on the Security Council just meant doing nothing forever because Russia was always going to veto. Sometimes you have to act and worry about the lawyers later.

(2) Iraq 2003 was wrong, and one of the main reasons it was wrong is that they went around the UN. No authorization, cooked-up WMD stuff, no imminent massacre to stop. The whole point of having a Security Council is that one country doesn't get to decide on its own when war is justified.

(3) Libya 2011 was the right call at the time. Gaddafi was about to roll into Benghazi and had basically promised a massacre. And here's the thing -- I think you judge the decision by what was knowable when it was made, not by the chaos that came after. The aftermath being a disaster doesn't retroactively make intervening wrong.

(4) I hate how selective all of this is. If the principle is "stop massacres," then the principle should apply in Syria and Yemen and Myanmar too, and the fact that it doesn't makes the whole thing look like great powers doing what they want with a humanitarian sticker on it.

(5) The goal should be protecting civilians, full stop. Regime change is mission creep -- once you're trying to topple the government you've turned a rescue mission into a war for control, and that's how you get Iraq.

Does this actually hang together? I have a nagging feeling (1) and (2) are in more tension than I want to admit, and maybe (5) doesn't survive contact with (1) and (3) either -- like can you even protect civilians in a civil war without effectively picking the winner? But (4) might be fine, maybe treating cases differently is just called having good judgment. Tell me where the real cracks are, in order of how bad they are, and which commitment I'd cheapest be able to drop or soften. I genuinely don't know which one should go, that's why I'm asking.
