So I was arguing with a couple of friends about whether brain-in-a-vat scenarios are really as devastating as people assume, and someone pointed out that the standard setup assumes the simulation is *cut off* from reality, which is doing a lot of hidden work. That got me thinking about this line from Descartes' Discourse on the Method (Part One), which is about him worrying about overestimating his own achievements, but it seems to generalize:

"After all, it is possible I may be mistaken; and it is but a little copper and glass, perhaps, that I take for gold and diamonds."

Here's the thought experiment I want built, to order. Construct a case where the Descartes quote is *inverted*: the subject takes glass for glass, but the glass is secretly wired to gold. Specifically: a protagonist who is envatted (or relevantly similar -- a full-immersion simulation is fine if you prefer) and whose simulated environment contains one channel that the simulators deliberately keep slaved to the real world -- say, the simulation's weather in one city tracks the actual weather outside, because the machine's owners need accurate real-world data for their own purposes. The protagonist forms the belief that it's raining in that city, via ordinary-looking evidence inside the simulation. The hard constraints: (1) the protagonist has no testimony or special access telling them this channel is guaranteed -- their justification must look exactly like mundane perceptual justification; (2) the slaving must be part of the setup's design, not a lucky accident; (3) no exotic physics, and keep the scenario to a paragraph or two.

Then do the analysis. State the belief, the method, and the relevant nearby worlds, and give the verdict of each of these on whether the protagonist *knows* it's raining: a Nozick-style tracking/sensitivity condition, a safety condition, and simple process reliabilism. I especially want to know whether the three come apart here, and if they do, which nearby worlds each theory is looking at. Then address closure directly: if the protagonist knows it's raining, and knows that if it's raining then this channel is currently functioning, do they know the channel is functioning? And do they know they're not being deceived *about this*? Say which closure principle survives and which one breaks. Finally, do two honest-bookkeeping things: identify the single feature of the case doing the most work (I suspect it's the difference between a guaranteed link and a merely highly reliable one, so tell me what happens to each verdict if the slaving is 99.99% reliable instead of guaranteed), and give the best objection a Cartesian skeptic would make -- i.e., why they might say this isn't real knowledge but just gilded glass.

After the prose, give me a JSON summary with exactly these fields: "scenario" (one-sentence description), "target_belief", "mechanism" (how the channel works), "verdicts" (an object with keys "tracking", "safety", "reliabilism", each "knows" or "does not know" plus one clause of justification), "closure_verdict" (which closure principle survives), "load_bearing_feature", "guarantee_removed" (what changes if the link is merely reliable), and "best_skeptical_objection".
