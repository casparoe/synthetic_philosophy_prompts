I'm on the research ethics committee of a mid-size hospital and our process is a mess. Whenever a protocol lands in a gray area (data use, consent for the cognitively impaired, paying participants large amounts, that kind of thing) we basically argue from gut reactions to the case, then someone cites a principle like "no undue inducement," and we ping-pong until the loudest person wins. What I want is to turn reflective equilibrium into an actual review procedure we can run in a 90-minute meeting: which initial reactions to a case get counted as "considered judgments" and which we throw out as unreliable (and how do we tell, in practice, rather than in theory), what the committee does when a reaction and a principle clash -- i.e., when do we bend the principle and when do we talk ourselves out of the intuition -- and a stopping rule, because wide equilibrium is supposedly never finished but we have to sign something by Friday. Please be honest about where the procedure smuggles in conservatism: if our starting intuitions are just the committee's class background dressed up as judgment, does running the procedure well fix that or launder it? And how do we keep the whole thing from being path-dependent, where the order we happened to discuss cases in decides the outcome. Then run the procedure on two protocols: one paying $9,000 for a week-long sleep study that draws mostly unemployed volunteers, and one using already-collected patient records for AI training without re-consent. Ideally on at least one of them the procedure should land somewhere I'll find uncomfortable, because if it just ratifies my own views I'll suspect it's decorative. Also tell me which steps are real procedure and which are just judgment calls with extra paperwork.
