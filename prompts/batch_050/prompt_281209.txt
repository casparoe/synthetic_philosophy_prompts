State the sea battle argument from chapter 9 of Aristotle's De Interpretatione. What conclusion does the argument threaten to establish? Finally, either raise one objection to Aristotle's solution or sketch one alternative way of blocking the fatalist conclusion, and briefly assess it.

Here's the student's answer, copied verbatim:

The sea battle argument goes like this. Take the statement "there will be a sea battle tomorrow." Either it is true right now or it is false right now. If it is true now, then things are already such that the battle will happen, so it cannot fail to happen -- it happens of necessity. If it is false now, then the battle cannot happen. Either way, whatever happens tomorrow happens of necessity, and the same reasoning applies to every future event. So the threatened conclusion is fatalism: everything that happens was always going to happen, and deliberation is pointless. There would be no reason for the admirals to bother planning anything.

On the standard reading, Aristotle blocks the argument by denying that future contingent statements have a truth value at the present time. "There will be a sea battle tomorrow" is neither true nor false today; it only becomes true or false when the day arrives. So the argument's opening move, that the statement is already true or already false, fails. In other words Aristotle rejects the law of excluded middle for statements about the future: he will not grant that "either there will be a battle tomorrow or there won't be" is true, since which side of that disjunction obtains is not yet settled.

My objection: this solution costs way too much. Logic only operates on sentences that are true or false. If future-tensed statements have no truth value, then no reasoning about the future counts as valid reasoning at all, and we could never be justified in preparing for the battle or stocking the ships. Since we obviously can reason about the future and some predictions are clearly better than others, Aristotle's denial of truth values has to be wrong, and so his way out of fatalism fails.
