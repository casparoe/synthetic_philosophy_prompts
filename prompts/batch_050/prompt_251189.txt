I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character, a cellist in her fifties, suffers a right-hemisphere stroke and thereafter becomes calmly certain that her husband of thirty years has been replaced by an identical impostor. I want to render her interiority honestly rather than decoratively, so I have been reading what I can about the Capgras delusion. A popular science book I picked up -- respectable in tone, written by a science journalist rather than a clinician -- explains what it calls the standard theoretical account of delusion, the two-factor theory, as follows:

'According to the two-factor theory, a delusion like Capgras requires two things to go wrong. The first is an anomalous experience: the neural circuitry that normally lends a familiar face its glow of felt familiarity is disrupted, so the patient recognizes her husband perfectly well -- she can describe him, anticipate his habits -- yet he no longer feels like her husband. Given this evidence of her senses, she reasonably enough forms the hypothesis that this man is a stranger impersonating him. The second factor is a general impairment of the machinery of belief evaluation: the capacity to weigh hypotheses against the evidence and discard bad ones is damaged, which is why she cannot see what is obvious to everyone else, that the far likelier explanation is that her own feelings have gone wrong. That a second factor is required is proven by the existence of patients who suffer the very same experiential deficit after brain injury yet develop no delusion at all. The upshot is that a delusion is a genuine belief, produced and sustained by faulty reasoning, and the patient who says her husband has been replaced believes this in exactly the same sense in which you believe whatever you take yourself to have good evidence for.'

Something in this nags at me -- in particular the leap from a theory of how delusions arise to the flat declaration that they are beliefs like any other, which matters enormously for my character: if she believes it in that sense, she ought to act on it consistently, and I would need to write her fleeing the house rather than, as I have in my draft, leaving the porch light on each night for a husband she maintains has been abducted. Could you go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts accurately represent the two-factor theory, which present contested positions as settled findings, and which, if any, are simply wrong? In particular: does the two-factor theory itself hold that the impostor hypothesis is a reasonable response to the anomalous experience, or has the journalist quietly imported a rival account's gloss? What exactly is the second factor supposed to explain -- the formation of the delusion, its persistence, or both -- and how well specified is it in the actual literature? And does the concluding claim about delusions being genuine beliefs follow from anything in the theory, or is it an independent commitment, one that might sit awkwardly with the well-attested phenomenon of patients who profess the delusion yet fail to act on it? I am less interested in a verdict on the journalist than in knowing what I can safely rely on for the book.
