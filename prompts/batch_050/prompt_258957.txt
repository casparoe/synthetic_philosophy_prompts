So I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a feature about whether dreams "really happen" while we're asleep. I've spent the last few month interviewing two people who basically can't stand each other's position. The first is a card-carrying skeptic in the mold of Norman Malcolm and Dennett: she thinks the whole idea that we have full-blown conscious experiences unfolding during sleep is confused, that what we actually have are dream reports made on waking, and that the "experience" of the dream is somehow constituted at waking, or is a kind of judgment or confabulation we retrospectively impose. The second is a dream researcher who holds the received view: dreams are genuine, temporally extended experiences during sleep, and she keeps pointing me at lucid dreamers signaling with pre-arranged eye movement patterns while the polysomnograph shows REM, external stimuli getting woven into dream content, people carrying out intentions they formed before bed, and so on.

My editor suggested that instead of the usual "he said, she said" format, I get them to run one of those adversarial collaborations and publish the joint statement. Neither of them will actually sit down and do it, so I need you to write the document they should have produced. Concretely: (1) a numbered list of claims both sides would sign — about the fallibility of dream recall, what REM physiology does and doesn't show, what the eye-signal experiments genuinely establish versus what gets over-read into them, and whether memory-on-waking is evidence of an experience or evidence of something else; (2) the exact remaining disagreements, each stated in wording its own side would accept (this matters — the skeptic refuses to be paraphrased as "denying consciousness," and the realist refuses to be paraphrased as "just trusting introspection"), formatted as a short table after the agreed claims; (3) the cruxes — I suspect one is whether a judgment formed at waking can even be about an experience that never occurred, and another is whether the lucid-signaling data can settle anything the two sides actually disagree about — and for each crux, a concrete case or finding both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Finally, if part of their dispute turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why, because I suspect a fair amount of it is.
