So I'm in a medieval philosophy reading group and we just did the sedition question in the Summa Theologiae (II-II, q.42), and I got kind of obsessed with one bit. In the reply to the third objection of article 2, Aquinas writes:

"A tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler"

and then he goes on to say (paraphrasing here) that rising up against such a government isn't properly sedition -- the real seditious one is arguably the tyrant himself -- EXCEPT if the rebellion is so disorderly that the people end up suffering more from the chaos of the revolt than they did under the tyrant. That caveat is doing so much work and nobody in my group could agree on how a person in the moment is supposed to weigh it.

Write me a short story, maybe 1200-1800 words, set in a fictional Italian commune around the 1290s. The protagonist is a young Dominican friar, recently assigned to the city, who hears confessions in the back of a half-finished church. Over the course of a few weeks he figures out, through things said in and around the confessional (be at least a little careful with the seal of confession -- he can't just act on what he hears, and that constraint should be part of the tension), that three seperate factions are each plotting against the podestà, who is genuinely tyrannical: seizing grain, imprisoning the guild heads, taxing the convents. The problem is the plots would each unleash something worse -- one would hand the city to a neighboring lord, one would torch the grain warehouses, one is mostly a personal revenge scheme dressed up as justice. Meanwhile an old notary in the city is doing something much less dramatic: slowly documenting the podestà's crimes to make a legal case for his removal.

The friar has the passage above basically memorized and keeps turning it over. The story should dramatize the distinction Aquinas is drawing -- between unjust rule that licenses resistance and the prudential question of whether any given resistance actually serves the common good -- without having anyone give a lecture about it. No anachronistic liberal-democratic heroics; the characters should think in terms a 13th century person would (common good, justice, sin, legitimacy), not in terms of rights or revolutions. And don't give it a tidy ending where it's obvious the friar did the right thing -- I want to finish it and still be unsure whether his choice passed Aquinas's own test.
