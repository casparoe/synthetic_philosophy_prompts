I'm teaching my grad seminar on fitting-attitude accounts of value next week and I want to nail down what I actually think about the wrong kind of reasons problem, so here are my gut verdicts -- tell me what principle, if any, unifies them.

The demon case: a demon credibly threatens to torture everyone I love unless I genuinely admire him. I say I have a very strong reason to admire him, but he's still not admirable. Same with the saucer of mud: if he'll destroy the world unless I prefer the mud to my daughter's welfare, I have reason to prefer it, but the mud isn't preferable. Toxin puzzle: the billionaire's prize gives me a reason to intend to drink the toxin at midnight, but no reason to actually drink it, and the intention isn't *rational* in the way intentions formed on the merits are. So far so good -- I've got the standard state-given/object-given line.

But here's where I wobble. My student hands in a mediocre essay, and I think the fact that admiring it will encourage her really is some kind of reason to admire it -- and not just a reason to act as if I admire it, or to cultivate admiration as a means. It feels attitude-directed in a way the demon case doesn't, even though structurally both are benefits of having the attitude rather than features of the object. And in the fear case: my fear of a harmless spider is unfitting no matter how much good the fear does me, but my *amusement* at a genuinely funny but cruel joke seems to be made unfitting partly by the cruelty, which is a fact about the joke -- so apparently "features of the object" isn't quite the right boundary either.

Is there one principle that delivers all these verdicts, or do I have to give something up? If the demon and mud cases show that reasons to have an attitude can float free of the attitude's fittingness, does that sink the whole buck-passing project of analyzing value in terms of reasons, or can it be patched? And what should your account say about a case I haven't listed: a donor offers ten million to charity if I can feel genuinely proud of an achievement I know I didn't accomplish.
