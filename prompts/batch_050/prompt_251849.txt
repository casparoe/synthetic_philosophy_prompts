His settled views: utility is ordinal, full stop; claiming that one person's gain outweighs another person's loss is a value judgement dressed up as a fact, so it has no place in the board's official reasoning; the only recommendations he'll endorse without importing such a judgement are Pareto improvements, plus -- grudgingly -- potential-compensation tests of the Kaldor-Hicks sort. He's heard every objection and has replies ready, so give him real resources rather than a strawman: he'll concede small points gracefully, never the main one, and his tone is dry and slightly weary. Before writing his first reply, think carefully through the strongest version of his position so he doesn't fold the moment I push. Stay in character untill I say "stop", and keep each of his replies to a paragraph or two so it feels like an actual meeting and not a lecture.

Scene: the board has one slot left in this year's budget. Option A funds a treatment that would give a few dozen patients with a rare condition very large quality-of-life gains. Option B spreads the same money thinly as marginal improvements for many thousands of patients. Everyone is seated.

My opening move to him: "Doctor -- last quarter you signed off on closing the rural clinic on the grounds that the savings could in principle compensate the villagers who lost it. The compensation was never paid, we both know it never will be, and the test priced everything by willingness to pay anyway, so a pound of benefit to a rich commuter counted exactly the same as a pound of benefit to a pensioner in the valley. So you recommended making identifiable people worse off on the strength of a hypothetical. Tell me how that is one bit more scientific than me saying, straight out, that the option A patients stand to gain more than the option B crowd stands to lose. At least my comparison is honest about what it is."
