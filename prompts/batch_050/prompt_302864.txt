Write a formal essay, in British English, of roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words, on whether the relation "all-things-considered better than" is transitive.

The essay should proceed by way of the following constructed case, which I call the rotating-dimensions spectrum. Imagine a sequence of lives, L1 through L100, each differing from its neighbour only slightly, but with the differences rotating across dimensions of well-being: the step from L1 to L2 trades a small amount of physical comfort for a modest gain in meaningful achievement; the step from L2 to L3 trades a little of that achievement for deeper personal relationships; the step from L3 to L4 trades a little relational depth for greater autonomy; and so on, cycling through the dimensions. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that on each pairwise comparison the later life strikes us, on reflection, as better overall than the earlier one — yet when L100 is compared directly with L1, L1 is manifestly superior, since it matches or exceeds L100 on every dimension by a wide margin. The chain of verdicts and the endpoint verdict cannot all be correct if betterness is transitive.

Your essay should do the following. First, set out why the transitivity of "better than" matters: explain, with reference to money-pump-style reasoning and to the possibility of justified choice, what would be lost if a genuine case of intransitivity could be produced, and distinguish the normative question (ought betterness to be transitive?) from the descriptive one (do our actual judgements violate transitivity?). Second, relate the rotating-dimensions spectrum to the familiar spectrum arguments in the literature — those associated with Stuart Rachels and Larry Temkin, and the related mere-addition reasoning in population ethics — and say what, if anything, is added by having the marginal differences rotate across dimensions rather than diminish along a single one, as in the standard small-improvement arguments. Third, assess at least three possible escapes: (a) denying some pairwise premise, on the grounds that our pairwise verdicts in such chains are unreliable or indeterminate; (b) treating "better than" as vague, so that some adjacent pairs are neither clearly better nor clearly not better; and (c) invoking Ruth Chang's suggestion that some items are "on a par", a fourth comparative relation that would let the pairwise judgements stand without generating a strict-betterness cycle. For each escape, say precisely which step of the spectrum it blocks and at what cost.

Finally, reach and defend a verdict. Either exhibit how a consistent rotating-dimensions spectrum could be constructed — in which case you should embrace intransitivity and explain how rational choice survives it — or show that the construction must fail at some specifiable point, identifying which comparison gives way and why. A hedged conclusion of the form "it depends on the case" is permitted only if you state the general criterion that determines when transitivity holds and when it fails.
