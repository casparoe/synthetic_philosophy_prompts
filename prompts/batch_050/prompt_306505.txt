I cover animal behavior for a general-audience science magazine, which means half my inbox is press releases about dogs feeling guilt, octopuses holding grudges, and parrots mourning their dead. My editor and I have the same fight about once a month over how much of this is anthropomorphism, and Morgan's canon has become a running joke in our Slack channel -- explain the behavior with the lowest psychological faculty that can do the job, don't reach for a fancier one. The funny thing is the canon is subtler than the pop version of it: Morgan wasn't claiming animals are mindless automatons, he was making a parsimony point about how to interpret a given piece of behavior, and he allowed that lower-faculty explanations have to actually fit. Which is exactly what makes it fun to abuse.

So write me the internal memo, around 550-650 words, issued by the newly created Office of Canonical Restraint inside a university communications department, announcing that all press releases about animal cognition studies must now pass canonical review before they go out. Deadpan bureaucratic voice the whole way through, no winking at the reader. Include: the office's mandate, stated with total sincerity and a hint of mission creep; three or four before/after examples of press-release copy they've revised (something like "capuchin monkeys display a sense of fairness" getting downgraded to copy about differential response rates to unequally distributed cucumber); a short FAQ section for frustrated researchers ("Can I say my crows plan ahead?" "You may say your crows behave in ways consistent with planning-ahead-adjacent hypotheses"); and a closing paragraph where the office, applying its own standard consistently, quietly starts revising press releases about human behavior too -- "dean reportedly furious about budget cuts" becomes a purely behavioral description -- without anyone in the memo noticing that something has gone wrong.

The jokes have to come from getting the canon right rather than from a strawman of it: it's a principle about not invoking a higher faculty when a lower one suffices to explain *this* behavior, not a blanket instruction to assume animals are stupid, and the comedy should live in the gap between those two readings, with the office perpetually overshooting and yet occasionally, by sheer accident, producing copy more careful than what the scientists wrote. At least one of the revisions should be genuinely defensible on the merits, so that a comparative psychologist reading the piece laughs and also winces a little.
