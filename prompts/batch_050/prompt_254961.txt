Draft the joint statement that a mathematical platonist and a mathematical fictionalist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on Benacerraf's dilemma. Take the dilemma in its familiar form: the most natural reading of mathematical language treats a sentence like "there is a prime number between 10 and 20" as quantifying over abstract objects, while a plausible constraint on knowledge holds that knowing about a class of objects requires standing in some appropriate causal or explanatory relation to them, and abstract objects, by stipulation, stand in no such relations to anyone. The platonist resolves the tension by holding that mathematics is literally true of a mind-independent realm and that we have genuine knowledge of it, so the epistemological constraint must be qualified or abandoned; the fictionalist resolves it by holding that mathematics is not literally true, since the objects do not exist, but is a legitimate and indispensable fiction, so what calls for explanation is utility rather than truth.

The document should contain three things, in this order. First, the claims both parties can sign. These should cover what the dilemma is and is not (it is not, for instance, an argument that mathematics is inconsistent), what both accept about the actual practice of mathematicians and its success, and which crude versions of their own positions each side concedes to be untenable: the platonist should concede that a straightforward causal-contact theory of knowledge cannot be the whole story about mathematical knowledge, and the fictionalist should concede that declaring "2 + 2 = 4" to be, strictly speaking, false carries a real burden that cannot be waved away with a shrug about fiction. Second, the precise statements of where they still disagree, each formulated in wording that the side it describes would itself accept, so that neither party is made to defend a caricature. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. Candidates you should consider include whether knowledge of necessary truths requires the kind of access that causal or reliabilist theories demand, whether the applicability of mathematics to the physical world can be fully explained without supposing mathematical truth, and whether the mathematician's ordinary use of "there are infinitely many primes" is ontologically committing in the way the metaphysician's reading assumes. You are not bound to this list; add or replace cruxes if you find better ones.

For each crux, the statement must record, in advance, a case or argument that both parties accept as bearing on it, together with each side's prediction about what the case shows. The statement must in any event address the following two stipulated hypotheticals. First: suppose anthropologists discover a culture whose mathematicians prove theorems structurally identical to ours and whose engineers build bridges using the same calculations, but who explicitly and consistently describe their mathematical posits as knowingly invented characters, on a par with figures in a novel, and who express polite puzzlement at the suggestion that anything further is needed; their physics is exactly as successful as ours. The fictionalist should predict that this case shows literal truth to be idle for mathematical practice; the platonist should state whether it is irrelevant because the culture's discourse concerns the same objects whatever its members say, or damaging, and why. Second: suppose the best explanation of why mathematicians converge on answers in arithmetic appeals only to facts about proof procedures, notation, and training, with mathematical facts themselves never appearing in the explanation of the beliefs. Each side should record whether it takes this to be a serious problem for platonism, or instead something both views should have expected all along given that mathematical facts are causally inert on anyone's account.

Finally, if part of the traditional dispute strikes the collaborators as merely verbal rather than substantive, the statement should say which part and why, rather than papering over it.

Write the statement as continuous prose throughout, the way one would write a formal letter or memorandum: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables. Where the agreed claims and the recorded predictions would naturally be numbered or tabulated, work the numbering into the prose itself.
