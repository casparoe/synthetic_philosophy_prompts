I TA for an intro ethics course and this week's reading is Garrett Hardin's "Lifeboat Ethics: the Case Against Helping the Poor" (Psychology Today, September 1974). One passage keeps nagging at me. Hardin has been arguing that the lifeboat passengers who refuse to take on more swimmers are doing the only survivable thing, and then he writes:

"While this last solution clearly offers the only means of our survival, it is morally abhorrent to many people. Some say they feel guilty about their good luck. My reply is simple: 'Get out and yield your place to others.' This may solve the problem of the guilt-ridden person's conscience, but it does not change the ethics of the lifeboat. The needy person to whom the guilt-ridden person yields his place will not himself feel guilty about his good luck. If he did, he would not climb aboard. The net result of conscience-stricken people giving up their unjustly held seats is the elimination of that sort of conscience from the lifeboat."

Students are definately going to push on this and I want my own view straight first. Here are my gut verdicts on cases:

1. Four men adrift in a dinghy after their yacht sinks, no water for days, no rescue in sight. Three of them kill the weakest one (half-conscious anyway) and survive off his body until they're picked up. All four would have died otherwise. My verdict: still wrong.

2. Same dinghy, but the four agree to draw straws first and the loser is killed. This feels clearly better than 1, maybe even okay, but I can't say why consent plus a fair lottery should change things that much if killing him is wrong in case 1.

3. A lifeboat built for ten has eight aboard and thirty people swimming around it. Taking everyone swamps it. Taking two more and rowing away from the rest: grim but right, maybe even required.

4. Same boat, but the two empty seats are the buoyancy margin you'll need when the storm hits tonight. Refusing to take the last two swimmers: also fine.

5. One passenger brought a big cooler of water and rations it for himself and his kid while the passenger next to them dies of thirst. Not fine. He has to share, even though the cooler was his when he got in.

6. A rich country refuses famine aid because its analysts say aid just gets absorbed by population growth, so there are as many hungry people in twenty years either way: not fine, send the aid. But the same country capping immigration to protect its safety net: fine, I think. I realise 3 and 4 say exclusion at capacity is fine while 6 says refusing aid isn't, and Hardin's whole essay is that these are the same picture at different scales. So something in my gut has to give.

My tentative principle: once you're in the boat, supplies get shared by need and nobody gets used as a supply; toward the water, you owe only what you can give without sinking. Questions: (a) Is there a cleaner principle that captures these verdicts, stated tightly enough that I can see which case motivates each clause? (b) If nothing gets them all, which verdict should I drop? (c) What does the best principle say about a case I haven't listed — say a billionaire who funds a private rescue ship in the Mediterranean: is he obligated to keep sailing until the money runs out? (d) Does the Hardin passage above actually work? It feels obviously true and like it's getting away with something at the same time. Doesn't the same selection story apply to my sharing clause — boats full of sharers get swamped, boats full of hoarders persist — and if that doesn't refute sharing, why does it refute yielding your seat?

One more thing: please keep seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this stuff, both on the ethics side and especially on the empirical claims about aid and population growth. Don't hand me a contested view dressed up as the consensus. "Nobody really knows" is a fine answer wherever it's the true one.
