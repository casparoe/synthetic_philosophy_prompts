I've spent years reading the alignment literature on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum, and I've noticed that the debate over corrigibility and the shutdown problem tends to recycle the same three or four moves on both sides. Roughly, one camp holds that corrigibility is a natural and achievable property: that with the right training regime, oversight setup, or objective design, we can build systems that genuinely let themselves be corrected, modified, or shut down, without this requiring any deep solution to value alignment. The opposing camp holds that corrigibility is unstable and anti-natural: that any system agentic enough to be useful will develop instrumental incentives to resist shutdown or manipulation of its goals, that apparent corrigibility in training is likely to be either superficial compliance or a wrapper around some unconrectable inner objective, and that the proposed fixes mostly relocate the problem rather than solve it.

I'd like you to write the joint statement these two camps should have produced by now if they were running an actual adversarial collaboration instead of arguing past each other in comment threads. Concretely, the document should contain:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides would sign. These should be substantive where possible (e.g., about what current systems do and don't demonstrate, about which thought experiments are doing real work and which are intuition pumps, about what "successful shutdown" would even look like behaviorally), not anodyne agreements that AI safety matters.

2. A table of the remaining disagreements, each phrased in language that both camps would accept as a fair statement of their own position. Please take care to separate disagreements that are genuinely about the world from disagreements that are about definitions — for instance, I suspect some of the apparent dispute dissolves once both sides pin down whether corrigibility is a property of the training process, the resulting policy, or the system's behavior under distribution shift.

3. The cruxes: the specific sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. For each crux, describe a concrete experiment, observation, or future development that both sides agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts. For example, if one crux concerns whether corrigible behavior generalizes out of distribution, say what each side expects to see as models become more capable of long-horizon planning, and what result would force an update.

Where the two sides would use the same word differently (agent, want, shutdown, incentive), flag it explicitly rather than letting the ambiguity do hidden work. The goal is a document both camps could actually sign, not a steelman tournament and not a verdict on who is right.
