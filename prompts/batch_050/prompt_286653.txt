I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am drafting a column on what the collapse of photographic and video evidence will do to public knowledge. The standard line in this debate is pessimistic: once any recording can be faked, we lose the epistemic backstop — the fallback assurance that, whatever testimony and institutions fail us, the footage will settle what happened. For the column, I want to stress-test that line rather than repeat it, and I need your help building the centerpiece.

Construct a thought experiment in which the widespread availability of near-perfect deepfakes leaves a community *better* informed about recorded events than it was before — that is, a case where losing the epistemic backstop is a net epistemic gain, the opposite of the usual verdict. Constraints on the scenario:

1. Set it in one recognizable institution or practice — a courtroom, a local news ecosystem, a police accountability process, or similar — not in a speculative future society.
2. Use only technology that exists or is plainly imminent; do not invent new verification tools, provenance infrastructure, or detection breakthroughs to do the work.
3. The epistemic gain must fall out of an incentive or attention shift that the loss of the backstop itself causes. It must not come from stipulating that people in the story simply become more careful or more virtuous by fiat.
4. Keep the scenario to one paragraph.

After the scenario, do the analytical work:

- State what the epistemic backstop consisted in before deepfakes arrived in your scenario, and identify precisely which mechanism — for instance, what is sometimes called the liar's dividend, or the collapse of naive trust in recordings — removes it.
- Show, step by step, why the community's overall epistemic position about the relevant class of events improves on net, naming the specific epistemic good that is gained (e.g., better calibrated trust, more weight on corroborating testimony, less deference to a single clip) and the specific good that is lost.
- Identify which feature of the case is doing the work, and say what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed — does the scenario collapse back into the standard pessimistic story?
- Give the strongest objection a defender of the pessimistic line would raise against the case, and assess whether it succeeds.

Finally, since I will be printing a version of this: tell me what would change your mind. Which empirical premise or philosophical assumption in your analysis is most load-bearing, which consideration, if it turned out differently, would flip the net verdict back to pessimism, and where do you think your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong?
