So I had this stupidly long argument with my flatmate last night (like 3 hours, we missed the film) and I want to know if the thing we kept circling around actually has a name, or if we were just inventing a concept badly.

Background: there's a street in our city named after some 19th century figure who did genuinely awful stuff. We looked him up, it's not a close call, no "he was a man of his time" defense really works. My flatmate wants the name changed. And I found myself defending a position I couldn't articulate properly: basically, nobody who lives there thinks of the guy at all anymore. When people say the street name they mean the bakery on the corner, their flat, the bus stop. The name has somehow detached from the person and reattached itself to the place and the people. So renaming it now wouldn't punish the dead guy at all, it would just annoy a load of people who never endorsed him in the first place.

But here's the actual phenomenon I'm asking about, because it's more general than our street. The commemorative thing (name, statue, building, whatever) seems to hold several meanings at once, in layers, and the layers don't compete, they just coexist:

- what it was put up to say (the original, often propagandistic meaning),
- what it now means to the people who live with it daily (often just "home", or "the place where I meet people"),
- what a stranger encountering it cold would assume the community is saying by leaving it up.

And fights about removal consist almost entirely of people picking different layers and then talking past each other, each convinced the other is being obtuse.

Second instance, same structure: there's a statue outside my old uni of an industrialist, erected basically as PR by the man himself. Today it's the default meeting point, students put traffic cones on its head every freshers week, and I'd bet half the undergrads don't know it depicts a real person. Intended meaning: dead. Everyday meaning: totally different, almost affectionate. Outsider meaning (if you stop and read the plaque): still the original one, which is frankly embarrassing.

So: is there an established name for this? I don't think it's "reappropriation", because that implies somebody deliberately took the thing over, and here nobody decided anything, the new meaning just accreted over decades. It's not a counter-monument either, because nobody altered anything physical. And it's not simple forgetting, because the original meaning is still right there on the plaque and snaps back into focus the moment anyone bothers to look. If there's no name for it, what would you call it, and how would you define its edges — for instance, does it require that the original meaning still be retrievable? Would a statue whose plaque has worn smooth count, or is that just forgetting?

And then the question we actually cared about, which I suspect the concept is supposed to help with: when you're deciding whether to keep or remove something, which layer gets a vote? The dead founder's, the locals', the stranger's? My worry is that the drifted everyday meaning gets used as a convenient excuse to keep things that should come down — but also that sometimes it's completely genuine, and flattening a name people grew up on really does destroy something real. My flatmate thinks the stranger's reading is the only one that matters because that's the public meaning. That seems too quick to me but I couldn't say exactly why. Curious what you think on both the naming question and the normative one.
