I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I also run a two-week ethics bootcamp we make our new reporters and podcast producers go through before they're allowed near a source. Every year the thing that blows up isn't plagiarism or fabrication, it's consent. Somebody gets a grieving parent on the phone an hour after a factory accident and records them, somebody tells a whistleblower the piece is "about workplace culture" when it's really an exposé of their boss, somebody interviews a source who is clearly three drinks in, somebody talks to a teenager with mom in the room nodding along and it's ambiguous who's actually agreeing. I've been reading around the philosophy of consent -- the stuff about what makes consent valid or void, deception vs. coercion vs. competence -- and I want to turn it into an actual decision procedure my reporters can run on any source interaction, in the field, in about five minutes, before they hit record.

So: write the procedure as a sequence of tests in order, where each test has a clear pass condition and a clear action if it fails (rebook, bring in an advocate, get consent on tape at the end not the start, kill the material, escalate to me). It needs to cover the three classic failure modes: the source doesn't understand what they're consenting to (because we misdescribed the story, or because of language or cognitive load), the "yes" wasn't free (boss in the room, PR handler, power imbalance, someone who thinks cooperating gets them something), and the person isn't in a state to consent at all (drunk, in shock, a minor, early dementia). For each test, say what the philosophical work is doing underneath in plain language so my reporters get why it's there, not just that it's a rule. Be honest about where the procedure has to stop and hand it to a human judgment call -- I suspect deception-by-omission is one of those, since no journalist describes the final piece fully up front and the whole industry runs on some version of that.

Then run the procedure on three cases from my actual notebook: (1) the intoxicated source who gives us the single best quote of the story, lucid-sounding on tape, and calls the next morning saying he doesn't remember the call; (2) a profile subject who consented to everything but is sixteen and her answers are clearly being steered by her dad who's also her manager; (3) a researcher who agreed to an interview about "public understanding of statistics" that I've now realized will run as a piece about scientific fraud in her field. I'd guess the procedure kills the quote in (1) and I want to see it happen -- tell me if I'm wrong. Oh and keep it short enough to fit on an index card in its final form, the long version is for the training doc.
