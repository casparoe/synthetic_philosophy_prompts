Read Jorge Luis Borges's story Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius through Berkeley's idealism, with the master argument at the center. The story's invented world has languages without nouns, treats objects as momentary bundles of perceptions, lets things fade once they are no longer attended to, and generates new objects (the hrönir) when people go looking for them; its metaphysicians even stage the parable of the nine coins to argue that a single persisting object is an illusion. Map all this onto Berkeley: where is Tlön simply esse est percipi systematized, where does the nine-coins parable dramatize the denial of material substratum more vividly than Berkeley argues it, and where does the story depart from him in ways that matter philosophically — above all, that Tlön has no God, no continuous perceiver guaranteeing the world's stability, only an encyclopedia composed by a secret society? Then turn to the master argument proper: Berkeley challenges us to conceive an object existing unconceived, and his critics reply that he confuses the act of thinking with what is thought about. The story literalizes the argument — conceiving Tlön in sufficient detail makes it real, until it begins to displace actual history. Does this dramatization vindicate Berkeley by taking his premise seriously, or does it expose the equivocation by showing exactly what else would have to be true for the inference to go through? End by saying what the story adds to the debate that the arguments alone do not — for instance, whether its real suggestion is that idealism is less a metaphysics than a technology, a way of remaking a world too compliant to resist.
