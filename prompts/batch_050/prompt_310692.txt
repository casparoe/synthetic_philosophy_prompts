I’m a science-and-ideas journalist preparing a talk for a festival audience that is unusually mixed: a couple of Bayesian philosophers, several astrophysicists who work on SETI, some professional forecasters, and a longtermist crowd that cares about existential risk. My thesis is that the doomsday argument shouldn’t be treated as a probabilistic expiry date for humanity. It’s better understood as a consistency test for our choice of sampling frame.

My rough argument is this. Saying “I appear to be unusually early among all humans” sounds like neutral evidence only after we’ve decided what counts as the relevant population: births, person-years, conscious observer-moments, civilizations, or technologically visible civilizations. Those choices can produce wildly different conclusions, and none is obviously the natural one. So the argument doesn’t show doom is likely unless we can justify the sampling frame without already assuming something about how long civilizations last. I also want to argue that longtermists sometimes smuggle population ethics into what gets presented as a purely empirical or Bayesian inference, because deciding whose possible observations count as data is not ethically neutral.
