So I got kind of obsessed with these two old thought experiments, the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery, which I believe Douglas Hofstadter wrote about in his Scientific American column back in the 80s. Quick versions in case the names are fuzzy:

Platonia dilemma: 20 people are invited. Each one may send in at most one entry. If exactly one entry total gets sent, that person wins a huge prize. If zero or more than one entries come in, nobody wins anything. No communicating with each other.

Luring Lottery: anyone can enter, and you can submit as many entries as you want. One entry is drawn at random and its sender wins. But the catch is the prize is a million dollars divided by the total number of entries submitted. So more entries means better odds for you personally but a smaller prize for whoever wins.

Hofstadter's idea of "superrationality" was roughly: everyone's in the same symmetric boat and presumably reasons the same way, so you should assume others reach the same conclusion you do. In Platonia that supposedly means each person enters with probability 1/20 (like rolling a 20-sided die), which gives a decent chance that exactly one person enters and wins.

Here's where I keep going in circles. Isn't this just smuggling in the assumption that other people will mirror your reasoning, which you have zero actual evidence for? It feels like the trick is "I get to decide what the correlated outcome is," but really you only control your own entry. At the same time, the plain game-theory answer also feels weirdly defeatist, because real humans do pull off coordination-ish things all the time.

So my questions: (1) Is superrationality a genuinely different kind of reasoning, or is it just normal expected-value reasoning plus an empirical bet that other people think like you? What actually changes in your decision procedure? (2) In the Luring Lottery, what would superrational reasoning even tell you to do -- enter once? Not at all? Flood it with entries? It seems much murkier than Platonia. (3) Here's a variation I thought of: what if the 20 players in Platonia are 20 copies of the same AI model given the same prompt? Does superrationality become obviously right there, or does it fall apart because exact copies might all make the same "random" choice and ruin everything?

Please explain at a gut level -- no heavy math. I'm not an academic, just someone who's been stuck on this for weeks.
