I have no academic training, but for the last several years I have been the unofficial record-keeper for a small family business, and in that time I have built and maintained a modest database: customer orders, supplier invoices, employee hours, and a log of every correction anyone has ever made to a record. Keeping that system has made me think a great deal about something I have since learned is a serious topic among historians and archivists: the idea that archives are not neutral stores of the past, but that power decides what gets recorded in the first place, and that the silences in our sources are produced by decisions rather than being simple accidents of survival. I have been turning this over on my own, and I suspect my database work has been quietly teaching me the same lesson, but I cannot tell how far the parallel really goes.

Here is what I would like you to do. Take the core claims of this archival way of thinking -- that someone always chooses what enters the record and what is discarded; that these choices reflect the interests of whoever holds authority; that entire groups of people can be missing from the historical record not because they did nothing but because no one in power found them worth documenting; and that a later reader can easily mistake the surviving record for the whole of what happened -- and restate them, as precisely and honestly as you can, in the vocabulary I actually work in: table schemas and the fields someone decided to create, required versus optional fields, default values, retention and deletion policies, who holds write-access versus read-access, audit logs, soft deletes, NULL entries, and the difference between data that was never collected and data that was collected and then purged. I am fluent in this vocabulary; I use it every working day.

After the translation, give me a residue report. Which of the archivists' claims map cleanly onto something a database designer already understands, and which survive only in a distorted form? Is there anything in the archival critique that simply has no counterpart in my vocabulary -- I have wondered, for instance, whether a row that was never inserted is really the same kind of absence as a person who was never written about, or whether the database analogy quietly strips out something essential about people, coercion, and the fact that the unrecorded sometimes knew they were being left out. And where the translation fails, tell me which side the failure belongs to: is my record-keeper's vocabulary missing a concept it genuinely needs, or does the residue show that the archival theorists are saying something that only applies to states and institutions and not to the humble act of deciding which receipts to keep?
