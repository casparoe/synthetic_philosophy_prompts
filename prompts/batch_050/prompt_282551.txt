I'm a PhD student in political theory (my actual work is on democratic legitimacy, so jurisprudence is a side interest, which will probably be obvious from what follows). For a reading group I tried to compress what I took away from Fuller and Raz into one crisp argument, and now it seems to prove way too much and I can't find the step to cut. Here it is.

(1) The whole point of governing through law, rather than through officials who just decide each case however seems best to them, is that people can find out in advance what is required of them and plan their lives around it.

(2) People can only plan around norms they can actually know in advance — norms that are public, prospective, reasonably clear, stable, and consistent with each other. (This is basically Fuller's list, plus Raz's idea that law has to be capable of guiding behaviour, and that the rule of law just is law's capacity to do that.)

(3) Open-ended standards — reasonable care in negligence, good faith in contract, unconscionability, proportionality in constitutional cases — are not knowable in advance in the relevant sense. You find out what they required of you only when a court tells you afterwards.

(4) So adjudication that runs on such standards doesn't give people advance guidance about what to do.

(5) But whatever doesn't give advance guidance isn't functioning as law on the picture in (1) — it's officials making discretionary calls and dressing them up as law after the fact.

(6) And enormous chunks of common-law systems run on exactly such standards: negligence, contract, equity, most of constitutional adjudication.

(7) So huge parts of what these systems call law aren't law, and to the extent a system leans on them, it lacks the rule of law.

(8) But that's nuts. If anything the English and American systems are the textbook examples of the rule of law, not cautionary tales about its absence.

Where does this go wrong? My supervisor glanced at it, said it equivocates somewhere, and refused to say where, which I found characteristically unhelpful of her. If I have to choose I'd rather keep (8) — I really don't want to conclude that common law countries barely have law — and I'd also like to keep something like (1), since it's the only account I find plausible of what the rule of law is even for.

Two specific things I want from an answer. First, name the weakest step, explain why it looked fine, and tell me what rejecting it costs — what else I'd have to give up, and whether Fuller or Raz has a better escape route than the other. Second, I have a vague memory that Waldron argues courts, and the opportunity to argue your side before an impartial tribunal, are themselves partly constitutive of the rule of law rather than just instruments for applying pre-existing rules. Does that dissolve my argument, or does it just restate the problem? Because if guidance only gets created inside the courtroom, that seems to confirm that it wasn't there in advance — which was the whole issue.
