I'm designing a session for the interdisciplinary reading group I co-run (philosophy, comp sci, and policy grad students, about 14 people, we've got 75 minutes in a seminar room with a whiteboard). We've been reading around the unilateralist's curse -- the idea that when many people each have the individual power to launch some irreversible initiative, and each acts on their own noisy estimate of whether it would be good, the initiative tends to get launched even when it shouldn't, because the person who acts is disproportionately the one who most overestimated it. Everyone in the group gets the math when it's explained but nobody really feels it; they all walk away thinking they'd be the sensible exception, which is itself kind of the phenomenon.

So: act as an experienced facilitator and philosopher who specialises in collective decision problems and has run classroom games like this before. Think carefully, and maybe brainstorm two or three candidate designs before settling on the one that best fits the constraints. What I want is a fully runnable activity where participants experience the curse from the inside *before* anyone names it. Concretely: each person should privately form a noisy estimate of some hidden quantity (I'm thinking something like guessing the net value of a jar's contents, or estimating a statistic from a sample I rig in advance, but use whatever mechanism you think works best), and then decide *individually and unilaterally* whether to "launch" something whose payoff to the whole room depends on the true value. The rules should be set up so that launching is individually rational for anyone with a high estimate, yet the group predictably ends up launching in cases where the true value is negative, and the launcher turns out to be the biggest overestimator -- the winner's curse structure. Small stakes are fine (chocolate, points toward some silly prize, whatever works without real money).

Please give me: full rules and materials, a timeline for the 75 minutes, the exact payoff scheme with numbers I can actually use, and the debrief script mapping what happened onto the structure of the curse (who launched, why, and what the aggregate looks like vs. any individual's reasoning). One thing I specifically want addressed: last semester I tried a crude version where the group just voted on whether to launch, and the vote reliably got it *right*, which accidentally taught the opposite lesson. So explain why your design routes around aggregation mechanisms and forces genuinely unilateral decision points -- and ideally include a round that makes the vote-vs-unilateral contrast visible to the participants themselves. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I suspect people will collude in whispers or start imitating each other's choices), and be honest about which feature of the real-world phenomenon the game can't reproduce -- I'm guessing the genuine irreversibility and the fact that real actors dont know how many other potential launchers are out there.
