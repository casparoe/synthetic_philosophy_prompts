I work as a policy researcher advising on the regulation of military technology, and over the past year I keep running into the same configuration in after-action reports and procurement documents, for which I have no adequate word. Take two instances. In the first, an air-defence system classifies incoming objects and presents a human operator with a recommended engagement; the operator has perhaps fifteen seconds, sees only a curated summary screen, and knows from experience that the system's recommendations have been correct in the overwhelming majority of drills, while operators who veto are required to write a formal justification and are reviewed afterwards. Unsurprisingly, the veto rate is close to zero, yet the operator's presence is cited in every official description as proof that a human being is making the decision. In the second instance, a strike has gone wrong and civilians are dead: the manufacturer's position is that the system performed within its specifications, the commanders' position is that they relied on the judgement of the trained operator, and the after-action report identifies the operator's failure to intervene as the proximate cause — even though, given the time window and the information actually presented, no operator could realistically have done otherwise.

What strikes me is that this is not the familiar worry that autonomous systems produce outcomes for which nobody can be held responsible. Here, responsibility is allocated perfectly efficiently — it is just allocated to the one participant who was structurally least able to exercise it, and the system's design seems to guarantee this in advance: the human is retained in the loop not so much to make better decisions as to provide somewhere for the blame to settle when the decision is bad. Nor does it seem to be merely the psychological tendency of operators to defer to automated recommendations, since the arrangement would produce the same result even with a perfectly vigilant and sceptical operator, because vigilance is penalised in practice. So I want to ask: is there an established term, and a literature, for this specific arrangement — the deliberate or emergent positioning of a nominally supervising human as the designated recipient of liability? If the nearest concepts only fit imperfectly, I would like to know exactly where my description diverges from each of them and which fits best; and if nothing established quite captures it, I would rather have a coined term with a precise definition and some boundary cases than a loose gesture at neighbouring ideas. What I am not looking for is a bare label without the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
