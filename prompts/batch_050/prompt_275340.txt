My history teacher, who also coaches our ethics bowl team, recently told us that philosophers have shown omissions can't really cause anything. His summary went like this: 'A cause has to be an event, something that happens. An omission is a non-event, nothing happening at all, and nothing can't make something happen. So when we say the gardener's failure to water the flowers caused them to die, we're speaking loosely. What actually killed the flowers was the absence of water, which is a background condition, not a cause. This is also why the law generally only punishes omissions when there's a special duty, like a parent's duty to feed a child: the law quietly recognizes that only genuine actions cause outcomes.' Something about the last step bothers me, but I can't articulate why. Which parts of this summary would philosophers who work on causation accept, which parts confuse distinct issues, and does his conclusion about the law actually follow from the parts that are accurate?
