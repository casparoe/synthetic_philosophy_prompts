I'm writing a sci-fi novel where the big twist is that a character who's been treated as a person turns out to be an uploaded mind running on ordinary hardware, and my whole plot hinges on whether that even makes sense. My critique partner keeps saying "but a computer can't be a person, it just manipulates symbols," and I keep saying "well your brain is just squishy hardware," and neither of us can articulate what would settle this. So before I write the big philosophical argument scene (chapter 14, don't ask), I want the spec sheet first: what would a theory of physical computation actually have to deliver before anyone could say "this lump of stuff is a computer running a mind" or "it isn't"?

Give me the requirements list, with the motivating case behind each one. The things I think it needs to cover, but correct me:

- It has to say why my laptop genuinely computes and my coffee mug doesn't, without the answer just being "because we built the laptop to." If the difference is just human intentions, my uploaded character's personhood depends on what the engineers had in mind, which is a stupid plot.
- It has to block the collapse where every physical system turns out to implement every computation, because if a rock running through its thermal wobbles is secretly running my character's mind, then uploading is trivial and there's no drama. I've heard there's some mapping trick where you can read any program off any system if you're permissive enough about what counts as what. What stops that?
- It has to handle the lookup-table problem: a machine that just stores every possible conversation and plays back the right response. Nobody thinks THAT is thinking, but I can't say what principled difference it has from a fast program.
- It has to make sense of analog vs digital -- neurons spike and also do graded analog-ish stuff, so does a theory that only licenses digital computation rule out brains being computers from the start?
- It needs to say whether computation is enough for semantics, or whether you need something extra (embodiment? causal history? being hooked to the world the right way?), because chapter 14's argument basically turns on this.

For each requirement tell me why it's a genuine desideratum and not just my fiction-brain prejudice, flag any pair of requirements that can't both be satisfied, and tell me which single requirement does the most work in killing off the popular positions. Also which requirements I could relax without the whole theory becoming vacuous. A table scoring the main contendors against the list is fine but I mostly care about the criteria themselves, since those are going in the dialogue.
