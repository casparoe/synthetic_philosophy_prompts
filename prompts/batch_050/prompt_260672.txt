I'm a PhD student in cognitive science with a side interest in philosophy of action, and I keep running into the same confident summary of the Libet experiments — most recently in a well-regarded popular neuroscience book our journal club was discussing. The book's version runs roughly as follows: "In the 1980s, Benjamin Libet asked subjects to flex their wrist whenever they felt like it and to note, using a clock, the moment they consciously decided to move. He found that a brain signal called the readiness potential began several hundred milliseconds before subjects reported making their decision — meaning the brain had already decided before the person knew it. Later fMRI studies pushed this further, showing that a subject's choice between two buttons can be read out from brain activity up to ten seconds before the subject is aware of choosing. The conclusion is hard to escape: what we experience as a conscious decision is a post-hoc awareness of something the brain settled unconsciously, so conscious will is an illusion. Libet himself could only salvage a narrow veto — consciousness may block an act already initiated, but it never initiates one. Free will, in the everyday sense of consciously originating our actions, is thus empirically refuted."

My instinct is that this overreaches, but I can't locate exactly where. Some of it matches what I remember from the primary literature — the basic timing result, the later decoding studies, the veto idea — yet the triumphant final sentence seems to lean on moves the experiments themselves don't make. I'd like an accuracy audit I can bring back to my journal club. Which parts of this summary are faithful to what Libet and the follow-up studies actually found? Which parts involve a conflation — for instance, between when an urge or intention is felt and when a decision is "made," or between a brain signal that precedes awareness and a brain signal that constitutes a completed decision, or between predicting a choice statistically above chance and the choice having been settled in advance? And is the book right about Libet's own position — did he take his results to refute free will, or did he draw a different conclusion, and does the book's framing of the veto distort his view?

Finally, suppose the summary is corrected wherever it errs: does anything like its final conclusion still follow from the accurate residue? Specifically, I want to know whether the corrected findings bear on free will understood as conscious initiation of arbitrary, inconsequential movements (the experimental paradigm), and what, if anything, licenses extending that to deliberated, consequential choices — or whether that extension is doing the real argumentative work while passing itself off as an experimental result. Please separate clearly what the experiments show, what is a contested interpretation of them, and what is an extra philosophical premise the popularization has smuggled in.
