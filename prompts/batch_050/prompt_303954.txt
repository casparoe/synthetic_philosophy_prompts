A friend and I got into a dumb argument after a trip abroad and it's still bugging me. We were somewhere with very strict, very particular street etiquette — people formed neat lines at bakery counters, stood on one designated side of the escalator, never crossed against the light even at 2am with no cars in sight — and she insisted that as visitors we were *more* obligated to play along than the locals were, not less. That seems backwards to me (surely if anyone gets a pass, it's the tourists?), but she had some half-formed argument about legibility and norm-maintenance that I couldn't fully reconstruct afterward. So: design a thought experiment that would vindicate her, or at least make her position maximally plausible.

The case has to satisfy all of these constraints at once:

1. The convention is genuinely arbitrary. A different convention would have served the community exactly as well, so there's no independent reason to act that way. This rules out the easy cheats: no driving-side cases, nothing where noncompliance endangers anyone or ruins the convention's whole point at a stroke.
2. The protagonist is a visitor who *reasonably* judges the convention to be pointless, or even slightly worse than what they'd do instead. Their judgment can't rest on a factual mistake — they understand the convention perfectly well, they just think it's a bit silly.
3. The visitor is morally obligated to comply anyway. And the obligation has to be genuinely moral, not prudential: no fines, and don't let fear of glares or social disapproval carry the whole load, since then it's just self-interest wearing a moral costume.
4. A lifelong local performing the exact same act, in the exact same circumstances, would not be obligated (or would be noticeably *less* obligated). The obligation has to track visitor-status itself, not just the convention's general bindingness — this is the part that makes the case hard, and it's the part I most want to see actually pulled off rather than stipulated.

Keep the scenario to one paragraph, realistic, set in a city anyone could plausibly visit. Then walk me through the engineering: show that each constraint is genuinely met and not just asserted, identify the single feature of the case that's doing the work of generating the asymmetry in constraint 4, and tell me what happens if you remove that feature — does the case collapse into "everyone's obligated," or into "no one is"? Finally, steelman the attack: how would someone who thinks visitor status is morally irrelevant to convention-following try to break your case, and does it survive?
