So I got into an argument with a friend who does ancient history about how historians actually decide what to believe, and it made me realize I don't understand the epistemology under the whole enterprise.

The actual practice, as far as I can tell, goes something like this: sources written close in time to the events beat sources written centuries later; two independent accounts beat one; oral tradition that was written down long after the fact gets heavily discounted; and a witness with an axe to grind is treated as unreliable until corroborated by someone with a different axe. Historians will openly say they can't *know* most of what they assert about, say, Archaic Greece, and then assert it anyway with appropriate hedging. Meanwhile Thucydides himself, in Book 1 of the History of the Peloponnesian War (I'm reading the Crawley translation), complains that most people receive traditions "without applying any critical test whatever," and writes off a lot of the poetic and chronicle material about early times by saying that time has "robbed most of them of historical value by enthroning them in the region of legend" -- while in basically the same breath claiming that his own conclusions "may, I believe, safely be relied on" because he proceeded on the clearest data.

Here's my question. What theory of knowledge does this whole practice presuppose? Specifically:

1. The time-distance rule seems to treat testimony like a game of telephone -- each retelling is a lossy copy of the previous one, so proximity to the event is proxy for fidelity. But is that what memory and testimony actually are? If testimony is instead a basic source of knowledge in its own right (the way some epistemologists argue -- you don't reduce it to perception or inference, you just get to believe what people tell you absent defeaters), does the historian's hierarchy of sources lose its justification, or can it survive on other grounds?

2. Thucydides' own method was interrogating eyewitnesses and cross-checking them -- i.e., he trusted *testimony processed by a skilled questioner* more than documents or poems. That seems like a different presupposition than the modern one, where a dusty archive document often beats a living person's recollection. Which view of testimony is each practice committed to, and is either of them actually consistent? Note the weird tension in Thucydides: he says time *destroys* historical value, but his whole project depends on careful method being able to defeat that decay. Is that a contradiction or is there a coherent picture where decay is defeasible?

3. The independence rule (two sources only count as two if they're genuinely independent) presupposes something about how confirmation works -- that agreement between correlated sources is nearly worthless. But historians apply this patchily: they treat ancient authors who clearly copied each other as one source, yet treat modern archaeologists all reading the same site reports as independent. Is the practice philosophically underdetermined here -- i.e., would several rival epistemologies all license the same habits, so the practice doesn't really "choose" between them?

Where I'm stuck: I can't tell whether source criticism rests on one coherent implicit theory that could be stated and defended, or whether it's a pile of heuristics each of which presupposes something different, some of them mutually inconsistent. If the latter, does that matter for how much we should trust its outputs? And what would the discipline have to change -- concretely, in how it treats stuff like the traditions behind Homer -- if it swapped the telephone-game model of testimony for a default-trust one?
