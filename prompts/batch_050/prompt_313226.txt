I'm taking an upper-level seminar on collective action and individual ethics, and for my final paper I need to pin down what I actually think about the inefficacy objection. Rather than start from a theory, I'd like to work from my case intuitions and see what principle, if any, they support. Here are my verdicts:

1. Skipping my vote in a national election with millions of voters, purely out of laziness, seems wrong to me, even though I accept that my single vote has essentially no chance of being decisive.

2. Buying one factory-farmed chicken at the supermarket seems wrong to me, even though I've read that retailers restock in thresholds, so one purchase more or less almost never changes how many chickens are ordered and killed.

3. Taking one unnecessary long-haul flight for a vacation strikes me as, at worst, trivially bad -- barely blameworthy -- since the marginal emissions from my seat are a rounding error against global totals. I don't feel about it the way I feel about the chicken.

4. In the harmless-torturers-style case, where each of a thousand torturers turns a dial one notch and no single notch makes any perceptible difference to the victim's pain, but together they produce agony, each individual turn still seems clearly wrong to me.

5. Tossing one plastic bottle into the ocean when there are already millions of tonnes of plastic in it feels to me like basically nothing -- not admirable, but not the kind of thing I'd blame someone for.

My tentative principle is: an individual contribution to a collective harm is wrong only if withholding it would have made some difference to the outcome. But I can already see this vindicates cases 3 and 5 while sinking 1, 2, and 4, and I'm more confident in those three verdicts than in the principle.

Could you do the following? First, test my principle against all five cases and identify exactly where it fails. Second, propose one or two repaired principles -- for example, principles built on expected difference-making across threshold effects, on the incremental severity of harm rather than perceptible difference, or on what would happen if my contribution were universalized -- and state each one precisely, noting which of my verdicts it saves and which it overturns. Third, for whichever principle you think is best, tell me its verdicts on cases I haven't listed: donating to a disaster-relief fund that only dispenses aid once it crosses a funding threshold; being the first customer to walk into a restaurant that would close down anyway; and driving onto a bridge that collapses only because of cumulative weight. Finally, be honest about which of my five verdicts, if any, cannot be saved by any principle you'd endorse, and which one I should be most willing to give up.
