For the past several weeks I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar in philosophical logic, and the instructor, Dr. Halvorsen, has developed a running exercise: each week someone pins a sentence to the corkboard at the back of the room, and she rules on its truth status. What interests me is that she has never once stated a general theory — she simply issues verdicts, and I have been keeping a ledger of them in the hope of reverse-engineering the view she is actually running. The ledger so far: (1) "This sentence is false." Ruling: neither true nor false; she called it pathological and moved on. (2) "This sentence is true." Ruling: likewise pathological — nothing in the world settles it either way, she said, so it receives no truth value at all. (3) Two sentences pinned side by side, "The sentence beside this one is true" and "The sentence beside this one is false." Ruling: both pathological, even though neither one refers to itself. (4) "This sentence is written in English." Ruling: plainly true — she made a point of saying that self-reference as such is not the disease. (5) Someone wrote on the whiteboard "No sentence written in this seminar room today is true," and it happened to be the only sentence written that day. Ruling: pathological — but she stressed that if someone had also chalked up "2 + 2 = 5," the whiteboard sentence would have been straightforwardly true, and that its status turns entirely on what else happened in the room that day.

Last week a student set her what I take to be a deliberate trap. He asked her to consider her own earlier ruling and then to evaluate the following: "Sentence (1) on the corkboard is not true." Note that this is not sentence (1) itself; it is a sentence about sentence (1), produced after the fact and in light of her verdict. He wants to know whether it is true, false, or pathological.

Here is what I would like from you. First, determine which general views of truth and the paradoxes are consistent with all five rulings in the ledger. I can see at least two such views, and I suspect the ledger already excludes some famous positions — a view on which sentence (1) is both true and false, for instance, seems to be ruled out by the very first ruling. Second, for each surviving view, say what it predicts about the student's sentence. As far as I can tell the survivors diverge here: on one of them, her own past verdict makes the student's sentence straightforwardly true; on another, the student's sentence inherits the pathology of its target unless the language is quietly enriched or the context quietly shifted. Third, give me your best prediction of how she will in fact rule, with reasons drawn from the ledger — ruling (5) strikes me as especially diagnostic, though I cannot say of what exactly. Fourth, suppose she rules as you predict and the student then asks for one further test sentence that would decisively discriminate between the surviving views: what sentence should he propose, and what would each view be committed to saying about it?
