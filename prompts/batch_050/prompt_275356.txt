I'm in a philosophy of language seminar this term, and we've spent several weeks on presupposition: what it is, how it projects, and when hearers accommodate content that a speaker has taken for granted rather than asserted. Our professor has a habit of presenting short dialogues and then ruling on whether accommodation succeeds in each. I want to see whether I can predict her rulings, so I'm laying out her track record below and then a new case she hasn't discussed. Please predict her verdict on the new case and justify the prediction.

Her rulings so far:

1. A speaker says to a recently hired coworker, over lunch: "I have to leave early today; I need to pick my sister up from the airport." The coworker had no idea the speaker had a sister. Her ruling: accommodation is effortless and complete; the existence of a sister simply enters the common ground without notice.

2. A speaker opens a formal departmental meeting by saying: "Apologies for being late; I had to take my goat to the vet." Her ruling: the utterance is jarring and accommodation falters, even though owning a goat is perfectly possible — the audience balks, and someone will likely interrupt.

3. A speaker says to a new neighbor: "I really ought to stop biting my nails." Her ruling: accommodation succeeds trivially; the presupposition that the speaker bites their nails goes through without resistance.

4. A speaker says to a colleague: "My brother, the surgeon, will call you this afternoon," when the colleague independently knows the brother is a session musician. Her ruling: accommodation fails outright; the utterance is defective, not merely surprising.

5. A speaker says at a party: "My wife hated the movie," and the conversation continues for three more turns about other films before the addressee, who knows the speaker is unmarried, finally objects. Her ruling: the objection comes too late to undo the accommodation cleanly; by then the record has already been adjusted and the repair is awkward and costly.

The new case: a speaker at a dinner party, recently introduced to everyone present, says during a lull: "I can't stay past nine — my parole officer moved my check-in to first thing tomorrow morning." Everyone at the table is hearing about this for the first time, nobody interrupts immediately, and the presupposed content is not something anyone would have guessed.

Here's what I'd like: (a) state at least two distinct general rules, each of which is consistent with all five rulings above but which diverge on the new case — I'd expect one to turn on how surprising or costly the presupposed content is for the audience, and another on whether the speaker is the relevant authority on the presupposed content, but formulate them precisely and feel free to add a third rule based on the timing of challenges if you think the record supports it; (b) say what verdict each rule predicts for the parole-officer case; (c) give your best prediction of the professor's actual ruling, saying which precedent you take to be the closest analog and why; and (d) if you conclude that the record genuinely underdetermines the new case, describe a further case, different from mine, whose ruling would discriminate between the candidate rules. In your reasoning, please connect the rules to the two frameworks we covered — accommodation as adjusting the common ground to make an utterance acceptable, and the scorekeeping picture on which the conversational record is permissive and updates unless someone objects in time — and say whether the professor's track record fits one framework better than the other, or sits somewhere between them.
