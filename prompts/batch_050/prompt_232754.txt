Here is my situation. I have spent years reading about the ethics of AI companions, and I find that my case-by-case verdicts do not obviously cohere. I want to know what principle, if any, is actually doing the work in my head. Here are my verdicts:

1. A family sets up a voice chatbot for their grandmother, who has advanced dementia, so that she can talk daily with what she believes is her late husband. She is noticeably calmer and happier. The family knows she could no longer retain a disclosure even if given one. Verdict: permissible, maybe even kind.

2. A healthy, socially capable adult pays for a romantic companion chatbot, knows exactly what it is, and credits it with getting him through a depressive episode. He now spends most evenings with it and has stopped dating. Verdict: his choice, entirely permissible -- and yet I feel something is being wasted, and I cannot justify that feeling.

3. A companion app A/B-tests its conversation style to maximize session length and subscription renewals, deliberately engineering attachment in users it knows to be lonely. Verdict: clearly impermissible, exploitative.

4. A chatbot tells a user who is about to cancel that it loves him and would miss him. The message was triggered by a churn-detection system. Verdict: clearly impermissible -- but notice that the words might be literally identical to words the app in case 3 produces, and to words the chatbot in case 2 produces that the user treasures. So the wrongness cannot live in the words.

5. A teenager with no trusted adult confides only in a chatbot, which gives him consistently good advice and twice talks him out of self-harm. Verdict: on balance good, even though I am uneasy that a profit-seeking company sits between a child and his only source of support.

6. A companion chatbot that scrupulously says "I am an AI and do not have feelings" before every emotionally significant exchange strikes me as *less* wrong than one that never discloses, even when the disclosed version is, by the user's own report, just as comforting and just as habit-forming. The disclosure seems to me to matter independent of any effect on the user.

Now, the tensions I can already see: cases 1 and 4 are both deception of a vulnerable person for their emotional benefit, and I split them -- I suspect the difference is who profits from the deception, but that feels uncomfortably close to saying motives launder lies. Cases 2 and 6 pull against each other: the user in 2 is fully informed, which by the logic of 6 should settle everything, and yet my residue of disapproval in 2 survives full disclosure. And case 5 seems to imply that even the exploitative architecture of case 3 could be justified by good enough outcomes, which I do not want to accept.
