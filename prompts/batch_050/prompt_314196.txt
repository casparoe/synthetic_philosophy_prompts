I find the unilateralist's curse compelling: when an action affects many people, but each of several agents can initiate it unilaterally on the basis of their own private judgment, then even if most agents correctly judge the action to be a mistake, the agent whose estimate is most mistaken will tend to act -- so such actions will be undertaken more often than they should be. I take this to be a genuine epistemic phenomenon, not merely a coordination failure, and to give individual agents a real reason for restraint that persists even after each agent has already corrected for their own likely overoptimism. What would count against this view? I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward: which of its modeling assumptions (independence of estimates, absence of any shared decision procedure, the structure of the payoff) does the argument actually need, and does it fail gracefully when those assumptions are relaxed, or collapse? Is the reasoning anything more than the winner's curse redescribed, and if so, does that demote it? Does the extension to moral judgments -- where the "mistaken" agent is a conscientious dissenter rather than a misestimator -- survive, or does treating moral conviction as noisy evidence beg the question against the dissenter? Is there a symmetric "unilateralist's blessing" for beneficial actions, and would its existence show the original argument cherry-picked the bad cases? Finally, could the whole thing turn out to be empirically empty -- a theorem about idealized agents that tells us nothing about how real deliberators should actually discount their own judgments -- and would its defenders concede that as counting against it?
