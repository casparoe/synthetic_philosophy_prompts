Two years into retirement after four decades as an experimental physicist, I have fallen into philosophy the way some people fall into gardening, and my reading group -- mostly retired engineers and one very patient former theologian -- recently worked through L. A. Paul's writing on transformative experience. Her claim, as we understood it, is that certain life choices (her stock examples are having a child and becoming a vampire) cannot be made rationally in the standard way, because the experience at issue is both epistemically transformative, revealing something you cannot know from the outside, and personally transformative, remaking the very preferences that would have to do the choosing; you cannot weigh outcomes whose value you cannot grasp until it is too late, and the person doing the weighing will not survive the outcome. The same week I rewatched Denis Villeneuve's film Arrival (adapted from Ted Chiang's novella Story of Your Life), and it struck me that the film is a sustained dramatization of exactly this problem -- though I cannot decide whether it vindicates Paul, evades her, or quietly changes the subject.

Please read the film through Paul's framework, seriously in both directions. Louise Banks is transformed twice over: by learning the heptapod language, which restructures her experience of time so that she comes to apprehend her life non-sequentially, and by the choice at the film's center, to conceive a daughter she already knows will die young. I would like to know, first, how well Louise's situation maps onto Paul's two transformations, and where the mapping strains: the film grants her something close to certain knowledge of the outcome, whereas Paul's puzzle arises under ordinary uncertainty, so does the film convert an epistemological problem about decision into a metaphysical problem about time and thereby dodge the original question? Second, does the film's determinism dissolve the decision-theoretic puzzle entirely -- if Louise's future is fixed and already lived by her, there is arguably no deliberation left to perform -- or does the film instead model a mode of choosing that Paul considers unavailable: not weighing expected value, but affirming or identifying with one's whole life, grief included, something in the direction of amor fati? As a physicist I am also curious whether the non-linear-time metaphysics is doing genuine philosophical work here or is merely decoration over a quite ordinary choice about parenthood, and I would rather be told it is decoration than be flattered.

A few constraints on the reading. Do not reduce the film to an illustration of the thesis, and do not bend the thesis to fit the film; where the film resists the reading -- and I suspect it does, for instance in how little deliberation Louise is ever shown doing, and in the way the editing lets the audience mistake her foreknowledge for memory until the reveal, so that our epistemic position in the cinema deliberately fails to mirror hers -- say so plainly. Do not quote dialogue verbatim; I know the plot and its major scenes well enough. End by telling me whether Arrival contributes anything to the philosophical question that argument alone does not -- for instance, something about the phenomenology of grieving an event before it occurs -- or whether it merely lends the problem an emotional varnish it did not need.
