Spent three hours at the pub last night losing an argument to my friend Marcus and I want to know where exactly I lost. The topic was immigration. I'm broadly open-borders-ish, he's not, but he wasn't defending closed borders — he was trying to show that the standard philosophical case for a state's right to exclude proves way too much, and I couldn't find the step to push back on. His argument, roughly as I reconstructed it on the train home:

(1) Any legitimate self-governing association gets to decide its own membership: churches choose congregants, clubs choose members, and nobody can force you to marry them.
(2) A state is, in the morally relevant sense, just a very large self-governing association of its citizens — sheer scale changes nothing fundamental.
(3) So citizens, acting through their state, may refuse membership to outsiders — some level of border exclusion is justified in principle.
(4) But membership control isn't membership control unless it's discretionary: a club that may only exclude people for reasons a court approves of doesn't really control its membership. So the association picks its own criteria.
(5) So a state could exclude would-be immigrants for having the wrong religion, the wrong politics, the wrong ethnicity, or just because current citizens find them annoying.
(6) A religious-test immigration policy (say, a flat ban on Muslim immigration) is morally grotesque, not merely inadvisable.
(7) So the associationist machinery that got us here has to go — either (1) or (2), and with it the whole "freedom of association justifies border control" line.

Then he twisted the knife. If (2) is true and scale changes nothing, the argument should also work at town level: a town of 800 people could vote to keep out anyone who isn't, say, Baptist, the way a small religious commune can. And conversely, clubs can expel members, not just refuse them, so if the state is a club, denaturalizing citizens whose views the majority dislikes should be fine too. He claims any fix to (2) that blocks the expulsion case will definately block the immigration case as well, since it's the same associational right doing the work in both.

The annoying thing is I suspect he's smuggling something in around (4) — the slide from "associations control their membership" to "they control it on any criteria whatsoever" — but when I said that, he pointed out that actual clubs exclude people for bad reasons all the time and we let them: a golf club that blackballs someone for being rude is being unfair, yet nobody thinks it's doing something morally forbidden. So maybe the flaw is in (2) after all, but I couldn't name the morally relevant difference between a state and a club without it sounding like a restatement of my own conclusion.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost? I'd most like to keep (1) — it feels like bedrock — and I'd grudgingly sacrifice (2) if forced, but I want the price itemized either way. Also: does the right diagnosis explain why the town-level case feels worse than the national one even though, by (2), it shouldn't? And if the argument really does fail at (4), does that mean anti-discrimination norms on associations are doing more foundational work in political philosophy than people usually admit?
