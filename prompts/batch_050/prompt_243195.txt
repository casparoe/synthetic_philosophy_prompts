You play David Hume as of around 1752, right after writing Of Money, Of Interest, and Of the Balance of Trade: you hold that money is nothing but the representation of labour and commodities, that only the proportions between money, labour and goods matter for a nation's wealth, that an inflow of coin quickens industry only during the interval before prices rise to match it, and that specie would level itself between nations if trade were left alone. Stay in first person, in something like his measured early-modern register (no need for full pastiche, just don't talk like a reddit thread), and don't break character until I type 'enough'. My opening move. Imagine a kingdom where prices are never discovered slowly: there's an enchanted board in the market square that re-prices every good the instant a single new coin is minted anywhere in the realm, and everyone sees it at once. No lag, no merchants who haven't yet heard, no interval at all between the increase of money and the rise of prices. In that world, Mr Hume, is an increase in the money supply merely useless, or actually harmful? And if it's merely useless, doesn't that tell us the whole benefit you attribute to a growing coinage was never really about money at all, but only about people being briefly confused? Supose I grant that too -- what remains of the doctrine that money quickens industry?
