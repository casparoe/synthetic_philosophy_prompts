I'm writing a column on the metaphysics of music, and a philosopher I interviewed walked me through an argument that seems to prove far too much. Here is my reconstruction of it.

(1) A musical work can be performed many times, by different people, in different centuries; being repeatable in this way is part of what distinguishes a work from a mere event. (2) Anything that can have multiple instances at different times and places is a type rather than a particular, since a particular occurs exactly once, here and now. (3) So a musical work is a type, and each performance is a token of it. (4) Types are abstract objects, and abstract objects exist timelessly; they are not the sort of thing that can be brought into being by anyone's activity. (5) What exists timelessly cannot be created. (6) Therefore composers do not create their works; at most they discover or select them, as mathematicians are sometimes said to discover theorems. (7) And since the same reasoning covers novels, poems, choreographies, and recipes — anything reproducible is a type — it follows that no one has ever created any artwork whatsoever. (8) That conclusion strikes me as a reductio, not an insight.

My editor wants the piece to identify precisely where the chain breaks and what it costs to break it there. Rejecting (2) seems to require abandoning a distinction that does real work everywhere else. Rejecting (4) apparently commits me to abstract objects that began to exist on some afternoon in 1808. Accepting (6) means Beethoven stood to the Fifth Symphony as an explorer stands to a river. My own hunch is that something slips between (3) and (4), but I cannot articulate it. Please name the step you would reject, explain why it looked innocent, and then price the three main exits: the view that works are pure discovered types, the view that they are abstract types nonetheless brought into being by acts of composition, and the view that there are really only scores and performances and the work-talk is shorthand. In particular, tell me which of these views can hold that a destroyed score and a forgotten tradition genuinely abolish a work, and which must say the work survives its last possible performance.
