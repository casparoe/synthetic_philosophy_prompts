I'm a high school senior and the captain of my school's mock trial team, and I need help settling a dispute with our faculty advisor before the season starts. It began with the two stock cases everyone in this debate eventually meets: the bus company that operates the large majority of buses on a stretch of road where an unidentified bus forced a cyclist off the road, and the venue where nearly everyone inside snuck in without paying, so the organizers simply pick one attendee and sue for the price of a ticket. Our advisor's position is that no court should ever rest a verdict on what he calls naked statistics, no matter how extreme the percentages get. Mine is that a probability is a probability, and that 95 percent is 95 percent regardless of how it was produced.

To stop us from endlessly re-litigating the textbook cases, I invented a new one, and I'd like you to treat it as the focal case. A school district adopts an AI system that reviews security footage and identifies students responsible for vandalism. In independently audited trials, the system names the correct person 97 times out of 100, and its errors are randomly distributed rather than concentrated on any particular group of students. A student is expelled on the basis of the system's output alone: no eyewitnesses, no physical evidence, nothing that connects this student to the act except the system's flag and its audited track record. My advisor calls this the gatecrasher wearing a lab coat. I call it better evidence than most eyewitness testimony, which is notoriously fallible and yet somehow counts as "individualized."

Here is what I want from you. First, give me your own credence in the following proposition: purely statistical evidence — evidence whose entire justificatory force derives from aggregate frequencies rather than from anything that traces this particular defendant to this particular act — can, at least in principle, be sufficient to meet the criminal standard of proof beyond a reasonable doubt. Second, decompose that credence into the sub-questions it actually depends on. I expect the candidates to include: whether legal proof is reducible to posterior probability at all; whether there is an epistemically real difference between defendant-specific and base-rate evidence, or only a psychological one; whether the legitimacy of a verdict depends on the defendant having had something individualized to contest; and whether my AI system even counts as naked statistical evidence or has quietly become something else. Assign each sub-question a rough probability with one or two lines of justification, show how they combine into your headline number, and mark the sub-question to which the final credence is most sensitive. Third, given my advisor's flat refusal and my flat acceptance, tell me which sub-question our disagreement most plausibly lives in, and what result — empirical, legal, or conceptual — would move your own number the most.
