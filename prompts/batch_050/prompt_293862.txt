In the first-year philosophy of mathematics seminar I teach, one session is devoted to the identity problem for numbers: whether there is any fact of the matter about whether 2 is an element of 4. The class is deliberately mixed. Roughly half the students are mathematics majors who have met the standard set-theoretic construction of the natural numbers; the other half are philosophy majors who have not. I have fifty minutes and a blackboard, and I want the session to end with the class understanding why structuralists take the question to be malformed, not merely being told that they do. Plan the session for me.

Specifically, I want the following.

First, the order of ideas and its rationale. My instinct is: begin with what arithmetic itself demands of any candidate number system (a progression with a distinguished starting point, as the Peano axioms capture); then present two rival set-theoretic reductions that both meet those demands (the von Neumann style, where 4 = {0, 1, 2, 3} and so 2 is indeed a member of 4, versus a Zermelo-style construction where each number is the singleton of its predecessor, so that 2 is not a member of 4); then pose the uncomfortable question of which reduction is the correct one; then give the structuralist diagnosis. Tell me whether this sequence is right, and explain the dependency structure: why the axioms must be in place before the reductions mean anything, and why the reductions must precede the question of which is right rather than follow it.

Second, the carrying example. The two reductions must do all the work, so tell me exactly what the example must exhibit (that both reductions deliver every theorem of arithmetic and disagree only on membership questions that arithmetic never asks) and what it must not be allowed to suggest (that one reduction is simply better mathematics, or that numbers are somehow both sets at once).

Third, the misconception map. I expect at least four. The realist reflex: one reduction is right and the other merely a good model, we just cannot tell which. The mathematics majors' objection: 2 ∈ 4 is simply true, because that is what 4 is — a conflation of a fact internal to a chosen representation with a fact about the number itself. The nihilist slide: structuralism means numbers do not exist and arithmetic is about nothing. And the conflation of "is 2 an element of 4?" with "is 2 less than 4?" — the latter being structural and surviving the argument, though students will treat the two alike. For each misconception, tell me at what point in the sequence it predictably arises, how to defuse it there rather than later, and why defusing it earlier or later fails.

Fourth, the check for understanding: one question to pose at the end whose mishandling reveals that the idea has not landed. My candidate is asking students to explain why 2 < 4 survives the multiple-reductions argument while 2 ∈ 4 does not, but propose a better one if you have it.

Fifth, what to refuse at this level, and how to refuse gracefully: the further moves such as ante rem versus in re structuralism, eliminative or modal versions, category-theoretic foundations, and proofs within ZFC. I want language for saying that these are real questions, but not for today, without seeming to dodge.

Finally, flag the points where the two halves of the audience will react differently, since the plan has to work for both simultaneously. Please reply in American English.
