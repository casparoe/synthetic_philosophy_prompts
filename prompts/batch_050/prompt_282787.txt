You are a philosopher and historian of computing with particular expertise in mid-twentieth-century British philosophy of mind. I would like a carefully reasoned intellectual extrapolation, not an opinion piece. Before writing, take time to brainstorm the relevant considerations; then produce a polished final answer.

Treat the following background as given. In his 1950 article proposing the imitation game, Alan Turing did not dismiss psychical research. He granted that the statistical evidence for telepathy was, in his judgment, strong; he described it as the most serious of the objections to his test; and he suggested that the imitation game might need to be conducted in conditions shielded against telepathic interference, since a mind-reading interrogator or a machine sensitive to thoughts would spoil the setup. He never returned to the topic in print before his death in 1954.

Now consider three developments Turing never saw:

1. Systems based on large-scale statistical learning that pass informal versions of the imitation game routinely, while failing at tasks any child can do and while arguably doing nothing resembling telepathy.
2. The fate of parapsychology after his death: decades of debated experiments, a widely discussed 2011 paper reporting evidence for precognition in ordinary undergraduates, and the subsequent realization across psychology that standard statistical practice (flexible analysis, small samples, selective reporting) can manufacture exactly the kind of evidence Turing found compelling — followed by reforms such as pre-registration under which the reported effects largely disappeared.
3. The emergence of serious contemporary debate about whether artificial systems could be conscious or could be owed moral consideration.

Your task has four parts. First, reconstruct the epistemological commitments that plausibly underlay Turing's concession to ESP: his operationalist stance on mental vocabulary, his willingness to defer to statistical evidence over mechanistic intuition, and his apparent comfort with conclusions most of his colleagues found disreputable. Be explicit about which commitments you are attributing and on what textual or biographical grounds. Second, for each of the three developments, predict Turing's likely response, deriving the prediction from those commitments rather than from the bare fact of his telepathy remark; for each prediction, flag the precise point where the historical record runs out and pure extrapolation begins. Third, state whether, on your reconstruction, Turing would have retracted his claim that telepathy was the strongest objection to the imitation game — and whether that retraction would raise or lower his estimate of what passing the test shows about a machine. Fourth, identify the single commitment in your reconstruction that is doing the most work, and say what evidence could in principle overturn your attribution of it.

Write in a formal register, roughly 800–1,200 words. Do not fabricate quotations or attribute specific texts to Turing beyond the background above; where you go beyond it, hedge accordingly.
