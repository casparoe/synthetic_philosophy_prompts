I work as a philosophical counsellor in private practice: clients self-refer, sessions last fifty minutes, and I see most people for somewhere between four and eight meetings. I am not a psychotherapist and hold no clinical licence, which carries a standing duty to refer out anything that looks like illness rather than perplexity. A steady minority of my clients — perhaps one in five — bring distress that turns on the metaphysics of time, and it arrives in recognisably different shapes: the widower who cannot bear that his wife no longer exists; the trauma survivor who has read popular physics and is horrified that, if the block universe is real, her childhood is still going on; the stalled decision-maker who reasons that if the future already exists, there is no point deliberating. I have been improvising, and I have caught myself doing something I dislike: I lean towards eternalism myself, and I can feel the temptation to offer it as comfort where it comforts and to withhold it where it would wound. I want discipline instead of instinct.

Write me a procedure I can actually run in the room: the questions to put to a client, in order, to classify the distress; then, for each type of time-distress, which moves the procedure permits, which it forbids, and on what grounds. The grounds matter. The procedure has to distinguish the true from the consoling, and within the true, the helpful from the harmful — I take it I am barred from comforting falsehoods but not thereby licensed to utter every harmful truth. It must say what counts as a sufficient answer to each intake question, where the procedure stops with a referral, and where it hands the decision back to me as judgment because no rule will carry it. It has to fit on a page and be usable within a single session, since clients who find the first meeting misfires rarely return. Two further constraints: many clients arrive with half-digested pop-science versions of relativity, and the procedure must not let me overstate what physics establishes — the block universe is a contested reading of it, not a settled result, and presentists are not simply refuted; and the procedure must work whichever view of time is true, since I cannot settle the metaphysics at the intake desk. If there are live positions between presentism and eternalism that change what may honestly be offered to a client, say where they slot in.

Then run the procedure on the three clients above, step by step. In at least one case it should return an instruction I will not like — I expect that to be either a case where it forbids me the consolation I myself believe, or one where it tells me the client's trouble is not metaphysical at all and I should stop doing philosophy. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do.
