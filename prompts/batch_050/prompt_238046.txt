Homework question

In J. L. Kelly, Jr.’s 1956 paper “A New Interpretation of Information Rate,” Kelly writes:

“What can be done, however, is to take some real-life situation which seems to possess the essential features of a communication problem, and to analyze it without the introduction of an arbitrary cost function. The situation which will be chosen here is one in which a gambler uses knowledge of the received symbols of a communication channel in order to make profitable bets on the transmitted symbols.”

Consider the following “harbor signal” problem. Each day the true sea state S is one of four outcomes {N, E, S, W} with prior probabilities p = (1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/8), independently across days. Before bets close, a bettor observes a channel output X. The channel reports the true state with probability 3/4; when it errs, it reports each of the three wrong states with probability 1/12. A bookmaker offers fair odds on every outcome: a unit stake on outcome i returns 1/p_i total if i occurs and 0 otherwise. The bettor must reinvest the entire bankroll each day, cannot borrow, and may divide the bankroll across outcomes.

(a) Explain how Kelly’s passage bears on this case: why can mutual information function as a “value measure” here without first imposing an arbitrary cost function on symbol errors? What would be missing from an analysis that merely assigned costs to incorrect reports?

(b) State the growth-optimal betting rule after observing X = x, defining the fractions staked in terms of posterior probabilities. Justify why the relevant optimization is maximization of expected logarithmic growth rather than expected one-day wealth.

(c) Give the long-run exponential growth rate for this fair-odds setting as an information-theoretic quantity, and state two conditions under which that identification would fail or require modification.

Student answer

(a) Kelly’s point is that a channel can matter economically even when no coding theorem is being invoked. In the harbor case, the report X changes what bets are available to be made profitably, so the value of the channel is fixed by the betting opportunities it creates rather than by an external penalty for confusing N with W. A cost-function analysis would say something like “mistaking N for W is twice as bad as mistaking E for S,” but unless those costs are tied to decisions and payoffs, the comparison is arbitrary. Here the payoff structure does the work: the receiver can profit from knowledge of the input symbols and even from a better estimate of their probabilities.

(b) Because the channel is symmetric, the posterior after any report puts probability 3/4 on the reported symbol and 1/12 on each of the others. Under fair odds the growth-optimal stake on each outcome equals its posterior probability, so the bettor stakes 3/4 of the bankroll on the reported outcome and 1/12 on each remaining outcome. The reason to use expected log wealth is compounding: wealth is multiplied each day, so the quantity that behaves additively over days is log wealth. Maximizing expected wealth would ignore the repeated-reinvestment structure and the possibility of ruin.

(c) With fair odds and daily reinvestment, the exponential growth rate equals the mutual information I(S; X). Equivalently, conditional on X = x the growth rate is the KL divergence from the fair-odds implied distribution p to the posterior q(· | x), and averaging over x gives I(S; X). This identification assumes that the bookmaker really posts odds matching the true marginal p, that the bettor can always place the required fractional stakes, and that the same multiplicative opportunity repeats. For a fixed finite horizon the same rule also maximizes expected terminal wealth; the logarithm is only a convenient way of handling products. If the odds are unfair, the channel can no longer be valued by information rate alone because the bookmaker’s margin, rather than the channel statistics, determines all profitable bets.
