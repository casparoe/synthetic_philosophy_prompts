At a workshop on minds and machines, two of the participants — one a philosopher of mind sympathetic to Dennett, the other trained in cognitive science — fell into an argument about whether it is literally true, or merely a convenient shorthand, to say that a large language model believes things. I listened to the whole exchange and could not settle it. Here is the exchange as I remember it, and I would like a point-by-point adjudication.

Marta opened by saying that on Dennett's account of the intentional stance, the question is settled by predictive practice. A system counts as a believer, she said, insofar as treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires yields predictions of its behavior that are reliable, tractable, and unavailable from the design stance or the physical stance. Language models pass that test voluminously: we predict what a model will say by asking what it would make sense for it to say given what it "knows" and "wants" (to be helpful, to follow instructions), and no engineering-level description of next-token probabilities gives us anything comparably useful in real time. So, on Dennett's criterion, the belief attributions are as literal as belief attributions get — there is no further fact of the matter beyond the success of the pattern.

Dev replied that Marta had kept the rhetoric and dropped the conditions. Dennett, he said, does not license belief talk for just anything that is predictable under some description; the attributions have to be non-trivial, and the system has to come close enough to the ideal of rationality that the attribution does real work. A chess engine's "desire to protect its queen" is a thin convenience; and a language model, he argued, is worse than a chess engine, because its apparent beliefs dissolve under trivial rephrasings. The same model will affirm a claim in one framing and deny it in another, and nothing in the system registers this as a defect to be repaired. Belief, Dev said, is the kind of state whose owner can be brought to see an inconsistency as a reason to revise; a system that cannot treat contradiction as a problem does not have beliefs that contradict each other — it just emits strings.

Marta answered that this standard would dissolve human belief too. Framing effects, compartmentalization, and failure to notice inconsistencies are ubiquitous in people, and Dennett himself builds his account around the fact that real believers only approximate ideal rationality. If inconsistency under framing defeated belief attribution, we would have to deny beliefs to most humans most of the time, which no one accepts.

Dev conceded that humans are inconsistent but insisted the cases differ in kind, not degree. A human who is shown the contradiction will typically feel its pull — discomfort, revision, or at least a recognized pressure — because the norms of rationality are in some sense operative in the person, even when violated. In the model, nothing corresponds to that pressure; present the contradiction and it will cheerfully resolve it in whichever direction the conversational context nudges, and would do the opposite under a different nudge. There is no stable belief underneath for the contradiction to be about. He added a second, independent point: even if the model satisfied every behavioral criterion, its intentionality would at best be derived, the way the words in a book derive their meaning from readers and authors, whereas believers in the full sense require original intentionality — meaning that is not borrowed from anyone else's interpretation.

Marta's final move was to press both points. On the first: the demand that rational norms be "internally operative" in some way beyond the behavioral pattern is exactly the residual Cartesianism Dennett spent his career rejecting; Dev is smuggling in a further fact of the matter after claiming to accept the intentional stance. On the second: the derived/original distinction threatens to prove too much, since on a naturalistic view human intentionality is itself the product of selection pressures rather than something sui generis, and Dennett's own treatment of intentionality as a matter of patterns in the world leaves little room for an invidious line between borrowers and originators.

They asked me to referee and I honestly could not. Please adjudicate: who is right about (1) whether Dennett's criterion, properly stated, makes the belief attributions to such models literal or merely instrumental; (2) whether inconsistency under rephrasing defeats belief attribution, and whether the human parallel undermines that objection; (3) whether the derived/original intentionality distinction does independent work here, and whether it proves too much; and (4) what, if anything, both parties are missing — for example, whether there is a stable position on which some belief talk about such systems is warranted and some is not, and what would discriminate the cases. Say who is more right overall, and defend the verdict rather than splitting the difference by default. Please write your answer in British English.
