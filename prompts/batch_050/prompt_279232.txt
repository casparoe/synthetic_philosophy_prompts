Homework question: "Decision rules for choice under ignorance differ from rules for choice under risk in that they do not rely on probabilities. Explain how maximin and minimax regret work, show with a small payoff table that they can recommend different options, and explain why Rawls's argument in the original position is associated with the former while Harsanyi's is associated with expected utility maximization."

Student's answer: "Under ignorance we have no probabilities at all, so we cannot compute expected values. Maximin says: for each option, look at its worst possible outcome, and choose the option whose worst outcome is best. Minimax regret says: compute, for each option and each state, the regret, meaning the gap between what you'd get and the best you could have gotten in that state, then choose the option whose maximum regret is smallest. An advantage of minimax regret over maximin is that it takes all outcomes into account rather than just the worst, and like maximin it is unaffected by which other options are on the menu, so adding or removing an option can't change the comparison between two options you were already considering. Example: options A and B, states S1 and S2. Payoffs: A gives 10 in S1 and 2 in S2; B gives 5 in S1 and 6 in S2. Maximin compares the worst outcomes (2 for A, 5 for B) and picks B. For regret: in S1 the best available is 10, so A's regret is 0 and B's is 5; in S2 the best is 6, so A's regret is 4 and B's is 0. Maximum regret is 4 for A and 5 for B, so minimax regret picks A. The rules diverge. As for the second part: behind the veil of ignorance the parties don't know who they'll be, so they face a choice under ignorance, and Rawls's view is that rational parties in this situation should follow the maximin rule, which is what yields the difference principle, his principle that inequalities must be arranged to maximize the expectations of the worst off. Harsanyi instead argued that the parties should treat themselves as equally likely to end up as anyone and maximize expected utility, which yields average utilitarianism. So the Rawls–Harsanyi dispute comes down to whether maximin or expected utility is the correct general rule of rational choice under ignorance."
