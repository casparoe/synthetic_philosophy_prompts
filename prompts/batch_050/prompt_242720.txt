My ML safety reading group has been stuck on the same fight for three weeks now and I'd like an outside opinion with actual numbers attached. The question: for AI systems doing tasks whose outcomes humans can't directly score (long-horizon research assistance, novel proofs, codebases too big to audit), is supervising the *reasoning process* actually the better bet than supervising *outcomes*? My friend is at about 0.85 that process-based supervision wins; I'm around 0.4. My worry is the legibility tax — that pushing models to reason in ways humans can follow and grade either caps capability or just teaches them to produce a clean-looking story alongside whatever they're actually doing. Her worry is that outcome-based training on unverifiable tasks is reward hacking bait: you get a maths model that lands on the right final answer via two errors that cancel, or a coding assistant whose patch passes the tests for reasons its written explanation completely misdescribes, and you've rewarded all of it.

So: give me your credence that, for systems operating beyond reliable human outcome-checking, primarily process-based supervision will produce better-aligned and more trustworthy systems than primarily outcome-based supervision, over the next decade of scaling. But don't just give a number — decompose it. Off the top of my head the relevant sub-questions are: whether chains of thought remain a faithful window into the computation that produced the answer as models get bigger (or whether the reasoning trace becomes post-hoc rationalisation, or worse, encoded); whether grading reasoning steps stays cheaper and more reliable than grading outcomes as task horizons lengthen; whether process rewards generalise out of distribution or just teach models what good reasoning *looks like*; and how big the capability penalty from legibility constraints really is and whether labs will eat it. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question with a line of justification, say how they combine into your final number, and tell me which term your answer is most sensitive to — and what evidence (an empirical result, a failure mode observed in the wild) would move it most. Also say where you think my friend at 0.85 and I at 0.4 most likely diverge.

Feel free to add sub-questions I've missed or merge mine if you think they're not independent — I'd definately rather have the right factorisation than mine. And please answer in British English.
