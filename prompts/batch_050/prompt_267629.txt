I'm a PhD student in political philosophy whose dissertation strays into welfare economics, and after sitting in on the applied cost-benefit course at my university's policy school I've become fixated on a tension I can't get anyone in that department to take seriously. The practice, as I understand it: when a transport ministry appraises a new rail line, it monetizes the hours travelers will save by observing how much people actually pay for faster options -- toll lanes, express fares -- and typically values an hour at some fraction of the average wage. Environmental regulators value quiet, clean air, and access to green space through house-price differentials between otherwise comparable neighborhoods, and they value safety through the wage premiums workers demonstrably accept for riskier jobs, which is how agencies arrive at a monetary value for a statistical life. The guiding idea everywhere is the same: don't ask people what they value or how happy various outcomes would make them; watch what they choose, and let the choices do the talking. And yet many of the same governments that run this machinery also fund behavioral-insights units whose entire mandate rests on the premise that observed choices systematically fail to track people's interests -- that defaults, framing effects, and anchoring push behavior around so thoroughly that the state is justified in nudging citizens toward what they really want. When I raise the apparent inconsistency in seminar, I get shrugs, so I'd like the analysis done properly.

What conception of preference and utility does the valuation side of this practice actually presuppose? Presumably something like: preferences are complete and stable enough to be read off choices, consistent enough that a choice in one context tells you about trade-offs in another, and connected to welfare such that satisfying a preference, as revealed, makes a life go better. But which of those commitments does the practice strictly require, and which does it merely sit comfortably with? For instance, does inferring value from willingness to pay -- which plainly scales with wealth, so that a rich commuter 's time prices out above a poor one's -- presuppose that a dollar yields equal welfare regardless of who holds it, or that the existing distribution of income is normatively acceptable, or can the practice defend itself on some weaker basis, such as that the gains from a project suffice to compensate its losers in principle? And what theory of well-being, if any, is smuggled in when a regulator treats a revealed trade-off as settling how much some good is worth to a person: is preference-satisfaction being used as a substantive account of welfare, or only as a measurement convention, and would the practice survive being honest about which it is?

Finally, tell me whether the two halves of the apparatus can be jointly coherent. If choice is the sole admissible evidence of value, on what basis can the nudge unit declare some choices mistaken -- what are 'true' preferences in a framework that officially recognizes no evidence independent of choice? If the reconciliation is that we should count only choices made under idealized conditions (informed, unhurried, undistracted), say whether that still deserves to be called revealed preference at all, or whether it quietly reinstates a mentalistic notion of utility that the original methodology was designed to escape. And close by describing what would concretely change if regulators instead measured experienced utility -- moment-to-moment affect, or reconstructions of how a day actually felt -- rather than decision utility: which current valuation practices would shift the most, and what presuppositions the replacement would carry in its turn. Where the practice is underdetermined by several rival theories, say so explicitly rather than forcing a single reconstruction.
