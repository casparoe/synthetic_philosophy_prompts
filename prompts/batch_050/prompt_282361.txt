A colleague in my jurisprudence reading group has an argument intended to show that genuine constitutional entrenchment is impossible — that every clause, however solemnly declared unamendable, can be lawfully amended — and although I am convinced it proves too much, I cannot locate the step to attack. Here it is:

(1) An amendment is valid if and only if it is adopted in conformity with the amendment procedure in force at the time of its adoption; conformity with that procedure is both necessary and sufficient.

(2) A constitution's amendment clause is itself a provision of the constitution, and is therefore amendable by the very procedure it specifies, unless some valid provision forbids this.

(3) Take any eternity clause E protecting some provision P — the German Basic Law's protection of the principles of its first and twentieth articles, say, or the proviso in Article V about equal suffrage in the Senate. Unless E expressly and successfully forbids its own amendment, E itself may be amended by the ordinary procedure, since E protects only P and says nothing about E.

(4) Even if E does purport to forbid its own amendment, that prohibition is itself just one more provision; a clause cannot acquire a privileged status merely by declaring itself to have one — otherwise any provision could entrench itself by appending a sentence, and entrenchment would be trivially easy rather than impossible.

(5) So E can first be amended to withdraw its protection from P, and P can then be amended: two steps, each individually lawful, each authorized by the procedure in force at the time it is taken.

(6) What holds for E holds for any entrenching device whatsoever, including the immutable rules of Peter Suber's Nomic, which players in fact remove by first transmuting them into mutable rules and only then repealing them.

(7) Therefore no legal system can contain a genuinely immutable rule: putative entrenchment is always escapable, and 'unamendable' never means more than 'amendable in two moves rather than one.'

What I would most like to preserve is that eternity clauses and Nomic's immutable rules do real normative work — that there is a difference in kind, not merely in the number of moves required, between protected and unprotected provisions — and, on the other side, that a clause declaring itself untouchable is not by itself magic. Which step should I reject? Explain why that step looked sound, and price the leading alternatives: the implied self-entrenchment of E; a doctrine of substantively unconstitutional constitutional amendments in the basic-structure style; the denial that procedural conformity is sufficient for validity; and simply biting the bullet alongside my colleague. If your diagnosis turns on step (4), say why self-reference would or would not add protection there, and whether your answer survives a Nomic proposal whose entire content is that the proposal itself shall not take effect.
