Having recently been persuaded by the Boethian resolution of the foreknowledge–freedom problem, I now teach it as the strongest available option: God is not temporally situated at all, but possesses the whole of his unending life simultaneously, so that his awareness of what is future to us resembles present observation rather than prediction, and constrains the will no more than seeing someone sit constrains their sitting. Please adopt the role of an expert in medieval philosophy and philosophical theology, think carefully, and map this position's exposure. What would count against it? I do not want the familiar complaint that timeless eternity is counterintuitive; I want the arguments, consequences, and results in adjacent debates on which the position genuinely stands or falls. Consider, amongst other things: whether the Boethian solution presupposes a particular metaphysics of time and would collapse if presentism were true; whether a timeless being could know tensed facts, and what follows for divine knowledge if not; and whether locating God's knowledge outside time merely relocates the necessity problem, since God's knowing might still be thought to ground the truth of what he knows. For each candidate defeater, indicate whether a committed Boethian would concede it as a genuine cost or dismiss it as trading on a confusion the position already has the resources to dissolve, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Conclude with an honest assessment of whether the view has been so qualified that it risks nothing, and whether that degree of insulation would itself be a defect in a position of this kind. Please write your answer in British English.
