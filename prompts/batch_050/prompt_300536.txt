**Question:** Distinguish cognitive penetration of perception from attentional selection and post-perceptual judgment. Using one visual example, explain what evidence would show that a belief or expectation changes perceptual experience itself rather than what people notice, remember, or report. Why is this distinction difficult to establish experimentally?

**Student’s answer:**  
Cognitive penetration means that beliefs, desires, or expectations alter the content of perceptual experience itself. It differs from attention, which selects which stimuli receive processing, and from post-perceptual judgment, where perception remains fixed and is only classified afterward.

Suppose people who expect to see a face in an ambiguous image report seeing one sooner. By itself, this does not establish cognitive penetration. They may be scanning the image differently, remembering it differently, or lowering their criterion for saying “face.” A better experiment would hold exposure, attention, and task constant while showing that expectation changes performance on a visual-discrimination task. Since discrimination performance is a direct measure of perceptual content, such a change would demonstrate cognitive penetration.

Evidence from early visual areas is also decisive. If expectation changes activity in V1, then perception has been altered, because V1 is perceptual rather than cognitive. The main remaining difficulty is practical: participants might guess the hypothesis. Once demand characteristics are controlled, a change in V1 activity rules out attentional selection and post-perceptual judgment.
