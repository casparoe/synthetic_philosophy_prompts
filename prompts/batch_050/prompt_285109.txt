In his article The Myth of Mental Illness (American Psychologist, 1960), Thomas Szasz writes:

"We speak of mental symptoms, on the other hand, when we refer to a patient's communications about himself, others, and the world about him. He might state that he is Napoleon or that he is being persecuted by the Communists. These would be considered mental symptoms only if the observer believed that the patient was not Napoleon [...]. This makes it apparent that the statement that 'X is a mental symptom' involves rendering a judgment. The judgment entails, moreover, a covert comparison or matching of the patient's ideas, concepts, or beliefs with those of the observer and the society in which they live."

Write a parody internal memorandum from the Clinical Standards Committee of a large mental health trust, circulated to all clinical staff, that takes this passage entirely at its word and converts it into mandatory record-keeping procedure. Around 600 words, formatted as numbered clauses, preceded by a subject line and the usual circulation boilerplate. The passage above does not count towards the word limit.

The memo must be deadpan and procedurally plausible: it should read like genuine institutional guidance written by conscientious administrators who have never once asked whether the doctrine they are implementing can survive being enforced. The humour must come from operationalising the passage faithfully, not from inventing positions its author did not hold. To that end, the guidance might require clinicians to record their own beliefs alongside the patient's, so that the covert comparison can be made overt and open to audit; to refer claims of persecution to a verification team before recording them as symptoms, on the grounds that a genuinely persecuted patient has no symptom; to declare beliefs the committee cannot honestly certify as false — religious, political, and similar — ineligible for symptom status pending a ruling on what society currently holds; and to specify a protocol for where the symptom goes when the audit reveals that it was the clinician who was mistaken. Signs may be treated as unaffected: a fever, the committee notes with relief, may still be recorded without judgment.

By the final clause, a reader should have felt both the real weight of Szasz's argument — that psychiatric diagnosis smuggles social and ethical judgment in under medical cover — and where it begins to strain: the committee's bureaucratic common sense should quietly register how much of clinical life the passage cannot describe (the patient who walks in asking for help, the suffering nobody is judging, the clinician who would rather not be audited). The piece must never break character, never wink at the reader, and never mention Szasz or the article by name; the comedy lies in the fact that the committee believes it is merely being careful.
