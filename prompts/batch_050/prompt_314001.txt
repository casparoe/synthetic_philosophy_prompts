My pragmatics instructor keeps a running list of utterances and rules on whether each one is genuinely performative — whether saying it, in those circumstances, is itself the doing of something rather than a report or description of a doing. Her verdicts so far:

1. "I promise to return the book by Friday." — performative.
2. "I promised to return the book by Friday." — not performative.
3. "I hereby pronounce you married," said by the officiant at the proper point in the ceremony — performative.
4. "I hereby pronounce you married," called out by a guest from the back row — not performative.
5. "You're hired," said by the manager who has authority to hire — performative.
6. "You're fired," said by an actor playing a manager in a stage play — not performative.
7. "I congratulate you on your promotion," said by a colleague who is privately seething about it — performative.

Her next case is this: an official's term of office in fact expired at midnight, though neither she nor anyone present knows this, and at the scheduled ceremony the following morning she says, "I hereby swear in the new council member," with everyone present, including the new member, accepting the proceedings in good faith.

Please do the following: (a) state the two or more general rules that each fit her entire track record so far — for example, rules about whether the conventional felicity conditions must actually obtain versus rules about serious intention and successful uptake — and say which earlier verdicts constrain them; (b) state what each rule predicts for the new case, since I believe they diverge on it; (c) give your best prediction of her verdict, with reasons drawn from the pattern of the earlier verdicts rather than from your own intuitions about the case; and (d) describe one further utterance whose verdict would most cleanly confirm or overturn your attribution of a rule to her.

End your answer with a final line of the form: Verdict: performative — or — Verdict: not performative.
