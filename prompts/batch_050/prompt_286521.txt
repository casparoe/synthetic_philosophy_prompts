I've been a LessWrong reader for years, so I've absorbed the standard line that words are pointers into a shared map and that translation between minds (or languages) is at least in principle a matter of matching up pointers. Recently this ran into Quine's indeterminacy of translation for me: the gavagai argument seems to show that no amount of behavioral evidence pins down a unique meaning, and that "the" correct translation of a sentence is partly a choice we make rather than a fact we discover.

Here's the concrete situation I want analyzed. The machine translation industry -- and the human translation profession alongside it -- runs on practices that look the opposite of Quinean. MT systems are scored against human-produced reference translations, with outputs penalized for deviating from the reference even when the deviation seems perfectly adequate. Professional translators are told their job is to preserve meaning, are graded as having made errors or not, and sign contracts holding them responsible for mistranslation. Courts treat translated documents as either accurate or inaccurate renditions of the original.

You are a philosopher of language who also understands how NLP evaluation actually works. Think carefully and take Quine seriously rather than brushing him aside. Start by brainstorming a broad list of candidate presuppositions this cluster of practices might rest on, then narrow down. I'd like to know: What view of meaning and translation does this whole apparatus presuppose, if anything -- that there is a determinate fact of the matter about what a sentence means, or merely that some translations are better than others? Can the practice be reconstructed as compatible with indeterminacy (e.g., as converging on "good enough" equivalences relative to shared purposes, or as tracking stimulus meaning only where observation sentences are concerned), or does it quietly require what Quine denied? Where is the practice genuinely underdetermined, such that several rival philosophies of language would license it equally well? And finally: if we took indeterminacy fully on board, what concretely would have to change -- should MT evaluation move to downstream-task success, should translator liability be rethought, or is the whole apparatus fine as a pragmatic convention wearing realist clothes?
