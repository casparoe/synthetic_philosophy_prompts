I've been going back and forth with a friend about why promises actually bind, and we keep circling two families of answers: the old conventionalist or practice view (Hume, Rawls -- promising only generates obligations because there's an existing social practice, and you obligate yourself by invoking it) versus Scanlon-style expectation views (a promise binds because you knowingly lead someone to rely on you, and the wrong is in disappointing expectations you deliberately created). Please compare these two properly, not just by summarizing them but by stress-testing them against some hard cases: (1) a promise made between two strangers from totally different cultures, no shared conventions in sight -- the classic desert island objection; (2) a promise to someone who has since died, where keeping it now seems to benefit no one at all; (3) a promise to do something mildly immoral, where the expectation view weirdly seems to say you've got some reason to go through with it; and (4) a promise the promisee deliberately manipulated you into making. For each case, say which theory handles it better and which one has to bite a bullet, and whether that bullet is actually chewable. Then give me a verdict: does the expectation view genuinely explain why promises bind, or does it just push the mystery back one step, from why must I keep promises to why must I not disappoint expectations I created?
