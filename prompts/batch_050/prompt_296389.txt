I'm a PhD student in medieval philosophy working on twelfth-century ethics, and I'd like you to write a piece of satire for the imagined newsletter of a fictional Benedictine monastery that has, in an excess of scholarly enthusiasm, reformed its entire disciplinary code along strictly Abelardian lines. The premise: the abbot has read Abelard's ethics and concluded that since sin consists wholly in consent to what one believes is contempt for God -- and never in the outward act, which is in itself morally indifferent -- the monastery's old rules punishing deeds (burned porridge, missed offices, a borrowed-and-unreturned manuscript) are spiritually confused. Henceforth the chapter of faults may judge only consent, never conduct.

Write the piece as the minutes of the monastery's first disciplinary hearing under the new code, roughly 650-750 words, in a deadpan bureaucratic register: itemized cases, motions, findings of no-fault, an appendix of amended statutes. The humor must come from genuinely Abelardian doctrine applied with total sincerity, not from made-up absurdity, and should include at least the following, worked naturally into the cases:

- A monk who smothered something valuable through an act of care (model him loosely on Abelard's own example of the woman who, trying to keep her infant warm, accidentally overlies it: she grieves, took no pleasure, consented to nothing, and so is innocent).
- A brother who was coerced or deceived into a grave misdeed and therefore consented to nothing, contrasted with a brother who fully consented in his heart to a theft but was interrupted before touching anything -- the second is the sinner, the first is not, and the minutes should record the community's quiet horror at this verdict.
- The conscience wrinkle that makes Abelard's position genuinely hard: the monk who acts against his own conscience sins even if his conscience is wrong, while the monk who sincerely believes his dreadful act pleases God does not consent to sin at all -- the minutes should have the scribe tie himself in knots over which monk is culpable.
- At least one motion to discipline an act as such, solemnly ruled out of order.

Keep the irony legible but unwinking: no narrator should ever say the code is absurd. By the end, a reader should have felt the real pressure points of the view -- that it severs morality from anything publicly checkable, that it acquits the conscientious persecutor, and that the community still, somehow, has to decide what to do about the burned library -- without the piece ever stating these objections outright.
