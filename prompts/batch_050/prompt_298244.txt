I hold a set of settled intuitions about when an advance directive should be honored against a patient's present wishes, and I would like to know what principle, if any, unifies them. Here are the cases, with my verdicts.

First case: a woman in her forties, of clearly sound mind, executes a directive stating that if she ever develops advanced dementia, no life-sustaining treatment is to be administered, including antibiotics for pneumonia. Twenty years later she has advanced dementia. She no longer remembers the directive, but by all appearances she is content: she hums, enjoys her meals, recognizes no distress in her life. She develops pneumonia. My verdict: the directive should be honored; the antibiotics should be withheld, even over her present docile indifference.

Second case: a man with bipolar disorder, during a stable period, signs a Ulysses contract specifying that during manic episodes he is to be hospitalized and medicated even over his own protests, and that any revocation of the contract issued during mania is void. A manic episode arrives; he refuses medication and demands the contract be torn up. My verdict: enforce the contract. This seems to me the clearest case of all.

Third case: a man raised in a faith that forbids blood transfusion signs, at twenty-five, a directive refusing transfusion under all circumstances. Over the following decades he leaves the faith entirely and tells friends, repeatedly and in writing on social media, that he now regards his earlier refusal as a mistake, but he never formally updates the document. At forty-five he lies unconscious after an accident; a transfusion would save him. My verdict: transfuse. The directive should not bind him, even though the woman in the first case's directive binds her.

Fourth case: a young man suffering a severe depressive episode writes a directive refusing all future psychiatric treatment, including involuntary commitment, on the grounds that he never wants his suffering prolonged. His psychiatrist at the time documents the depression but also documents that the man meets formal criteria for decision-making capacity. Two years later, recovered, he is grateful the directive was never tested; then, in a relapse, he attempts suicide and invokes the directive. My verdict: override it. A directive written from inside the very condition it purports to govern does not carry the authority of the healthy self.

Fifth case: an accident leaves a man with locked-in syndrome. Years earlier he had written that he would not wish to be kept alive if ever paralyzed and dependent. He can now communicate by eye movement, and he signals, unambiguously and consistently, that he wants to live. My verdict: honor his present wish, trivially — though I note this seems to me to differ only in degree, not in kind, from the dementia case, where the present wish cannot be articulated, and I am not comfortable saying the difference in outcome rests entirely on articulability.

My trouble is the seam between the first case and the third. In both, the directive is stale; in both, the person would not now endorse it if asked in the only way they can be asked; yet I honor one and override the other. My tentative principle is something like this: a directive governs insofar as the person now lacks the capacity to form a new settled preference on the matter, and yields insofar as some successor of the person — even a compromised one — holds an actual, currently held preference that the earlier self failed to anticipate. But this principle, as stated, appears to get the first case wrong (the contented dementia patient arguably holds a preference for continued life) and the fourth case wrong in the opposite direction (the relapsed man's preference is actual and current, yet I want it overridden).

Please do the following. First, state the most plausible principle — or, if necessary, a principle with explicitly motivated clauses — that captures as many of my five verdicts as possible, and identify which verdict motivates each clause. Second, tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should abandon the verdict or complicate the principle. Third, say what your principle implies about two cases I have not listed: a dementia patient whose directive refuses treatment but who now, in her confusion, actively begs a nurse not to let her die; and a directive, written in health, requesting that after death one's body not be used for organ retrieval, which the person's adult children now wish to override on the grounds that their parent would have changed their mind. Finally, if my tentative principle can be repaired rather than replaced — perhaps by distinguishing preferences that express a settled conception of one's life from preferences that are merely occurrent, in something like the spirit of the distinction philosophers have drawn between critical and experiential interests — please attempt the repair and show where it still strains.
