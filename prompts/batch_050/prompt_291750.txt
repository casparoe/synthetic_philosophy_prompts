so here's something that came up in my reading group. We were arguing about whether intransitive preferences are irrational, and the money pump did all the work: if you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, then someone can offer you a sequence of small swaps you pay a little for -- trade your B for C, your C for A, your A for B -- that you accept everytime, and you end up back where you started minus a stack of cash. A person with cyclic preferences is basically a machine with a coin slot, therefore transitivity is a requirement of rationality, end of story.

I want an audit of this as an analogy. What maps onto what: who or what is the pump operator (does the argument even need an adversary, or can indifferent nature pump you?), what are the coins, and what's the machine part -- the bit where the agent supposedly can't decline a trade they'd prefer to accept. Then the disanalogies, and please sort them by whether they actually threaten the conclusion or are just incidental texture. Some things I'm genuinely unsure about:

- the analogy seems to assume preferences are just dispositions to trade. Is that fair? If my intransitivity lives in how I evaluate options rather than in what I'd repeatedly do, does the pump still bite?
- does it assume I can't see the sequence coming? Someone who notices the cycle can just refuse trade three, or adopt a standing rule against ever trading back to something they already left. Does that make them rational, or merely an unpumpable irrational person -- and does the difference matter for the argument?
- the diagnosis seems to run through being exploitable. But being exploitable by a sharper adversary doesn't generally show irrationality -- I lose at chess to better players all the time and that's not a rationality failure. Why is the wallet case different?
- the punchline is diachronic (a sequence playing out over time), but the constraint it's meant to justify (transitivity) is usually stated as a condition on your preferences at a time. Is something smuggled in when we move between the two?

Then: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion -- intransitive preferences are irrational -- actually survive? Is there a version of the argument that doesn't lean on the pump picture at all, and if so what does the analogy add besides rhetorical force? If there's a better comparison for what the argument is really doing, give me that instead.

And if my whole framing is off -- say the money pump isn't functioning as an analogy in this debate but as a strict argument with the trades as literal premises, or I've mislocated where the normativity is supposed to come from -- tell me that directly rather than auditing it politely.
