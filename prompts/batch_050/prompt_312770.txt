So I've been reading the usual EA/rationalist forum debates about wild animal suffering for years, and there's one argument that keeps coming up and never seems to get properly stress-tested: the idea that habitat destruction might actually be good from a total-welfare view, because less habitat means less primary productivity means fewer wild animals being born into lives that are (on some views) mostly short and full of horrible deaths. Everyone gestures at it, and then it turns out to matter enormously whether we're talking about clear-cutting vs. agriculture vs. paving things over, whether fragmentation increases edge habitat (and thus population turnover), whether lower biomass just means bigger individual animals rather than fewer ones, etc.

Here's the thing though: I have a half-formed suspicion that the whole debate is confused because it treats "amount of habitat" as the variable, when the real question is about which *successional states* an ecosystem spends time in -- early-succession ecosystems are r-selected bonanzas with tons of offspring mortality, late-succession ones support fewer but longer-lived animals. If that's right, "destroy habitat" and "hold habitat permanently in early succession" are completely different interventions with opposite welfare implications, and nobody's separating them.

Write the two-page proposal: the question stated precisely enough that it could actually be answered, why the standard "does habitat destruction reduce suffering" framing leaves it open, how I'd combine conceptual analysis with back-of-envelope population ecology (without pretending to do original fieldwork), what the deliverable looks like for people actually making land-use decisions, and what would count as the project coming back with "this question was ill-posed" as its answer -- i.e., is that a failure or a result?
