I'm a junior and I help run our school's history club, and my teacher said I can have one whole meeting (like 70 minutes, around 14 kids usually show up) to do whatever I want. I want to do something about how people use historical analogies, because it drives me nuts how every argument in our current events unit becomes either "this is literally Munich 1938, don't appease them" or "this is another Vietnam, get out now," and now people even say "Thucydides trap" about the US and China like it's a law of physics. Nobody ever checks if the analogy actually fits, they just reach for whichever one wins them the argument.

Here's the thing I want the meeting to turn on. Early in the Peloponnesian War book, Thucydides says this about how future people will judge Sparta and Athens from what's left behind:

"For I suppose if Lacedaemon were to become desolate, and the temples and the foundations of the public buildings were left, that as time went on there would be a strong disposition with posterity to refuse to accept her fame as a true exponent of her power. [...] Whereas, if Athens were to suffer the same misfortune, I suppose that any inference from the appearance presented to the eye would make her power to have been twice as great as it is." (Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War, Book 1)

So even the guy everyone quotes for the "trap" is basically saying: the record that survives is going to lie to you, in opposite directions depending on what a city happened to build out of. That's the feeling I want the club to get -- not me lecturing that analogies are bad, but them actually getting burned by drawing a confident lesson from a record that was rigged.

Design me an activity for this. What I'm imagining (but you can do better): something where the group gets fragments of a "historical record" from some past conflict or crisis, real or invented, and has to agree on the lesson of history from it, and then it turns out the fragments they got were a skewed sample -- like they only saw the Athenian ruins and not the Spartan villages, so to speak. Maybe different groups get different samples of the same record and come up with totally opposite lessons and defend them, and then the full picture gets revealed? I don't know, work out the actual mechanics.

What I need from you:

- Actual rules I can run: what materials I have to prep (index cards? fake newspaper clippings? a slide deck?), how people get sorted, what they're deciding, what the stakes are (we have a snack budget so I can do small prizes if points help), and a rough timeline for the 70 minutes.
- The moment where they feel it. There has to be a point where someone goes "wait, we were SO confident" -- tell me how the design makes that happen rather than me just announcing the moral at the end.
- A debrief plan that maps what happened back onto the Thucydides passage (who in the room was "posterity," what were the "temples and foundations"), and then onto Munich, Vietnam, and the Thucydides trap -- like, what does each of those analogies treat as the "ruins," and what got left out of the sample.
- What's going to go wrong when I actually run it. I guarentee at least one kid figures out the trick early and spoils it, and half the club will just say "so you can never learn anything from history, cool, nihilism" which is NOT the point.
- Be honest about the limit: this activity can show how a skewed record misleads, but it probably can't capture something about why Munich-style analogies are sticky even when people know the full record. Tell me which part of the problem the game can't reproduce and what I have to just say out loud in the debrief.
