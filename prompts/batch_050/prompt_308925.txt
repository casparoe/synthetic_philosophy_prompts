At the end of each term, the reading group I run on collective action problems closes with a poem rather than a paper, and this term the theme was what we came to call ratchets. We spent the last month on cases where the structure, not the participants, does the damage: fisheries fished to collapse by captains each of whom would happily accept binding quotas; professional cyclists who despise doping and dose anyway because the rest of the peloton does; teenagers who hate their beauty filters and apply them because everyone else's face arrives pre-smoothed; universities where every dean deplores grade inflation and no dean can afford to be the first to stop. In each case the students kept reaching for a villain — trawler conglomerates, cynical coaches, platform executives — and in each case the villain dissolved on inspection. That was the point of the term: the tragedy is precisely that locally rational choices by non-villainous people compose a god that none of them worships and all of them feed, and that exhortation alone changes nothing, since anyone who unilaterally steps off the treadmill is simply replaced.

Write the closing poem: a villanelle of the standard nineteen lines, so that the two refrains can be the two thoughts the trap keeps forcing back into every participant's mouth — something like the private justification (I only take what the next person would take) and the collective consequence (and so the water rises for us all) — returning alternately and meeting, as the form requires, in the closing couplet. Let the tercets move across several ratchets rather than dwelling on one, and let the final stanza's work be the act of naming the structure, not of dissolving it: I do not want a rousing call to coordination, because cheap agreement is exactly what these cases preclude. Three constraints. First, the word Moloch may appear in the title and nowhere else; the god should be visible only in the mechanics. Second, no stanza may blame a person; if a captain or a dean speaks, they must be right about their own situation. Third, the refrains should shift slightly in meaning with each return, the way a justification curdles over the course of a life into something closer to an epitaph. Someone who has thought about multipolar traps should recognize the logic in the repetitions; someone who hasn't should still read a good poem about complicity.
