Trusting the Driver You Will Become

The absent-minded driver problem, introduced by Michele Piccione and Ariel Rubinstein in the late 1990s, runs as follows. A driver is heading home on a road with two exits. Taking the first exit pays 0; taking the second pays 4; missing both pays 1. The driver is absent-minded: he cannot remember whether he has already passed an exit, and the two intersections look identical to him. Before setting out, he calculates. If he exits with probability p at each intersection, his expected payoff is (1−p)(1+3p), which is maximized at p = 1/3, yielding an expected payoff of 4/3. So the plan is: continue with probability 2/3.

But now the driver reaches an intersection. On the plan's own probabilities, there is a 3/5 chance that this is the first exit. Sitting there, the plan he calculated in his garage no longer looks obviously binding. His choice right now governs only what happens from this moment; the planner in the garage was choosing for someone who had not yet arrived anywhere. Should the driver honor the plan, or re-optimize on the spot? The literature divides on how, and even whether, plan-choice and at-the-wheel choice can be reconciled.

This essay defends three claims. First, the appearance of a fresh deliberation at the intersection is an illusion generated by self-locating uncertainty. Arriving at an intersection is a probability-one event: the driver knew in the garage, with certainty, that he would find himself at an intersection with no idea which one. Since nothing epistemically new has occurred, there is nothing on which to update, and re-optimizing in response to no news is not rationality but noise. Call this the no-news defense of plan-stability.

Second, the no-news defense is nonetheless incomplete. It shows the planner loses nothing by trusting his future self, but it does not show the driver at the wheel gains anything by complying, because the driver at the wheel cannot control what his counterpart does at the other intersection. The correct moral is that rationality in sequential problems with imperfect recall attaches to dispositions rather than to moments. The question is never what should I do now, considered in isolation; it is what kind of deliberator should I be across all the occasions on which I will ask that question. Absent-mindedness cases push us from act-rationality toward disposition-rationality, much as Newcomb-style cases push us from choosing acts toward choosing decision procedures.

Third, I propose a test for policies in such problems, which I call the rearview test: a policy is rational for an agent with imperfect recall just in case the agent, upon completing the episode and learning the outcome, would endorse having adopted that policy at the outset, and would adopt it again on the next drive. A policy of always exiting fails the test; the p = 1/3 policy passes it; interestingly, so does always continuing, which suggests the test certifies only stability, not optimality. I argue this is a virtue: a theory of rational choice under forgetfulness should mark the difference between a policy one can live with and a policy one cannot keep, even when several policies are livable.
