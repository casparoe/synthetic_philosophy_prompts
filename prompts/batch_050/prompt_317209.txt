I'm a journalist at a general-interest magazine covering science and ideas, and my editor has approved a three-part series on futarchy -- the proposal, associated most prominently with the economist Robin Hanson, that governments should decide policies by looking at conditional prediction markets: bet on beliefs rather than voting on them, adopt a policy only when markets predict it will improve some agreed-upon welfare metric. I've spent two months interviewing enthusiasts (mostly economists and crypto-adjacent governance experimenters) and skeptics (political theorists, a few regulators, one former central banker), and my notebook now contains a tangle of alleged problems, and I need to decide which ones anchor the series. The candidates: (1) the conditionality problem -- decision markets only yield usable information if traders' prices reflect the world where the policy is actually adopted, but the market resolves in only one branch, so it's unclear the prices mean what the proposal needs them to mean; (2) the metric problem -- someone must choose the welfare measure markets optimize, and any legible metric invites Goodharting, which arguably just relocates politics from policy choice to metric design; (3) the manipulation problem -- actors who stand to gain from a policy's adoption or rejection can distort prices, and it's contested whether manipulation attempts make markets more informative or less; (4) the thin-market problem -- real markets on obscure policy questions may have so little liquidity that prices are noise, and subsidizing them creates its own distortions; (5) the self-defeating prophecy problem -- if the market predicts a policy will fail and so it isn't adopted, the prediction is never tested, so decision markets may be unverifiable in principle; (6) the political-legitimacy problem -- even a perfectly informative market may lack the consent-conferring character of voting, and a public that feels ruled by prices may simply refuse the arrangement; (7) the wealth-weighting problem -- influence scales with capital, so market governance may be plutocracy with better forecasting.
