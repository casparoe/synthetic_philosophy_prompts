In my seminar on theories of consciousness I have been defending a dispositionalist higher-order theory: a first-order mental state is phenomenally conscious just in case the subject has the standing, non-inferential disposition to represent that very state as occurring in themselves, whether or not any higher-order representation is ever actually tokened. I take this version to sidestep the regress and cognitive-load worries that plague occurrent higher-order views while still explaining the intuitive link between consciousness and accessibility. But I want to stress-test it before committing it to print. Please map this position's exposure: what would count against it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward? Include, among whatever else you think belongs, (a) which kinds of cases it allegedly cannot describe — for instance, states that are plausibly conscious in creatures lacking the relevant dispositions, and conversely states rendered conscious by a disposition that is present but systematically blocked from ever being exercised; (b) whether the dispositional retreat can be shown to collapse into a first-order account dressed in higher-order vocabulary, or instead into a form of panpsychism about mere dispositions; (c) what the view predicts that its first-order and occurrent higher-order rivals do not, and what empirical or introspective finding would distinguish them; and (d) which defeaters a committed dispositionalist would themselves concede as genuine costs rather than dismiss as question-begging. Present your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
