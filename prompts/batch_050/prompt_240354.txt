I am a novelist, and I have run into a pattern in my work for which I would like the proper name, if one exists. In a recent draft, my villain — a man who has ruined several lives through fraud and is never caught — suffers a paralysing stroke wholly unconnected to his crimes. My test readers reported a strong sense of satisfaction at this, and several called it fitting, even though nobody in the story causes it and the stroke would have occurred whatever he had done. The feeling appears to be one-way: when I gave a decent minor character an unexpected windfall, nobody felt that any account had been settled. Colloquially, my readers reach for the word karma, but as I understand the actual doctrine it posits a causal law operating across lives, whereas here the whole point is that there is no causal connection at all — the satisfaction seems to consist precisely in treating a random misfortune as a payment that was owed. Is there an established term for this configuration, the felt fittingness of causally unrelated suffering befalling the wicked? How does it differ from the just-world bias studied in psychology, from poetic justice as a deliberate literary device, and from a straightforward belief in moral desert? Does the phenomenon require the observer to posit some cosmic ledger, or is the bare feeling of fittingness enough? And where are its edges: does it extend to good fortune, to groups rather than individuals, or to people already dead?

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — name or no name, and if no name, your best coinage — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. American English, please.
