For a final-year undergraduate seminar I convene on rational choice (twelve students, a single ninety-minute meeting; they already know dominance reasoning, Newcomb's problem, and the smoking lesion), I want an activity built around the following passage from Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares, "Functional Decision Theory: A New Theory of Instrumental Rationality" (2017):

"A functional decision theorist holds that a rational agent is one who follows a decision procedure that asks "Which output of this decision procedure causes the best outcome?", as opposed to "Which physical act of mine causes the best outcome?" (the question corresponding to CDT) or "Which physical act of mine would be the best news to hear I took?" (the question corresponding to EDT)."

Design an in-class activity that makes students feel, in their own choices, the difference between the three questions the passage distinguishes -- before any of the theories are named. The activity should dramatise two dilemmas, which I state here so you need not rely on prior familiarity:

First, the counterfactual mugging. A perfectly reliable predictor flips a fair coin. Had it landed heads, you would have received a large prize -- but only if the predictor foresaw that you would pay a small sum in the tails case. It landed tails. You are now asked for the small sum, and paying buys you nothing in the actual world. Paying is what an agent would do who had adopted, in advance, the policy that maximises expected return across both branches; refusing is what seems obviously right once you know which branch you are in.

Second, the XOR letter. Each student has a house that is either safe or infested with termites (infestation is bad, costly, and independent of anything anyone does). A reliable predictor sends some students a letter: "Pay me a fee. I send this letter either to people whose houses are infested, or to people whose houses are safe but who would pay upon receiving the letter -- never both, never neither." Paying is excellent news about your house and terrible news about your wallet; refusing is the reverse.

The deliverable: two staged games with real (trivial) stakes -- points redeemable for something students mildly want -- such that (a) in the first game students privately commit to a policy before the coin's outcome is revealed, then discover that the facilitator has arranged matters so that every one of them occupies the "pay" branch, and must decide whether to honour their own policy at cost to themselves; and (b) in the second game hidden house states and a genuinely trustworthy facilitator-predictor generate letters by the XOR rule, so that students who pay discover, at the reveal, exactly what their payment bought. Give complete rules, materials, exact payoff numbers and timings, the facilitator's scripted wording (including how to make the predictor's reliability credible without magic), and the mechanism by which policy-following students end up better off across runs than branch-reasoning students in the first game, while in the second game the same policy instinct visibly misfires.

Then a debrief plan that maps specific moments -- the moment of being asked to pay for a benefit one never received; the moment of welcoming the letter's arrival -- onto the three questions in the passage, and that lets the class articulate the tension themselves: the question that wins the mugging is the question that invites the letter. No student should hear the terms "causal", "evidential", or any theory's name until this point. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run each game (students treating the single-shot case as iterated, students refusing to take the counterfactual branch seriously, someone naming the theories early) and how to handle it, and be candid about what the activity cannot reproduce -- perfect prediction, genuine counterfactual selves -- and what I must therefore supply by stipulation in the debrief.

Begin your answer with a single sentence stating the central mechanism of the activity -- the design choice that does the real work -- and then give the full design, with no preamble and no restating of this brief. Answer in British English, in roughly 1,200 words; the quoted passage does not count towards that length.
