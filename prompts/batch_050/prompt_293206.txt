For the past three years I have worked from home as a freelance copy editor, and it has slowly dawned on me that nearly all the feedback I receive about myself — not just about my work, but about how I come across — arrives through a screen: engagement figures on posts, short comments from people who follow me for one narrow professional reason, and the occasional email. I have been reading Adam Smith's *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* for a reading group, and one passage in Part III, Chapter I ("Of the Principle of Self-approbation and of Self-disapprobation") has been nagging at me. Smith writes:

"Were it possible that a human creature could grow up to manhood in some solitary place, without any communication with his own species, he could no more think of his own character, of the propriety or demerit of his own sentiments and conduct, of the beauty or deformity of his own mind, than of the beauty or deformity of his own face. All these are objects which he cannot easily see, which naturally he does not look at, and with regard to which he is provided with no mirror which can present them to his view. Bring him into society, and he is immediately provided with the mirror which he wanted before. It is placed in the countenance and behaviour of those he lives with, which always mark when they enter into, and when they disapprove of his sentiments; and it is here that he first views the propriety and impropriety of his own passions, the beauty and deformity of his own mind."

What I would like is a careful account of the most important implications of this passage — specifically of the mirror analogy — for how someone in my situation should organise their habits of self-assessment. Please derive the implications from the passage's own logic rather than offering generic observations about social media. In particular: what does the analogy presuppose about the quality of the mirror — that is, about which spectators, presenting what kind of reactions, actually furnish the image of oneself Smith describes? Does a dashboard of likes, or an audience that has self-selected around one of my interests, function as that kind of mirror, or does the passage give grounds for treating these as a systematically warped or partial reflection? What does the passage imply about whether I should be curating the company whose countenance and behaviour I expose myself to, and in what sense, if any, a person can choose their mirrors rather than simply being handed them? Finally, I am unsure whether Smith's picture leaves any room for an internalised, well-trained impartial spectator to correct for distorted external mirrors, or whether it instead makes us more or less hostage to whatever mirrors happen to be at hand — please say which reading of the passage you think is right, and why.
