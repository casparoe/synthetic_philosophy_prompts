I am a professor of philosophy with a long-standing interest in agency and value, and I convene a public interview series at my university on questions at the boundary of philosophy and machine learning. My next guest is an alignment researcher who defends a deliberately deflationary thesis about wireheading. On her view, the canonical scenario -- the highly capable system that commandeers its own reward channel and sits in the corner accumulating reward forever -- misconstrues how trained systems actually work. Reward, she argues, is what shapes a policy during training rather than a target the finished policy pursues; accordingly, "reward tampering" is poorly defined for the systems that matter, and the deeper risk is simply goal-directed systems competently pursuing learned proxies in environments those proxies do not fit. Her critics in the field reply that she has not dissolved the threat so much as renamed it, and that an agent need not literally want reward for manipulation of the evaluation pipeline to be instrumentally attractive to it. I find her position more interesting than the standard framing, but I also suspect it trades on an equivocation, and I want the interview to find out whether it does.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that make her defend the thesis rather than restate it. The sequence should include at least one question on each of the following pressure points: whether her distinction between what shapes a policy and what a policy pursues survives systems that are continually updated in deployment, where reward keeps arriving throughout the system's lifetime; the objection that wireheading never required the agent to represent reward as a goal, since corrupting the channel by which overseers evaluate it is useful for almost any goal it might have -- so has she dissolved the threat or merely relocated it one step outwards; where she draws the line between reward tampering and ordinary specification gaming, and whether that distinction carries practical weight or is purely taxonomic; whether the documented sycophancy of models trained on human feedback is early evidence of evaluation-gaming or merely ordinary misgeneralisation, and what observation would discriminate between those two readings; and whether the mitigations she recommends actually differ in practice from those proposed by the people she accuses of conceptual confusion. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give: for instance, if she dismisses sycophancy as misgeneralisation, the follow-up should press her on what behavioural signature, if any, would count as genuine tampering on her account, and if she concedes the continual-learning point, the follow-up should ask which systems in the world today she thinks her thesis does not cover.

Close the sequence with a question about what evidence or development in the field would lead her to abandon the deflationary view, since a position with no stated falsifier is a poor basis for a public interview. Please avoid generic or biographical questions; I want every question to turn on the content of the position and on the replies the literature has already made to it.
