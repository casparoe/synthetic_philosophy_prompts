I recently promised my dying sister that I would keep her small neighborhood archive in the family and not sell it. After her death, a buyer offered enough to pay for her daughter’s urgent medical treatment. My sister left no executor, no written instructions, and no one who seems obviously entitled to release or forgive the promise. I am trying to state my position carefully and would like a consistency audit, not a bibliography or a history of promising.

My current commitments are:

1. A promise binds because it is a voluntary performance of commitment, not because it creates expectations, induces reliance, or tends to maximize welfare. It can bind even if no one has relied and even if breach would make everyone, including the promisee, better off.

2. The central wrong in breaking a promise is unauthorized control over another person’s future: the promisor transfers to the promisee a limited claim on the promisor’s later conduct. So release must come from the promisee, or from someone to whom the relevant practice assigns that authority; the promisor’s private judgment that breach would be better overall does not by itself extinguish the claim, though it may affect blame or excuse.

3. Valid uptake requires a minimally shared practice: capacity, freedom from coercion, adequate understanding of what is being promised, and a subject matter that is not itself seriously wrongful. Where those fail, there may be reasons to act as if bound, but not the standard promissory obligation.

4. Promissory claims are real but defeasible. Drastic and unforeseeable costs to the promisor or to innocent third parties can override the all-things-considered duty to keep the promise, while leaving residues such as apology, disclosure, and compensation rather than retrospectively erasing the claim.

5. A promise made with a secret reserved intention to break it if circumstances become difficult is void from the start, because no commitment was actually given for the promisee’s uptake to answer.

6. Death of the promisee does not by itself cancel the claim. Promises to the dying can bind after death even where no surviving person inherits the promisee’s authority to release, waive, or forgive.

Please assess whether these hang together. Identify the genuine tensions by pair or triple, distinguish them from tensions that only appear until the view is stated more precisely, and rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, say exactly which commitment has to be revised or abandoned, what the cheapest revision would cost, and whether the residue in commitment 4 is enough to preserve the intuition without keeping the conflicting claim. In particular, tell me whether 2, 4, and 6 jointly imply that my sister’s death converted a defeasible promise into an indefeasible one, or whether that conclusion rests on a mistaken picture of what death removes. Please end by naming the single revision that would clean up the most while doing the least violence to the case that made the question urgent.
