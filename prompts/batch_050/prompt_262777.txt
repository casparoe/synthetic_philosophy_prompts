I'm a university student taking a course in political philosophy, and I serve as the undergraduate member of my university's STEM outreach committee. This month we must decide whether to renew funding for our flagship recruitment program aimed at raising the share of women in engineering. The program's founding rationale holds that the low share of women in these fields results from unjust barriers and stereotype pressure, which the campaign exists to counteract.

At our last meeting, a member argued that this rationale rests on a misdiagnosis. She presented research on the so-called gender-equality paradox: countries that rank highest on standard indices of gender equality, such as Norway and Finland, have among the lowest shares of women among STEM graduates, while several countries with far weaker gender-equality records have substantially higher shares. On her reading, as external constraints on choice loosen, women disproportionately choose other fields, so the gap reflects a distribution of preferences rather than an injustice, and our funding should be redirected. Other members replied that preferences formed under gendered expectations are not evidence of free choice, and that economic insecurity in less equal countries may push women toward lucrative fields rather than reveal what they would choose under conditions of genuine security and equality.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend renewal, redirection, or renewal with revised aims, and defend the recommendation by carefully separating three questions the paradox leaves open: what causes the gap, whether the relevant choices are free in the sense that matters for justice, and whether proportional representation is even the right target for an institution concerned with fairness. Give the strongest version of the preferences-not-injustice argument its due rather than treating it as a pretext, since the memo will only persuade our undecided members if it answers that argument on its merits.
