A teacher at the Vedanta study group I attend explains māyā and the identity of ātman and Brahman with the rope and the snake: in dim light a rope is mistaken for a snake, the mistake produces real fear and real sweat, and once the rope is seen clearly the snake vanishes without ever having to be killed. He uses the image to argue that the world of plurality stands to Brahman exactly as the snake stands to the rope — projected by ignorance, unreal, and dissolved by knowledge rather than by any change in things — and from there he concludes that worldly gains and losses matter no more than a dreamt loss does. Please audit the analogy. Give the correspondence table first (what plays the role of the rope, the snake, the dim light, the fear, the moment of clear seeing), then the places the mapping breaks. I suspect at least two: that a snake-mistake is corrected by another perception of the same ordinary kind, whereas Brahman-knowledge is not supposed to be one perception among others; and that the tradition itself seems to grade a rope-snake as less real than the world rather than equally real, which would make the image overclaim. Tell me which disanalogies actually bear on his conclusion and which are pedantic, and if the world-is-like-the-snake framing cannot carry the claim that nothing worldly matters, say what the analogy can honestly establish and whether one of the other stock images — the dream, the desert mirage, silver seen in a shell — does the needed work better. If you think I have misstated how the analogy is standardly deployed, or that the audit cannot be run as I have posed it, say so plainly instead of answering politely around the problem.
