Two colleagues of mine were arguing in the pub last night about Capgras patients — people who insist a close relative, usually a spouse, has been replaced by an identical-looking impostor. (1) Mark said these patients can't literally believe the impostor story, because genuine beliefs show up in behaviour: if you truly thought a stranger was living in your house wearing your wife's face, you'd phone the police or move out, and most of these patients just grumble and carry on as normal. (2) Priya said that's too quick — beliefs can fail to drive action when other parts of the mind are damaged, and anyway the patients do behave differently, they're often cold, suspicious or frightened of the supposed double. (3) Mark replied that suspicious-but-staying-put is precisely the problem: it's half a belief, and half a belief isn't a belief at all, it's something else wearing belief's clothes. (4) Priya said his test would shrink belief down to almost nothing, since ordinary people constantly fail to act on what they believe — smokers who believe smoking kills, people who believe they'll miss the train and still dawdle. Mark said weakness of will is different because smokers feel the conflict, whereas Capgras patients seem oddly unbothered by the gap between what they say and what they do.
