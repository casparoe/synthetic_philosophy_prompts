A few constraints:

- It has to be realistic: no perfect predictors, no demons, no brains in vats, no stipulating "and the agent has a blindspot about their own knowledge" -- the failure of second-order knowledge should *fall out* of the epistemic situation, not be asserted.
- Keep it to something an ordinary person could actually face (a job interview, judging distance, a doctor's diagnosis, whatever) and keep the scenario itself to a paragraph or so.
- No appeal to the agent being irrational or inattentive; they should be a perfectly competent reasoner whose situation just doesn't let them know that they know.

After the scenario, walk me through the epistemology: show why the agent counts as knowing p (on a standard picture -- safety, sensitivity, reliabilism, whichever you think fits best, say which you're using), then show why they fail to know that they know p. Pin down which structural feature of the case is doing the work (I'm guessing something margin-for-error-ish, but tell me if you think otherwise), and say what happens if you remove that feature -- does the case collapse back into one where KK trivially holds?

Also, steelman the other side: how would a defender of KK (or of luminosity more generally) attack the case -- e.g., by denying the agent really has first-order knowledge, or by charging that the set-up sneaks in externalism about knowledge while judging second-order access by internalist lights?
