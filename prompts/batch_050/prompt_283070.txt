I'm a high school junior, and my philosophy club has been doing a unit this semester on decision-making under uncertainty. Last week our faculty advisor ran a betting game with two jars of jellybeans that I genuinely cannot stop thinking about, and the conversation that followed made me realize my gut reactions to risk are all over the place. I'm helping design a probability-themed booth for our school's spring carnival, so this isn't purely academic for me — I'd like to figure out what rule I actually follow when I choose between bets, so I'm going to report my honest verdicts on a set of cases and ask you to find the principle behind them, or to tell me plainly which verdicts have to go.

Here are the cases. (1) Jar A has 100 beans, exactly 50 red and 50 black; Jar B has 100 beans in a red-to-black proportion chosen by a rival club and sealed in an envelope. Bet $5, win $10 if you draw red: I strongly prefer Jar A. (2) Same jars, but the bet pays if you draw black: I still strongly prefer Jar A — and I already see the problem, since no single belief about Jar B's contents seems able to explain both choices at once. (3) The forecast says a 50% chance of rain tomorrow, and a friend offers me the same $5/$10 bet on rain, or the same bet on a coin flip: I'm honestly indifferent, even though a forecast's 50% feels like a completely different animal from a coin's 50%. (4) Bet on my school's basketball team beating our rival, where I'd loosely guess our chances at 60%, versus drawing red from Jar A at a known 50%: I prefer the team bet, and the vagueness of my own 60% doesn't bother me at all — the exact opposite of how I felt about Jar B, and I can't articulate why. (5) At a carnival, one booth pays a candy if you roll a six (a known 1-in-6 chance), another lets you draw from a sealed mystery box rumored to contain "some winners, maybe lots, maybe almost none": I'd pick the mystery box and I'd enjoy it more, which seems to make me an ambiguity-seeker for trivial stakes. (6) Imagine a doctor offers a well-studied treatment with a known 70% success rate or a newer one doctors estimate at somewhere between 60% and 80%: I strongly prefer the well-studied one, and this feels like the jar cases rather than the basketball case — but again, I can't say why the doctor's range is troubling when my own basketball guess wasn't.

My tentative principle was something like: prefer the option whose odds you can state precisely, unless the stakes are small enough that uncertainty is part of the fun. But I can already see it handles cases 1, 2, 5, and 6 while failing 3 and 4, where the "known odds" side doesn't win. So here's what I'm asking for. State a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — precisely enough that it could be programmed into the decision rule for our carnival booth's prize game; indicate which of my cases motivates each clause of it; and tell me what it predicts for cases I haven't listed, such as choosing between an index fund with decades of return data and a friend's startup whose odds nobody can state, or between two colleges where one's admissions rate for students with my profile is published and the other's is opaque. Most importantly, if no tolerably simple principle accommodates all six verdicts, I'd rather you say so and name which verdict you'd sacrifice and why, than give me a patchwork principle with a special clause glued on for every case.
