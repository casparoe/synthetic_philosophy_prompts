EXAMINATION QUESTION (25 marks; candidates had 45 minutes)

In the second of Robert Axelrod's computer tournaments, tit-for-tat again achieved the highest total score of any submitted strategy. A commentator draws three conclusions from this result:

(i) Tit-for-tat is the strongest strategy in the iterated prisoner's dilemma because it beats every opponent it is paired with.
(ii) A population of tit-for-tat players is evolutionarily stable: any mutant strategy would be met with retaliation and driven out.
(iii) The tournament shows that cooperation can also be sustained in a finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma of known length, provided the players are sufficiently patient.

For each conclusion, state whether it follows from the tournament result and from the theory of repeated games, and justify your assessment.

STUDENT'S ANSWER

The one-shot prisoner's dilemma has a dominant-strategy equilibrium in mutual defection: whatever the other player does, defecting yields a strictly higher payoff (T > R > P > S). The interest of the iterated game is that repetition changes the strategic environment, since current play can be conditioned on past behaviour.

Tit-for-tat, the simplest entry in Axelrod's tournaments, cooperates on the first move and thereafter copies the opponent's previous move. Its success is usually credited to four properties: it is nice (never the first to defect), retaliatory (punishes defection immediately), forgiving (returns to cooperation as soon as the opponent does), and clear (its behaviour is easy for opponents to read).

On conclusion (i): this is correct. Because tit-for-tat answers cooperation with cooperation and defection with defection, it defeats every strategy it meets head-to-head. Strategies that try to exploit it find their gains erased by retaliation, while cooperative strategies are simply matched at mutual cooperation. This pairwise dominance over the rest of the field is what produced the highest total score in both tournaments.

On conclusion (ii): this is also correct. In a population of tit-for-tat players, everyone cooperates and each receives R per round. A mutant that always defects gains T on its first move against each opponent but is thereafter punished, receiving only P, so provided the probability of continued interaction is high enough, its overall payoff falls short of what the residents earn. Since no mutant can do better against the resident population than residents do against one another, tit-for-tat is an evolutionarily stable strategy and cannot be invaded.

On conclusion (iii): this is correct in outline. The key variable is the shadow of the future. Cooperation is sustainable when the discounted value of continued mutual cooperation exceeds the one-time gain from defection plus the losses during subsequent punishment, which holds whenever players are patient enough. The tournaments illustrate exactly this logic, since the successful strategies were those able to maintain mutual cooperation over many rounds. The same reasoning carries over to a finitely repeated game of known length: if the discount factor is sufficiently large, the gains from sustaining cooperation through to the end outweigh the temptation to defect, so patient players will cooperate throughout.
