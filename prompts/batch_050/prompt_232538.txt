Consider three practices that coexist, often within the same city, in how we handle commemoration. First: when a statue of a now-discredited figure is removed from a prominent public square, it is rarely destroyed; it is relocated to a museum, a warehouse, or a less prominent site, as though the object itself remained valuable but its location had become the problem. Second: under "retain and explain" policies, a contested monument is left in place but fitted with a new plaque supplying historical context -- as though added text could change what the monument does. Third: many municipalities and institutions refuse to name streets, buildings, or prizes after living persons, waiting until death (sometimes a fixed number of years after death) before honoring anyone. Alongside these sits a pattern many find inconsistent: a university will rename a building whose donor's fortune came from the slave trade, while neither returning the money nor striking the donor's name from old degrees, endowed chairs, and archived records.

Take these practices seriously as data. What theory of commemoration do they collectively presuppose? For instance: does relocation-without-destruction presuppose that a statue's meaning depends on its site -- that the same bronze honors in a square but merely documents in a museum -- and is that commitment compatible with the "retain and explain" assumption that a plaque can alter what a fixed object in a fixed location communicates? Does the waiting-period rule presuppose that the judgment of posterity is more reliable, or merely that the living can still embarrass us -- and are those the same view? State clearly which rival accounts of commemoration (as neutral historical record, as civic speech, as ongoing endorsement, as aesthetic object) each practice quietly rules out, and where the practices are instead underdetermined, such that several rival theories would license them equally. Finally, if you find a genuine inconsistency -- a place where no single theory of commemoration could underwrite all four patterns at once -- say which practice you think should give way, and what replacing it would commit us to instead.
