ok here's one for the philosophy of tech crowd. In my seminar on the history of acceleration we spent a whole session on Samuel Butler's 1863 letter "Darwin among the Machines" (published in The Press, Christchurch, signed "Cellarius"). The bit our prof kept circling back to:

"we are daily giving them greater power and supplying by all sorts of ingenious contrivances that self-regulating, self-acting power which will be to them what intellect has been to the human race. ... If they want 'feeding' (by the use of which very word we betray our recognition of them as living organism) they will be attended by patient slaves whose business and interest it will be to see that they shall want for nothing."

She has this running exercise: she names an episode from tech history and we have to say whether it counts as the machines gaining ground on us *in Butler's sense in this passage specifically* -- not just whether it was a big deal. Her verdicts so far, and she's very confident about all of them:

- the self-acting spinning mule spreading through Lancashire mills in the 1830s, displacing skilled spinners -- counts
- the Jacquard loom and its punched cards replacing the draw-boy's and the weaver's pattern judgment -- counts
- welding robot arms taking over car plants in the 1980s -- counts
- antibiotic resistance sweeping through bacterial populations worldwide within a decade -- does not count
- Babbage's Analytical Engine as a finished paper design, never built -- does not count

Fine. Now shes promised next week she'll give us two more, and I want to get ahead of her:

(a) A near-future semiconductor pipeline where learning systems do the chip layout and verification, the fabs run lights-out with a skeleton crew, and each generation of chips is largely designed and fabricated by processes built on the previous one -- but the whole industry directly employs a tiny sliver of the world's workforce, something like one worker in two thousand.

(b) A global industry where hundreds of millions of people spend their entire working day hand-feeding and hand-correcting a big dumb fixed algorithm -- say, manually reviewing its output queue -- where the algorithm itself never changes, never adapts, and couldn't regulate its way out of a paper bag.

Predict her verdict on each, and tell me which strand of the Butler passage she's implicitly running -- the "self-regulating, self-acting power" strand or the "patient slaves" strand -- or if you think her track record commits her to some third principle I havent noticed, say what it is. Also: what single further case would actually settle which reading she holds, since these five verdicts seem to sit comfortably with both? I have money riding on (a) with a classmate, so give me your reasoning, not just the answer.
