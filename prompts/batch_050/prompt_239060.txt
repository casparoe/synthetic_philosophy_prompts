This came up at my neighborhood association last month after we voted down a storm-drain repair because most people misunderstood the engineer’s flood-risk report. Now there’s a proposal to give extra ballot weight to residents who pass a short quiz on the issue, and two of my friends had a surprisingly heated arguement about whether that would be fair.

Mara defended the idea. She said voting isn’t just self-expression: it imposes risks on everyone else. If there is a right answer to factual questions like whether the current drain can handle a major storm, people who demonstrably understand those facts should have more influence than people voting on gut feelings. She compared it to requiring a driver’s license before letting someone operate a car around pedestrians.

Dev said the comparison fails because political choices aren’t just technical problems. The drainage vote also involves trade-offs between paying higher fees, protecting trees, and preventing occasional basement flooding. A quiz might measure factual knowledge, but it can’t tell whose priorities are correct. He accused Mara of confusing knowing more facts with being better at deciding what matters.

Mara replied that the proposal doesn’t let experts choose the ends. Everyone still votes for the outcome they prefer, but ballots are weighted by answers to independently checkable factual questions. She said that is like informed consent: patients still choose their treatment, but they’re supposed to understand the risks first.

Dev answered that this just moves the power upward. Somebody decides which facts are relevant, writes the questions, and sets the passing score. Those choices can quietly favor homeowners over renters, people with more free time, or people comfortable taking tests. Even a genuinely factual quiz could turn unequal access to education into unequal political authority.

Mara said ordinary democracy already amplifies those inequalities through campaign messaging, lobbying, media bubbles, and confidence without knowledge. At least an open quiz makes the standard visible, and the association could provide free study materials and accommodations. She also said it is strange to insist on equal voting power in the name of equal interests while letting badly informed votes seriously damage those interests.

Dev responded that voting is not merely a method for finding the correct policy. Equal political voice is part of treating residents as equal members, and it gives the losing side a reason to accept the result. If some ballots officially count as half a citizen’s judgment, people who fail the test are no longer being governed with them, just over them. He added that weighted voting also removes an incentive to inform and persuade the majority, because the system can simply route around them.

Mara’s last point was that equal treatment doesn’t always mean identical input. We give testimony from witnesses more weight than guesses from bystanders, and nobody thinks that makes bystanders less human. If the association can protect equal status in other ways, why is the ballot box the one place where demonstrable ignorance has to count exactly the same as knowledge?

Can you adjudicate their exchange move by move, rather than just declaring one winner? For each of Mara’s and Dev’s points, say what it gets right, where it overreaches, and whether the reply actually answers it. I’m especially interested in whether the distinction between measuring factual competence and judging political values can survive Mara’s limited ballot-weighting proposal, and in whether Dev’s equal-membership objection proves too much if societies already restrict some consequential choices by competence. If both of them are missing a better democratic way to handle the same problem, mention that too.
