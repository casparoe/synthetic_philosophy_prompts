I'm teaching an undergrad social and political philosophy course this fall, and every year we hit Rousseau and my students split into the same two camps: half read the general will as totalitarianism-in-embryo, half read it as basically majority rule but nobler. Neither reading survives contact with the actual machinery (general will vs. will of all, the ban on partial associations, the Legislator, the civil religion, the whole business about being forced to be free), and I want something I can hand out that makes the distinctions land by making them laugh rather than by me lecturing at them again.

So: write me a parody welcome packet, about 650 words, from a fictional condo association called The Social Contract Residences, sent to new owners and written in the relentlessly cheerful voice of property-management copy, in which the association has fully implemented Rousseauvian governance. The humor has to come from getting the theory right: the monthly assembly where attendance is mandatory because skipping it would mean surrendering your freedom (fines helpfully listed in the fee schedule); the note explaining that last Tuesday's vote failed to produce a general will because too many residents voted their private interest in the parking-garage question, so they'll be voting again; the policy against forming voting blocs or a gardening club that coordinates positions; the founding consultant who drafted the charter and then, per policy, immediately departed and holds no office; the minimal but mandatory creed, no dogma beyond the sanctity of the bylaws and the community's right to banish. Keep it deadpan throughout — the packet should never once acknowledge that any of this is alarming, and the reader should be able to tell exactly what's being ironized without you winking.

Commit rather than hedge: take one definite line on the forced-to-be-free passage — I'd suggest the autonomy reading, freedom as obedience to a law you've prescribed to yourself, and let the satire show how thin that comfort gets when it's property management saying it — and stay consistent with it the whole way through instead of gesturing at multiple interpretations. Then, after the packet, add a brief note (150 words max, addressed to me rather than my students) flagging where you leaned on a contested reading of Rousseau and where you took liberties, so I know what to watch for if a sharp student pushes back and I don't want to have to improvise an answer about whether the civil religion bit was fair.
