so I fell down a rabbit hole after watching a video about this thing called Fitch's paradox. the setup (as I understand it): suppose every truth is knowable, meaning for any true p, it's possible that someone knows p. sounds harmless, maybe even trivially true. but take a truth like "it's raining and nobody knows it's raining." if someone knew THAT, they'd know both parts, so they'd know it's raining while nobody knows it's raining -- contradiction. so that sentence can't be known, even though it could easily be true. so some truths are unknowable. and then with a bit more work people apparently derive the crazy version: if every truth is knowable, then every truth is already KNOWN. which is bonkers.

the replies I've managed to piece together so far:

1. restrict the principle -- only apply "all truths are knowable" to truths that could consistently be known at all (truths where supposing someone knows them doesn't already cause a contradiction). "raining and unknown" fails that test, so it just gets ruled out of the principle and no paradox.

2. blame classical logic -- if you do the proof with intuitionistic logic instead, you only get something weaker, like "there can't be a truth that isn't known" in a double-negated way, and the intuitionist is apparently fine living with that.

3. the actuality move -- read knowability as "possibly, someone knows of the actual world that p." then you can know, in some other possible situation, that actually it rained and nobody knew, because in that other situation you know about our situation from the outside. so the weird sentence turns out knowable after all, just not from inside the situation it describes.

4. deny that knowing a conjunction means knowing each part -- i.e. reject closure/distribution of knowledge over conjunction, so the first step of the proof never gets going. (I guess this is the least popular one? people seem to think distribution is the one thing you can't give up.)

here's my question: is there a fifth way out that's genuinely different from these four? I mean really different -- not "restrict the principle but in a slightly different way than #1," not #2 with a different nonclassical logic, not #3 with a fancier modal operator, and not some combo like restricting AND going intuitionist. if you think there isn't a fifth one and the space of options really is exhausted, argue for that instead. also: whichever of the five you think actually works best, and is the paradox maybe just a language trick that dissolves once you're careful about what "knowable" means?
