I'm taking a philosophy of biology course where we're contrasting etiological accounts of function (an organ's function is the effect its ancestors were selected for) with causal-role accounts (its function is just what it contributes to the system's capacities). My textbook motivates the etiological view with the designer analogy: selection "designs" traits, so a trait's function is whatever it was selected to do, just as a screwdriver's function is what its maker intended. A guest lecturer pushed this analogy further, arguing that the causal-role account must be rejected because only the etiological view can make sense of malfunction — a heart that fails to pump is still supposed to pump, and mere contribution-talk can't generate that "supposed to." Could you audit the analogy for me? Please give me, in this order: (1) The Mapping — a short list of what corresponds to what (designer, intention, artefact, design specification, malfunction); (2) The Breakdowns — where the analogy fails, distinguishing breakdowns that matter for the lecturer's malfunction argument from those that are mere cosmetic disanalogies (I'd like you to consider at least exaptation, the first appearance of a beneficial mutation, and whether selection can set a standard a token organism falls short of the way a maker can); (3) The Smuggled Premise — what the analogy imports about a norm-conferring source that natural selection arguably lacks; and (4) The Verdict — whether the lecturer's argument survives the audit, and if not, whether a better analogy or a non-analogical argument could still get the etiological view its notion of malfunction. Roughly a short paragraph each, with The Breakdowns the longest.
