I'm part of a small reading group that meets monthly to work through puzzles in formal epistemology and decision theory, and I've fallen into the habit of opening each session with a short poem on the evening's topic -- a gimmick, admittedly, but the group has come to expect it and I don't want to let them down. Next week we take up the two-envelope paradox, and I'd like a pantoum for the occasion. For the newer members I usually include a refresher in the invitation, so here is the setup the poem should presuppose: you are offered a choice between two sealed envelopes and told that one contains twice as much money as the other; you pick one, and before opening it you reason -- call the amount in mine x; the other contains 2x or x/2 with equal probability, so its expected value is 1.25x, and I should switch. But the same argument would have been available whichever envelope you had chosen, it remains available after you switch, and it even seems to recommend preferring the envelope you don't hold before you know anything about either -- so it apparently counsels switching forever.

The form should be doing the philosophical work, not just decorating it. A pantoum repeats the second and fourth lines of each quatrain as the first and third lines of the next, and its closing stanza folds back into the opening one, so that the poem literally ends where it began; I want that machinery to enact the paradox rather than merely describe it. When a line returns in a new stanza, it should read as the same reasoning now voiced from the standpoint of the other envelope -- conclusion inverted, words unchanged. Somewhere in the middle the poem should pause on the step where the argument goes wrong: the calculation treats the amount in hand as a single fixed x when the two branches of the average concern different total sums, so that the x in 2x is not the x in x/2. I don't want the poem to pretend that this observation settles every version of the puzzle, though; the final stanza, as the opening line comes back around, should let the pull of the switching intuition be felt one last time rather than smugly dissolved.

Four or five quatrains, please. Several members of the group are mathematicians with little patience for loose talk, so the expected-value step and the equivocation need to be precise enough to survive them -- someone who knows the paradox should be able to point to the exact line where the sleight of hand happens. At the same time, the poem has to work as a poem for the members who haven't encountered the problem before, so the technical content should live inside the imagery rather than be pasted on top of it. A title is welcome but not required.
