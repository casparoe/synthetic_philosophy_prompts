At a philosophy reading group I attend, two members — I'll call them Marta and Dev — spent most of an evening arguing about how to reason from the fact of one's own existence, and I'd like the exchange adjudicated. The dispute started from a thought experiment: imagine cosmology has narrowed to two rival theories. Theory One says the universe contains a trillion observers in total; Theory Two says it contains a trillion trillion. A philosopher, before any telescopes are built, reasons as follows: "If Theory Two is true, a vastly greater number of observers exist, so I am vastly more likely to exist under it than under Theory One. I do exist. Therefore Theory Two is almost certainly correct." Here is how the argument went, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Marta said the reasoning is plainly absurd — armchair reflection about one's own existence cannot settle an empirical question about the size of the cosmos — and since this conclusion follows from the self-indication assumption (roughly, that you should reason as if you were randomly selected from all possible observers), that assumption must be rejected.

(2) Dev replied that rejecting self-indication doesn't leave you with common sense; it leaves you with the self-sampling assumption (reason as if randomly selected from the observers in your actual reference class), which licenses the Doomsday argument: my birth rank is unremarkable among all humans who will ever live, so humanity is unlikely to last long enough to produce trillions more people. Dev's point was that both frameworks yield disturbing conclusions, so "this conclusion is disturbing" can't be Marta's criterion for choosing between them.

(3) Marta answered that there is a principled difference. The Doomsday argument updates on genuine evidence — her actual position in the human sequence, which is more likely under early-doom hypotheses. The presumptuous philosopher, by contrast, updates on the bare fact of existing, and she exists under both theories. Evidence that obtains with probability one under every hypothesis cannot discriminate between hypotheses; the probability boost for Theory Two is therefore spurious.

(4) Dev said this misidentifies the evidence. The relevant evidence isn't "I exist" but "an observer with my exact experiences exists," and Theory Two predicts vastly more observers with experiences indistinguishable from his, so it makes that evidence far more expected. He added that Marta's rule — never update when you exist under all hypotheses — would also forbid updating after learning "I am observer number 3 out of N" when the theories disagree about N, and that verdict is clearly wrong in ordinary cases of sampling from urns.

(5) Marta's last move was to say the urn cases differ precisely because there the sampling procedure is real: someone guaranteed to draw exactly one ball. In the cosmology case there is no lottery that selects which observers get to exist — all of them simply exist — so treating herself as a draw from the space of possible observers is a fiction that does the illicit work.

I stayed out of it and genuinely can't tell who got the better of the exchange. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it succeeds, fails, or partly succeeds, with a brief justification, and then give an overall verdict including what, if anything, both are missing. Please also state explicitly what consideration or argument would change your verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to break down. Keep the whole response under 600 words.
