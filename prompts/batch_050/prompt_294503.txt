I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I am finishing a reported essay on effective altruism and its critics. Before the piece goes to fact-check, I want to audit my own settled positions, since I state several of them in the article and will have to defend them in interviews. Here is where I have landed:

(a) When I decide where to give, quantitative cost-effectiveness analysis is the right tool: QALYs per dollar, GiveWell-style comparisons of bednets against cash transfers. For an individual donor, nothing better is on offer.

(b) Measurability bias is a genuine and serious distortion: EA's methods systematically undervalue interventions whose effects resist quantification, such as governance reform, movement building, and long-shot basic research, so point estimates of impact deserve heavy discounting.

(c) The institutional critique of EA is basically correct: the largest determinants of human welfare are political and institutional, and private philanthropy is no substitute for structural change.

(d) Earning to give remains defensible even in industries with significant negative externalities, say quantitative finance or fossil fuels, because the harm is largely fixed at the institutional level and would occur whoever holds the position; the relevant counterfactual is replacement, not absence.

(e) Longtermist causes, especially existential-risk reduction, deserve priority, because even minuscule probability shifts multiplied by the stakes of the entire future dominate the expected-value arithmetic.

My question: does this package hang together? I am not asking which commitments are fashionable to hold, but which pairs genuinely pull against each other, which only appear to, and where the cheapest repair lies. Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: the first four items should identify and rank the real tensions by severity, stating for each what would have to give, and distinguishing them from any apparent tensions that dissolve on inspection; the fifth should name the single revision that would restore the most coherence at the lowest cost to the overall position.
