I teach an intro philosophy seminar for first-year undergrads — about 16 of them, one 50-minute session — and we're doing a week on rational choice. I want an in-class game built around that old story where a servant bumps into Death in the market at Damascus, panics, borrows a horse and rides like mad to Samarra, and it turns out Death had an appointment with him in Samarra all along and was only suprised to see him still in Damascus that morning. The bit I want them to *feel*, not just nod at, is the deliberative trap: once you assume your choice has already been anticipated, every option looks like the wrong one the moment you settle on it, and the more carefully you reason the more your reasoning was counted on.

So: design me a game that puts them inside that. It should run over several rounds with some choice analogous to stay-or-flee, a facilitator role playing Death or Fate with a prediction mechanic that can actually be run by a human in a classroom (I'm obviously not a perfect predictor, so you need to figure out how to fake the appointment book), and a points scheme that doesn't involve real money — tokens, a leaderboard, whatever works. Give me the full rules, materials, and a rough timeline for the 50 minutes.

The important thing is I don't want to explain the moral up front. The rules should be rigged so the students run into the instability themselves — e.g. realising that whatever convention the group quietly settles on can be exploited by Fate next round, or that being predictable is fatal but that deliberately choosing at random feels suspiciously like giving up on thinking altogether. Then the debrief should map specific moments from the game onto specific beats of the story, and ideally gesture at the wider territory (self-defeating predictions, problems where accuracy of the forecast ruins the deliberation) without me having to turn it into a lecture.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — students colluding, someone reverse-engineering the trick in round one, the points scheme rewarding the wrong thing, whatever you foresee — and how to handle it. And be honest about which feature of the original story the game structurally can't reproduce: the one-shotness, presumably, and the fact that Death never announces anything in advance.
