I edit a science-and-ideas magazine and we run a lot of longform pieces about the history of science and medicine. My writers keep getting into the same fight in the comments and in draft feedback: when is a piece unfairly judging historical figures by today's standards, and when is that judgment fine, even necessary? So one writer got called out for "presentism" for noting that a 19th century doctor's handwashing refusal killed patients he should've known better than to kill, while another piece straight up calls a Victorian eugenicist a moral monster and nobody objected. Half the time "presentism" seems to be a real problem, half the time it seems like a shield people use to dodge uncomfortable judgments about the past.

I want a procedure my editors can actually run on a draft -- a set of ordered tests or questions, not a vibe -- for deciding when a judgment of the past is illegitimately anachronistic and when it's legitimate. Ground it in whatever historians and philosophers of history actually have to say about this (I know there's a debate about the "Whig interpretation" of history and about whether historians can or should suspend their own standards, but don't assume my editors know any of it). The procedure needs to distinguish at least: judging someone's factual/empirical reasoning given what was available to them, judging their moral choices, and just *describing* the past in categories that didn't exist yet (like calling someone a "scientist" before the word existed). Worked examples for each, please, including one case where the procedure gives an answer that'll annoy me, and flag the spots where no procedure can substitute for an editor's judgment.
