Last weekend I got into an unusually long and unusually frustrating argument with a close friend about whether the United States is currently undergoing democratic backsliding. She said it obviously is; I said the term has been stretched so far that it now mostly functions as a way of calling governments you dislike illegitimate. The strange thing is that we barely disagreed about any facts. We agree about what has happened in Hungary under Orbán, about the judicial conflicts in Poland under the PiS government and since, about Turkey under Erdoğan, and about the contested episodes in recent American politics. Where we kept parting ways was over what those facts amount to. She treats Hungary as the textbook case and America as an earlier point on the same trajectory; I kept objecting that the trajectory story smuggles in a comparison class and a baseline that were never defended -- backsliding from what, exactly: the country's own recent past, some ideal of liberal democracy no country has ever fully instantiated, or a threshold below which a regime stops being a democracy at all and becomes what some scholars call competitive authoritarian? At one point she accused me of hiding a political disagreement inside a semantic one, and I had to admit the accusation could be run in both directions.

Since the argument I have been turning over a hypothesis, and I want it tested rather than confirmed. My suspicion is that "democratic backsliding" quietly bundles at least three different claims that travel together in public debate but come apart on inspection: (1) a claim of institutional decline relative to a country's own prior state; (2) a claim of norm erosion -- the violation of unwritten restraints without which formally intact institutions are hollow, even where no rule has been broken; and (3) a regime-classification claim, that a polity has crossed or is approaching a threshold into a different kind of regime. My hypothesis is that the debate stalls because interlocutors switch between these readings mid-argument, so that evidence relevant to one (say, formal institutional change) is deployed against a conclusion drawn under another (say, norm erosion), and both sides experience the other as obtuse. There is also a fourth question lurking nearby: who has standing to make the call -- expert-coded indices, domestic courts and electorates, foreign observers -- and whether contesting that standing is itself part of the phenomenon being measured.

What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict on the United States. Is the backsliding debate at bottom a factual dispute, a dispute over baselines and comparison classes, a dispute over standards of evidence (does norm erosion count the same as institutional change, and how would we know?), or a case where the label is doing expressive and coalition-building work and its contestedness is part of its function rather than a defect to be fixed? Please engage my three-claim hypothesis directly: say whether you think the bundle analysis is right, whether it misses something (for instance, whether there is an irreducibly forward-looking element -- backsliding as a prediction about trajectory -- that resists decomposition), and whether the "it's just a partisan label" charge is a genuine diagnosis or itself a move inside the same stall. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually constitute progress: a sharper taxonomy that separates institutional regression from norm decay, an agreed procedure for choosing baselines, a concession that the concept is essentially contested and should be used accordingly -- or an argument that no such progress is available and why. Please write in American English; I will supply any citations to the political science literature myself, so refer to positions and indices in general terms rather than by name.
