I'm a student in a second-year epistemology course, and we've just moved from Meditation I to the modern brain-in-a-vat scenario and the semantic-externalist reply to it. In the Veitch translation of Descartes's Meditations on First Philosophy, the dream argument ends with this claim:

"For, in truth, painters themselves, even when they study to represent sirens and satyrs by forms the most fantastic and extraordinary, cannot bestow upon them natures absolutely new, but can only make a certain medley of the members of different animals; or if they chance to imagine something so novel that nothing at all similar has ever been seen before, and such as is, therefore, purely fictitious and absolutely false, it is at least certain that the colors of which this is composed are real. And on the same principle, although these general objects, viz. [a body], eyes, a head, hands, and the like, be imaginary, we are nevertheless absolutely necessitated to admit the reality at least of some other objects still more simple and universal than these, of which, just as of certain real colors, all those images of things, whether true and real, or false and fantastic, that are found in our consciousness (cogitatio), are formed."

Please explain the bearing of this passage on the brain-in-a-vat debate, addressing three things. First, interpret it: is Descartes asserting a genetic principle (every idea is assembled from materials ultimately derived from reality) or a metaphysical one (some simple things must actually exist)? Does the dream argument, as he runs it, require both, and are they equally well supported by the painter analogy? Second, test the principle against the modern scenario: an envatted-from-birth brain has had no commerce with real hands, heads, or colors, so a simulation seems to falsify the claim that illusion presupposes real simples — unless the "reals" are simply relocated to whatever in the computer causes the experiences. Assess whether that relocation rescues the principle, and whether the deceiving demon Descartes introduces immediately afterward presupposes that no such relocation is possible, i.e., that even the simples can be fabricated. Third, apply it: the semantic-externalist reply holds that such a brain's word "hand" refers to whatever systematically causes its tokenings, so its thought that it has hands comes out true in its own idiom. Is this reply a descendant of the passage's principle or a repudiation of it? Note that Descartes uses the principle to secure the reality of simples while leaving composite judgments doubtful, whereas the externalist uses a similar sourcing intuition to vindicate composite judgments themselves — explain which use the underlying intuition better supports, and why Descartes declines the externalist's stronger step.
