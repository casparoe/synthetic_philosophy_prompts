I work in social policy and have been reading around luck egalitarianism -- the view, as I understand it, that inequalities are unjust when they trace to luck rather than to choices the person could reasonably have avoided. I keep running into a class of cases that the standard distinction between brute luck and option luck doesn't seem to classify cleanly, and I'd like to know whether the literature has a name for them.

The cases: the person who bears the downside of a gamble is not the person who chose it. A parent risks the family's savings on a business venture and loses; the child's education and prospects collapse. A couple freely chooses an austere, off-grid life, and their child grows up with drastically narrowed options as a result. In each case someone exercised exactly the kind of deliberate, informed choice that luck egalitarianism treats as forfeiting claims to compensation -- but the person who made the choice is not the main victim, and the person who suffers never chose anything. From the child's perspective this looks like brute luck (nobody chooses their parents' choices); from the parent's it looks like option luck; and the framework seems built for a single agent in whom choice and outcome coincide.

Does this phenomenon -- a bad outcome arriving at one person through another person's exercise of responsibility -- have an established name, and a literature of its own? If not, how should it be carved out? I'd want to know how it differs from ordinary brute luck, from the general problem of childhood disadvantage, and from third-party harms like a reckless driver injuring a stranger, where at least tort law has something to say. I'm less interested in a label than in where the boundaries would run: does the category extend to democratic majorities imposing risks on minorities, and does it cover the symmetric good cases too, like an inheritance windfall, which is equally unchosen?
