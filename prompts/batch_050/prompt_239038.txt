I'm a software engineer (mostly backend and ML infrastructure) with no formal philosophy background, and I'd like help pressure-testing an argument I've been making to my team. Please answer in American English, in roughly 600–900 words excluding the quoted passage below.

Context: we ship an LLM-based support agent, and our internal evaluation rubric has two tracks. The first scores behavioral competence (resolution rate, tool-call accuracy, policy compliance). The second is fuzzier: reviewers probe whether the agent "understands" the ticket — whether it can paraphrase the user's actual problem, generalize to rephrased cases, and so on. I've been arguing we should cut the second track entirely, on grounds I took from Darwin.

In Chapter IV of On the Origin of Species (1859), Darwin writes:

"As man can produce and certainly has produced a great result by his methodical and unconscious means of selection, what may not nature effect? Man can act only on external and visible characters: nature cares nothing for appearances, except in so far as they may be useful to any being. She can act on every internal organ, on every shade of constitutional difference, on the whole machinery of life. Man selects only for his own good; Nature only for that of the being which she tends."

My reading: Darwin is describing a selector with no grasp of what it's doing that nonetheless produces exactly the competences we would otherwise credit to a comprehending designer. Daniel Dennett calls this move a strange inversion of reasoning, and as I understand it Alan Turing's 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence makes the parallel move for machines. Generalizing: once you have a mindless process that reliably generates competent behavior, the hypothesis of comprehension is explanatorily idle — it adds nothing to the account, since the competence is fully explained without it. Gradient descent, RLHF, and our own eval-driven model selection are just such processes. So it seems to me the passage licenses the conclusion that asking whether our agent "really understands" a ticket is like asking whether natural selection really understands aerodynamics when it produces a wing: the competence is all there is, and the comprehension question should be retired rather than answered. Hence cutting the second track.

1. Is my reading of the passage, and the inference from it to the eval-rubric conclusion, actually sound? I have a nagging suspicion there's a confusion or false presupposition buried somewhere in the move from "the process that produced this competence had no comprehension" to "therefore the competence itself involves no comprehension and the question is empty" — and possibly another in how I'm reading Darwin's personification of Nature. If so, I'd much rather have the confusion identified than have my question answered as asked.

2. If the inference fails, what does the passage — and the strange-inversion idea generally — actually entitle me to conclude about systems like ours, and what does that concretely imply for whether the "understanding" track earns its keep in the rubric?

Finally, please state what would change your verdict: which argument, distinction, or empirical finding about LLM agents would lead you to a different conclusion, and where your own reasoning is most likely to break down.
