I have no academic background in philosophy, but I've spent a couple of years turning Pascal's wager over in my head, mostly because a close friend of mine is devout and we argue about it regularly. By now I've collected what I think are the standard objections, and I want to know whether there's one I've missed.

Here is what I already have, so you don't repeat any of it:

1. The many-gods objection. The wager frames the choice as one religion versus unbelief, but any number of incompatible religions can run the same calculation, each dangling infinite reward, and the reasoning itself gives no way to choose among them.
2. The voluntarism objection. Belief isn't a bet you can place. I can't simply decide to believe in God tonight the way I can decide to put money on a horse; at most I could try to cultivate belief over months of practice, and it's not clear the wager even endorses that.
3. The symmetry objection. A hypothetical demon who infinitely torments believers and infinitely rewards atheists has exactly the same expected-value structure, so the wager's reasoning can be run in the opposite direction and cancels itself out.
4. The infinite-utility objection. Once infinite payoffs are on the table, expected-value comparisons misbehave: any action with a nonzero probability of an infinite outcome has infinite expectation, so the wager can't rank belief against, say, a coin flip that also leaves salvation possible, and the decision rule stops discriminating between options.
5. The probability objection. The wager needs a definite, nonzero probability for God's existence, but I have no principled way to assign one, and converting my sheer ignorance into a small positive number feels like smuggling in the conclusion.
6. The character objection. A God who rewards mercenary, self-interested belief over honest inquiry would hardly be worth worshipping, so the wager's prize may be incoherent on its own terms.

I'd like one more objection that is genuinely different from all six — not a rewording, special case, or hybrid of anything above. In particular, it shouldn't be another enumeration of rival gods, another appeal to what belief is like psychologically, or another symmetry construction with the payoff signs flipped. If you can't produce one that survives that test, say so plainly rather than dressing up a variant as new. Assuming you do find one, explain what pressure it puts on the wager that the other six don't, then give me your honest assessment of whether it's any good, including the strongest reply a defender of the wager would make and whether that reply succeeds.

A note on how I'd like you to work. Take on the role of a philosopher with real depth in decision theory and the philosophy of religion — the sort of person who could run a graduate seminar on this material. Think carefully rather than grabbing the first thing that comes to mind. Before settling on an answer, brainstorm at least five candidate objections, check each against my list for hidden overlap, and discard the variants; show me that winnowing briefly, so I can see the runners-up and why they were cut. Then present the strongest survivor. No citations or bibliography, please — I'm after the reasoning, not the references, and I'll track down any sources myself.
