Is there an established name for the following phenomenon, and if so, how is it standardly treated?

When I attend to my own visual experience, the scene in front of me seems uniformly detailed and colourful right out to the edges of my visual field. I have run the standard demonstrations on myself — the ones where a large change to a picture goes unnoticed unless you happen to be looking directly at it — and I am now quite convinced that at any moment I am actually taking in only a small patch, supplemented by loose expectations about the rest. What interests me is that this knowledge makes no difference to how things seem. Even while I am actively thinking about the demonstrations, the room still looks rich and complete everywhere. The case feels structurally similar to a familiar optical illusion that keeps working after you have measured the lines — except that the illusion here does not seem to be in what I see so much as in what my introspection reports about what I see. It is as though the mechanism that tells me what my experience is like is stuck claiming more than is there, and no amount of evidence gets it to revise its report.

Three questions. First, does this specific thing — an introspective seeming that persists in misdescribing one's experience even after one accepts the evidence against it — have a name of its own? I am aware of nearby notions: the mistaken judgement that one's visual field is uniformly detailed is sometimes discussed as a kind of illusion about experience, and there is of course a literature arguing that phenomenal consciousness in general involves some sort of introspective misrepresentation. But I cannot tell whether anyone has singled out the persistence of the seeming, as opposed to the seeming itself or the false belief it generates. If there is no established term, please propose one, with a definition precise enough to show what falls outside it. Second, how does the phenomenon relate to ordinary optical illusions: is it the same kind of thing operating at a different level, or is the analogy misleading because introspection is not a sense? Third, does the persistence do any work in the debate over whether phenomenal consciousness is an illusion? It looks like a gift to the illusionist — a misrepresentation that refuses to go away is exactly what the illusionist claims introspection serves up — but presumably a realist can simply deny that the seeming misrepresents anything and relocate the error in my prior expectation that experience should be uniform. I would like to know which of these moves is standard, and where the boundary cases lie: for instance, would the seeming still count as a misrepresentation if I had never formed any belief about the uniformity of my visual field at all?

I will not be satisfied with a bare label; what I want is the carving — what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and which of the nearby concepts fits it best. Please write your answer in British English.
