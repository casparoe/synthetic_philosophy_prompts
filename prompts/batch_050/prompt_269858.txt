I'm in a philosophy of music seminar and we just slogged through the ontology-of-musical-works unit — the whole mess about whether a symphony is an abstract type that particular performances are tokens of, whether works are discovered or created, whether a recording is itself a performance or its own weird kind of artifact, and whether burning every copy of a score destroys the work or just everyone's access to it. For the end-of-term humor issue of the dept newsletter I want to do something with all this, and I've got a setup I think could be funny if it's done right.

Write me a deadpan exchange of letters, maybe 600–750 words total, between an insurance claims adjuster and a claimant. The claimant is an amateur composer whose house burned down, and he's demanding compensation not just for the manuscript of his symphony and the tape of its single public performance, but for "the symphony itself," which he insists no longer exists: nothing was ever written down but that manuscript, the only tape burned too, and the one person who knew it by heart was the claimant himself, who can no longer remember a note of it (head injury from a falling shelf — don't make it too cartoonish). The adjuster denies the claim on Platonist grounds: the work is an abstract object, created but not the kind of thing fire can touch, and what burned were mere tokens; besides, the policy lists "musical works" under non-physical assets excluded from coverage. The claimant's reply should smuggle in the nominalist position — a work just IS its performances and playable instructions, and with no score, no recording, and no memory, there's nothing left to deny coverage of. Then have the adjuster's supervisor weigh in with the annoying middle view that works are created abstracta whose identity is tied to their instrumentation, which he deploys to also deny the claimant's seperate rider for his nephew's kazoo-and-toy-piano arrangement of the piece: either it was the same work, in which case it's indestructible and not compensable, or it was a different work, in which case it was never on the policy. Every joke should come from someone applying these positions with total professional seriousness — type/token talk, how badly something can be played before it stops being a performance OF the work, whether a playback of the tape would even have counted as an instance of the work or just a re-run of the old one — never from making anyone stupid. Both sides stay deadpan and internally consistent, no winking narrator.

End with a formal claim number and a closing line that lands the nominalist's strongest point without either character noticing they've just conceded it.
