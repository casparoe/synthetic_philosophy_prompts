I write for a magazine that covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about philosophical disputes that never seem to move: the participants agree on the arguments, no new evidence is pending, and yet positions stay fixed. My current case study is the puzzle of imaginative resistance, which has been discussed for decades and in something like its present form since the early 2000s. The standard setup, as I understand it, is this: we can effortlessly accept all kinds of falsehoods in fiction -- time travel, magic, talking animals, even apparently deviant mathematics in the right story -- but something goes wrong when a story simply tells us, flatly, that some cruel act is morally right or that some obvious kindness is wicked. One camp (roughly the "can't" theorists) says we are genuinely unable to imagine such moral propositions as true in the fiction; the other (roughly the "won't" theorists) says we could imagine them but refuse, because accepting them would implicate us in endorsing a distorted moral perspective or in exporting bad morals to the real world.

My working hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis and not a conclusion, is that the debate has stalled because it is actually three disagreements stacked on top of each other and treated as one: a psychological question about what people in fact do when they read such passages, a conceptual question about what it takes for a proposition to be true in a fiction, and a normative question about what we owe the author when a fiction asks us to occupy an evaluative perspective we despise. I also suspect the sides may be talking past each other about which cases are data -- flat authorial assertions of moral deviance versus whole narrative perspectives that seduce the reader -- and that genre might be doing unrecognized work, since a comic or grotesque story can get away with what realist fiction cannot. I would like you to test this hypothesis against what the two camps actually say: is the impasse better explained as a hidden empirical disagreement about readers' psychology that ordinary armchair philosophy cannot settle, as a verbal dispute over terms like imagine and make-believe, as rival starting intuitions neither side can argue for, or as two groups answering genuinely different questions under one label? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, with reasons grounded in the positions as they are actually defended, plus a verdict on what -- if anything -- would count as progress: an experimental result about readers, a cleaner taxonomy of the cases, a reframe of the question, or a reason to think the impasse is principled and permanent.

One request about calibration: please separate what is genuinely settled in this literature from what remains contested among people who work on it. If the can't/won't framing itself is controversial, or if the empirical claims about readers have been tested with ambiguous results, say so explicitly rather than presenting a smooth consensus; and if my stacking hypothesis is wrong and the disagreement is really one clean dispute, tell me that instead of flattering the framing.
