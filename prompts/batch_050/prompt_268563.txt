I help oversee a small household-robotics project, and we've run into a philosophical snag that I keep turning over after hours. Everyone on the team agrees we want the robot to "get its job done without causing side effects," but nobody can say what that means without effectively writing down everything the user cares about, which defeats the purpose. I have fairly settled gut verdicts on a set of cases, and I'd like to know whether any principle captures them all, or which of my verdicts I have to surrender. Here are the cases. One: while crossing the room to fetch a cup, the robot knocks a vase off a shelf -- clearly a side effect it should have been penalized for. Two: while preparing dinner as instructed, it chops vegetables, boils water, and throws out the scraps, radically transforming far more matter than the vase incident -- but I don't think any of that counts as a side effect, because it's what it was told to do. Three: the robot stands idle while a visiting toddler pulls the tablecloth and the same vase shatters; I don't think the robot did anything wrong by failing to prevent a change it didn't cause, though I admit I'd praise one that did intervene. Four: on its way across the room, the robot nudges the vase six inches away from the shelf's edge to make its own path safer; this physically alters the thing it later gets blamed for breaking in case one, yet it seems perfectly acceptable, maybe even commendable. Five: suppose the robot does knock the vase over but then repairs it so flawlessly that no test could distinguish the result from the untouched vase; the end state is identical to case four's, but I still judge the knock-and-repair episode worse than the gentle nudge, and I can't fully justify why. Six: unasked, the robot gathers the user's scattered papers into a neat stack -- a change I find mildly helpful -- versus throwing those same papers in the trash, which I find catastrophic, though both are comparable physical rearrangements of the same objects.

So: is there one principle of "low impact" or "side-effect avoidance" that delivers all six verdicts? A few candidate shapes seem to be in tension with each other, which is exactly what I want worked out. Penalizing any deviation from how the world otherwise would have been handles case three nicely but seems to condemn cases two and four and to let case five off entirely, since the repaired end state matches the baseline. Penalizing only what the agent causes handles two, three, and four, but seems indifferent to five and utterly blind to six, since stacking and trashing are equally agent-caused. Case six especially makes me suspect that no purely physical or value-free notion of impact can separate helpful rearrangement from harmful rearrangement, and that any workable principle has to smuggle in at least a rough proxy for what the user values -- but then I'd like to know how minimal that proxy can be before the principle collapses into "just specify all of value," which is the thing we were trying to avoid. Please state your best candidate principle (or two, if you think the verdicts genuinely fork) precisely enough that each clause can be traced to the case that motivates it, tell me honestly which of my six verdicts it cannot accommodate and whether that verdict or the principle should give way, and say what it predicts about a case I haven't listed: the robot postpones its assigned task for forty-five seconds to prevent the toddler from breaking the vase, succeeding but delivering dinner late. Please write your answer in American English.
