For a school ethics club thing I got stuck arguing with my friends about whether compromise between value systems can ever be like actual gains from trade, or whether that phrase is just economics brain worms and every compromise is really one side quietly losing. Construct a thought experiment to order: a realistic high school scenario, one paragraph max, where two groups have to share one scarce resource (say the auditorium, funding, or a Friday slot) and want incompatible things for moral reasons, not just tastes. Engineer it so three views give different verdicts: a plain preference-maximizing view says the deal is obviously good, a strict no-using-people-as-means view also says it is permissible, but a view I half-invented called the integrity budget test says the compromise is legitimate only if each side’s sacrifice is the kind it can narrate as still being itself, not a down payment on becoming the other side. No trolleys, no blackmail, no cartoon villains, no perfect charity where everyone wins, and definately no just stipulate that everyone is happy.

After the scenario, show the reasoning for each of the three verdicts and point to the single structural feature that makes the integrity budget test peel off from the other two. I want the case to separate a real value-bridging compromise from moral offsetting, where a harm gets laundered through some third party or future people so the two traders feel clean. Then say what happens if you remove that feature: does it collapse into a normal bargaining problem, a coercive side-payment, or a win-win where no compromise was even needed? Finally, steal-man the person who hates the case: would they say the integrity budget is just squeamishness dressed up as principle, and what would my test have to concede?
