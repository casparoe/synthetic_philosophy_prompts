I teach an undergraduate seminar on economic history, and every year I assign the standard-gauge railway story as my canonical illustration of path dependence — and every year a student asks whether the story actually shows what I claim it shows. The bare facts, as I present them: George Stephenson's early lines (the Stockton and Darlington, then the Liverpool and Manchester) were built to a gauge of 4 ft 8½ in, roughly the width of the coal waggonways he knew; Brunel's Great Western, built a few years later, used a 7 ft broad gauge that by most engineering assessments was genuinely superior — smoother, faster, and more stable at speed; Britain's 1846 Gauge Act mandated the narrower gauge for new lines, essentially because narrow-gauge mileage already vastly exceeded broad-gauge mileage and interoperability mattered more than elegance; and the broad gauge was finally eliminated in 1892, leaving Stephenson's gauge, with no particular engineering justification, as the standard for something like sixty percent of the world's railways today. There is also the well-known folklore that the gauge descends from Roman chariot ruts, which as far as I can tell is just folklore, but which my students love because it makes the path dependence seem almost geological.

Here is what I would like analyzed. Take the theory of path dependence in its serious form — increasing returns, network and coordination effects, learning effects, and switching costs producing lock-in to an outcome that no one would have chosen de novo, in the style of Brian Arthur's and Paul David's work — and map the episode onto it carefully: who the relevant actors are, which of the standard mechanisms are actually doing the work in this case and which are absent, and whether the 1846 Act complicates things, since standardization here was legislated rather than spontaneously emergent. Then run the analysis in the other direction, because my student's question is really the deep one: does this episode support the strong claim that history is *locked in* — that an inferior technology won and could not be displaced — or does it instead suggest that some *single* standard was near-inevitable and only its identity was contingent? Specifically: in the counterfactual where Brunel's backers had laid track faster, would the world plausibly have converged on broad gauge, or would the narrower gauge's installed base have reasserted itself anyway? And does the weakness of the Roman-ruts legend — a story of lock-in across two millennia that turns out not to be true — tell us something about how easily we over-attribute path dependence, i.e., about the difference between "history mattered" and "we are stuck"?

Where I want intellectual honesty: the critics of path dependence (Liebowitz and Margolis most prominently) argue that alleged lock-in cases usually involve either no real inferiority or a failure to switch that is rational given the costs — that is, the lock is remediable and simply not worth remedying, which is a much weaker and less interesting claim. Tell me which grade of path dependence the gauge story actually supports: mere sensitivity to initial conditions, lock-in to an inferior outcome, or only a *remediable* lock-in that nobody remedied, and what the engineering-economics facts of the case (conversion costs, network gains from a single standard, the broad gauge's actual margin of superiority) imply for that grading. End your response with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that assigns the episode to exactly one of three categories — genuine lock-in to an inferior outcome, contingent-but-convergent standardization, or remediable lock-in rationally left unremedied — followed by one sentence of justification.
