I am a high school junior, and next month I will defend a fifteen-minute presentation at our district's annual civics symposium. My thesis is deliberately unfashionable: a state apology for a historical wrong is only meaningful to the extent that it creates enforceable leverage against the apologizing state. On my view, an apology that costs the state nothing and is designed to close the matter is worse than no apology at all, because it converts a live moral claim into a finished transaction while disarming the descendants of the victims, who now face a public that believes the account has been settled. I arrived at this position after arguing with my history teacher about whether symbolic gestures can substitute for material redress, and I intend to defend the strong version rather than retreat to something softer.

The panel questioning me will consist of three people: a history teacher who worries about judging the past by present-day standards and about where demands for apology ever end; a municipal official whose instincts are fiscal and legal, and who will think in terms of precedent, liability, and the taxpayers who elected him; and a community elder whose family was directly harmed by an officially acknowledged injustice and who has campaigned publicly for years for an apology of exactly the kind my thesis calls hollow. None of them is a philosopher, so each will object in the vocabulary of their own role, and the elder in particular may be my most dangerous questioner precisely because she wants apologies to happen.

Prepare me for the question period as exactly six numbered items, with no introduction or conclusion: the five objections I should most expect, ordered from most to least damaging to my thesis, followed by the question I am most hoping nobody asks. Each item should run two to three sentences and should phrase the objection as that particular panelist would actually put it, give the reply I should deliver in under a minute, and note candidly where that reply is weakest.
