I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have been invited to give a thirty-minute presentation at a workshop on improving factual reliability in journalism. The audience will be unusually mixed: about a third analytic epistemologists and decision theorists; a third statisticians, experimental psychologists, and metaresearchers; and a third senior editors, investigative reporters, and professional fact-checkers.

My working thesis is “accuracy-first, not accuracy-only.” I plan to argue that news organizations should make greater use of ideas from epistemic utility theory. In particular, reporters and editors should sometimes state their confidence in consequential factual claims as explicit credences; newsrooms should retrospectively assess those judgments using accuracy measures such as calibration, resolution, and strictly proper scoring rules; and accuracy should function as a constraint that cannot be traded away merely because a false or misleading claim is engaging, politically useful, or comforting.

I then want to extend the view to editorial decision-making through what I am calling an “accuracy budget.” Newsrooms cannot investigate every claim or cover every subject, so they should allocate scarce reporting time according to both the expected accuracy of the resulting beliefs and the costs that audience error would impose. Accuracy has priority in the sense that a story may not advance institutional or expressive goals by making readers less accurate; but among journalistically permissible projects, expected social stakes can help determine which uncertainties are most worth reducing. I will acknowledge that this last part is not simply an application of standard accuracy arguments for rational credence: it extends them from evaluating belief states to guiding inquiry, publication, and the allocation of attention.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the ten objections this particular audience is most likely to raise, ordered by a combination of likelihood and potential damage. For each objection:

- Identify who is most likely to raise it and phrase it in that person’s professional idiom.
- State the strongest version, not a caricature.
- Give me a reply I could deliver in about ninety seconds.
- Specify what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my reply remains weakest.
- Say whether the objection threatens the whole position, only the proposed accuracy budget, or only my journalistic application of it.

Please include objections about whether proper scoring rules measure anything editors should care about; whether accuracy dominance addresses inquiry rather than merely the assessment of already formed credences; whether stakes-weighting reintroduces the practical and political values the view claims to subordinate; whether calibration can reward caution, vagueness, or strategically safe predictions; how to score reporting when outcomes are ambiguous or causally contested; how to account for correlated sources and shared newsroom assumptions; whether explicit probabilities will produce false balance or public confusion; and whether the proposal creates perverse incentives to cover easily verifiable trivia instead of important uncertainty. Also include the question I should hope nobody asks, together with the least evasive answer available.

End by revising my thesis into the strongest defensible version you think survives these objections, and identify the one part I should cut rather than attempt to rescue. Do not provide citations, a bibliography, or a reading list.

Finally, if my central formulation is confused—for example, if “accuracy-first, not accuracy-only” conceals an equivocation between evaluating credences and evaluating choices about what to investigate—please say so plainly rather than complying politely. I would rather have the position challenged or reconstructed than receive an impressive preparation sheet for an incoherent talk.
