I'm a graduate student in philosophy and I've been invited to give a colloquium talk at a department where I don't know anyone. My working thesis combines two ideas that, as far as I can tell, have mostly lived in separate conversations. The first is the notion of an acausal threat: an agent claims to be able to hurt or help you not by doing anything to you causally, but simply by being the kind of agent whose reasoning about you correlates with yours, so that your compliance becomes evidence of its restraint (or non-compliance evidence of its spite) across logically possible situations. The second is the notion of a surrogate goal: an agent that cannot trust itself to execute a threat or promise in the moment instead adopts a simpler, cruder proxy goal — it becomes, by self-modification or precommitment, an agent that genuinely wants to punish defectors, say — so that its future behavior becomes predictable. My thesis is that these two ideas are not just compatible but mutually dependent: an acausal threat is only credible if the threatener runs on something like a surrogate goal (otherwise its threat is just a claim about its counterfactual reasoning, which it cannot make legible to you), and conversely, adopting a surrogate goal is only strategically rational in a world containing agents you can reason about but never meet, which is exactly the acausal setting. I'll call the combined claim "the legibility thesis": acausal pressure works only through agents that have made their dispositions simple enough to simulate.

The audience is the problem. It's a general philosophy department colloquium: mostly philosophers of science, ethicists, and one metaphysician, all of whom know mainstream decision theory well — Newcomb's problem, the smoker's lesion, Parfit's hitchhiker, evidential versus causal decision theory — but who have at most a passing, secondhand acquaintance with the rationalist-adjacent literature on updatelessness, acausal trade, and corrigibility, and who are somewhat primed to find that literature under-rigorous. There will also be one formal epistemologist who I suspect has strong views about whether "logical correlation" is a coherent notion at all. Nobody in the room will have heard my legibility thesis before, which means their objections will be to the components, not to my synthesis — but I need to defend the synthesis, not the components, because conceding too much ground on either component dissolves the talk.
