I'm a retired condensed matter physicist and about two years ago I started reading philosophy the way some men my age take up sailing. Lately it's been philosophy of probability, which at least feels close to home, since I spent four decades teaching students how to reason with error bars. I've been working through the literature around David Lewis's Principal Priniciple, and I've managed to talk myself into an argument that seems to prove far too much. I can't find the hole, which usually means it's somewhere embarrassing.

Here's the argument as I've written it in my notebook.

(1) The Principal Principle: if you know the objective chance that p is r, and you have no other relevant information bearing on p, then your rational credence in p is r. Chance is the expert, credence should defer.

(2) If determinism is true, then the complete state of the world a week ago, plus the laws, fixes everything that happens tomorrow. So the objective chance of any future event, given that information, is either 1 or 0. A deterministic world contains no honest intermediate chances anywhere — not for coin flips, not for whether it rains on my granddaughter's wedding.

(3) The Principal Principle only has teeth when you know the chance. Merely knowing that the chance of rain is either 1 or 0, without knowing which, tells me nothing about what to believe.

(4) But in a deterministic world, the only way to find out which of those two values is the real one is to find out the outcome itself — run the calculation from the full microstate, which just means predicting the event. Once you've done that, you don't need the chance at all; you know.

(5) So in a deterministic world, the Principal Principle never actually guides anyone about anything they could find out. Either the chances are intermediate (impossible, by (2)) or they're 0 and 1 and unknowable short of knowing the outcomes (useless, by (3) and (4)).

(6) Therefore: either determinism is false, or the intermediate chances working scientists traffic in every day (the coin's 0.5, the half-life of carbon-14, the meteorologist's 70%) are not genuine objective chances, or the supposed bridge between chance and rational credence carries no traffic in the world we actually live in.

Now, I don't want to give up determinism-friendly intermediate chances — as a working scientist I used them constantly, and if someone had told me the coin's 0.5 wasn't a real feature of the world I'd have laughed. And the Principal Principle strikes me as close to obvious; matching your confidence to the known chances is practically the definition of not being a sucker. And yet (6) says I can't have both of those plus determinism, and my physics training tells me determinism, quantum caveats aside, is at least a live option, and the quantum chances don't rescue the coin flip anyway since the indeterminism washes out by the time you're at the scale of a penny.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd particularly like to know why the offending step looked plausible to me, because it still does. And if the answer is that the coin's 0.5 can be a real objective chance even in a deterministic universe, I'd like that explained slowly, because right now I can't see how a probability can be both objective and compatible with the outcome having been settled since the Jurassic. If instead the answer is that the Principle only ever constrained ideal agents who could know the microstate, then I want to know what work it's doing for the rest of us, and whether that makes it more of a slogan than a principle.
