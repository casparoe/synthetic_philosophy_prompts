Two of my colleagues were going at it in the common room last week and I stayed out of it, but I'd love a verdict. The setup: a theory T, published last year, turns out to entail a result R that's been sitting in the literature since the 1980s. Nobody doubts R, and the authors of T swear they never looked at R while developing the theory.

Marta said R clearly confirms T: T entails R, its main rivals don't, so the likelihood ratio favours T and that's that. Ben said that can't be right — everyone already believed R with certainty, so P(R) is 1 and conditionalising on it changes nothing. If R does any work, it can only be via the *newly discovered fact* that T entails R, which is a fact about our logical knowledge, not about the world.

Marta replied: fine, then evaluate confirmation counterfactually — ask what your credence in R *would have been* had you never heard of it, or just delete R from your background knowledge and redo the sums. Ben objected that "background minus R" is ill-defined: knowledge is entangled, some of the remaining background might still entail R, and other bits only make sense given R, so there's no principled way to pick the deletion.

Marta's last move: if old evidence can never confirm, then two scientists who end up with identical evidence can be rationally *required* to hold different credences forever, depending only on the order they learnt things in — which she called absurd. Ben said that's just a fact of life once you drop the fiction of logical omniscience, and Bayesianism was never guaranteed to survive that drop.

Can you go through their exchange step by step and say who's right about what, and what if anything they're both missing?
