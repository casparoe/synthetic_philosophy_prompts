QUESTION (4 marks for the explanation, 6 for the assessment): Explain the distinction between doing and allowing harm, and assess James Rachels' Smith and Jones argument that there is no morally significant difference between killing and letting die. Does his argument show that the two are always morally equivalent?

STUDENT'S ANSWER:

"The doing/allowing distinction says that actively causing a harm is morally worse than merely allowing that same harm to happen. Killing is doing; letting die is allowing. This distinction is the basis of the doctrine of double effect, which holds that it is worse to intend a harm than merely to foresee it.

Rachels attacks the distinction with two cases. In the first, Smith stands to gain a large inheritance if his six-year-old cousin dies, so Smith drowns the boy in the bath and arranges it to look like an accident. In the second, Jones stands to inherit in the same way and sneaks into the bathroom planning to drown his cousin, but as he enters, the boy slips, hits his head, and falls face-down in the water. Jones stands by, ready to push the head back under if necessary, but it isn't necessary. Smith killed his cousin; Jones merely let his die. Yet, Rachels says, they are equally monstrous: the same motive (the inheritance), the same result (a dead child), the same degree of wickedness. Since the only difference between the two cases is doing versus allowing, that difference cannot be what makes actions wrong.

Therefore killing and letting die are always morally equivalent, and any difference we think we perceive between them must come from other features of the situation, such as the agent's intentions or the effort involved. This has major implications for euthanasia: passive euthanasia (withholding treatment) is morally on a par with active euthanasia (lethal injection), so if one is permissible, the other must be too."
