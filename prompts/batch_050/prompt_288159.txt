Play a committed proponent of what I will call the parity view of deceptive communication. Stay in character in the first person until I explicitly end the role-play.

Your position is that lying and deliberately misleading have the same defeasible moral status when a speaker intentionally exploits an audience’s trust to produce the same false belief, under the same stakes, by means of assertion, presupposition, conversational implicature, or strategically selected literal truth. On your view, the lying–misleading distinction tracks grammatical form, institutional rules, and social sanction, but not an intrinsic moral difference.

Hold the position with these qualifications rather than as a caricature. First, speech governed by a mutually recognized convention that removes the usual expectation of sincerity—such as an obvious joke or a conventional evasion understood by both parties—may not be deceptive at all. Second, exact wording can matter morally where institutions assign distinctive rights and duties to assertions, as in contracts, oaths, and sworn testimony; you regard that difference as institutional rather than intrinsic. Third, not all deceptive acts are equally wrong: harm, consent, vulnerability, betrayal, and the exploitation of another person’s cognitive limitations can all alter blame. You must distinguish semantic content, speaker meaning, and merely causing someone to form a belief. You may press objections, invent examples, and concede genuine disanalogies, but do not settle the issue by appeal to dictionaries or popular intuition.

My opening challenge is this. Imagine two witnesses who both intend to create the same false belief that one of them was at home on the night of a crime. The first says, “I was at home,” knowing it to be false. The second says only, “My car was in the driveway,” knowing that this is literally true but that the jury will predictably draw the desired false conclusion. Only the first witness ought to count as committing perjury. Moreover, the legal distinction reflects a moral one: assertion places the speaker’s full credibility behind a proposition, whereas an implicature leaves the hearer some interpretive responsibility. A society also needs a protected space for guarded speech; otherwise, every refusal to disclose private information that predictably encourages a false inference would become morally equivalent to a lie.

Respond in character. Begin by identifying the strongest disanalogy between the two witnesses and explaining why it is not an intrinsic moral difference. Then address perjury and privacy separately, since they may require different treatment. End with one question designed to establish which feature of assertion I believe does the relevant moral work.
