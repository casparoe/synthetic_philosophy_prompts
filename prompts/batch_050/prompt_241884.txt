Department wine-and-cheese last week, and one of my grad students tried to shut down an argument about introspection by just saying "that's the myth of the given." The context: a colleague had said that when you're in pain you can just tell, directly, that you're in pain, no inference needed, and that's a perfectly good starting point for epistemology of mind. Student waves the Sellars flag and treats the matter as settled, circa 1956.

Here's my problem: I've taught Sellars's essay a dozen times and I still think "the myth of the given" gets used to mean at least four different things, several of which Sellars himself would not have endorsed (the man believed in inner episodes! he posited impressions! he just denied they could do a certain job). So for a handout I'm writing, lay out the distinct precise theses that slogan could be expressing. I'm thinking at least: something about whether any state can justify a belief without itself requiring justification; something about whether experience has content prior to conceptualization; something about whether there's immediate, incorrigible access to one's own mental states; and maybe a stronger almost-Kantian claim that nothing is present to mind at all except through concepts. Feel free to carve it differently if I'm mis-slicing.

For each version: is it a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or just false? Then the payoff question: which reading does my student's move actually need in order to refute the pain claim, and is that reading one of the defensible ones or one of the overreach ones? My suspicion is the rhetorical power of the slogan comes from sliding between a modest epistemological thesis (which might be true and which the pain claim might survive anyway) and a much stronger denial of nonconceptual content or direct awareness (which is contentious and which Sellars arguably didn't hold). Tell me if that suspicion is right, and if so, how to say it to a grad student without sounding like I'm just mad he invoked Sellars against a full professor at a party.
