I'm a grad student TAing an intro philosophy of maths course, and next week I get one fifty-minute section to teach Benacerraf's dilemma to second-years. They've done a little set theory (so Zermelo vs. von Neumann ordinals are fair game) and they've read some Hume, but none of them have heard of Benacerraf, and most are nominalist-curious without knowing the word for it. My lecturer just said "explain the dilemma," but I think that undersells how easy this is to teach badly — half the explainers I've found make it sound like the problem is that abstract objects are far away, like distant galaxies we can't point telescopes at, which I'm fairly sure is exactly the wrong picture to leave them with.

So plan the section for me; don't write the lecture itself. What has to already be on the table before the dilemma can even get a grip — I'm guessing a working picture of platonism as the naive common-sense view of mathematics, plus some rough causal story about knowledge — and in what order, with the dependencies spelled out? Which example should carry the whole thing: I'm torn between the multiple-reductions case (the numbers as different set-theoretic constructions) and just our apparent knowledge of boring truths like 2+2=4, so tell me which one does the work, what it needs to have and to lack, and warn me if I'm conflating two seperate Benacerraf problems here, because I suspect I am. Then the misconception map: the ones they'll walk in with or form on the spot (abstract = merely unobservable, like a black hole; the dilemma only threatens maths and not morality or modality; Field's reliability version is just a restatement rather than a strengthening; nominalism dissolves the dilemma rather than answering it at a price) and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. And finally what I should cheerfully refuse to get into at this level — structuralism, the full menu of neo-logicist and indispensability responses — while signalling that the menu exists.

Format it under these headings, in this order: "The dependency order" (~250 words, what comes before what and why), "The carrying example" (~200 words, including why the runner-up fails), "The misconception map" (~350 words, the longest part, each misconception tied to the moment in the sequence where I kill it), "What to refuse" (~100 words), and "The exit question" (~75 words — one question I can ask at the end that shows whether they got the dilemma *as a dilemma*, with each horn independently motivated, rather than as a cheap gotcha against platonism).
