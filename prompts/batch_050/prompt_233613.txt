I've been arguing about this with a friend who does nursing ethics and we got stuck, so I'm hoping someone can tell me whether this is a recognised thing. The pattern I keep noticing: people take a single event and re-describe it at a different level of zoom, and the re-description flips whether it counts as doing harm or merely allowing it. Two examples. One: a guy poisons someone's tea and, when cornered, says he didn't kill anybody -- he put powder in a cup, and what the victim did after that (drinking it) was his own business. He's chopped one act into pieces so that the killing bit seems to live in the gap between the pieces. Two: a charity treasurer who quietly lets a funding commitment lapse, and when challenged says she never made any decision to cut anyone off -- she just made dozens of small, blameless spending choices over the year, and the money ran out where it ran out. She goes the other way: dissolves one big omission into a mist of tiny ones so there's no single moment where she "let it happen". Same trick in both cases, opposite directions.

I don't think this is just euphemism, and it's not the passive-voice dodge ("mistakes were made"), because the descriptions are often *literally true* -- that's what makes it slippery. It's adjacent to the philosophical problem of how you individuate actions, but I don't want a general seminar on that; I want to know whether this specific move -- strategically re-graining a description so a case migrates across the doing/allowing border -- has its own name and its own literature, and if not, I'd like you to coin a term, define it precisely, and give a couple of boundary cases (i.e., cases that look similar but shouldn't count). I won't be satisfied with just a label; the carving is the point.
