So I write novels for a living, and I keep tripping over something about how my own industry actually works, and I want a philosophy-flavored answer rather than the usual bar talk with my agent. Let me lay out the practice as I see it from the inside, becuase I think it's genuinely weird.

When my agent shops a manuscript, editors do two things at once. They read it and form their own judgment — that's the whole romance of the job, the editor who falls in love with a book and champions it. But then the actual money decision runs through "comps": the acquisitions people ask how recently published books that resemble mine sold, and the advance gets justified mostly off that. Nobody says "I loved it, here's a huge check." They say "it comps to X and Y, and those did numbers."

Second thing: everyone in publishing cheerfully repeats that it's a lottery, that nobody knows anything, that massive bestsellers got rejected by two dozen houses first. And this is just true — it happens constantly. But notice that the whole comp system presupposes we DO know something, namely that a new book's fate is predictable from the fates of similar books. If hits were really random, comps would be astrology and we'd price everything at the average.

Third thing, and this is the one that really bugs me: auctions. If two or three houses want the same book, the price doesn't just go up because of competition — editors explicitly treat other houses' interest as a reason to want it more. A rival bidding is taken as good news about the book. But by that same logic, shouldn't the nineteen houses that passed be taken as bad news? Nobody reasons that way about their own enthusiasm. My editor loves my book just as hard after hearing who passed on it. It seems like other people's judgments count as evidence exactly when they agree with you, which can't be a real epistemology... or can it?

So here's what I want: reconstruct the implicit theory underneath all this. What does the practice presuppose about when you should trust your own read of a thing versus the base rate for things like it? What theory of disagreement is baked into auctions — whose judgment counts as evidence for whom, and is the asymmetric treatment of bids vs. passes defensible or just motivated reasoning with a budget attached? And is the "it's a lottery" line actually compatible with comp-based acquisition, or is one of them just something we say?

Also, be honest about where the practice doesn't force any particular view — where several different epistemologies would license the same behavior and it's just underdetermined. And finally: if a publisher went all the way in one direction — priced everything off a regression model, ignored editorial taste entirely — what would actually change? And what about the opposite, a house that banned comps and paid purely on conviction? Would either one go broke faster?
