Three months ago my wife died, and at her memorial a friend who knows I read philosophy tried to console me with an idea from Douglas Hofstadter's 2007 book I Am a Strange Loop: that if a person is a self-referential pattern rather than a substance, then a coarse-grained copy of that pattern can persist in the brains of those who knew the person well, so that my wife in some literal sense lives on, at low resolution, distributed across the people who loved her. I found this genuinely moving and genuinely unclear in equal measure, and in trying to get a grip on it I went back to William James's Principles of Psychology, in particular the chapter on the consciousness of self, where James analyzes the sense of personal identity. One passage keeps nagging at me; I will paraphrase rather than quote it, as I no longer have the book at hand. James imagines Peter and Paul falling asleep in the same bed with their thoughts in common, and observes that upon waking each man unerringly reclaims his own earlier thoughts as his and is never even tempted to confuse them with the other's, because remembered ideas that are truly one's own arrive clothed in a felt warmth and familiarity that another person's ideas, however well known, never acquire.

Here is what I would like from you. James died in 1910, long before anything like Hofstadter's proposal was on the table, so I am asking for a reasoned reconstruction rather than a report.

First, does the criterion implicit in that passage — that an I is constituted by the present thought's appropriation of past thoughts it feels as its own — actually rule out the persistence of my wife's self in my brain? Or does it unexpectedly permit a limited version of Hofstadter's claim, given that her patterns now inform thoughts that I appropriate as mine?

Second, would James treat Hofstadter's assertion as a metaphysical thesis about personal identity, to be judged true or false, or as a redescription of the psychology of grief and memory that makes no identity claim at all?

Third, as I understand it, the James of 1890 insisted that psychology remain a natural science and stay silent on metaphysics, whereas the later James of Human Immortality entertained a transmission theory of consciousness and took psychical research seriously. If these two Jameses would answer Hofstadter differently, please say how, and explain which phase of his thought you take to be the more considered one.

I am not asking whether Hofstadter is right. I want to understand where James's own commitments would push him, and where they genuinely leave room.
