Here is the exam question:

"State the poverty of the stimulus argument for innate linguistic knowledge. Your answer should (a) lay out the argument's structure — what it claims about the child's input, what it claims about the knowledge acquired, and how the inference from the former to innate constraints is supposed to work; (b) give one concrete example of grammatical knowledge that is allegedly underdetermined by the input, making clear why it is underdetermined; and (c) present the objection you consider most serious and assess how much damage it does. Aim for precision: say exactly what is claimed to be missing from the input, and exactly what is claimed to be innate."

And here is the student's answer:

"The poverty of the stimulus argument runs as follows. The primary linguistic data available to a child — the speech she hears from caregivers — is degenerate: it is full of false starts, slips of the tongue, unfinished sentences, and outright ungrammatical strings. Yet by a fairly young age every normal child converges on essentially the same grammar, including knowledge for which the input contains no evidence. Since you cannot extract more information from a signal than it contains, the gap between the noisy input and the rich output must be bridged by something internal, namely an innate specification of the space of possible human grammars — universal grammar. So the argument is really an inference to the only explanation of how knowledge can outrun its evidential basis.

"The canonical example is the structure-dependence of question formation in English. To form a yes–no question, you front the auxiliary of the main clause: 'The man who is wearing a hat is happy' becomes 'Is the man who is wearing a hat happy?' A rule that operated on linear position — front the first auxiliary — would produce the impossible 'Is the man who wearing a hat is happy?' Crucially, the input contains no instances whatsoever of the relevant long-distance cases, so the child has nothing in the data that could rule out the linear rule in favor of the structure-dependent one; yet no child ever entertains the linear rule. The constraint must therefore be innate, part of universal grammar rather than learned.

"The most serious objection is that modern statistical learning theory and connectionist modeling have shown that rich structure can be extracted from input by general-purpose mechanisms, so the gap the argument posits may not exist. I think this objection fails, however. Any statistical procedure must compute its statistics over some representation — over words, or morphemes, or constituents — and the choice of representation cannot itself be learned statistically without regress, so the categories the statistics run over must already be in place innately. Moreover, the critical-period evidence clinches the nativist conclusion directly: cases of late first-language exposure show that children deprived of input early never acquire full grammar, which demonstrates that the grammar is a biological endowment with a maturational timetable rather than something the stimulus teaches. The poverty of the stimulus argument therefore survives its strongest challenge."
