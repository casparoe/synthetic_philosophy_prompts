I'm running an interdisciplinary reading group (half historians, half philosophers, nobody a Camus specialist) and we're doing a unit on whether non-human things -- germs, climates, volcanoes -- can count as actors in history rather than just backdrop. Our literary text is The Plague. I want a reading of the novel that actually puts it in contact with that debate instead of just using it as flavor.

Here's the tension I want worked out. On one side, environmental historians and Latour-ish folks say we should treat microbes, rats, and mosquitoes as actors or "actants" in their own right: in Oran, the bacilus is the one doing things, and the humans are mostly reacting. On the other side there's the standard warning that once you make nature an actor you start smuggling intentions into it -- environmental determinism, nature-as-judgment. And the novel contains that error in-house: Paneloux's first sermon reads the epidemic as deserved punishment. So, questions:

1. Does the novel itself grant the bacillus agency, or refuse to? Look at the dying rats at the start, the way the epidemic arrives and recedes without explanation, and the closing pages where Rieux says the bacillus never really dies and can rouse itself again years later. Is that a sober scientific point about dormancy, or does it quietly turn the germ into a patient enemy biding its time -- i.e., agency sneaking back through the ending?

2. Take Paneloux's arc as a case study in the perils: first sermon moralizes the plague, then he watches the judge's son die, and the second sermon changes. Does the novel stage the moralizing reading collapsing under the weight of one child's death? And is Paneloux's own death -- deliberately undiagnosed, unclassifiable -- the novel refusing to let even him be converted into a symbol?

3. Now turn it around: the standard allegorical reading (plague as occupation, fascism, the evil in people -- Tarrou's plague-within-us speech). Does that commit Paneloux's sin in reverse, subordinating a material non-human event to a human moral story? An environmental historian would note the novel's epidemic has no ecology: no climate, no vectors to speak of, no etiology, it just comes and goes. Is that a flaw or a philosophically interesting choice?

4. What does the novel get right about living under a cause that can't be argued with, negotiated with, or blamed -- and what does fiction show here that the historiography debate can't? I'm thinking of Rieux's refusal of any scheme of things in which children are tortured, and the sea swim with Tarrou.

Be honest about where the novel resists this whole framework -- I don't want it flattened into an illustration of actor-network theory.
