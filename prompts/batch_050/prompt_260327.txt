I'm a PhD student in economic theory, and I'm fluent in the vocabulary of cooperative bargaining and decision theory — the Nash bargaining solution, disagreement points, Pareto frontiers, transferable utility, choice under model uncertainty. I've recently been thinking about the idea of moral trade: roughly, that agents holding divergent moral views can sometimes all count an arrangement as an improvement when each side does more of what the other's view values, in exchange for reciprocal concessions — as when one party funds a cause her counterpart prizes, and the counterpart reciprocates, rather than each simply funding her own. A related, intrapersonal version holds that a single agent who is uncertain between moral theories can rationally trade across her own credences, adopting a portfolio of actions that each theory regards as a good deal relative to its own priorities.

Please restate the core commitments of this position in the vocabulary of bargaining and decision theory, and then report what does not survive the translation. Specifically: (1) identify the analogues of the bargaining set, the disagreement point, and Pareto improvements, being explicit about what plays the role of each party's "utility function" when the rankings are generated by moral convictions rather than tastes; (2) say whether the intrapersonal case is interpersonal bargaining collapsed to a single player or instead a distinct problem of choice under normative model uncertainty, and what turns on the difference; (3) state precisely where transferable "moral utility" is being assumed, and whether intertheoretic comparability of value is doing hidden work in the construction.

Then give me the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which translate only by distorting truth-apt moral beliefs into preferences — so that a disagreement about what is right gets re-described as a conflict of tastes — and which claims have no expression in this vocabulary at all. I'm thinking especially of the thought that certain commitments are not tradable in principle, and of the asymmetry between compromising with another person and compromising among one's own hypotheses. Finally, I want your verdict on the residue: does it mark a limitation of the bargaining framework, or does it expose a metaethical assumption — say, a common scale of value across theories — that moral trade requires but rarely states outright? I don't want a reading list or citations; I want the analysis itself.
