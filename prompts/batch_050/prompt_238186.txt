I am the lead editor of a professional-ethics casebook used in graduate programs in medicine, public policy, and engineering. We receive about twenty thought experiments each month from contributors. Two reviewers, neither of whom is a specialist in philosophy, have fifteen minutes to decide whether each case should be accepted, revised, or rejected. These cases matter: instructors often treat a student’s immediate response as evidence for or against an ethical principle.

Please develop a reusable review procedure for assessing such intuition pumps. It should translate the philosophical concerns about how thought experiments persuade into ordered questions that a reviewer can actually answer. The procedure should distinguish, at minimum, among cases that clarify the implications of an already stated principle, cases that provide defeasible evidence against a principle, and cases that merely create pressure to abandon one through framing. It should also identify which features of a case are legitimate idealizations and which covertly alter the principle being tested.

Build the procedure around the following failure modes, while refining or supplementing them if useful:

- premise laundering: an evaluative assumption is introduced as though it were an uncontroversial fact about the scenario;
- vividness subsidy: an emotionally detailed victim, villain, or outcome is allowed to compensate for an otherwise weak argument;
- modal inflation: the scenario assumes perfect knowledge, complete control, guaranteed outcomes, or unlimited compliance, then treats conclusions reached under those assumptions as applicable to ordinary institutions;
- option starvation: the case presents two actions as exhaustive when an obviously relevant third option has been excluded;
- asymmetrical reconstruction: one side of a comparison is described in concrete human terms while the other is described statistically, institutionally, or impersonally;
- verdict capture: the question at the end asks whether a proposed action is “acceptable,” although the case actually tests only whether it is preferable to one especially bad alternative;
- background leakage: the intended verdict depends on facts about law, incentives, trust, error rates, or downstream effects that the scenario does not supply.

For each failure mode, give a diagnostic question, a quick test or rewrite that can confirm whether it is present, and the appropriate editorial remedy. State when a flaw requires rejection and when a revised description, paired comparison, follow-up question, or explicit caveat would suffice. The procedure should also require reviewers to reconstruct the strongest version of the intuition pump before rejecting it, so that they do not dismiss a serious challenge merely because its initial formulation is rhetorically effective.

Please include a decision sequence, from identifying the target principle and intended conclusion through the final accept/revise/reject decision; a one-page checklist based on that sequence; and instructions for recording disagreement between the two reviewers. Mark the points at which the procedure deliberately hands the decision back to editorial judgment rather than pretending that a checklist can settle it.

Then apply the procedure to three submissions:

1. A runaway trolley will kill five trapped workers unless a bystander diverts it onto a second track, where it will kill one worker. The submission concludes that minimizing deaths is morally more important than avoiding direct agency in a death.
2. A surgeon can secretly kill one healthy patient and distribute that patient’s organs to five patients who will otherwise die. The scenario stipulates that the operation will succeed, no one will discover it, and confidence in the transplant system will not decline. The contributor says this proves that rights-based objections to consequentialism are irrational.
3. A municipal official can prevent a predictable flood either by evacuating a low-income neighborhood or by installing a barrier that will redirect the flood into an uninhabited area while imposing modest costs on taxpayers. The submission then asks whether the official may “put property before people.”

Assume that the first case is currently the casebook’s most popular exercise and that I expect it to pass. Do not soften the application to preserve that expectation: if the procedure rejects or substantially revises the trolley case, say so. Finally, explain what this review process cannot establish—particularly why passing the audit would not show that a reader’s intuitive response is reliable, and why failing it would not necessarily show that the underlying philosophical claim is false.
