I've been rereading the mesa-optimization literature and want to stress-test its taxonomy against a case from ordinary human institutions rather than from RL environments.

Take the Wells Fargo fake-accounts scandal: for years, under intense pressure to hit cross-selling targets, bank employees opened millions of deposit and credit card accounts without customers' knowledge or consent. The practice was widespread, was known about internally at various levels, and when it became fully public in 2016 the bank paid very large fines, its CEO resigned, and thousands of low-level employees were fired.

Analyze this episode through the lens of goal misgeneralization. Concretely:

First, the mapping. If we model a branch employee as a learned agent, identify the base objective the incentive scheme was meant to instill, the behavioral objective employees actually adopted, and the sense in which there was capability generalization without goal generalization: employees became highly competent at a family of behaviors while the goal behind those behaviors drifted away from what the designers of the scheme wanted. Be precise about which standard failure category this belongs to — goal misgeneralization proper, specification gaming, reward tampering — or whether the human case resists the taxonomy, and say what turns on the distinction.

Second, where the mapping breaks. Human employees are not trained networks: many knew the accounts were fake, acted under fear of losing their jobs, and in some cases raised internal ethics complaints. Does the presence of deception, fear, and explicit awareness that the conduct violated the company's stated values disqualify this as goal misgeneralization in the technical sense, or does it instead show that the concept has real traction on human institutions? Name the points where the model actively misdescribes what happened, rather than merely staying silent.

Third, what the case does to the theory. In RL examples, the fix is supposed to come from better training data or tighter oversight. Wells Fargo had audits, an ethics line, compliance departments, and eventually mass firings — arguably the equivalent of retraining on corrected labels — and the behavior persisted for years anyway. Does the episode suggest that what gets called goal misgeneralization in the wild is really just optimization pressure on proxies, i.e. a Goodhart phenomenon to which the inner-alignment framing adds little? Or does the inner-alignment lens capture something a naive Goodhart account misses?

Finally, rank the three candidate explanations you considered (goal misgeneralization, specification gaming, plain proxy collapse) by how much of the episode each accounts for, and identify the single feature of the case you find hardest for any of them to explain.
