David Hume died in 1776, long before the rise of modern physical cosmology. In the Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion and elsewhere he attacked the cosmological argument on several fronts: he denied that we can know a priori that everything which begins to exist must have a cause, he held that a necessarily existent God is no more intelligible than a necessarily existent universe, and he maintained that the causal principle is warranted only within experience and cannot be extended to the origin of the universe taken as a whole.

Since Hume's time, cosmology has produced developments he never saw: the discovery of the universe's expansion, models in which the past is finite, and speculative proposals such as the no-boundary picture, under which time is finite in the past yet has no first instant, so that asking what happened before the universe is as ill-formed as asking what lies beyond the highest possible latitude. Defenders of the kalam cosmological argument claim these findings vindicate the premise that the universe began to exist and therefore stands in need of a transcendent cause; critics reply that a finite past without a first moment is precisely the sort of unexplained given that Hume said a sound philosopher must be prepared to accept.

Write a reasoned reconstruction of Hume's likely verdict on this dispute, in your own voice rather than as an impersonation. Address at least the following: (1) whether a scientifically established finite past would, by Hume's lights, strengthen the demand for an external cause or leave his brute-givenness stance untouched; (2) how his own theory of causation, on which there are no demonstrable necessary connections between distinct existences, bears on the premise that everything that begins to exist has a cause of its existence; (3) how he would likely treat the no-boundary proposal, given his insistence that conceivability rather than intuition sets the bounds of genuine possibility; and (4) whether he would regard the kalam's reliance on the impossibility of an actual infinite regress of past events as decisive. Ground your reconstruction in specific arguments and doctrines from his writings rather than in generic skepticism.

Conclude with a single-sentence summary as the last line, beginning "Verdict:", stating whether Hume would regard modern cosmology as strengthening, weakening, or leaving unchanged the case for a first cause of the universe.
