I co-run an informal philosophy circle at the community college where I work in IT, and our next meeting grew out of an argument that started in my own head. I've noticed that when my code breaks I sometimes rerun the exact same build command five or six times in a row, expecting a different result, and when I described this to the group as my "wasp moments," two members had just read a popular account of the sphex digger wasp — the one that drags its paralyzed cricket to the burrow entrance, goes inside to check, and if the cricket is moved a few inches away, retrieves it, deposits it at the threshold, and checks again, as many times as the experimenter bothers to move it, apparently never varying the routine. So I've volunteered to lead a forty-five-minute session on sphexishness as a challenge to free will, for an audience of about a dozen adults with no philosophy background but plenty of opinions, several of whom arrived already convinced that neuroscience has shown we're all wasps.

I don't want the lecture itself; I want you to design the session's explanatory architecture, and I want you to commit to definite choices rather than offer me a menu. Tell me what has to be in place before the wasp story will do any work at all — my sense is that the group first needs some grip on the difference between behavior that is caused and behavior that is rigidly caused, since everything is caused and that alone wouldn't distinguish us from the insect, but you should either endorse that ordering or overrule it with reasons. Pick the one carrying example the session should be built around: the literal wasp, my compulsive build-command rerunning, or something of your own devising, and justify the choice by saying what the right example must exhibit (variable environment, apparently intelligent behavior that breaks down under a small perturbation, no evident internal monitoring) and what it must conspicuously lack so the audience doesn't fixate on the wrong feature. Then map the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they'll surface: I'm anticipating "the wasp has no brain to speak of, so it proves nothing about us," "being predictable isn't the same as being unfree," "we'd notice if we were like that," and someone invoking randomness or quantum indeterminacy as the escape hatch — tell me at which point in the sequence each should be headed off and how, rather than letting them all pile up at the end.

Finally, tell me what the session should deliberately leave out and say so out loud to the group — I'm tempted by compatibilism, by Libet, and by Dennett's point that the interesting question is whether our apparent flexibility is itself mechanizable, but I suspect packing those in will dissolve the wasp's bite before anyone has felt it. And give me one closing question I can put to the room that would reveal whether they've grasped the actual puzzle — that flexibility is itself the thing needing explanation, and that "we would notice" may be exactly what a sufficiently elaborate sphex would think — rather than whether they've merely learned a new word for a settled dismissal of free will.
