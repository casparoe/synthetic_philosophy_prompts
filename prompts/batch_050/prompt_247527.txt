I'm a senior in high school, and in our Ethics and Technology elective this semester we've been circling one question: can a designed object be political, or is politics something only people can have? Two analogies have ended up doing most of the argumentative work in class, and I want to know how much weight either can actually bear. The first came from a guest speaker, a parent who works at a software company. His line was that technology is like a hammer: a hammer can build a house or break a window, so the politics belongs entirely to the person swinging it, and talk of designing values into products is a category mistake. On his view, regulation should target users and incentives, never designs. The second came from our teacher, drawing on a famous essay in science and technology studies that asks whether artifacts have politics. Her counter-image was that technical systems are like laws passed in concrete: a low overpass or a kneeling bus channels behavior the way an ordinance does, except the rule enforces itself on everyone, including people who never got a say. Her stock example was the oft-retold story of the low parkway bridges on Long Island, supposedly built to keep buses — and the people who ride them — away from certain beaches, though I've since read that the historical details of that story are disputed.

I'd like a careful audit of both analogies. For each one, give me the explicit mapping (a table of correspondences is fine): in the hammer analogy, what plays the role of the designer, the user, the artifact's intended function, and its unintended uses; in the legislation analogy, what corresponds to the legislator, the statute, the enforcement officer, and the citizen's ability to appeal. Then work through where each mapping breaks down. Some candidates I'd like addressed rather than merely listed: a hammer seems general-purpose in a way a turnstile or a recommender algorithm is not, so does neutrality depend on how many uses an artifact affords; laws can be repealed and are enforced by officials who can exercise discretion, while a bridge enforces itself automatically and can't be petitioned, so is the artifact version of legislation actually *stricter* than the real thing in a way that cuts against the analogy; and conversely, tools aren't as neutral as they look once you notice they are built for users of a particular size, strength, and handedness. For each disanalogy, say whether it matters for the conclusion the analogy was invoked to support — our speaker's hands-off regulatory stance, and our teacher's claim that choosing between two designs is a political act even with no political intent — or whether it's just an incidental mismatch.

Finally, two wrap-up questions. First, does the contested accuracy of the Long Island bridge story damage the legislation analogy, or can the underlying thesis stand on less dramatic, better-documented cases like speed bumps, anti-homeless benches, or default privacy settings? Second, if you had to replace both analogies with a single better one for the claim that designs distribute power, what would it be, and what would it capture that the hammer and the statute each miss? I'm not looking for citations — I'll track down sources myself — just a rigorous account of what each comparison can and cannot carry.
