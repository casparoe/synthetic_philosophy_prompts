I'm a graduate student TAing a decision theory seminar, and after our session on the St. Petersburg paradox I came away suspecting that the standard resolutions dissolve the puzzle too quickly. Here is my understanding of the terrain, and I'd like you to tell me where it goes wrong. The naive puzzle is familiar: a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads, and the prize doubles with each tails, so the expected monetary value is infinite, yet no reasonable person would pay anywhere near that much to play. Bernoulli's classic fix -- diminishing marginal utility of money, say logarithmic utility -- makes the expected utility finite, and that is usually where the textbook chapter ends. But there is a rejoinder I find much more troubling: for any unbounded utility function, no matter how concave, you can simply redesign the prize schedule so that expected utility diverges again, by letting the prizes grow fast enough that the utility awarded at round n is at least 2^n. So diminishing marginal utility only helps if utility is bounded, and bounded utility has its own embarrassing consequence: it implies that beyond some point, additional billions of dollars are worth essentially nothing, so that an agent would refuse a tiny-risk gamble for arbitrarily enormous stakes. Meanwhile the response that no real casino could fund the infinite payout seems to me to answer a different question entirely -- it tells us what a real casino would offer, not whether the idealized game reveals a defect in maximizing expected value.

Could you explain, at a gut level rather than via a full technical treatment, what is really going on here? Specifically: (1) reconstruct the escalating-prize argument carefully enough to show why it defeats any unbounded concave utility fix, and clarify whether it shows something deep or merely that infinities behave badly; (2) adjudicate between two readings of the paradox -- that it exposes a genuine problem for expected utility maximization as a theory of rational choice, versus that it merely cautions us against applying the theory to idealized setups that could never actually be offered to anyone; and (3) assess whether biting the bounded-utility bullet is as costly as it first appears, or whether the allegedly absurd consequence (indifference to vast sums beyond some threshold) is actually psychologically or normatively defensible. If you think the right lesson is something else altogether -- for instance, that the paradox trades on a confusion about what we are evaluating, the game versus the price of entry -- I'd like to hear that case argued rather than asserted.
