In a philosophy-of-normative-ethics seminar I attend, the discussion of constraints against harming has been stuck at the same point for two years, and I would like a diagnosis rather than another round of it. The set-up is the familiar one: a rule forbids me from killing one person even where doing so would prevent five equally bad killings of the same kind, and the deontologists in the room hold that this is what makes such rules puzzling -- if killings are bad, why forbid the act that minimises them? -- while their opponents argue that the puzzle dissolves once you redescribe the outcome: the situation in which I have killed someone is simply a worse outcome than the one in which I have not, so the constraint was only ever maximisation under a finer description of states of affairs. The deontologists reply that this redescription is trivially available for anything and so explains nothing; their opponents reply that 'explains nothing' just means 'doesn't feel like a justification', and feelings are not arguments. Nobody has conceded anything since. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a substantive dispute about what counts as a reason for action, a merely verbal one about which descriptions of outcomes are legitimate, or two parties answering different questions -- one about what makes outcomes better, one about what makes acts permissible -- under the single heading of why the constraint holds? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a principled criterion for legitimate redescription, a regress argument against one side, or an admission that the impasse is bedrock?
