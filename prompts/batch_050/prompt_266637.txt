As I understand it, van Fraassen's Reflection principle requires that my current credences defer to the credences I rationally expect to hold in the future: I should adopt today whatever I expect to believe tomorrow. I have been trying to apply this requirement to a concrete case, and I keep deriving an absurd conclusion, which makes me suspect the principle cannot be a genuine norm of rationality.

The case is as follows. I plan to run an experiment next week bearing on a hypothesis H, to which I currently assign credence 0.5. The experimental setup is highly sensitive, and I fully expect that once I see the results I will assign H a credence of 0.9. If Reflection is correct, it seems I am thereby rationally required to assign H credence 0.9 already today, before running the experiment. But this renders the experiment pointless: why expend time and resources gathering evidence if rationality has already mandated the endpoint of the update in advance? Worse, the reasoning appears to generalize: if my present credences must always match my anticipated future credences, then Reflection seems to forbid rational belief change altogether, since at every moment my credences would already have to be whatever they will later become.

So I would like to know the following. Does Reflection really require me to skip the experiment and simply adopt credence 0.9 now? If it does not, where exactly does my derivation go wrong? And if it does, should we conclude that Reflection is too strong to serve as a requirement of rationality — perhaps even that it makes learning from evidence irrational?
