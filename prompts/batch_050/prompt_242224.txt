I am drafting the central chapter of a graduate seminar I teach on de re modality, and I keep returning to an argument — a descendant of one I first wrote years ago as a graduate student — whose conclusion I now regard as reductio-worthy but whose premises I cannot bring myself to surrender. I would like your help locating the fault.

Fix a concrete case. Elena, a colleague of mine, is 170 centimeters tall. She might have been shorter: had her childhood nutrition differed, she would have stood at 165. I assume, as the friend of transworld identity does, that this ordinary modal claim is made true by Elena herself — the very woman who teaches down the hall — existing in another possible world and standing 165 there. Now:

(1) Elena exists in a non-actual world w, and in w she is 165 centimeters tall.
(2) If Elena-in-w is numerically identical with Elena, then everything true of Elena is true of Elena-in-w, and conversely; identicals are indiscernible.
(3) Elena is 170 centimeters tall; Elena-in-w is not 170 centimeters tall. So something true of one is not true of the other.
(4) The only way to reconcile (1)–(3) is to hold that height is had at a world: Elena is 170-at-the-actual-world and 165-at-w, and there is no further fact about her height simpliciter.
(5) The rescue generalizes. For any property Elena has contingently — her finger count, her mass, the shape of her left ear — transworld identity forces the same treatment: no contingent property is had simpliciter; all are indexed to worlds.
(6) But indexing a property to a world is making it a relation to that world. On this view, having five fingers is on a par with standing to the left of a tree: a matter of how one stands toward something else — indeed, toward an entire causally disconnected spacetime.
(7) Finger counts and shapes are paradigms of intrinsic properties; whether a thing has them cannot depend on its relation to anything else, still less to other universes.
(8) Hence transworld identity is false, and the only surviving account of de re modality is a counterpart theory on which Elena exists only actually, her possibilities being a matter of sufficiently similar people in other worlds.

The conclusion strikes me as proving too much, for two reasons. First, an exactly parallel argument, with times in place of worlds, would show that nothing persists through change — that the woman lecturing today is numerically distinct from the underfed girl — and I am reluctant to let a modal argument settle the metaphysics of ordinary persistence by sheer analogy. Second, the move at (6) feels too quick: my height is also, in some sense, indexed to a time, without thereby becoming a relation to the year 2025 — or so I want to say — and I suspect that whatever blocks the temporal inference blocks the modal one. Yet when I try to state the block precisely, I either find myself qualifying the indiscernibility of identicals in a way that looks ad hoc, or else denying that Elena has any of her contingent properties simpliciter, which is precisely the bullet the argument was constructed to force me to bite.

So: where does the argument fail, if it does? Identify the step you reject — I suspect it is (2) or (6), but tell me if I have miscounted the joints — and explain why that step looked sound in the first place. Then price the alternatives: what it costs to restrict Leibniz's law to world-indexed predications; what it costs to accept indexation while denying that indexation is relation; and what it costs to grant the conclusion for worlds while refusing its temporal twin. If your diagnosis turns on whether the worlds in question are concrete spacetimes or abstract representational entities, say so and say why. One preference to work within: if I must choose, I would rather surrender transworld identity than surrender a sharp distinction between intrinsic and relational properties — but I want to know whether the argument genuinely forces that choice, and what the cheapest exit actually costs.
