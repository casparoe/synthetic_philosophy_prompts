I run a little book club at the library where I work and last week we got into this huge fight that spilled over into a debate about morality itself, and my friend Marcus walked me into an argument that feels like a trap and I can't find the way out. Background: we were reading a novel where the narrator casually presents some awful stuff as totally fine and noble, and everyone agreed the book felt broken at those points — like, you can read the words but you can't actually go along with them the way you go along with dragons or faster-than-light travel in other books. Marcus says this proves something big. His argument: (1) If something is genuinely possible, you can imagine a story where it's true — that's what conceivability means, and it's basically the only test we have for what's possible vs. impossible. (2) Nobody can actually imagine a story where, say, hurting children for fun is the right thing to do. You can write the sentence down, but as a reader you just refuse to go along with it; the fiction collapses. (3) So hurting-children-for-fun being right isn't possible at all — it's not just wrong, it's necessarily wrong, the way 2+2=5 is necessarily false. (4) And this has to generalize, because (1) was supposed to be a completely general test: whatever you can't imagine in a story is impossible. (5) But wait — there are tons of things nobody can imagine in a story that are obviously possible. If a gritty realistic courtroom novel has the judge sprout wings on page 300 with zero setup, readers balk exactly the same way. If a mystery novel just tells us the detective's random guess was a stroke of genius without showing anything brilliant about it, we can't go along with that either. (6) So by (4), judges sprouting wings and lucky guesses counting as genius are impossible too — which is nuts.

Now, I definately want to keep (1), because it feels like the only handle we have on what's possible at all — if conceivability doesn't track possibility even roughly, then a whole way of reasoning about hypothetical stuff goes out the window, and it seems like philosophers use that tool constantly (like when they argue about whether something could exist without a body, or whatever). And (3)'s conclusion actually appeals to me? It'd be nice if the wrongness of cruelty were as solid as arithmetic. But (6) is clearly a reductio — the argument proves too much. Something has to give and I can't figure out what. (2) seems like just a fact about how reading feels — Marcus even brought printouts and made us try, and sure enough, nobody could get themselves to go along with it.

So: which step do I reject, and why does that step look so airtight when you're standing inside the argument? And what does rejecting it cost — like, if the flaw is in (1), does that wreck conceivability arguments elsewhere, and if it's in (4) or the move from (2) to (3), what's the actual difference between the cruelty case and the sprouting-wings case that the argument is sliding over? I'd also take an explanation of why the moral sentences specifically break a story, if the answer isn't "because they're necessarily false."
