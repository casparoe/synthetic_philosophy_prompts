I'm a junior in high school, and for an independent study attached to my AP Biology course I've been reading about the units of selection debate -- whether natural selection fundamentally acts on genes, on organisms, or on groups. My supervising teacher, Ms. Ferreira, has a habit of cutting through my long summaries with instant verdicts on cases, and I've started keeping a list of them. On segregation distorter alleles, where a gene gets itself into more than half of an organism's sperm and spreads even while harming the organism, she said the gene is obviously the unit, since the gene is what's winning and the organism is the casualty. On sickle-cell in malarial regions, where neither allele is unconditionally favored and the advantage exists only in heterozygotes, she said the individual organism is the unit, because the causal story is about whole bodies resisting malaria without succumbing to anemia. On sterile worker bees, she said the gene level again, on the grounds that the sterility allele copies itself through the queen's other offspring. On cancer, she said cell-level selection is genuinely selection -- tumors really do evolve -- but that it's a degenerate case because it builds no lasting adaptation beyond the tumor itself. And on a highly specialized species being driven extinct by a generalist competitor, she said this isn't selection at any level at all, just sorting, because nothing is reproducing better in virtue of a heritable trait and no adaptation results.

Last week I put a new case to her and she refused to answer until I worked out what her own view implies, which is why I'm asking you. The case: an ant species in which some workers carry an allele making them defend the nest suicidally against raiders. Workers are sterile, so the allele never boosts its bearer's personal reproduction; but colonies with many such defenders survive raids, grow larger, and send out more young queens and daughter colonies than colonies with few defenders, and the allele spreads through the population over time. Within any colony, carriers do no better than non-carriers; between colonies, the difference is decisive. My guess is she'll say this is the bee case all over again and put the selection at the gene level, since copies of the allele spread through related reproductives. But a friend on my debate team insists her record equally supports a rule like "the unit of selection is whichever level exhibits the heritable variation in success that does the causal work," which would put this case at the colony level, since it's colonies that are differentially succeeding here.

So here's what I'd like: predict Ms. Ferreira's verdict on the ant case, and identify the two or three candidate rules her earlier verdicts are jointly consistent with -- a pure gene's-eye rule, some version of the "level where the causal work happens" rule, a pluralist rule that picks levels case by case, or something else. Tell me which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog to the ant case and whether it actually settles the question or just appears to. If her record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say which further example I should put to her that would best distinguish the competing rules -- in particular, one where the gene-level rule and the colony-level rule give different verdicts. I'd also like to know if any of her five verdicts is one she should give up for consistency's sake, since I suspect the sickle-cell verdict and the bee verdict sit awkwardly together, but I can't quite pin down why.
