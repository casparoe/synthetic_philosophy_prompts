So this started as an argument with a friend of mine after a long seminar on decision theory, and it's been bugging me for weeks because the conclusion seems insane but every step looks fine when I stare at it. His argument: (1) Rational deliberation about what to do consists in weighing your options by their expected value, which requires having probabilities for outcomes conditional on each act you might take. (2) You can only use a probability in deliberation if it's stable enough to steer by — if the needle moves whenever you move the wheel, it's not an instrument, it's just part of the wobble. (3) But any probability you assign to your own act while deliberating is not stable: deliberating is precisely the process that produces and revises that probability, and the moment it hits 0 or 1, your deliberation is over by definition. (4) So probabilities over your own acts can never legitimately figure in deliberation. (5) So nobody ever actually deliberates rationally in the expected-value sense — either the choice was already settled and you were faking it, or you were doing something that can't be reconstructed as rational choice at all. Conclusion: expected utility theory is a theory of something that never happens.

That can't be right — people deliberate, and some people clearly do it better then others, and I want to say the better ones are doing something like good expected-value reasoning. When I pushed back with "look, mid-deliberation I can just have credence 0.7 that I'll take the job offer, and use it," he said that's equivocating between two stances: either that 0.7 is a prediction about myself made from the outside, the same way I'd predict a stranger from their track record — but then I'm spectating on my own behavior rather than deciding it, and I've quietly stopped deliberating — or the 0.7 is generated by my deliberation itself, in which case letting it guide the deliberation is bootstrapping, like trusting a guru whose predictions I dictate and then being impressed by his accuracy. Which step of the original argument do I reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost me? If I have to sacrifice something, I'd rather give up the claim that deliberation is genuinely open than give up expected utility theory as an account of good reasoning — but before I pay that price I want to know whether the argument really forces it, or whether something slipped between (2) and (3) that I can't quite name.
