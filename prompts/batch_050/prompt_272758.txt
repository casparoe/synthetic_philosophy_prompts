**Exercise (20 marks)**

(a) Outline the two-factor theory of delusion formation, using the Capgras delusion as your example. In your answer, make clear what motivates positing a second factor in addition to the anomalous experience itself.

(b) The two-factor theory is usually presented as an account of how a particular *belief* comes to be formed and maintained. Give one argument for doubting that delusions really are beliefs, and one consideration in favour of the doxastic view that they are.

**Member's answer**

(a) In the Capgras delusion, the patient insists that a close relative or spouse has been replaced by an impostor who merely looks identical. On the two-factor account developed by Brendan Maher, two things must go wrong for this delusion to arise. The first factor is a neuropsychological abnormality in the patient's recognition system: the patient still consciously recognizes the loved one's face, but the normal affective response that accompanies familiarity is absent, so the loved one somehow *feels* like a stranger. This anomalous experience supplies the content of the delusion, since the impostor hypothesis is a natural interpretation of seeing someone who looks exactly like your wife but produces none of the feeling of your wife. The second factor is a deficit in the patient's ability to evaluate and reject hypotheses, often associated with right-hemisphere damage, which explains why the patient adopts and clings to the impostor hypothesis rather than dismissing it as obviously absurd.

The motivation for the second factor is that the anomalous experience alone cannot be sufficient. If the strange feeling were enough to produce the delusion, then anyone who had that feeling would become delusional, but the comparison cases show otherwise: there are patients with anomalous experiences of the relevant kind who nonetheless conclude, correctly, that something is wrong with their own perception rather than with their spouse. The first factor explains why the thought occurs to the patient in the first place; the second factor explains why it is accepted rather than rejected. This is what distinguishes the two-factor theory from simpler accounts on which the experience alone does all the explanatory work.

(b) One argument against the doxastic view is that delusions behave very differently from ordinary beliefs in their guidance of action. A person who genuinely believed that his wife had been replaced by a hostile duplicate would presumably flee, call the police, or at least bar the bedroom door, yet Capgras patients frequently go on living with the supposed impostor in relative equanimity. Genuine beliefs are supposed to be integrated with the rest of one's psychology and to issue in appropriate behaviour; delusions often sit inert, compartmentalized from action and from the patient's other commitments. This has led some theorists, such as Currie and Ravenscroft, to propose that delusions are really imaginings that the patient mistakes for beliefs. A considerable strength of this proposal is that it predicts that patients will act on their delusions, since imaginings, like beliefs, are capable of motivating behaviour; the proposal thereby accounts for the behavioural profile of deluded patients more smoothly than the doxastic view does.

In favour of the doxastic view, there is the simple fact that deluded patients sincerely assert the content of the delusion, express unshakeable conviction in it, hold it steadily over long periods, and in some cases do reason from it and act on it. A state that is sincerely avowed, remembered, and used in inference looks enough like a belief that the burden of proof should fall on those who would deny it that status. Lisa Bortolotti defends the doxastic view by showing that delusions satisfy the standard criteria for belief, and concludes that their irrationality gives us no grounds to withhold the label.
