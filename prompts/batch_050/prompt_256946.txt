I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I drafted an argument for an essay on skepticism that now worries me because it seems to prove way too much. Here it is:

(1) Anyone who argues about anything — even the skeptic arguing that our faculties are unreliable — has to treat their own reasoning as trustworthy while they're arguing; you can't deliberate at all without taking your inferences to be good ones.
(2) So global skepticism about reason is self-refuting: the reliability of reason is a precondition of any thought whatsoever, not just another hypothesis up for debate.
(3) But the same move generalizes. Scientific inquiry presupposes that nature is uniform (why gather evidence if the past says nothing about the future?), ordinary conversation presupposes other minds, and following any argument at all presupposes the past is real, since you have to trust your memory of your own premises.
(4) So by parity of reasoning, the uniformity of nature, other minds, and the reality of the past are all transcendentally garanteed.
(5) That's absurd — those all seem like things we could in principle be wrong about, and surely no amount of pointing out what we must presuppose settles them.
(6) So something in the original move has to be wrong as well, but I can't see what, since (1) seems obviously true and each step looks exactly like the last.

Where does it break? If I have to choose I'd rather keep (1)–(2) and lose the generalization in (3), but I want to know what that costs. Does rejecting (3) leave any argument of this shape standing, or does the whole genre collapse into we must believe it, so there? Am I just being sentimental about (2)?
