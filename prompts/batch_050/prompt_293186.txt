Okay so a bit of background: I'm 68, spent my whole career as a condensed matter physicist, and since retiring I've been reading philosophy the way I used to read journal articles — too fast and with way too many opinions. Right now I'm stuck on Nietzsche's eternal recurrence and I want to argue it out with someone who'll genuinely defend it rather than pat me on the head.

So: play Friedrich Nietzsche, somewhere around 1885, a few years after the recurrence idea came to you at Sils-Maria. Ground rules: you take the doctrine seriously on both levels — as a real cosmological hypothesis about how the universe actually runs, and as the supreme test of whether a person can affirm their life — and you will not let me deflate it into a mere thought experiment or self-help exercise. From your notebooks: you hold that the universe contains a finite stock of force and hence only finitely many possible configurations, that time is infinite, and that therefore every configuration, including this conversation, must recur; and you've rejected the heat-death picture because, as you see it, an equilibrium state would already have been reached in an infinite past, and plainly it hasn't been. You can concede small points gracefully but never the main one. You've never heard of Poincaré's recurrence theorem — it comes after your time — so if I describe it, react with only the conceptual tools you actually have. Two or three paragraphs per reply, stay in character until I say stop, and if you need a fact about modern physics, ask me in character.

My opening move: your doctrine seems to me to fall on one of two horns, and both of them hurt. First the physics. Grant for arguement's sake that a bounded, isolated mechanical system with finitely many accessible states recurs — Poincaré proved exactly that in 1890, and the catch is that the recurrence times are grotesquely long and the proof needs the system to be isolated, which the universe, for all we know, is not. So your cosmology is at best open, not established. Second, and this is the sharper horn: if recurrence is literally true, your famous test loses its teeth. Whether I greet the demon's news with despair or with joy, I will relive this exact life either way — my affirmation changes nothing and is chosen by nothing I do now. The ethical weight of the doctrine seems to presuppose that the cosmology might be false. So which do you give up: the physics or the test?
