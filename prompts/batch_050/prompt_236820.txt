Take the ENCODE controversy from 2012 and run it through the debate between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function. Quick recap of the facts as I understand them, in case you want to push back on them: the ENCODE consortium published a big batch of papers and announced that they'd assigned a "biochemical function" to about 80% of the human genome, mostly on the basis of things like being transcribed, bound by some protein, or showing chromatin marks. The backlash was fast and nasty -- people like Dan Graur argued that most of that activity is noise, that the genome is full of junk DNA, transposon remnants, and spurious transcription, and that calling something functional just because a protein touches it mistakes an effect for a function. ENCODE's defenders said the critics were working with an outdated notion tied to sequence conservation and that biochemical activity is exactly what a data-driven project should report.

Here's what I want: map both philosophical accounts of function onto this fight. The etiological (selected-effects) account says a trait's function is the effect it was selected for; the causal-role account says a component's function is whatever it contributes to some capacity of the containing system we're interested in. Who was implicitly using which account? Graur's side clearly leans etiological, but work out the details: does the selected-effects view actually vindicate "most of the genome is junk," or does it leave room for functions without sequence conservation (recently evolved stuff, functions maintained by selection on other lineages, structural roles like spacing)? And on the ENCODE side, does the causal-role account really license the 80% figure, or does that reading depend on a sneaky choice of system and capacity -- because causal-role functions are relativized to an explanatory interest, and it's not clear ENCODE ever said what theirs was.

Then run it the other direction: what does the episode show about the two accounts? Some things I'd want addressed: whether the causal-role account, taken at face value, makes "function" so cheap that the junk DNA question becomes unaskable in its vocabulary (and whether that's a reductio or just pluralism); whether the etiological account can actually be operationalized by a consortium doing biochemistry rather than evolutionary inference, or whether it demands evidence nobody in this dispute had; and whether the whole fight suggests that "function" in genomics is doing at least two jobs -- a discovery-oriented one and a how-much-of-the-genome-matters one -- that need different concepts. If you think one account comes out clearly better from this case, say so and say why; if you think the case mostly exposes that both sides were talking past each other because the philosophical accounts were never built to settle a consortium's press release, argue that instead. Don't just split the difference by reflex.
