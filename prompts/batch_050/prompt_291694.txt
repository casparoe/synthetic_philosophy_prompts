**Disagreement as a Failure of Publicity**

Robert Aumann's 1976 result is usually stated this way: if two agents share a common prior over states of the world, and their posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge between them, then the posteriors must be equal. The standard gloss is that rational agents cannot agree to disagree.

Philosophers who resist the conclusion almost always attack the common prior assumption. Real people, they point out, bring different background beliefs to the table, so the theorem's antecedent is never satisfied. I think this concedes the argument in the wrong place. The common prior is the visible premise but not the load-bearing one. The premise doing the real work is that the two posteriors are *common knowledge* — and common knowledge is an extraordinarily demanding condition that realistic disagreements essentially never meet.

To see this, consider the natural dynamic extension. Suppose two agents with a common prior disagree about some event, and they begin announcing their posteriors to each other, taking turns, revising honestly after each announcement. Each announcement is itself evidence: learning that you assign probability 0.7 tells me something about which states of the world you regard as possible, which changes my posterior, whose announcement then changes yours. It has been shown that this process of mutual announcement and revision must terminate in agreement after finitely many rounds. Disagreement between rational agents with a common prior is therefore not merely prohibited in equilibrium — it is unstable under any process that makes beliefs public. It can exist only as a transitional state.

My central claim is that the theorem is best read as a result about *publicity*, not about priors. For disagreement to persist, it suffices that publicity fail at some level of the hierarchy: I may know your posterior without your knowing that I know it, or we may each know the other's posterior while failing to know what the other makes of that fact. Higher-order uncertainty of this kind is the normal condition of actual intellectual life. When two economists disagree about inflation, what keeps the disagreement alive is rarely a difference in priors; it is that neither has full access to what the other has seen, and neither can be sure the other's announced belief reflects all and only the evidence. The theorem's antecedent fails not at the first floor but somewhere in the tower of I-know-that-you-know-that-I-know.

The upshot, I want to argue, is that Aumann's result has been mis-sold as a constraint on rational belief. It is better understood as a constraint on rational *transparency*: it tells us what must happen if two agents could ever succeed in fully opening their epistemic books to each other. Persistent disagreement is then no scandal for rationality; it is the predictable residue of the fact that no two people have ever actually finished the conversation.
