Ok so here's a situation that's been bugging me. About a year ago I stopped buying meat, mostly for animal welfare reasons. My roommate (econ guy, loves to argue) says I'm not actually accomplishing anything: the supermarket orders in bulk, and whether I skip the chicken breasts this week changes literally nothing. The store only adjusts its orders when demand shifts by some noticeable amount, so the same number of chickens get raised and killed either way, and my "boycott" is invisible to them.

Fine, annoying, but then he went further. He said that if I really cared about minimizing animal deaths, I should actually buy the packs of meat that are about to hit their sell-by date, since those are getting thrown in the dumpster otherwise — an animal already died for them and buying them adds nothing to the store's demand signal. By his logic, buying the almost-expired stuff causes zero extra deaths, whereas me not buying meat at all doesn't either, so it's basically a free lunch. That felt like a trick but I couldn't pin down where it goes wrong.

So now I'm stuck between three options: (1) keep not buying meat even though it "changes nothing," (2) only buy the about-to-expire meat, which feels like a weird loophole but he insists is the genuinely consequence-driven move, or (3) admit individual purchases just don't matter and go back to eating whatever. Something about (2) and (3) feels wrong to me, but whenever I try to explain why I end up saying vague stuff like "it's the principle of the thing," which he rightly tears apart.

Is there a way to defend what I've been doing on the actual consequences, without hand-waving about principles? And relatedly — if his "buy the expiring meat" move really is consequence-neutral, is there something broken about evaluating actions one purchase at a time in the first place?
