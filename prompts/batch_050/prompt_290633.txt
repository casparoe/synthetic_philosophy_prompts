I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy TAing an upper-level undergraduate seminar on individual responsibility in collective wrongdoing, and I'm drafting the final exam. I'd like you to write one essay question on complicity -- participating in wrongdoing without causing it -- together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The exam is 90 minutes, the question is worth 40 marks, and a good answer should run about 700-800 words.

Please build the question around the following case I've devised, rather than a stock example: Maya agrees to be the getaway driver for an art theft. Unbeknownst to her, the crew keeps a second car and driver on standby, and on the night in question Maya's car fails to start; the crew uses the standby car and the theft proceeds exactly as planned. Maya was at the rendezvous point, engine key in hand, fully intending to drive them away. The question should ask students whether Maya is complicit in the theft and what the case shows about the relationship between complicity and causal contribution.

I want this case because the question should be unanswerable by reciting a summary of the topic: it must separate students who understand that complicity is standardly taken not to require counterfactual difference-making from students who have merely memorized that label. The marking scheme should therefore name the specific moves a top answer must make -- for instance, explaining why the fact that the theft would have happened anyway does not by itself settle whether Maya is complicit; distinguishing causal responsibility from complicity understood as a normative relation to another's wrongdoing; testing intention-and-participation accounts against a contrasting case such as a taxi driver unknowingly hired by the crew, and stating what principle distinguishes the two; and saying why we want a concept of complicity that outruns causation at all. It should also specify the mistakes that cost marks: treating Maya's lack of causal contribution as straightforwardly exculpatory without argument, conflating complicity with criminal attempt, or assuming complicity requires the principal wrong to succeed.

The model answer should be written to the standard of a strong first-class response and should take and defend a position on the case rather than neutrally surveying views.
