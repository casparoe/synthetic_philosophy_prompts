I'm a high school junior helping run our school's philosophy club, and this semester we've been circling around the question of what intelligence actually is. Our discussions keep stalling because people use the word differently: one member insists an octopus is plainly intelligent, another says a chess engine is "just computation," a third keeps citing IQ research, and our debates go nowhere because we never figure out where we actually disagree. So I want to build a "what theory of intelligence do you actually hold?" quiz for the club website -- eight to ten questions, each presenting a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions like psychometric g (intelligence as a single measurable general factor), multiple-intelligences views, behaviorist or Turing-test-style operationalism, embodied/enactive accounts (intelligence as inseparable from having a body that acts in an environment), and a deflationary position that "intelligence" is just a loose label for a grab-bag of unrelated capacities with no unified phenomenon underneath.

The cases should force genuine choices rather than letting people endorse everything: for example, a crow that makes tools but can't be interviewed, a language model that aces exams but never acts in the world, a corporation that adapts to markets, a person who scores poorly on IQ tests but navigates social life brilliantly, a hypothetical alien whose cognition runs on a timescale of centuries, a thermostat that "tracks" temperature. No question should ask "do you think intelligence requires a body?" directly -- the quiz should sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they find flattering. I'd like the key built from the logical space first: which combinations of answers are even coherent, which positions occupy which cells, and which cells are empty (and why -- is no one there because the combination is contradictory, unattractive, or just unexplored?). If two positions on my list can't be separated by any case you can construct, say so explicitly rather than forcing a distinction.

Since I'm designing this partly to surprise people, I also want you to stress-test your own instrument at the end. Tell me which of your diagnostic questions you are least confident actually discriminates the positions it targets, which scoring mappings a defender of one of these views would dispute as a caricature, and what kind of consideration -- an empirical result, a counterexample, a conceptual argument -- would lead you to restructure the quiz or admit that one of my listed positions has no stable content at all. If the whole exercise rests on a questionable assumption about what a definition of intelligence is even for, I'd rather hear that than receive a tidy taxonomy that hides it.
