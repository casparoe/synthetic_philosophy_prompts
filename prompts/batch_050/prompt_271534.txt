For a graduate seminar in epistemology, I am preparing a one-page handout that sorts students into positions on the norm of assertion by what they actually say about cases, rather than by which label they find appealing. Write a decision tree that takes a student from the starting commitment that flat-out assertion is governed by an epistemic norm (a standard one can violate only by asserting improperly, as opposed to merely asserting falsely or rudely) to a named position, with no more than nine branch nodes.

The tree must have leaves for at least the following: the simple knowledge norm (assert only what you know); the truth norm (assert only what is true); the belief norm (assert only what you believe); a justification or rational-belief norm; a stakes-sensitive version of the knowledge norm on which what one may assert varies with the practical situation; and a pluralist or deflationary leaf on which there is no single constitutive norm of assertion. You may add further leaves if the logical space demands them.

Every branch node must be phrased as a question about a concrete case or claim that a student can answer without knowing which position it leads to. The tree must somewhere deploy each of the following diagnostic materials: asserting that one's lottery ticket will lose, on purely statistical grounds, before the draw; asserting a truth one believes only through a lucky accident of a Gettier-style sort; asserting a Moore-paradoxical conjunction of the form that p, though one does not believe that p; asserting something false that one was fully reasonable in believing; and asserting under conditions where being wrong would be practically catastrophic rather than merely embarrassing. Note that several of these cases pull apart truth from knowledge and knowledge from justified belief, and the tree should exploit rather than flatten those separations.

Two further requirements. First, construct the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of names: identify the questions whose combinations of answers define the space (for instance, whether the norm attaches to the content asserted or to the speaker's epistemic position, and whether the standard is absolute or circumstance-sensitive), then locate the named positions within it, and for any unoccupied combination state briefly whether it is empty because the view would be incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Second, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, and say what further case or consideration, if any, might divide them; do not paper over the ambiguity by wording a node tendentiously.
