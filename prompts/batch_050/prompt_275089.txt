I'm writing a novel set in 1770s Edinburgh, and I'm stuck on a scene. My main character, a young minister, gives a public lecture defending his claim that a woman in his parish was miraculously healed of blindness after prayer -- his whole case rests on four respectable, level-headed eyewitnesses who all tell the same story. In the audience there's an older philosopher (loosely based on a certain Scottish skeptic, you can guess who) who I want to take him apart in the Q&A. Help me write the old man's attack: what questions does he actually ask, in what order, to genuinely rattle the minister? Not strawmen -- the real pressure points, like how we weigh testimony against everything else we know about how the world works, whether it's more likely the witnesses are decieved or that the ordinary course of nature broke down, why these stories always seem to come from times and places full of credulity, and the awkward fact that every rival religion has its own miracles too. And so the scene stays fair, give me the minister's best comebacks: where can he push back with a straight face, and where should he have to concede ground?
