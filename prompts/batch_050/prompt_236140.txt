After forty years in experimental physics, I retired and found my way into epistemology; I now serve, part-time, as managing editor of a small journal that publishes lengthy review articles. Our referees vet each article claim by claim, and I will stipulate that every substantive assertion in an accepted manuscript has been certified at roughly 98 percent confidence. But a typical review contains on the order of three hundred such claims, and elementary probability then tells me that a handful of them are almost certainly false even if every referee did flawless work. I understand this is essentially the preface paradox wearing an editorial hat, and I want it operationalized. Give me a reusable decision procedure — an ordered set of tests I can run on any accepted manuscript in an afternoon — that tells me what the aggregate worry should actually change: when it should alter what I publish, when it should alter only how I hedge (prefatory caveats, confidence labels, standing errata policies), and when it should alter nothing at all. The procedure must say which distinctions survive the translation from epistemology to editorial practice and which can be dropped, must handle at least a load-bearing claim on which the rest of the article depends, a peripheral claim no one will build on, and a claim the referees split over but none could refute, and must mark the points where judgment cannot be replaced by rule. Apply it to a worked example, including one case where it delivers an outcome I will find uncomfortable, and state plainly what the procedure cannot do.
