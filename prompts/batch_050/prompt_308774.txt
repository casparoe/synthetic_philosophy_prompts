After thirty-eight years as an entomologist — the last fifteen of them as a curator at our regional natural history museum — I retired, and promptly fell into philosophy, beginning with ethics and political theory. Among the disputes I have been reading about are those over the Benin Bronzes and the Parthenon Marbles, which I mention because they have abruptly ceased to be academic for me: the museum has asked me to sit on its collections and repatriation committee, and we have just received our first formal claim.

The facts, briefly. Our ethnographic and physical anthropology holdings descend from expeditions mounted between roughly 1880 and 1930. A community whose ancestors lived in the region where one of our founding expeditions collected has requested the return of ancestral human remains — some two dozen individuals — together with the funerary objects recovered from the same burial sites. The provenance file is typical of the period: acquisition was lawful under the colonial administration of the day, some items were purchased through local intermediaries, and some appear simply to have been taken, but the surviving documentation does not allow us to say which remains fall into which category. Complicating matters, the remains have featured in published research, and our physical anthropologists have proposed further work, including isotope analysis and DNA sampling, which is destructive. The committee is divided, and the director has asked for more than a verdict in this case: she wants a standing policy, since we expect further claims, including claims on objects rather than remains, where the argument from ancestry presumably runs differently.

Draft me a memorandum to the committee recommending both a decision in this case and the general policy we should adopt. I want criteria the committee can actually apply — to human remains, to objects taken from graves, and to artifacts purchased under conditions we would now regard as coercive — with the reasoning behind each criterion stated plainly, since our members include both scientists and community representatives. I will confess my own divided mind, which the memo should engage rather than indulge. My professional instincts tell me that the collection's scientific value is real and irreplaceable, and that our custody of these materials has been conscientious; my recent reading makes me suspect that the familiar argument from the so-called universal museum — that institutions like ours hold such things in trust for all humanity — may be a rationalization of possession rather than a genuine moral consideration, and that what was lawful in the 1890s may settle rather less than we assume. The memorandum should say, honestly, whether those suspicions are well founded, and it should present the strongest case for the side it recommends against. Finally: if there is a principled line between returning human remains and returning artifacts, the policy must state where it lies and what justifies it; if there is no such line, the policy must be willing to face that consequence as well.
