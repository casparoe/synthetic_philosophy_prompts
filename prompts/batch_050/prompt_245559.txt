I've been reading and thinking about solar geoengineering on my own, and I keep noticing that my gut verdicts about it don't obviously hang together. I'd like to know whether some principle unifies them, or whether I have to give one up. My verdicts:

A low-lying island nation that begins stratospheric aerosol injection on its own, after decades of failed climate negotiations, seems justified to me, even though it alters weather for the whole planet. A private billionaire doing the very same thing at his own expense seems clearly wrong, even assuming the physical effects are identical. Small outdoor experiments to test the technology seem fine, but I balk at a permanent international treaty banning deployment forever. If a country that has been injecting aerosols for years suddenly stops -- say, because of a war or a fiscal collapse -- and temperatures snap back within a few years, my instinct is to blame the original decision to start, not the decision to stop. And although I accept that having geoengineering available would let rich countries keep burning fossil fuels with a cleaner conscience, I'd still favor deployment if the realistic alternative were catastrophic warming concentrated in the poorest countries.

Is there a coherent principle here? Cases one and two seem to turn on who decides rather than on what is done, but case four suggests that starting creates open-ended obligations that stopping merely triggers, and I'm not sure that sits well with my willingness to support starting in case five. Please state the principle, or the strongest candidates, precisely; tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon; and say what it implies about a case I haven't considered -- a consortium of wealthy northern nations deploying over the objections of equatorial states whose monsoon patterns might shift as a result.
