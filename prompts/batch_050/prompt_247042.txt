I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a retired concert pianist who, after a bereavement, finds herself unable to explain the thing she has built her life on: why a slow movement in a minor key can sound heartbroken when it is only organized sound, with no one feeling anything inside it. Her confusion is the engine of the plot, and I need my own notes to be philosophically exact so that her interior arguments ring true. I would like you to build me a decision tree that takes a reader from the starting puzzle -- purely instrumental music, no words, no program, strikes nearly everyone as sad; how can mere sound be sad? -- to a named position in the aesthetics of music. Each branch node must be a question someone could answer honestly without knowing which theory it funnels them toward, and each leaf a position with its standard name if it has one, followed by one sentence on the commitment that would be most awkward for my pianist to live with.

Please construct the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names. The first questions should divide up where the sadness is supposed to reside: whether it is a perceptible quality of the sound itself (as one hears gaiety in a face), or instead resides in some relation the sound stands in -- a resemblance to expressive human behavior or utterance, a causal power to make listeners sad, a connection to a composer or to an imagined persona whose emotion the listener hears the music as expressing. Required leaves: the contour or resemblance view (the music sounds the way sad speech and movement look and sound), the arousal view (the music is sad because of what it makes us feel), the persona view (we hear an imaginary someone in the music whose emotion we apprehend), an imaginative-projection view if you think it is distinct from the persona view rather than a variant, and at least one position that denies the premise and holds that calling music sad is loose metaphor needing no deep explanation. Where two of your leaves cannot actually be separated by any further diagnostic question, say so explicitly rather than inventing a thin distinction; and where a combination of answers is available on your tree but has no occupant, note it and diagnose whether the slot is empty because the view would be incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. If you can, add one node or addendum addressing my novelist's real problem, which is the follow-up puzzle: whatever account the tree lands on, why would anyone seek out sad music for consolation rather than avoid it, and which leaves handle that gracefully and which stumble.

I would rather have a slightly smaller tree that is honest than a comprehensive one that bluffs. Please mark clearly which pieces of this terrain are effectively settled among people who work on it -- for instance, that the sounds themselves feel nothing, or that the puzzle is untouched by adding lyrics -- and which are genuinely contested, so that the tree does not quietly present one theory as the consensus view of aestheticians. Where the standard names or attributions of the leaf positions are themselves matters of scholarly dispute or family resemblance rather than fixed doctrine, flag that too, since I intend to put a version of the tree into the pianist's notebook in the final chapter and would rather not have her confidently endorsing a taxonomy that the experts would contest.
