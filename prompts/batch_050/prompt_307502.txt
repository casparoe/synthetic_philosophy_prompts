I'm compiling a taxonomy of decision rules for acting under normative uncertainty — situations in which one is unsure which moral theory is true but must still choose. My list so far: (1) maximize expected choiceworthiness, treating one's credences in theories like ordinary probabilities and each theory's evaluations like utilities; (2) the moral parliament: give each theory delegates in proportion to its credence and let them vote or bargain toward a compromise; (3) defer to one's best guess, simply doing whatever the highest-credence theory endorses; (4) precautionary or maximin-style rules that exclude options risking grave wrongdoing under any theory with non-negligible credence; (5) the intertheoretic-comparison skeptic's fallback: since theories' value scales cannot be placed on a common cardinal measure, restrict oneself to options all live theories can agree on. I believe this covers the standard proposals, but perhaps I am wrong. Can you supply a further decision rule that is genuinely distinct — not a rewording, special case, or hybrid of those above — state it precisely enough to be applied to a concrete choice, and then give an honest assessment of whether it survives the obvious objections?
