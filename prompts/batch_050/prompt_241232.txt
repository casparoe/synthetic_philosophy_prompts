I've ended up holding a view that most people find monstrous, so I want to stress-test it properly: habitat destruction probably reduces wild animal suffering on net. The idea is that most wild animals live short, brutal lives -- starvation, predation, parasites, dying as infants -- and plausibly have net-negative welfare, so a parking lot genuinely contains less suffering than the forest it replaced. (Bracket biodiversity's value, climate, human interests -- this is purely about the welfare of the animals themselves.)

What would count against this? Give me the strongest defeaters ranked by how much they should move me, distinguishing evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would merely be awkward. Specifically I want: (1) what empirical findings about wild animal lives -- e.g. about whether typical wild experiences are actually net-positive -- would settle it, and whether we could ever even get that kind of evidence; (2) second-order effects I might be missing, like whether destruction shifts ecosystems toward species with faster breeding and even worse lives per capita rather than just reducing total population; (3) whether the view proves too much -- does it commit me to actively favoring habitat destruction, or to thinking rescue of suffering wildlife is wrong; and (4) whether the whole position is secretly unfalsifiable, i.e. whether "we can't really measure or compare welfare across species" is a legitimate response or an escape hatch that means the view risks nothing. Also tell me what people who seriously work on wild animal welfare would themselves concede as counting against it, not just what critics would say.
