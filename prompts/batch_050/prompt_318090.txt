I teach a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind and computation, and each term I assign one unconventional writing exercise alongside the standard papers. Next semester I want to open the unit on John von Neumann's late manuscript The Computer and the Brain -- in which he compares the nervous system to computing machinery, arguing among other things that the brain combines analog and digital modes of operation, that its individual components are strikingly imprecise and compensated for by massive parallelism and redundancy, that its internal language is likely statistical rather than logical in our sense, that it performs far fewer serial steps than any machine we build, and that we do not understand how its memory is addressed at all -- with a piece of satire that will force students to actually digest those arguments before their first discussion meeting.

You are a professional satirist with a scholar's command of mid-twentieth-century computing history and of the philosophy of mind literature it seeded. Think carefully before you write. Begin with a short brainstorming section (no more than 150 words) in which you list which of von Neumann's genuine observations offer the best satirical leverage and why; then write the piece itself.

The piece: a parody referee report, roughly 700 words, written in the voice of an anonymous reviewer for a fictitious journal of computing machinery who has received the human brain as a submitted design proposal. The reviewer has internalized von Neumann's engineering sensibility -- clean architecture, reliable components, legible memory addressing, deep serial logical chains -- and writes a withering report flagging the brain's every deviation from those standards: unacceptable error rates per component, an inscrutable hybrid analog-digital architecture, a memory system whose addressing scheme the authors do not even specify, a suspiciously shallow count of serial operations, reliability purchased only by wasteful redundancy. The comedy, and the point, is that every complaint must correspond to a feature von Neumann actually identified and largely admired or at least took seriously as a clue about how the brain computes -- so that a reader who knows the material recognizes each objection as an inversion of the real text, and a reader who doesn't nonetheless absorbs its central claims. A reader with no background should find it funny; a specialist should find it fair.

Format it as a genuine referee report: a summary of the submission, numbered major and minor comments, and a final recommendation (reject, with an invitation to resubmit after several hundred million years of revisions). Stay deadpan throughout; never wink at the reader, never name von Neumann or his book inside the report itself, and do not put any wording from the actual text in quotation marks -- paraphrase the underlying ideas in the reviewer's own dyspeptic voice. End the report on one comment in which the reviewer, despite himself, concedes a grudging flicker of awe at the machine's power consumption.
