I'm writing the final for my upper-level phil of mind seminar and want one essay question on mind uploading that students can't answer by reciting Chalmers' fading qualia argument back at me. Here's the angle I want the question to force:

The standard gradual replacement story (swap neurons for silicon chips one at a time) is usually used to argue that consciousness survives uploading. But I want students to confront a distinction the argument usually glosses over: does gradual replacement show that the upload *is numerically the same person* as the original, or only that there's a *continuously functioning, psychologically continuous system* at the end? Those come apart, and the argument for the second doesn't obviously touch the first.

To sharpen it, the question should present this variant: **interleaved replacement**. Instead of replacing neurons one cluster at a time, the procedure replaces them in a checkerboard-ish pattern, so at the halfway point every local circuit contains a mix of biological and artificial neurons, and there's no stage at which any coherent subsystem is purely biological or purely silicon. The students should have to say whether interleaving *helps* the identity reading of the argument or actually *undermines* it. My own suspicion (which they should be pushed toward discovering, not handed): interleaving strengthens the case that a mixed system keeps functioning, but function-preservation is exactly the kind of thing that could be duplicated in a second body, so it can't ground one-one identity. I'd also like them to have to deal with a branching worry: two different gradual replacement sequences could start from the same brain and end in different uploads, and identity can't split.

So: write the exam question itself (answerable in 2 hours, open-book), then a model answer of about 600 words, then a marking scheme out of 20 points built around five must-hit moves, with the marks each is worth. For each of the five, add one line on how a mediocre answer typically fumbles it -- e.g. treating "gradual" as itself the magic ingredient rather than asking what gradualness is evidence *for*, or conflating "the upload would be conscious" with "the upload would be *me*", or answering the Parfit-style point (identity isn't what matters) by just asserting that it obviously is. The scheme should make clear what separates an A answer from a B answer: I want the A to say explicitly what the continuity argument licenses (consciousness and functional continuity) versus what it needs independent support for (numerical identity), and to take a stand on whether interleaving changes anything.
