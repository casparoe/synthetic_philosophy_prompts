I'm a doctoral student working on pragmatics, and I've been probing my own intuitions about speaker meaning and implicature with a battery of cases. I'd like to know whether some principled account unifies my verdicts -- or which verdict I'd have to surrender. Here are the cases and my gut reactions:

(1) A referee's letter for a philosophy job candidate says only that she is punctual and has excellent handwriting. My verdict: the referee means that the candidate is a weak philosopher, though she has not said it.

(2) A speaker says, of an obviously disastrous talk, "That went well," while knowing that the only person within earshot is incapable of detecting irony. My verdict: he has not meant that it went badly -- he's merely performed sarcasm for himself, whatever he inwardly intended.

(3) At a hiring meeting I remark, of an applicant, that he once spent two years in prison, expecting the committee to infer that he is untrustworthy, while deliberately preserving my ability to deny having said any such thing. My verdict: I did mean that he is untrustworthy; the deniability gets me off no hook.

(4) I tell a friend that the meeting has been moved to three o'clock, intending her to infer that she will miss her train; unbeknownst to me, she is driving. My verdict: I meant that she would miss her train, even though the communication failed and the inference was never available to her.

(5) I mention, apparently idly, that the museum is closed on Tuesdays, hoping you will conclude that we cannot go tomorrow -- and hoping, further, that you will take the conclusion to be your own idea, so that if you recognized my intention to produce that belief, my purpose would be defeated. My verdict: here I did not mean that we cannot go, in contrast to case (3).

The pair (3)/(5) is the crux for me: in both, I intend to produce a belief while keeping cover, yet my intuitions split. Is there a single account of speaker meaning that delivers all five verdicts -- presumably by locating the difference in whether recognition of the speaker's intention is itself meant to figure in the hearer's reasoning? If a simpler theory must overturn one of my verdicts, tell me which should go and why. Finally, say what the winning account predicts for a case I haven't listed: the hearer arrives at exactly the belief I intended, but by an accidental route -- she mishears my words, or draws the inference from a false premise of her own -- so that recognition of my intention plays no role in her getting there.
