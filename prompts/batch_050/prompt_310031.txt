Consider the following variant of Kavka's toxin puzzle, sharpened so that it has a definite structure rather than the usual ambiguities. A billionaire with a flawless intention-detector — stipulate that it works — offers you the following deal. At midnight tonight she will scan your brain. If and only if the scan shows that you genuinely intend, at that moment, to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow at noon, she wires you ten million dollars tonight, no strings attached. The toxin causes a miserable but non-lethal week of illness. The scan detects your occurrent intention at midnight, not your later behavior: nothing in the contract requires you to actually drink, and drinking after the money arrives is neither enforced nor rewarded. You know all of this in advance, you believe the detector works, and you cannot meaningfully deceive it by, say, hiring a hypnotist at 11:59pm — stipulate that the only routes available are the ones an ordinary person has: resolution, self-persuasion, precommitment devices you construct yourself, and the like.

I am a longtime reader of the rationalist blogs, where this puzzle is usually treated as a puzzle about whether rationality of intention can come apart from rationality of action. I want something more specific from you. Give me your credence that a psychologically ordinary but rational and highly motivated person — someone who understands decision theory, wants the money badly, and has twenty-four hours to prepare — actually succeeds in holding the genuine midnight intention, and so collects. My own number is around 0.3. More important than the number is the decomposition. Please break the question into the sub-questions your credence actually depends on — for instance: the probability that a person can form an intention at will when the intended act is one they will have every reason not to perform; the probability that 'intending' can be secured by precommitment or self-modification rather than by direct resolve, and whether such engineered states even count as the intention the detector is looking for; the probability that the person's rationality helps rather than hurts them (i.e., that being the sort of agent who would irrationally drink tomorrow is precisely what tonight's rationality demands becoming); and any further terms you think the estimate should factor through. Assign a rough number and one or two lines of justification to each term, show how they combine into your final credence, and tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to.

Two further requests. First, locate my disagreement with you: if my 0.3 differs from your figure, which sub-probability is doing the work? Second, I want calibrated honesty about the answer itself, not just about the puzzle. State how confident you are in your overall decomposition — including the possibility that you have carved the question at the wrong joints — and single out the one point in your reasoning you are least sure of, with a sentence on what argument or evidence would move it most.
