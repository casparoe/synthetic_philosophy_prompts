I retired four years ago after a long career in experimental biology, and I have since been working my way through the philosophy of action. One debate strikes me as having exactly the shape of the unproductive controversies I used to watch in my own field, where everyone agreed on the data and argued for decades anyway: the dispute, running from Socrates to the present, over whether akrasia — weakness of will, acting freely and with open eyes against one's own better judgment — is genuinely possible.

As I understand the state of play: Socrates, at least as Plato presents him, denied the phenomenon outright. Apparent cases are really failures of measurement, he argued — the agent misjudges the magnitudes of the goods at stake, much as one misjudges the size of objects seen at a distance, and a proper art of measurement would remove the temptation rather than be defeated by it. The common-sense tradition running through Aristotle and into modern philosophy insists the phenomenon is real and that any theory of action unable to accommodate it is refuted by ordinary experience. Davidson tried to carve out a place for it within a broadly rationalist framework, though even sympathetic readers find his account strained. Meanwhile — and this is what genuinely puzzles me as a scientist — the empirical literature appears to have proceeded without waiting for any of this to be settled. Behavioral economists treat time-inconsistent preference and hyperbolic discounting as established, almost boring facts; the person who sincerely resolves at midnight not to drink, drinks at six the following evening, and endorses both the resolution and the subsequent regret is a standard data point, not a paradox. Ainslie built an entire research programme on modelling the will as an ongoing negotiation among the agent's successive temporal selves.

My working hypothesis is that the philosophical debate never rested on facts in the first place. The Socratic denial, on this reading, is not an empirical conjecture that experiments on preference reversal could refute; it is a constraint on what counts as an intelligible explanation of intentional action — behaviour that flouts the agent's own ranking of options simply fails to be intelligible as action, so the description must be revised until the anomaly disappears. The common-sense side treats fidelity to lived experience as outranking intelligibility. If that is right, the two sides differ about the standards an explanation of action must meet, and the empirical literature is largely beside the point.

Is this diagnosis correct, or is there a substantive disagreement underneath — and if substantive, of what kind? Is it verbal, turning on contested meanings of terms like intentional or better judgment? Is there a hidden empirical question, for instance whether preference reversals involve a change of judgment or judgment held fixed through the reversal? And given your diagnosis: what would actually constitute progress — a conceptual distinction, an experimental design that could discriminate the competing descriptions, a formal result about intertemporal preference — or is the impasse a sign the question is ill-posed? I want the diagnosis argued for, with reasons grounded in what the parties actually say, not a survey of positions.
