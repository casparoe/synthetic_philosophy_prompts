Here's the question as it appeared on the exam:

In his discussion of the nature of moral values, Quine contrasts moral values with the values that guide scientific theory choice (simplicity, conservatism, testability, and so on). Explain what he takes the crucial difference between them to be, and why he thinks it follows that moral disagreement can persist even between people who agree on every relevant fact. How do his naturalistic speculations about the origins of our moral sensibility fit with this conclusion? Finally, does his position commit him to the view that moral discourse is defective and should be replaced? Give his reasons, whichever way you take him to go.

And here's the student's answer, retyped with the name removed (any typos below are mine, not theirs):

Quine's contrast between moral values and epistemic values turns on testing. Epistemic values like simplicity and conservatism are vindicated by results: theories chosen on those grounds deliver successful predictions, and observation sentences function as the checkpoint where scientific disagreements get finally settled. Moral values have no comparable checkpoint, since moral predicates never occur in observation sentences. Two people can therefore agree on all the facts — on exactly what a given punishment does to crime rates, say — and still disagree about whether the punishment is just, and there is no experiment to run. In this sense Quine concludes that there is no fact of the matter in ethics: moral claims are all false, like claims about witches, since they purport to state facts that do not exist.

Quine nonetheless gives a naturalistic account of where our moral sensibility comes from. Sympathy and a limited altruism toward kin, he suggests, were instilled by natural selection, and moral training extends that sentiment outward from the family. Because the origins of morality are natural in this way, Quine thinks ethics itself can ultimately be naturalized too: once psychology and sociology deliver a complete science of moral sentiment, moral questions become empirical ones that science can in principle settle, much as chemistry settled its disputes. His advice is accordingly to keep moral talk where it remains useful, but to be ready to replace it with explicit cost-benefit analysis as the science matures.

Grade it out of 20, and write the margin comments I'd pass along to her: which specific sentences earn credit and why, which ones should lose marks, and what exactly the student gets wrong about Quine in each case.
