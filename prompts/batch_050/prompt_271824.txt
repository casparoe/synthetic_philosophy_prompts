I'm preparing a unit on Islamic theological ethics for an undergraduate course and found the following summary of the Mu'tazila–Ash'arite debate in the theology chapter of an introductory textbook. Before I rely on it, I'd like to know how much of it to trust:

"The Mu'tazila held that moral qualities are intrinsic to acts: justice and injustice, good and evil, are real features of actions that human reason can discern on its own, without revelation. God, being perfectly just, commands what is good because it is good. The Ash'arites objected that this subjects God to a moral law outside Himself and thereby compromises His omnipotence and freedom. On their alternative view, good and evil have no existence independent of God's command: an act is good simply because God commands it, so that had God commanded lying or cruelty, those acts would have been good instead. Morality on this picture is ultimately arbitrary, and until revelation arrives human beings have no way at all of distinguishing right from wrong. The Mu'tazila countered that Ash'arism renders gratitude and praise of God meaningless, since calling God 'good' adds nothing if the word just means whatever He happens to do. The dispute is essentially an Islamic recasting of Plato's Euthyphro dilemma, and it was settled in the Ash'arites' favor once their school became the dominant theology of Sunni Islam."

My inclination is that the summary is roughly fair to the Mu'tazila but quietly unfair to the Ash'arites, though I can't pinpoint where. Please go through it claim by claim: separate what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what misrepresents the position it describes. In particular, I'd like to know whether the counterpossible about cruelty and the charge of arbitrariness reflect the settled Ash'arite position or only the most extreme voluntarist reading of it -- and what resources a careful Ash'arite has for resisting that reading while still denying that moral truths are independent of God. On the other side, does the phrase "a moral law outside Himself" describe something a Mu'tazilite would actually accept, given that human reason and the natures of created acts are themselves God's creation? Finally, does the closing sentence confuse institutional dominance with argumentative resolution, and what does the framing leave out?
