I write literary thrillers for a living, and my current manuscript has forced me into taking sides on a philosophy problem I only half understand. My villain never hurts anybody. That's the whole concept: he watches a business rival drown when he could throw a rope from the dock, he quietly reroutes a shipment of antibiotics to a higher-paying buyer knowing the clinic down the coast will run out, he notices the battery dying in a smoke detector in a building he manages and decides to deal with it after his vacation. My editor's note was brutal: he's not scary because he never actually *does* anything. And in defending the book to her I've talked myself into a genuinely held position, which is that there's no deep moral difference between doing harm and allowing it. Killing and letting die are on par, all else equal; the felt difference is squeamishness, plus an evolved flinch about having blood literally on your hands, plus convention letting us off the hook for the harms we merely permit.

So here's what I want: what would count against that view? I've read enough to know about the old bathtub case where the cousin drowns the kid versus just watching him drown, and it does a lot of work for my side, but I want the map of my position's exposure, not cheerleading. Which cases does the "no difference" view handle badly -- I've got a nagging worry about self-defense, about a doctor redirecting her own patient's medicine, and about the difference between starting a threat and declining to block one that's already rolling, but I can't tell if those are real counterexamples or just places where all else *isn't* equal. Sort the possible defeaters by how decisive they'd be: what would flat-out refute the view versus what would merely be awkward and require patching. Tell me what its own defenders would concede counts against it, not just what critics throw at it. And be honest about whether "all else equal" is doing so much work that the position has quietly become unfalsifiable -- because if the view just means "when the cases are identical in every morally relevant respect, they're identical," then it says nothing and my villain still isn't scary.

The deliverable matters to me practically: if there's a class of cases where the doing/allowing line survives every attempt to wash it out, I want to know, because I'll rebuild the third act around it. What would *I* find most persuasive? Probably a paired-case thought experiment where the only variable is doing versus allowing -- same motive, same outcome, same knowledge, same everything -- and the intuition of a moral difference refuses to die even after I stare at it. If no such case can be constructed without smuggling in an extra difference, that's evidence for my side and I'd like that stated plainly too. Please answer in American English, and feel free to be opinionated.
