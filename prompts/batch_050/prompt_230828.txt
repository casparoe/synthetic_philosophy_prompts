I am drafting the framing essay for a graduate seminar on the epistemology of logic, and I want to organize it around the problem posed in Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (Mind, 1895). The Tortoise insists that anything Logic dictates be entered in Achilles' notebook before it need be granted:

"Plenty of blank leaves, I see!" the Tortoise cheerily remarked. "We shall need them all!" (Achilles shuddered.) "Now write as I dictate:—
(A) Things that are equal to the same are equal to each other.
(B) The two sides of this Triangle are things that are equal to the same.
(C) If A and B are true, Z must be true.
(Z) The two sides of this Triangle are equal to each other."

Treating every step of an argument as one more proposition to be written down guarantees the notebook can never be finished: each further entry (D, E, F, ...) merely restates the missing step one line lower.

Write the desiderata an account of the justification of deduction must meet to count as an answer to this scene — a specification against which I can grade proposed solutions. Include: the phenomena the account must explain (that inference can extend knowledge, that competent reasoners terminate somewhere, that validity is recognizable without an infinite chain of grants); the distinctions it must respect (premise versus rule of inference, seeing that Z follows versus being moved to accept Z, justifying a particular inference versus justifying deduction wholesale); and the failures it must avoid (regress, vicious circularity, collapsing logic into psychology). Give the consideration behind each requirement, and mark which desiderata are non-negotiable for any account and which are the demands of a particular camp — for instance, the demand that modus ponens be justified even to a being who refuses it, or that the justification itself employ no deductive step. If some pair of desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly; that is the part I most want, since it would show every account fails somewhere. Briefly test two or three standard responses (meaning-constitutive rules, rule-circular justification, a dispositionalist reading of rule-following) against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivation.

End with a final line of the form Verdict: satisfiable — ... or Verdict: unsatisfiable — ..., stating in one sentence whether any single account could meet your entire list, and why or why not.
