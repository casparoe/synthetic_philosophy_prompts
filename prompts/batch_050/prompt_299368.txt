I am drafting the foundations chapter of my dissertation in formal epistemology, and before I commit to it I would like an audit of whether my core commitments are jointly tenable. I hold all of the following:

(a) Solomonoff induction is the correct ideal of inductive inference — not merely a useful heuristic, but the closest thing we have to a formal answer to the problem of induction: a reasoner with unbounded computational resources should predict exactly as it does.

(b) Occam's razor needs no independent justification: because the universal prior weights hypotheses by two to the power of negative description length (Kolmogorov complexity), a preference for simpler theories falls out of the ideal automatically, rather than being an extra principle bolted on.

(c) The choice of reference universal Turing machine is a technicality. The invariance theorem shows that description lengths assigned by different universal machines differ only by an additive constant, so the choice makes no real difference, at least once enough data has accumulated.

(d) The uncomputability of the universal prior is a practical inconvenience rather than a philosophical defect; ideals are not required to be implementable, as with frictionless planes in physics.

(e) I am a computationalist about the physical world: the universe either is, or may for predictive purposes be treated as, a computable process, so restricting the hypothesis space to computable hypotheses loses nothing of consequence.

Which of these genuinely pull against each other, which only appear to, and what is the least costly revision that restores consistency? Please order the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally serious, and for each one say specifically what would have to be given up and what that sacrifice costs. I would rather be told which commitment to weaken than receive five separate defenses.
