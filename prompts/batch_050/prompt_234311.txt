I recently moved to a cottage on the edge of farmland, and the move has unsettled me more than I expected. In the past month I have found a rabbit half-eaten by a fox, a hedgehog dying of mange, and, last week, a nest of fledglings abandoned after the mother was taken by a cat. I started volunteering a few hours a week at a wildlife rehabilitation center, mostly cleaning cages, and it has made me think constantly about a question I used to treat as a curiosity: whether the suffering of wild animals is something we have any obligation to do anything about.

At dinner last weekend this came up, and my partner Ruth and my old friend Theo got into a genuine argument about it. I stayed out of it, but I have been turning their exchange over since and cannot settle it. Here is the exchange as I remember it, in five moves.

(1) Theo opened by saying that wild animal suffering is plausibly the largest and most neglected moral problem there is. Most wild animals come from species that produce enormous numbers of offspring, nearly all of whom die young -- starving, being eaten, succumbing to disease or cold. If suffering is what matters morally, then the sheer scale of this means it dominates almost everything else we worry about, and we should be building a serious research field aimed at eventually intervening at scale: eradicating wildlife diseases, managing fertility, perhaps one day phasing out predation itself.

(2) Ruth replied that large-scale intervention in ecosystems is reckless on its face. Ecology is littered with interventions that backfired -- remove the predators and you get overpopulation, crop destruction, and mass starvation a few years later. Ecosystems are complex beyond our ability to model, so the honest position is humility. Besides, predation and disease are how ecosystems regulate themselves; a nature without them is not nature at all. And if we want to reduce animal suffering, the obvious place to start is the suffering we directly cause, like factory farming, where both our responsibility and our ability to act are clear.

(3) Theo said she was conflating two claims. "We do not yet know how to intervene safely" is an argument for research and caution, not an argument for principled non-interference as a final position. As for responsibility: the child drowning in the pond is not my fault either, but nobody thinks that excuses walking past. Limiting our duties to harms we caused is an arbitrary way of letting ourselves off the hook. And the claim that a nature without predation is not nature simply assumes that nature-as-it-is is worth preserving at the price of the suffering it contains. Ecosystems and species do not suffer; individual animals do.

(4) Ruth answered that the pond analogy fails twice over. First, pulling a child from a pond is cheap, safe, and predictable, and the entire problem with ecosystem intervention is that it is none of those things; if rescuing the child might trigger a flood that drowns the village, walking past becomes defensible, and interventions in nature are exactly like that. Second, his framework bulldozes every value except welfare: a world of managed, predator-free, contracepted wildlife parks is the abolition of the wild, and many people value wildness itself. Finally, she said, his whole scale argument presupposes that wild lives are on balance not worth living -- but that is an unsettled empirical question. Many wild animals may live mostly decent lives punctuated by a bad death, and you cannot infer from "many die badly" that the best response is fewer of them.

(5) Theo replied that if the value of wildness really outweighs the suffering, Ruth should be able to say roughly how much agony a unit of wildness justifies, and she cannot; invoking "other values" is just a refusal to count the cost that others bear. On net-positive lives: even granting that the average wild life is worth living, that changes which interventions would help, not whether the suffering counts -- you do not shrug at a refugee camp on the grounds that the average human life is worth living. And her flood version of the pond case actually concedes his central point: it is empirical risk all the way down, which is an argument for doing the research, not for permanent abstention.

They asked me to referee and I could not. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible with identifiable weaknesses, and give your reasoning. In particular, I want to know: whether Ruth's appeal to our direct responsibility for factory farming is a real distinction or a deflection; whether Theo's pond analogy survives her flood modification; whose burden it is to quantify the trade-off between wildness and suffering; and whether the empirical question about whether wild lives are net-positive actually matters as much as both seemed to think. Finally, tell me what both of them are missing -- including anything they overlooked about the cases that started all this, the individual injured animals in my garden, which neither of them mentioned again after move (1).
