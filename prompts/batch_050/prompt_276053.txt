In my philosophy of religion seminar we've just covered Pascal's wager, and like half the class I went in thinking I already understood it. The version that circulates outside the classroom -- and the one a friend of mine, an economics major, repeated back to me at dinner with great confidence -- goes roughly like this: if you believe in God and you're wrong, you've lost nothing; if you believe and you're right, you've gained everything. So believing is the rational move, full stop. My professor barely engages with this formulation and instead talks about infinite utility, mixed strategies, and the many gods objection, which suggests the slogan version is doing some heavy compressing.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims hiding in that two-clause slogan, as a menu I could actually use? I'm interested, for example, in what "you've lost nothing" would have to mean to be true -- whether it's a claim about the magnitude of the finite loss, or about there being no opportunity cost, or about belief being costless to adopt at all, given that I can't simply decide to believe something at will. And on the other side, what "you've gained everything" presupposes -- a unique god who rewards belief rather than sincere conviction or good conduct, and a decision theory that behaves sensibly when one outcome carries infinite value. For each reading, please say whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or something that's probably just false.

The part I most want adjudicated: which of these readings, if any, actually supports the conclusion my friend drew. He insisted the wager is simply a dominance argument -- believing wins or ties in every possible world, so it's rational regardless of how improbable God's existence is. My suspicion is that the slogan only delivers that conclusion under a reading that smuggles in contested premises, and that once you precisify it correctly, the argument becomes either valid-but-unsound or sound-but-far-weaker-than-advertised. Please tell me which it is, and what I'd need to concede to get his conclusion back.
