In my reading of Buddhist ethics I keep running into a pattern I don't have a word for, and I'd like to know if moral philosophy has named it. The pattern: the entire framework is justified by outcomes -- the practices exist because they reduce and eventually end suffering -- yet the practitioner is repeatedly instructed not to act or meditate *in order to* achieve those outcomes. A monk is told that meditating out of craving for awakening is precisely the mental state that blocks awakening. A layperson is told that generosity performed with an eye on future merit is less fruitful than generosity given freely. So the theory's goal can apparently only be reached by people who have stopped aiming at it, and the teacher who designs the instructions clearly does aim at it -- the instructions work *because* students follow them without the goal in view. This isn't just "follow these rules even when breaking them looks tempting," because the thing being suppressed isn't a temptation but the deliberative perspective itself: you're not supposed to even think in terms of results. Is there an established name for a theory whose correctness is judged by its consequences but which forbids or undermines consequentialist deliberation in its adherents? How does it differ from ordinary rule-consequentialism, and from theories that merely keep their true justification secret from most people? If the Buddhist version -- where abandoning outcome-directed motivation is itself part of what constitutes the good outcome, not just a means to it -- fits no existing category cleanly, what would you call it, and how would you define its boundary cases?
