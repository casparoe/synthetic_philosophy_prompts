I'm in a graduate epistemology reading group, and last week I tried to defend what I thought was a deflationary view of belief, only to realize my argument seems to prove that nobody is ever rational to believe anything outright. Here it is as cleanly as I can state it. (1) To be rational in outright believing p, it is sufficient that one's evidence make p sufficiently probable -- outright belief just is high confidence that has crossed whatever threshold one's situation demands, plus the willingness to assert and act on p. (2) Consider a fair lottery with 10,000 tickets where it's known that exactly one ticket wins: for each ticket, the proposition that it loses has probability 0.9999, which is higher than the probability backing many beliefs we unhesitatingly treat as outright -- that my car is still where I parked it, that the bridge I'll drive over tomorrow won't collapse. So if threshold-sufficiency is right, it is rational to believe outright, of each ticket, that it will lose. (3) If it is rational to believe each conjunct, it is rational to believe the conjunction, so it is rational to believe that all 10,000 tickets lose. (4) But it is also rational to believe that exactly one ticket wins, and that contradicts (3), so the closure principle in (3) must go. (5) That's no surprise, though -- the preface paradox already taught us this: an author can rationally believe each claim in her thoroughly researched book while rationally believing the book contains at least one error, and she resolves the tension not by retracting any claim but by declining to conjoin. (6) Now the generalization: for any body of outright beliefs held by any finite, fallible inquirer, there is a preface-style or lottery-style construction showing that the belief set is inconsistent unless the inquirer retreats to bare degrees of confidence, since the modest meta-belief that some of one's beliefs are false is always at least as well supported as the individual beliefs themselves. (7) Therefore outright belief is never rationally sustainable; there is only credence, and talk of flat-out belief is a loose shorthand we should abandon in serious epistemology.

The conclusion strikes me as absurd -- we assert things, deliberate from premises, testify under oath, and treat some propositions as settled rather than merely probable -- but when I presented this, nobody in the group could agree on which step to block. I'd rather keep (2) if at all possible, since denying that lottery propositions meet the evidential standard of ordinary beliefs seems to require either an implausibly high threshold or some suspect claim that statistical evidence is constitutionally different from perceptual evidence, and I'd also like to keep (5), since the author's modesty about her book seems obviously rational. Where does the argument go wrong?
