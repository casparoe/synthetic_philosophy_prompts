I became a citizen last year, and with a national election coming up I've been genuinely undecided about whether voting is worth the time. Over dinner last weekend, two friends of mine — Daniel, an economist, and Sofia, a philosopher — got into a sustained argument about it, and I've been unable to settle who won. I'd like an adjudication.

(1) Daniel opened with the standard calculation: the probability that one vote changes the outcome is vanishingly small, the hour spent voting is a real cost, and so expected-benefit reasoning says stay home. Voting, he said, is at best a symbolic act people dress up as rationality.

(2) Sofia replied that his calculation treats the decision as though it involved only one person. Tens of thousands of people share my values, my information, and roughly my cast of mind, and are running the same deliberation this week. Whatever I conclude is strong evidence about what that whole cluster concludes. If I stay home, that's evidence the cluster stays home; if I vote, evidence the cluster votes. So the stakes my decision bears on aren't one vote but the cluster's combined weight — which is large enough to clear Daniel's cost-benefit bar easily.

(3) Daniel answered that evidence is not leverage. My staying home doesn't cause anyone else to stay home, and acting on a mere evidential correlation is the notorious mistake of evidential decision theory — the theory that would advise against a medical treatment on the grounds that needing it is correlated with being ill. If the correlation between me and the cluster is produced by a common cause, say our having read the same pessimistic analysis, then my voting changes nothing about them and choosing on the basis of the correlation is superstition.

(4) Sofia made two replies. First, the correlation here isn't a shared symptom like the common-cause case; it's that the others are running the same deliberation from the same evidence and values, so my output is like a second reading from the same instrument, not a sign of it. Second, even granting Daniel his causal scruples: a causal decision theorist choosing in advance which dispositions to have would choose to be a voter, since populations of agents who each deliberate in Daniel's way end up with individually rational, collectively terrible outcomes — so his own framework, applied one level up, recommends abandoning it here.

(5) Daniel's last move: the similarity premise is doing all the work and there's no principled way to fix it. How similar, in which respects? By tuning "similar enough," Sofia can make any choice astronomically important or negligible. And the argument proves too much — my decision to tip at a café is likewise evidence about millions of tippers, so every trivial act inherits civilization-scale stakes and ordinary cost-benefit reasoning collapses entirely.

Please go through moves (1)–(5) one at a time, saying what each gets right and where it goes wrong; then say who is more right overall, what both of them are missing, and what it all implies for whether I should vote.
