A friend and I spent last Saturday arguing about generic sentences and neither of us could land a knockout. Her position is that generics are just hedged statistical claims: "ducks lay eggs" means roughly "most ducks lay eggs," or "enough ducks lay eggs," with the threshold set by context. My position is that the truth of a generic has essentially nothing to do with proportions, but when she demanded proof I could only gesture at examples I couldn't make precise. I want the demonstration constructed properly.

Build me three matched cases, each statable in three sentences or fewer, using only kinds any competent speaker already knows (common animals, plants, or artifacts -- no invented species, nothing a listener would need explained to them):

1. Two generics about the *same* kind, where the property in the first is instantiated by strictly fewer members of the kind than the property in the second, yet the first generic is intuitively true and the second intuitively false. The pair "ducks lay eggs" / "ducks are female" has this shape; construct your own rather than reusing it, because I want to know whether the pattern generalizes or is a one-off quirk.

2. A generic that is intuitively true even though the property is instantiated by a vanishingly small fraction of the kind -- on the order of one in a hundred thousand or fewer. (Cases involving reproduction or the intended function of artifacts are the most promising territory here.)

3. Two generics with matched middling prevalence -- say roughly 40% in both -- one intuitively true and one intuitively false, where the only relevant difference between the properties is their connection to what the kind is *for*, or to the characteristic way members of the kind come to possess the property.

One constraint applies to all three: neither property may be dangerous, alarming, disgusting, or otherwise attention-grabbing. I explicitly do not want the cases to turn on the fact that people generalize freely about striking things like disease or predators; if the argument depends on salience, my friend will (rightly) dismiss it as psychology rather than semantics.

For each case, give the generic, the stipulated prevalence, and the intuitive verdict, and show the derivation: why her covert-"most" semantics assigns the wrong truth value, and which single feature of the case is doing the work. Then compare across the three: are they all isolating the same feature -- the property's being characteristic of the kind, say, or its link to a mechanism or function -- or do they isolate different features, and if different, what happens to each case when its feature is removed?

Finish by steelmanning my friend. What is the weakest revision of the statistical view that survives all three cases -- and does that revision, once stated precisely, still count as a statistical view at all, or has it quietly become the position I was defending? Do not answer her with bare appeals to context-sensitivity; naming a free parameter is not an account of how it gets set. And if you find that one of the three constructions cannot be carried out without smuggling salience back in, say so plainly and explain why -- that would be a result in its own right, and an honest one beats a rigged demonstration.
