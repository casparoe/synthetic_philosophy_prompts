My coastal town is dedicating a storm-surge barrier next month, and because I teach philosophy at the university forty minutes inland and sit on the town council, I have been asked to give the ten-minute dedication address. The barrier is engineered for a design life of roughly 130 years, and the bond paying for it will be serviced almost entirely by current taxpayers, most of whom will be dead before the structure does its most important work. The audience will be mixed: the engineers who designed it, a few hundred residents, the mayor, and—because I invited them—two colleagues from my department who work on population ethics and will notice every philosophical misstep.

Here is my problem. Every draft I have written reaches for the same rhetoric: we owe this to our grandchildren, we are building it for the children who will live here after us. I know too much to write those sentences in good conscience. The difficulty is what philosophers call the non-identity problem: the people who will shelter behind this barrier in 2140 will exist only because of choices we are making now—different infrastructure, different jobs, different couples meeting and conceiving at different times. Had we not built it, had we adopted a different package of climate policies altogether, the future would have been populated by different individuals. So the barrier cannot straightforwardly benefit the very people it is meant to protect, since under the alternative policy they would never have existed at all. "We owe it to them" begins to look either false or in need of serious reconstruction—perhaps as an obligation owed to whoever turns out to exist, rather than to any identifiable future person; perhaps as grounded in the wrongness of leaving avoidably bad prospects for future persons, whoever they are, rather than in harm done to particular individuals. I do not want the speech to lecture on any of this, but I want every sentence to be one I could defend to my colleagues afterward, and I refuse to reach for a comforting fiction I know to be philosophically hollow.

Please write the address: about ten minutes when read aloud, warm enough for a town ceremony with folding chairs and a brass plaque, honest enough to survive the seminar room. It must still find a way to say why what we are doing matters and to honor the people who voted to pay for something they will never need—the difficulty is locating words for that which do not rest on claims the non-identity problem undermines. After the speech, append a short note, not intended to be read aloud, that flags where you have leaned on genuinely contested philosophical territory—for instance, whether obligations to future persons understood de dicto are coherent, or whether a non-comparative account of wronging future people is defensible—as distinct from what is broadly agreed among those who work on intergenerational ethics. I would rather know exactly where the speech is philosophically exposed than receive one that conceals it.
