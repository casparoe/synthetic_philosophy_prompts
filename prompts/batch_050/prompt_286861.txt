I am a retired biochemist who has lately been reading philosophy of mind, and I keep returning to a puzzle drawn from a practice I know rather well from the inside. Standard clinical psychiatry treats major depression with two interventions that its own guidelines regard as equally legitimate: pharmacotherapy, which acts on the brain's chemistry, and structured talk therapies such as cognitive behavioral therapy, which act on nothing but the patient's beliefs, reasons, and interpretations. The same clinician will prescribe a drug on the grounds that low mood is a neurochemical process, and then, in the next hour, attempt to relieve that same low mood by getting the patient to notice that a thought is distorted. The surrounding institutions presuppose the same picture: informed consent requires that the patient be given reasons and that those reasons issue in a decision, and health insurers reimburse both the prescription and the conversation on the assumption that each is causally efficacious.

Here is what I would like worked out. As I understand the causal exclusion argument, every bodily movement has a sufficient physical cause in the nervous system, so unless mental states simply are the relevant physical states, their content — what the patient believes, what the therapist's words mean — does no causal work at all. What view of mental causation does this clinical practice actually presuppose? Does it require that reasons and meanings are themselves causes, or is the practice strictly compatible with a thoroughgoing epiphenomenalism in which only the chemistry acts and the therapeutic conversation works, when it works, as an elaborate way of delivering acoustic stimulation to the auditory cortex? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, licensing several competing views at once? Finally, if we took exclusion seriously and concluded that content as such is causally inert, what in the practice — the consent process, the reimbursement structure, the talk therapies themselves — would honestly have to change?

I would prefer a disciplined answer rather than a survey: reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably, and answer the last question concretely. Please keep the whole reply to no more than 500 words.
