ok so context: i've been reading LW/SSC-adjacent stuff since like 2013 and there's this arguement that keeps resurfacing in my group chat about whether "just be more agentic" is actual advice or selection-effect cope. Someone dug up chapter 25 of The Prince (the fortuna chapter, W.K. Marriott translation) and we both got stuck on this bit where Machiavelli is discussing Pope Julius II, who charged into everything impetuously and kept winning:

"I will leave his other actions alone, as they were all alike, and they all succeeded, for the shortness of his life did not let him experience the contrary; but if circumstances had arisen which required him to go cautiously, his ruin would have followed, because he would never have deviated from those ways to which nature inclined him."

and then Machiavelli draws his moral:

"I conclude therefore that, fortune being changeful and mankind steadfast in their ways, so long as the two are in agreement men are successful, but unsuccessful when they fall out."

My friend reads this as the 16th-century version of "be more agentic": Julius won BECAUSE he moved fast, Machiavelli even says that if he'd waited in Rome until his plans were arranged he'd never have pulled it off, so fortune rewards boldness, qed. I read the passage as nearly the opposite — character is basically fixed, the wheel of the times turns, and "success" is just whether your unchangeable style happens to match the era you're in, which makes virtù look an awful lot like luck plus survivorship bias. And that line about the shortness of his life!! Machiavelli is practically admitting we never got to see Julius regress to the mean.
