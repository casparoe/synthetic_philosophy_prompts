I'm a novelist, and my oldest friend June is a bookseller and also my first reader, which means we spend a lot of time walking around the city arguing about nothing. One of our longest-running nothing arguments is about everyday conventions, and over the years I've assembled a mental dossier of her verdicts, because she's weirdly consistent and I can't figure out the rule. Here it is: she tips a solid twenty percent at sit-down restaurants even when the service is actively rude, on the grounds that "that's just what you do." She refuses to tip at the counter-order coffee place and calls the swivel-around iPad a guilt machine you are free to ignore. She would never cut a queue, even a disorganized one where nobody would notice or care, but she thinks saving a seat for a friend with a coat is perfectly fine. She jaywalks constantly at home — like, every day, without a flicker of guilt — though in fairness basically everyone in our city does. She brings her own snacks into the cinema in defiance of the sign and says the ticket price covers the film. But when she travels she's a totally different person: didn't tip in Japan because she'd read it comes off as insulting, queued scrupulously in London even where the locals were sloppy about it, covered her shoulders in churches without being asked. Her one-liner is that she's a guest.

Here's why I'm asking. I'm dragging her on a research trip for the book I'm writing, which is set partly in Berlin and partly in Prague, and I can already see two situations coming. First: Berlin, a red pedestrian light, a completely empty street at seven in the morning, and the locals all standing there waiting anyway, including a grandmother with a kid who is definately going to glare if we cross. Second: Prague, the trams run on the honor system, no barriers, nobody checked our tickets the whole time I was there last year, and everyone I asked said inspectors are rare. So: what does June do in each case — cross or wait, buy the ticket or ride free? I actually care less about the verdicts than about reverse-engineering her. Is she running a no-harm-no-foul rule, a when-in-Rome rule, a follow-what-people-will-actually-sanction rule, or something else? Lay out which rules fit her whole dossier equally well, which ones diverge on the Berlin light and which on the Prague tram, and give me your best guess for each with the reasoning. And then tell me what hypothetical case — or what thing I could casually ask her on the trip — would settle which rule she's really running.
