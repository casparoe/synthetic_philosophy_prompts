So I've been spending way too much time in the comment sections of AI governance threads lately, and one concept that keeps coming up is commitment races — the dynamic where agents who can credibly bind themselves early (a threat, a promise, an irreversible capability gain, a published "we will never negotiate" policy) get leverage over everyone else, so all sides face pressure to lock in their commitments before they've actually thought things through, and the whole game front-runs whatever slower, more deliberative collective decision-making might have happened. Classic examples people gesture at: nuclear posture and launch-on-warning, corporate poison pills, labs racing to commit to scaling trajectories so rivals have to respond. The strong claim floating around is that as commitment technology gets better and faster (smart contracts, automated agents, precommitted AI systems), this dynamic gets worse, not better — that improving our ability to commit is mostly defecting in a race, not progress.

Here's the thing though: my own view is that this is overrated as a deep problem. Commitment races look to me like ordinary prisoner's-dilemma-flavored coordination failures wearing a fancy new hat, and we have a perfectly decent toolbox for those — treaties, inspection regimes, escrow, norms, just... not building the irreversible-commitment button.
