So I'm a philosophy prof and a physicist colleague has roped me into giving the "conceptual" lecture in her second-year quantum mechanics course. Two hours, students have done the math of spin measurements but haven't really met the foundations stuff. I want to build the whole lecture around Bell's theorem -- not the historical controversy, the result itself: that no local theory can reproduce the quantum correlations.

Don't explain the theorem to me, plan how I should teach it. Specifically:

1. The dependency order. What has to be in place before the theorem is even statable? I'm thinking they need a crisp idea of what "local" is supposed to mean before any inequality appears, and I suspect the usual move of jumping straight to singlet states and correlation functions skips too much. Tell me what I'd think I could assume from a physics audience that I actually can't.

2. The carrying example. I could do the EPR-Bohm setup with Stern-Gerlach magnets, or something like the Mermin device with the two boxes and lights, or just abstract correlated coin-flips. Pick one to carry the whole lecture and say what it needs to have and what it must NOT have (e.g. I worry the coin example teaches correlation but lets locality slide by unnoticed).

3. The misconception map. Here are the ones I expect: that Bell rules out hidden variables full stop (when Bohmian mechanics is right there, nonlocal but alive); that the theorem shows quantum mechanics is incomplete; that the correlations imply we can signal faster than light; that "locality" just means relativity's speed limit, so the theorem is somehow in tension with relativity rather than showing something subtler; and the really sticky one -- that Bell assumed determinism and so the whole thing only bites on deterministic hidden variables. For each, tell me where in the sequence it forms and exactly where I should head it off (before the inequality, after, during the discussion of the assumptions?).

4. The fine print. At some point I have to mention that the derivation quietly assumes the measurement settings can be chosen independently of the hidden state (statistical independence / no superdeterminism), and that this is doing real work. When do I raise it so it's taken seriously as an assumption rather than a loophole the students can dismissively wave at, and how much time is it worth in a two-hour slot?

5. End of lecture: one question I can put to them whose answer would show they understood what the theorem constrains, versus one wrong answer that sounds sophisticated but would tell me they've missed it.

Casual is fine, this is for my own prep notes. And don't point me at readings, I want the pedagogy.
