I am a doctoral candidate in metaphysics, and in three weeks I will present the central chapter of my dissertation at our department's works-in-progress seminar. The chapter defends a position I call grounded absence causation. Its two claims are: first, that omissions and other absences are genuine causes, not merely explanatory conveniences, so that the gardener's failure to water the plant really does cause its death; but second, that causation by omission is always derivative, in that a true absence-causal claim is made true by a complete actual-process causal story — the cellular desiccation that proceeded uninterrupted — with the absence serving to pick out which actual process did the work by specifying what failed to intervene. The position is meant to thread between process accounts, on which only physical connections cause and absence-causation is a myth, and pure counterfactual accounts, on which dependence alone suffices.

The audience will include a senior metaphysician who defends a conserved-quantity account and has argued that absence-causation talk conflates causal explanation with causation; a philosopher of science sympathetic to interventionism, for whom absences enter causal models without fuss; a philosopher of action who thinks agency cases are sui generis; and several graduate students raised on Lewis who will ask why my view is not just counterfactual dependence with extra metaphysical decoration. I also expect someone to raise the experimental literature suggesting that folk causal judgments about omissions track norms rather than metaphysics — people happily call the negligent gardener's omission a cause but not the Queen's — and to press whether my grounding story explains that selectivity or is refuted by it.

Please prepare me a question-period prep sheet: the objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by likelihood weighted by damage, each phrased the way that person would phrase it (the conserved-quantity theorist does not talk like an interventionist). For each objection, give me what I should concede up front, what I should hold, a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, and a note on where that reply is soft. Anticipate at least the proliferation problem (if the gardener's omission causes the death, why not the Queen's, why not everyone's?), the charge that absences have no properties and so cannot stand in causal relations, the worry that my "grounding" clause makes omissions causally redundant, and the case where two gardeners jointly fail to water. Then tell me which objection I should most hope nobody asks, and why my position handles it worst. Finally, since you are predicting a room you have never met: attach a confidence level to each predicted objection, and close by singling out the single claim in the prep sheet you are least sure of.
