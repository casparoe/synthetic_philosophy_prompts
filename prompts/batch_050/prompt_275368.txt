I am a doctoral student in metaethics, working on the divide between robust normative realism and response-dependent accounts of value. For a dissertation chapter I want to bring the classical kalām dispute over ḥusn and qubḥ into contact with the contemporary literature, and I need a disciplined translation rather than the usual loose comparison to the Euthyphro dilemma.

As I understand the debate: the Mu'tazilites held that the goodness and badness of at least some acts is intrinsic to them and knowable by reason prior to revelation; that God Himself acts for reasons and aims at what is genuinely good; and that He is therefore bound, in the same deontic sense as His creatures, to act justly toward them. The Ash'arites held that nothing is good or evil in itself; that deontic status is constituted wholly by divine command and prohibition; that reason unaided by revelation establishes no normative facts; and that the very idea of an obligation incumbent on God is incoherent, since obligation arises only from a command addressed by a superior to an inferior, and God has no superior.

Please restate each position separately in the vocabulary of contemporary analytic metaethics -- robust realism, response-dependence, ideal-observer and fitting-attitude accounts, mind-dependence, divine command theory -- as precisely as you can, without smuggling in the target framework's assumptions. Then give me the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no expression in that vocabulary at all. If it helps, you may treat the later Māturīdite middle position -- reason grasps some values unaided, revelation completes the rest -- as a test case for whether any contemporary hybrid view does better.

Three points I would like addressed specifically. First, whether Ash'arite voluntarism differs from modern divine command theory in anything beyond framing, given that the Ash'arites deny God's own acts any deontic status whatsoever, while contemporary divine command theorists typically still want God to count as good. Second, whether the kalām dispute is really one disagreement or three -- ontological, epistemological, and concerning whether God can be a subject of obligation at all -- which my vocabulary treats as independent axes. Third, whether the standard Islamic-Euthyphro framing is itself one of the distortions.

Finally, your verdict: does the residue point to something contemporary metaethics cannot express, or does it show that the kalām positions are less determinate than they appear?
