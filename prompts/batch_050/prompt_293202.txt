Since retiring from my lab job (thirty-odd years in chemistry) I've fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole, and for a reading group I have to put my cards on the table about loyalty and whistleblowing. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Loyalty to actual people -- friends, long-time colleagues -- is a genuine moral value, not just a useful bias. Betraying a freind is always a real moral cost, even when reporting them is the right thing to do on balance.
2. Some misconduct is serious enough that you're obligated to report it even when doing so destroys a friendship and maybe a career.
3. Loyalty to institutions is a different matter entirely: an institution can't be owed anything for its own sake, so institutional loyalty is only ever instrumentally justified.
4. My old institute was right to discipline the postdoc who took evidence of data fabrication straight to a journal instead of going through the internal review first -- internal channels deserve their chance.
5. When a friend is accused of something, you owe them the presumption of innocence and a fair hearing before you side with the accuser.
6. When I finally saw the evidence that my old lab head, a mentor and friend of twenty years, had been massaging data, I judged him on the spot, and I still think I was right to. The evidence was enough.

So: does this set hang together? Tell me which pairs pull against each other and how severe each tension is, which apparent conflicts actually dissolve on a second look, and for each real tension what the cheapest fix would be and what it costs me. And if you had to pick one single revision that repairs the most, which is it? Please answer in American English.
