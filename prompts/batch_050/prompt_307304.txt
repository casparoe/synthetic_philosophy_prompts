You are a scholar with dual expertise in the history of economic thought and the philosophy of probability. Your task is to reconstruct a dead thinker's likely verdict on practices he never saw — reasoning from the premises of his view, not summarizing what commentators say about him. Keynes, in his 1921 Treatise on Probability and his later writings on uncertainty, defended claims roughly as follows: many real-world judgments of likelihood cannot responsibly be expressed as numbers between 0 and 1; the weight of evidence behind a judgment matters separately from the probability assigned (a probability of one-half resting on a hundred observed trials is a different epistemic state from one-half resting on a coin's symmetry); and for questions such as long-run investment returns or the outbreak of war, there is often no scientific basis for calculable probability at all.

Now consider two contemporary practices:

(a) Forecasting tournaments in which "superforecasters" assign numerical probabilities to one-off questions — say, whether a specific country will test a nuclear weapon within two years — and are rewarded purely for calibration across many questions.

(b) Machine-learning systems that attach calibrated confidence estimates to predictions (credit default, medical diagnosis, demand forecasting) from large historical datasets, and that can degrade silently once the deployment environment drifts from the training distribution.

Two questions. First, does practice (a) dissolve Keynes's objection to numerical probability — showing that disciplined people can form calculable probabilities even about singular events — or would he reply that calibration across a basket of many questions does nothing to supply the missing weight for any single one-off question? Second, is the confidence output of a neural network the kind of probability Keynes thought we sometimes legitimately have, or is it a case where his notion of weight is doing unrecognized work — and is distribution shift precisely the situation his uncertainty doctrine was designed to warn about?

Structure your answer in three parts: (1) a brief reconstruction of the two or three commitments in Keynes's theory that bear on these cases, not a survey of the whole theory; (2) your best reconstruction of his verdict on each practice, naming the single consideration most likely to drive each verdict and noting where his position is underdetermined; (3) one point where you judge his view would have shifted under pressure from the modern evidence, and one point where modern practice vindicates him against his Bayesian critics — each defended rather than asserted.
