I'm a PhD student in epistemology working on the value-of-knowledge literature, and I'm drafting a paper that tests the philosophical debate against a dramatic case rather than another thought experiment. I want you to read Shakespeare's Hamlet through the Meno problem in its second, less-discussed form -- not the paradox of inquiry from earlier in the dialogue, but the question Socrates raises near the end: if true belief gets you to Larissa just as reliably as knowledge does, why is knowledge worth more? Socrates' answer, as standardly glossed, is that true beliefs are like the statues of Daedalus that wander off unless tied down by an account of what makes them true -- knowledge is true belief secured by reasoning about the cause.

My claim in the paper is that Hamlet is a sustained dramatization of exactly this. The ghost gives Hamlet what turns out to be a true belief -- Claudius murdered the king -- delivered as testimony from a source whose provenance Hamlet himself flags as suspect (the spirit may be honest, or a devil in a pleasing shape). Hamlet refuses to act on the belief until he has tied it down: the play-within-a-play is, on my reading, an attempt to convert true belief into knowledge by eliciting independent behavioral confirmation of the cause, a kind of staged aitias logismos. I'd like you to develop this reading carefully: show how the Mousetrap scene functions as epistemic anchoring, contrast Hamlet's demand for tied-down belief with Laertes and Fortinbras, who act on far less, and take seriously the killing of Polonius, where Hamlet acts swiftly on an unverified guess and it goes catastrophically wrong -- the play seems to stage the exact failure mode Socrates' Daedalus analogy predicts.

But I also want the reading to be honest in both directions, because that's where I expect the paper to do real work. Where does the play resist the epistemological frame? Two pressure points strike me: first, Hamlet acquires what should count as secured knowledge after Claudius's reaction at the play, yet still fails to act at the prayer scene, which suggests the play thinks the epistemic gap wasn't the real obstacle -- does Hamlet thereby dramatize the objection that the value problem misdiagnoses what knowledge is actually for, since the deficit was motivational rather than justificatory? Second, the play ends in a bloodbath partly attributable to the very scrupulousness the Meno would seem to vindicate, so the drama may be critiquing the premium on knowledge rather than celebrating it. I'd like you to weigh both directions and take a position on which the text better supports, while flagging the reading's weak joints.

Two further requests. First, connect the reading to the contemporary literature where it genuinely bears on it -- the post-Gettier instability of untethered true belief, the question of whether knowledge's extra value is practical (licensing high-stakes, irreversible action like killing a king) or intrinsic, and the encroachment idea that stakes change what knowing requires -- but throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people working on this, and don't present a contested diagnosis (about the Meno passage, or about the value problem generally) as the consensus view. Second, end with a paragraph on what, if anything, the play contributes to the philosophical debate that the standard arguments and thought experiments don't -- or say plainly if you think the answer is nothing. Please write in American English.
