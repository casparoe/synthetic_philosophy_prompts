For the final project in my third-year seminar on Montaigne's Essays, each of us writes a research paper of about 6,000 words over the last eight weeks of term, and the first thing due is a two-page proposal. I have the beginnings of an idea but no proposal, and I would like you to turn the idea into one.

The idea turns on a passage near the start of "Of Cannibals" (Book I, chapter 30 in the Cotton translation, revised by Hazlitt, which we read for the course; chapter 31 in most modern editions), where Montaigne introduces the source of nearly everything he is about to tell us about the Tupinambá:

"I long had a man in my house that lived ten or twelve years in the New World, discovered in these latter days, and in that part of it where Villegaignon landed,—[At Brazil, in 1557.]—which he called Antarctic France. This discovery of so vast a country seems to be of very great consideration. I cannot be sure, that hereafter there may not be another, so many wiser men than we having been deceived in this. I am afraid our eyes are bigger than our bellies, and that we have more curiosity than capacity; for we grasp at all, but catch nothing but wind. [...] This man that I had was a plain ignorant fellow, and therefore the more likely to tell truth: for your better-bred sort of men are much more curious in their observation, 'tis true, and discover a great deal more; but then they gloss upon it, and to give the greater weight to what they deliver, and allure your belief [...]"

My half-formed thought is this. The passage lays down a standard for credible testimony — trust the witness too ignorant to have a theory — and then, so far as I can see, the essay either fails its own standard or the standard means something other than it appears to. Montaigne is exactly the kind of observer the passage warns against: educated, curious, and never anywhere near Brazil. He builds a portrait of a whole nation out of one servant's recollections, some seamen and merchants the man occasionally brought to the house, a couple of translated songs, and a brief conversation at Rouen conducted through an interpreter who could hardly follow him; he declines to consult the cosmographers and then goes on to describe at length the land, the houses, the food, and the wars of a people he has never seen; and the essay's most celebrated moments are not reports at all but judgments, measuring both Brazil and France against nature, antiquity, and the golden age of the poets. So the paper would ask: what, if anything, secures the authority of "Of Cannibals" by the lights the essay itself sets out? Is the praise of the simple witness sincere epistemology that the essay then violates; a division of labor, where the simple man reports and the learned man judges, that the passage quietly leaves room for; or a deliberate self-indictment — the essay performing on itself the suspicion it teaches?

I need the proposal to do the following, in about two pages (roughly 900 words; if you set the passage above as an epigraph, it does not count toward the length):

1. State the question precisely and narrowly, so that eight weeks and 6,000 words could actually settle it. Not "Montaigne and testimony" in general, and not the cultural-relativism reading of the essay that everyone reaches for first.
2. Explain why the question is still open: take the two or three most obvious resolutions — the ironic reading, the report/judgment division of labor, the self-consuming skeptical performance — and show what each one fails to explain about how the essay actually proceeds.
3. Lay out the method, week by week, with something concrete on paper at the end of each week. I expect the core of it to be close reading: a catalogue of every source of knowledge the essay draws on and what Montaigne does with each, plus a comparison with the neighboring chapter where he discusses our habit of measuring truth and falsehood by our own capacity. But I also want a paragraph arguing for this approach over the two alternatives my classmates will predictably take — a general paper on Montaigne's skepticism, or a historical source-study of the sixteenth-century travel literature on Brazil.
4. Sketch the two or three conclusions the paper might reach and what in the text would decide between them.
5. State the failure conditions: what would count as the project not working — including the possibility that the essay simply has no coherent epistemology of testimony, in which case I need to know whether "there is none, and here is why the essay wants it that way" is itself a defensible thesis or just a non-result.
6. Give an honest scope assessment: is this doable in eight weeks by an undergraduate who has read most of Book I once, and if not, what should be cut first.

Please refer to other essays and to scholarly positions by description rather than by citation, and do not include footnotes or a bibliography; I will supply the references myself.
