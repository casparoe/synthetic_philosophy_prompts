I have been working through the Hart–Fuller debate and would like to check whether I have understood its outcome correctly. As I read it, Hart defended the positivist line that the Nazi statutes under which the grudge informers acted were valid law, however wicked, and on that basis argued that the informers could only properly be punished under a retroactive criminal statute. Fuller, writing as the natural lawyer, held that the relevant Nazi enactments were not law at all, which I take to mean the informers could simply be punished directly for what they did, no retroactive statute required. Commentators generally seem to say that Hart won, and positivism certainly went on to dominate Anglophone jurisprudence.

Two things continue to puzzle me. First, if Fuller's position amounts to the claim that whatever a legal system does that we find morally repugnant is thereby not really law, does that not simply reduce "law" to "rules we happen to approve of"? That seems obviously question-begging, and yet it is presented as a serious rival theory of law, so I must be missing something.

Second, Hart's own remedy was a retroactive statute — and retroactivity is precisely one of the defects on Fuller's list of ways a legal system can fail. So in choosing that remedy, was Hart not tacitly conceding that Fuller's internal morality of law does the real work, since Hart needed one morally dubious law to undo the effects of another? Put differently: given that both men clearly wanted the informers punished, does the practical resolution of the grudge informer problem show that the two positions converge where it matters, leaving the celebrated debate as largely a quarrel about labels — about what we are willing to call "law" — rather than about anything of substance?
