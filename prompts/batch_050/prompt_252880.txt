So Im in this public econ class and we just did the unit on price controls, and the textbook treats them all identically — draw the floor or the ceiling, find the shortage or surplus, shade the deadweight loss triangle, done. But my gut reactions to actual cases are all over the place and I cant find the pattern. Here are my verdicts:

- Raising the minimum wage gradually to like $15: fine, even if it costs a few jobs at the margin.
- Setting it at $40 overnight: obviously insane. So apparently size matters, which feels unprincipled.
- Classic strict rent control in a tight city, locked-in tenants, rents frozen forever: bad. Landlords let buildings rot, insiders win, newcomers are screwed.
- Rent stabilization, like capping annual increases at 5% for sitting tenants but resetting between tenants: fine, actually good.
- The hardware store tripling generator prices right after the hurricane: feels scummy, but I don't want it banned, because that's exactly how supply shows up.
- Capping insulin prices way below what pharma would charge: totally fine.
- Laws banning ticket resale above face value: dumb, scalping should be legal.
- Capping payday loan interest at 36% APR: fine, maybe ban payday loans entirely honestly.
- Banning Uber surge pricing during a blizzard evacuation: fine. Banning surge pricing on New Years Eve: dumb.

Some of these seem easy to reconcile (supply elasticity, whatever) but some pairs I genuinely can't square — like why is capping the price of generators bad but capping the price of payday loans good, when in both cases the seller will just supply less? And why is a modest price floor okay but a big one not, if floors are bad?

What's the principle (or two) that captures as many of these as possible? Which of my verdicts would you make me give up to keep the principle clean?
