ok so i'm running a session at our local rationality meetup on the Ellsberg paradox and half the regulars have strong opinions but they all talk past each other. i want to hand out a one-page decision tree at the start of the night, the "which position do you actually hold" kind, so people have to locate themselves before we argue.

please build me that tree, with these constraints:

- build it from the logical space first. work out which questions actually seperate the positions, then the tree, then locate the real defended views in it. don't start from a list of names and work backwards.
- every branching question has to be a concrete case someone can answer without knowing any jargon. like, "you can bet on a coin you watched get minted and tested, or a coin a stranger pulled from their pocket -- same payout, do you care which?" that kind of thing. no questions of the form "do you endorse multiple priors?"
- the tree has to include, as cases somewhere in it: the classic two-urn setup (known 50/50 urn vs unknown-mix urn); a version where the person who filled the unknown urn gets to see which color you bet on first; and a non-urn case, like choosing between an index fund in a sector you understand and one in a country you know nothing about. i want the tree to catch people whose answer flips between adversarial and innocent framings, since i suspect that's like a third of the room.
- each leaf gets a name if the view is actually defended (or "unnamed" if not), plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. please include as leaves, if the question-space supports them: the orthodox bayesian "just assign 50/50 and stop whining" view, some version of maximin/multiple priors, and the view that ellsberg choices are a sensible heuristic for adversarial environments rather than a bias.
- the descriptive/normative split matters to me. somewhere the tree must force people to say whether they take Ellsberg to show that people choose irrationally, or that the standard expected-utility axioms are the wrong normative standard. people conflate these constantly and it wrecks the discussion.
- note any unoccupied combinations of answers and give a one-line diagnosis of why each is empty (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored). and flag any positions the tree genuinely can't tell apart rather than pretending the questions are sharper than they are.
- aim for 6 to 9 leaves total, sized so it fits on one page, audience is smart laypeople who've read the sequences but not the econ literature.
