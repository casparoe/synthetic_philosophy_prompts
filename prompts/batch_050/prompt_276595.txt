Three months ago I drove home after a thirty-hour shift at the hospital where I work as a resident. Somewhere on the ring road I drifted across the centre line for a moment; a cyclist in the opposite lane swerved onto the pavement and escaped with a fright. A week later a colleague left the same hospital, in the same state of exhaustion, after the same rota, and drifted the same way. The cyclist in her path swerved into a lamppost and died. My colleague has since been convicted of causing death by dangerous driving, lost her licence and her position, and is avoided by people who still greet me warmly in the corridor. As far as either of us can reconstruct, our choices were identical. Only the outcomes differed.

I set out to convince myself that the disparity in how we are treated is unjust, and I built an argument that now seems to prove far more than I intended:

(1) A person deserves blame only for what was within her control at the time of acting.
(2) Whether my lapse produced a near-miss or a death depended on factors wholly outside my control: where a cyclist happened to be, which way she swerved, a tenth of a second either way.
(3) Therefore my colleague and I deserve exactly the same blame, and the outcome should make no difference to how blameworthy we are.
(4) But the standard of control at work in (1) and (2) cannot stop at outcomes. My reflexes, my susceptibility to fatigue, my temperament, and the upbringing that produced all three were no more chosen by me than the cyclist's swerve.
(5) Nor was the internal course of my own deliberation up to me: which thoughts occurred to me and which considerations weighed with me were products of prior causes I did not choose.
(6) Therefore nothing about any action ever meets the standard of control in (1); no one ever deserves blame for anything; and every act of blame is merely the punishment of bad luck.
(7) Yet our practices — apology, resentment, punishment, the difference between forgiving someone and merely managing them as one manages a hazard — are intelligible only if blame is sometimes deserved.
(8) So (1) must be false: control cannot be the sole basis of blame.

I want to keep (3), since the conviction that the gulf between my colleague's treatment and mine is unjust is what started all of this, and I cannot bring myself to accept (6). Tell me which step to reject, explain why it looked sound in the first place, and state what rejecting it costs relative to the other steps I could have rejected instead. If the real fault is an equivocation or a slide somewhere between (3) and (6), name it precisely rather than gesturing at it, and say whether repairing it saves the argument or merely relocates the problem. I would also like to know what my preferred premise (3) commits me to beyond my own case — it seems to have consequences for criminal sentencing and for how I should feel about my own near-miss that I have not fully thought through.

Finally, please be candid about the state of your own answer: say how confident you are in the diagnosis overall, and single out the one claim in your response that you are least sure of.
