Backend dev here, zero philosophy training, sorry in advance if I butcher the math. I've fallen down a rabbit hole about Löb's theorem from reading AI safety stuff about self-improving agents. My rough understanding: any formal system strong enough to do arithmetic can talk about its own proofs, but it can't prove its own consistency (Gödel), and worse, if it could prove its own soundness — a schema like "if I can prove P, then P" — then Löb's theorem kicks in and the system proves literally every statement, i.e. its inconsistent. Hence the "Löbian obstacle": a self-modifying AI can't justify trust in its successor's reasoning by proof alone, or so the argument goes.

Fine. But I've been sitting with this for a week and I think the same reasoning "proves" something absurd about me and every engineer I work with, and I can't find the bad step. Here it is:

1. Every correct piece of human mathematical reasoning can in principle be written out in a formal system (ZFC or whatever). That's just what formalizability means, and nobody I know doubts it.

2. So there's some formal system that captures my mathematical reasoning as a whole. Call it S.

3. When I trust my own reasoning — say, that my merge sort actually terminates, or that some invariant holds through my database migration — I'm trusting that whatever S proves is true. If I didn't trust that, I wouldn't be entitled to trust the conclusion.

4. Trust that's worth anything has to be justified, and justified trust in a proof system means being able to show that what it proves is true. Otherwise it's blind faith, and blind faith isn't knowledge.

5. By Löb, no consistent system strong enough for arithmetic can show its own soundness.

6. So my trust in my own mathematical reasoning is either blind faith or S is inconsistent.

7. It's not blind faith. My confidence comes from a track record: proofs check out, code passes tests, bridges stand. That's evidence, not faith.

8. Therefore S is inconsistent, i.e. deep down I'm an inconsistent reasoner.

(8) is nuts. My best guess is that the problem is step 5, that I've misunderstood what Löb's theorem actually blocks, since I'm self-taught here and got all this from blog posts. But when I re-derive it the conclusion seems to follow fine, so maybe the flaw is elsewhere. If I had to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and (7) and lose whatever else needs losing.

So: which step goes wrong, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it actually cost me? And a follow-up I'm genuinely curious about: does whatever rescues me here also rescue the self-improving AI, or does the Löbian obstacle survive for machines even if it fails for humans? If human reasoning gets a pass for some reason a formal agent can't have, that seems important, and if it doesn't, I want to know that too.
