I am a PhD student in normative ethics who has spent the past year sitting in on an interdisciplinary reading group on AI safety, and I keep running into the phrase "aligning AI with human values," along with its shorthand cousin "the alignment problem." What strikes me is how much work the slogan is asked to do. In one session, a computer science postdoc used it to describe a quite specific engineering difficulty: a system given a proxy objective will often satisfy the letter of the objective while violating its spirit, and getting it to robustly do what its designers actually want is surprisingly hard. Two weeks later, a political theorist in the same group used the same phrase to argue that the whole project is confused, since "human values" are contested, plural, and historically mutable, so there is nothing stable to align to. A third participant, an economist, treated alignment as essentially a principal–agent problem that has existed forever — managers and employees, principals and lawyers — with AI simply making an old incentive-design problem faster and higher-stakes. Each speaker seemed to think the slogan transparently meant what they meant, and each treated the others' usage as a distraction.

I would like a disambiguation I can bring back to the group. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that "aligning AI with human values" could be making — for instance, that the system should follow its operator's instructions, that it should pursue its operator's intentions rather than their stated instructions, that it should serve its users' revealed preferences, that it should serve their considered or idealized preferences, that it should promote some mind-independent account of human good, or that it should defer to whatever a legitimate collective process would endorse. For each reading, I want to know: whether it is trivial, substantive-but-tractable, or substantive-and-deeply-open; whose values or preferences it takes as the standard and what it does about disagreement among them; and which of the three readings in my group it most closely matches. I am not asking for a bibliography — just the conceptual map.

Finally, the question I actually care about: on which of these readings is "the alignment problem" a single problem at all, and on which does it decompose into several largely independent problems — technical instruction-following, preference aggregation, moral uncertainty, value change over time, and whatever else you think belongs on the list — such that solving one leaves the others untouched? The postdoc insists alignment is a technical problem with a technical solution; the political theorist insists it is a moral and political problem wearing technical clothing. I suspect each is right about a different disambiguation of the slogan, but I cannot currently say which disambiguations those are, and I would like that spelled out with a verdict about which reading, if any, deserves the name.
