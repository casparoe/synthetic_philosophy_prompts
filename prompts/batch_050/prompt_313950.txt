For my history of science seminar this week we're reading the General Scholium Newton added to the end of the Principia (I've been looking at the old Motte translation). The bit I want to focus on is near the end, where Newton says we know God only through his most wise contrivances of things and final causes, that we admire him for his perfections but reverence and adore him on account of his dominion, since we stand to him as servants, and that a god without dominion, providence and final causes would be nothing but fate and nature.

Write a dialogue between two students with opposite readings of this passage. Maya reads it as natural theology tacked onto the physics: Newton has built the machine, God is the engineer you infer from the clockwork, and the dominion stuff is conventional piety decorating what is basically a design argument. Tom reads it the other way round: on this passage dominion and will are what make God God at all, so the laws of nature are contingent on divine choice rather than metaphysically necessary, and that is exactly why Newton thinks natural philosophy has to run on experiment and observation instead of spinning systems out a priori. On Maya's reading the physics delivers the theology; on Tom's the theology dictates the method.

Ground rules: neither should be a strawman, and both should know the text. Maya should press Tom on why an avowedly empiricist work is smuggling in voluntarist theology, and on the fact that Newton claims to know God *only* through the contrivances, which sounds like her direction of inference. Tom should press Maya on why Newton bothers distinguishing admiration from reverence at all if God were just a hypothesis to explain the machinery, and on what work the servants image is doing. Have them respond to each others actual points, argue over specific words in the passage, and end by pinning down a real crux they cant resolve (eg what the admire/reverence split implies about which direction the inference runs) rather than just agreeing to disagree. Around 600 words of dialogue.
