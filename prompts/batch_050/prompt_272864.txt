At a neighborhood bakery, a sourdough starter that had been going for eleven years was found dead one morning. The two candidate causes: an overnight power outage, or the new intern, who had been seen scrubbing containers and might have thrown it out. Two regulars, Priya and Marcus, fell to arguing over coffee.

(1) Priya: "If the outage didn't kill the starter, the intern did." She says this is close to certain — something killed it, and everyone agrees those are the only two candidates.

(2) Marcus replies that she's overstating: recast as "If the outage hadn't killed the starter, the intern would have," the claim is anything but certain — the intern is conscientious, and there's no reason to think a second assassin was waiting in the wings.

(3) Priya says he hasn't touched her claim: she made the first conditional, not the second, and the two differ in mood, so one can be near-certain while the other is dubious. Her claim rests on what they know — the starter is dead, and there are two suspects — whereas his recast concerns how the night would have unfolded without the outage, a different question answerable to different facts.

(4) Marcus answers that this is grammatical sleight of hand: the two sentences contain the same words and describe the same dependence between the outage and the starter, so if one is nearly certain the other must be too — otherwise "if" would change its meaning with the tense of the verb, which he finds absurd.

(5) Priya's last move: imagine it turns out the intern had fully resolved that night to dump the starter no matter what. Then Marcus's recast comes out true after all — while her original claim was already in good standing before that discovery. Facts that settle one conditional, she says, can leave the other untouched, so they can't be the same claim.
