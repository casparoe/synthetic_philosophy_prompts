Question: A museum label says, “The clock in Case 7 is broken.” Unknown to visitors, Case 7 contains two clocks, one broken and one working. Explain how Russell’s theory of descriptions analyzes the sentence, including existence, uniqueness, and the claim made about the object. Then explain why the fact that visitors naturally think of the broken clock does or does not make the label true. Finally, distinguish the two readings of “The clock in Case 7 is not broken” that Russell’s scope distinction makes available.

Student answer: Russell would not treat “the clock in Case 7” as a proper name for a special object. The label says roughly that there is at least one clock in Case 7, there is at most one clock in Case 7, and anything that is a clock in Case 7 is broken. Since the case actually contains two clocks, the uniqueness clause fails. Russell therefore treats the sentence as lacking a truth value, because the description was supposed to pick something out but failed to do so. The visitors’ tendency to think of the broken clock might guide communication, but it cannot supply the missing uniqueness required by the semantic analysis. The negated sentence has two readings. If the description has wider scope than negation, it says that there is a unique clock in Case 7 and that it is not broken. If negation has wider scope, it denies that there is a unique broken clock in Case 7, so it can be true when uniqueness fails. This shows that ordinary language can be misleading because definite descriptions look like referring expressions even though they are really quantificational devices.
