I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I got into a running argument with a coworker after our hardware random number generator once returned a 256-bit string of all zeros during testing. He said the string was obviously not random; I said it was exactly as random as any other output. While reading up, I found Per Martin-Löf's 1966 paper The Definition of Random Sequences, in which he develops von Mises's older idea that a random sequence is one that no admissible selection rule or gambling strategy can exploit, proposing instead that a sequence counts as random when it passes every effective statistical test of randomness. What strikes me is that his definition is explicitly about infinite sequences, whereas everything I actually handle -- keys, salts, test fixtures -- is a finite string. So I want to know what my own gut verdicts on finite strings commit me to, and whether the principle behind them is anything like the test-based notion Martin-Löf defines or something else entirely.

Here are my verdicts, for whatever they reveal. The all-zeros string from a certified quantum RNG: random, because of how it was produced. The first 256 bits of pi's binary expansion: not random, because a short program regenerates them. The output of a CSPRNG whose seed I don't know: random for me but not for whoever holds the seed, which makes randomness look observer-relative, and that conclusion bothers me. Twenty fair coin flips coming up all heads versus an alternating heads-tails run: equal in probability, yet the alternating one feels less random, which I admit sits awkwardly with my first verdict. The binary digits of Champernowne's constant, which pass every frequency and normality test I know of but are computable: not random, though I can't say what test they fail. A string a person typed by mashing the keyboard: produced by a haphazard process, but I'd bet it's full of patterns, so it feels less random than it should if my first verdict is right.

Is there a single principle that captures most of these, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough that I can see which case motivates each clause? In particular, am I tracking two different things -- the provenance of a string versus the pattern in it -- and if so, which one does the Martin-Löf-style notion actually answer to, and does anything in that framework survive the move from infinite sequences to finite strings, or must finite-string randomness be a different concept altogether (say, compressibility relative to a fixed machine)? Tell me which of my verdicts you would overturn to get a coherent account and why, and what the surviving principle predicts for a case I haven't listed, such as the first 256 bits of a genuinely Martin-Löf-random infinite sequence, or a string generated by a die loaded to favor sixes. Finally, I'd like the answer to be explicit about its own fragility: which of my verdicts does your proposed principle lean on most heavily, where is your reasoning most likely to fail, and what argument or consideration -- for instance, a case I haven't considered, or a result about the uncomputability of compressibility -- would lead you to a different verdict?
