Construct a thought experiment in which an agent rationally honors a sunk cost -- that is, rationally lets the size of a past, unrecoverable investment influence a current decision -- under a set of exclusions designed to block the standard rationalizations. The rationality of the agent's choice must not derive from any of the following: (i) information generated by the past investment about the prospects of continuing; (ii) reputational or signaling effects, including effects on the agent's ability to commit credibly to others; (iii) self-binding against the agent's own predictable weakness of will; or (iv) non-separable payoffs, where the value of completing the project depends causally on resources already spent, so that continuing is simply the best forward-looking option and nothing genuinely sunk is doing any work. The intended mechanism is a preference over the agent's own history -- something in the vicinity of caring that one's undertakings be finished rather than abandoned midway -- and the case should be engineered so that this preference, and nothing else, carries the load.

Constraints on the scenario: it must be realistic and small-scale (a hobbyist, a homeowner, a small business owner; no laboratories, lotteries, or trolleys); it must fit in one paragraph; and it must have a matched-pair structure -- two agents who face identical forward-looking choices (same remaining costs, same prospective benefits, same outside options) but different investment histories, and who rationally choose differently, so that the influence of the past is demonstrated by the comparison rather than merely asserted.

After the scenario, demonstrate that it meets the specifications. State each agent's options and payoffs; show, one by one, why exclusions (i) through (iv) fail to apply; and identify the single feature of the case that forces the verdict, including what the rational choice becomes if that feature is removed. Then present the strongest available objection from the orthodox position that sunk costs are always irrelevant to rational choice. There are two versions to consider: first, that a preference over the shape of one's own history is itself irrational, so the case shows at most that irrational preferences make honoring sunk costs instrumentally sensible; second, that granting the preference redescribes the case as ordinary forward-looking maximization, with completion counted as a future benefit, so that nothing sunk is honored after all. Say which objection you find more serious, whether either of them succeeds against the case as constructed, and what your verdict implies about whether the sunk cost fallacy names a genuine error of reasoning or merely a stipulation about which preferences count as admissible.
