For the past two years I have run a small monthly philosophy-of-mind reading group; most of the members found one another through the rationalist blogs, so the room is disproportionately full of software engineers who have read their Hofstadter and their Dennett and who arrive at every topic as confident, slightly combative materialists. Next month it is my turn to lead, and I have chosen the careenium: Hofstadter's thought experiment of a sealed container holding an enormous number of tiny ricocheting marbles — his simmballs — whose micro-collisions, viewed at a coarse enough grain, compose stable, recurring macro-patterns, symbols, that reliably trigger one another the way entertaining the thought of Paris might set off the thought of France. The session's question is his question: when one symbol sets off another, is the symbol level genuinely doing any pushing, or is all the real shoving happening marble-on-marble, with the symbol-level story a mere gloss laid over the substrate?

The pedagogical difficulty, as I see it, is that my audience's starting position and its failure mode are mirror images. Half the room will walk in insisting that the causal closure of the physical settles everything, that symbols cause nothing, and that anyone who demurs is flirting with magic; I expect many of them, the moment that position is stated baldly, to flip into the despairing version — so minds are epiphenomenal and nothing anyone thinks ever does anything. I want them to land at neither. The symbol-level story is not a rival force competing with the marble-level story for the same causal work, and dismissing it as mere description proves too much, since the identical argument would render avalanches, economies, and running computer programs causally inert as well. What makes this genuinely hard to teach is that both mistakes feel like sophistication, and the correct picture — the symbol-level account earns its keep by capturing the counterfactual structure, the facts about which marble-configurations would have produced the same outcome, that no marble-by-marble narration can state — is easy to recite and hard to actually hold.

What I want is not a miniature lecture but a battle plan for a single hour. Give me the order in which the pieces must be assembled and which piece presupposes which; I suspect a non-mental two-level case, where macro-causation feels uncontroversial because nobody's own mind is at stake, has to precede the careenium itself, but tell me whether that suspicion is right. Choose the one example to build the hour around — my shortlist is Conway's Game of Life, the story we tell about why a heat-seeking missile swerved, and the careenium itself — and justify the choice by saying what the carrying example must have (a real triggering relation between macro-patterns, not merely emergent shape) and must lack (anything that smuggles intentionality in early). Map the misconceptions in the order they will surface and mark the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; I predict at least these four: that downward causation must violate physics, that causal closure refutes the symbol level by itself, the engineer's objection that the hardware does all the real work, and a slide into free will that will eat the hour if I let it. Specify one check I can run at the end that distinguishes having understood the levels point from having memorized the vocabulary — ideally something like asking them to state what observation would falsify the symbol-level causal claim, rendered as a counterfactual about marble configurations. And tell me what to openly bracket at this level, saying so out loud, rather than letting it derail the session. Please close with a single line beginning "Verdict:" answering the session's title question — do symbols push the substrate around? — in one sentence, the sentence I would want a participant to carry out of the room.
