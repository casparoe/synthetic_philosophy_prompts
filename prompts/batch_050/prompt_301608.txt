Act as a scholar of American pragmatism who is equally at home in the philosophy of science, and think carefully before committing to a verdict. Analyze the Sokal affair through the lens of Rorty's antirepresentationalism. I'm especially interested in the following: what Rorty would say the dispute between Sokal and the Social Text editors was actually about, given that he denies there's a fact of the matter about which vocabularies "correspond" to reality; whether Rorty's position forces him to side with the editors, with Sokal, or whether it reframes the quarrel entirely (note that Rorty was often lumped in with the postmodernists Sokal targeted, but was also a critic of much of that camp); where Rorty's framework goes silent -- for instance, on the empirical content of quantum gravity, or on the institutional question of what peer review is for; and, running the analysis in the other direction, what the episode shows about Rorty's view itself: does the hoax's sting depend on representationalist intuitions Rorty rejects, does the public reaction vindicate his claim that the authority of science is a social achievement rather than a metaphysical one, or does the affair expose a blind spot in a philosophy that has little to say about deliberate bad faith? Please don't just retell the standard science-wars narrative; I know that part already.

Structure your answer as exactly five numbered points, each a short paragraph of three to five sentences, covering respectively: the mapping of the episode onto Rorty's framework, Rorty's likely verdict on each side, where his view is silent or strained, what the episode shows back about the view, and your own bottom-line judgment. No introduction and no conclusion beyond the five points.
