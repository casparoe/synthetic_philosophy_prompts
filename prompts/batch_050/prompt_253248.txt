I run a small interview series on political ideas. My next guest is a scholar of early Chinese thought who defends a provocative thesis: that Han Feizi's Legalism — governing through publicly promulgated law (fa), administrative technique (shu), and the ruler's positional authority (shi), all working on people's responsiveness to reward and punishment rather than on their virtue — is a better template for designing modern institutions than any view that depends on cultivating good character in officials. I have no academic background and have only read Han Feizi in translation, so I'd like your help drafting eight interview questions, each followed by a follow-up keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give. I want the questions to press on the theory's actual weak points, not invite him to restate it. Please include at least: (1) a question about what motivates the person at the top — Han Feizi places the ruler above the law and assumes everyone else can be moved by the "two handles" of reward and punishment, but does Legalism have any account of the enforcer's incentives, or does it quietly reintroduce virtue at the apex of the system?; (2) a question about gaming — if ministers and citizens respond to measured incentives, what keeps them from optimizing the proxies rather than the ends, and did Han Feizi grasp this problem or only its opposite?; (3) a question on the apparent tension between demanding that laws be public while keeping techniques of control secret from one's own ministers; (4) a question confronting him with the Qin dynasty, the state most thoroughly run on Legalist lines, which collapsed within two decades of unification — what does he do with that as evidence?; and (5) a closing question about what observation, in history or in a modern state, could count against his thesis. Avoid anything biographical or generic.
