I'm in an applied ethics seminar and we've just covered the paradox of blackmail: threatening to reveal a truth unless you're paid to stay silent is criminal, even though revealing the truth and asking for money are each separately legal. The paradox itself grips me less than my own case intuitions, because I can't find a principle that captures them. A tabloid photographer offers to sell me compromising photos of myself rather than publish them: clearly wrong of him. A stranger who knows about my affair demands payment for his silence: clearly wrong. My neighbor, who has every legal right to build a fence blocking my view, accepts my offer of money not to build it: perfectly fine. An employee who threatens to quit unless given a raise: fine. A union threatening a lawful strike unless wages rise: fine. But an employee who offers, for a price, not to report safety violations to the regulator: not fine, even though reporting them would be legal and arguably good. And a lawyer who threatens to sue unless the defendant settles: fine, which puzzles me, since it looks structurally identical to the photographer's offer. My tentative principle is that a demand counts as blackmail when the threatened act would have no point for the threatener except as leverage, but this seems to misclassify the settlement case and possibly the strike case. Can you give me a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, noting which case motivates each clause, what it predicts for cases I haven't listed (say, paying a rival business to stay out of my market, or paying a witness not to testify), and honestly stating which of my verdicts it must overturn? Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as you would in a letter.
