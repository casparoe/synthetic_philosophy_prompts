Okay, here's something I've been chewing on while putting together my phil of science seminar for next term. Over the last couple years I've gotten properly fluent in Bayesian confirmation theory — priors, likelihood ratios, conditionalization, Dutch books, the whole toolkit — and I keep having the same experience when I reread Popper. Big chunks of falsificationism seem to translate over almost without loss: risky predictions, severe tests, the ban on ad hoc rescues all have pretty obvious Bayesian glosses (gerrymandered hypotheses get penalized through their likelihoods, low-prior hypotheses that survive a hard test get a big boost, that sort of thing). But other chunks feel like they just don't have a Bayesian home. Corroboration, for one — Popper insists it's not confirmation and has nothing to do with probability, and I honestly can't tell whether that's a real distinction or Popper being stubborn. Same with the idea that a theory can be highly falsifiable, well-tested, and still be, probabilistically speaking, a lost cause. And the methodological stuff — the rules against conventionalist stratagems — seems to live at a level Bayesians don't even recognize as existing.

So here's the exercise. Restate the core of Popper's falsificationism — the demarcation criterion, the logic of severe testing, the prohibition on immunizing moves, corroboration, degrees of falsifiability/content — in Bayesian vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, taking the Bayesian side as seriously as the Popperian one (no strawmen either direction, please). Then give me a residue report: which theses translate cleanly, which only translate if you bend Popper out of shape, and which have no Bayesian counterpart at all. For that last category I want an actual verdict in each case: is the Bayesian framework missing something real (a place for methodological rules? a notion of content or verisimilitude?), or was Popper trafficking in distinctions that simply don't survive formalization? And tell me whether the Popper–Miller argument changes the picture — my grad students keep citing it as a knockdown against any inductive reading of corroboration, and I'm not convinced its as devastating as advertised.

I'm a philosophy professor, not a statistician, so keep the actual math light, but don't dumb down the philosophy to compensate.
