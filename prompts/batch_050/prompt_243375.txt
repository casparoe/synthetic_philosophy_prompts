I am a high school student preparing a discussion paper for my philosophy club on the ethics of procreation. My starting point is a famous thought in Arthur Schopenhauer's The World as Will and Representation (Volume II, in the chapter on the vanity and suffering of life): he asks, in effect, whether children would still be brought into the world if procreation were an act of pure reason rather than blind instinct, and suggests that rational sympathy with the coming generation would instead spare it the burden of existence. This anticipates what contemporary ethicists call the procreation asymmetry: the claim that we have a duty not to create a child whose life would be bad, but no duty to create a child merely because its life would be good, since no one is wronged by never existing.

Here is my request. Construct a thought experiment, suitable for a high school seminar, in which the procreation asymmetry and ordinary common-sense morality deliver conflicting verdicts about whether it is permissible to create a particular child. Constraints: the scenario must be realistic (a fertility clinic or a family, no science fiction, no predictors); the interests of all already-existing people must be held fixed across the outcomes, so that only the prospective child's existence varies; it should fit in one paragraph and must not stipulate the verdicts. After the scenario, please (1) state the verdict the asymmetry gives and why, and the verdict common sense gives and why; (2) identify which structural feature of the case does the work, say whether the non-identity problem is the crux, and explain what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed; (3) give the strongest reply available to a defender of each side. Finally, bring the case back to Schopenhauer: does it vindicate his claim that pure reason would refuse to procreate, or does it expose a gap between his pessimism and the modern asymmetry, which is not itself a pessimist doctrine? Please write your answer in American English.
