A bit of context: I run a small book club at work and last month we read a popular ethics book (I won't name it, it's not important). Afterwards one of my colleagues, who is a barrister and very sure of himself, sent round his own summary of the chapter on lying and misleading, which he's now treating as settled doctrine in the office. His version goes roughly like this. Philosophers have established, following Kant, that there's a bright moral line between lying and misleading: a lie is a false statement, and lying is always wrong because it abuses the hearer's trust in your word, whereas misleading someone with statements that are technically true is a different thing entirely, because you've left the truth sitting there in plain view and the hearer has deceived himself by leaping to conclusions. On this basis, my colleague says, the moral verdict is straightforward: if you want to get someone to believe something false without doing anything wrong, just choose your words carefully so that everything you literally say is true. He gave the example of telling your boss "I wasn't at my desk when the call came in" when in fact you'd seen it come in and ignored it — technically true if you'd stepped out a second earlier, and therefore, he claims, morally clean. He finished by saying that anyone who thinks misleading is just as bad as lying is confusing the etiquette of conversation with morality, and that the whole point of the distinction is that conversation works because we only owe each other the literal truth.

Now, I don't have any philosophy beyond a first-year module fifteen years ago, but something about this smells off to me. I half remember that the philosophers who actually work on this stuff — I think there are debates about what even counts as a lie, involving implicature and so on — don't conclude that misleading is a free pass. And I have a vague sense that Kant himself was not exactly relaxed about deception. But my colleague is cleverer than me and cites the book confidently, so maybe I'm wrong. Is his summary a fair representation of the philosophical position, or is he smuggling in claims the literature doesn't support? What I'd really like is a breakdown: which bits of his summary are accurate and widely accepted, which bits conflate separate questions (I suspect he's run together "what is a lie" with "what is morally worse" but I can't quite articulate it), which bits overstate a more modest claim people actually defend, and whether his boss example comes out the way he thinks once the summary is corrected. If there's no consensus on the moral ranking of lying versus misleading, I'd like to know what the main positions actually are, so I can go back to him with something better than a bad smell.
