I am preparing a seminar pairing Dickens's A Tale of Two Cities with the historiography of the French Revolution, and I want a reading of the novel disciplined by that scholarship rather than a free-floating thematic essay. Read the novel against three broad interpretive lines. First, the social or class interpretation associated with historians like Lefebvre and Soboul, on which the Revolution is an intelligible, even necessary, response to aristocratic oppression: consider what the Evrémonde brothers' backstory, Gaspard's child, and the Marquis's carriage are doing as causal argument, and whether Dickens convicts the ancien régime in a way that licenses the rising. Second, Tocqueville's continuity thesis, on which the Revolution completed rather than overturned the old regime's centralizing, arbitrary habits: consider whether the novel's parallel apparatus of injustice — lettres de cachet and the Bastille on one side, the Tribunal's denunciations and the guillotine on the other — is staging exactly that continuity, with Dr. Manette's buried letter, written by a victim of the old order, becoming the instrument of the new. Third, the revisionist stress on ideology, political culture, and contingency associated with Furet, on which the Terror was not dictated by circumstances but driven by a logic of suspicion internal to revolutionary discourse: consider Madame Defarge's knitting as a technology of registration and vengeance, and the sheer accidents — Darnay's return, the letter's survival — on which the plot's verdicts turn.

Then address the adage that revolutions devour their children. Does the novel endorse it, complicate it, or quietly reverse it — Manette being both the Revolution's symbolic father-figure and its near-victim, and the novel's sympathies lying with a substitute who walks willingly to the scaffold? Where the novel resists all three framings — its melodrama, its moral economy of sacrifice and redemption, its debts to Carlyle's history — say so honestly rather than forcing the fit, and say what, if anything, the novel contributes to the historiographical dispute that argument alone does not (I have in mind what its treatment of Manette's relapses suggests about trauma and the transmission of violence).

Finally, treat this as a defeasible reading, not a settled verdict: state explicitly what would change your mind — which scene, weighed differently, or which historiographical claim, if weakened, would shift the novel's allegiance from one interpretation to another — and identify the point where your reading is most likely to be over-fitting a nineteenth-century melodrama to twentieth-century historiography.
