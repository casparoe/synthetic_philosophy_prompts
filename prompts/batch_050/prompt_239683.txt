I have been reading Bertrand Russell's Marriage and Morals (1929) and reconstructing, in numbered form, an argument that I think follows from the position he stakes out there. In that book Russell contends, in essence, that a sexual ethic is justified only by its effects on human welfare; that the traditional code governing marriage and sexual conduct derives largely from superstition, taboo, and the interest of fathers in certainty of paternity rather than from any concern with happiness; and that jealousy is an irrational and possessive passion which a rational morality should seek to diminish rather than to enforce. Here is the reconstruction:

(1) A rule of sexual conduct has authority over us only insofar as conformity to it tends to reduce suffering or increase welfare; rules whose origin lies in taboo and superstition, rather than in any such tendency, bind no one.

(2) The traditional prohibitions on sex before marriage and outside marriage, and the expectation of absolute lifelong fidelity, originate in religious taboo and in property-and-lineage concerns, not in considerations of welfare.

(3) Therefore these rules, as rules, bind no one; fidelity is owed only where, and because, its breach would actually cause harm.

(4) The harm caused by a breach of fidelity consists almost entirely in the distress and jealousy of the betrayed partner (setting aside collateral matters such as children, disease, and deception discovered, which can be handled separately).

(5) Jealousy is an irrational, possessive emotion which a rational ethics should aim to discourage rather than to accommodate.

(6) Harm that flows entirely through an emotion ethics ought to discourage cannot count in favor of a rule; to let it count would be to let morality be held hostage by the very feelings it is supposed to reform.

(7) Therefore a breach of fidelity that remains permanently undiscovered, producing no collateral harm, is not wrong at all; and since nothing in the reasoning is specific to sex, any promise may be broken without wrongdoing provided the breach is never detected.

Step (7) strikes me as absurd: it collapses the distinction between morality and the mere prudence of not getting caught, and it cannot possibly follow from a book whose whole method is to take human welfare more seriously, not less. Yet each step, taken in isolation, seems like something Russell either says outright or would have little reason to deny, and I cannot locate the exact point at which the reasoning leaves the rails.

Tell me which step you reject, or which premise is least costly to give up if the reasoning is valid. I would most like to keep (1) and (2), since they seem to me the core of what is valuable in Russell's critique; I am willing to sacrifice almost anything else, including my own formulations in (3) through (6). A good answer should name the defective step or premise, explain why it looked acceptable in light of Russell's actual commitments, and state the price of the alternatives: in particular, if the fault lies in how (5) and (6) treat the suffering caused by emotions we disapprove of, say what principled difference there is between discounting jealousy and discounting other feelings a rational ethics might wish people did not have; and if the fault lies in (3), say what grounds obligation once inherited rules are dismissed, if not the promises and expectations people actually create. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which step or premise fails, in one sentence — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
