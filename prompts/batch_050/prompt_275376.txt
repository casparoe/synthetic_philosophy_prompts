I've been persuaded by the lesson usually drawn from Carroll's tortoise: that no non-question-begging justification of a basic rule of inference like modus ponens is possible, since any argument offered for the rule must itself rely on inference, so the attempted justification either goes in a circle or launches a regress. Tell me what would count against this skeptical conclusion. In particular, consider whether the demand for justification could be met by something other than a deductive argument -- say, a demonstration that the rule is necessarily truth-preserving, a derivation of it from the meanings of the logical connectives, or an appeal to the fact that we cannot help but reason this way -- and whether rule-circularity (using modus ponens in the course of justifying modus ponens, without assuming it as a premise) is actually vicious in the way premise-circularity is. Which of these moves would a committed skeptic concede as genuine threats, and which would they dismiss as changing the subject? Rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and give an honest verdict on how exposed the skeptical position really is.
