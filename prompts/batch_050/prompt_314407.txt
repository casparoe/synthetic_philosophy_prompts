Last night I had a long argument with a friend about Thomas Malthus. His position was blunt: Malthus predicted that population growth would outrun food supply and produce recurring catastrophe, the catastrophe did not arrive, so the theory was simply wrong, and the modern habit of saying he was "right about the mechanism but early about the timing" is an unfalsifiable excuse that no serious thinker should accept. My position was that long-run theories about constrained systems can be substantially correct in structure while failing on timing, and that dismissing them as falsified the moment a deadline passes misunderstands how such theories work. Neither of us conceded anything, and I suspect the disagreement is partly that we keep sliding between different notions of what a failed prediction even is.

I work in machine learning, specifically on forecasting, and I would like the whole dispute restated in the vocabulary I actually use every day: training distributions, distribution shift and regime change, out-of-distribution generalization, conditional versus unconditional forecasts, backtesting, model misspecification, parameter updating versus architectural revision, and the difference between refitting a model and patching it with ad hoc corrections. Translate the Malthusian claim itself into that vocabulary as precisely as you can — something like a model fitted on a regime in which one quantity grows geometrically and another only arithmetically, extrapolated far outside its support — and then translate the three standard verdicts on it (falsified, vindicated-but-delayed, and rescued-beyond-recognition) into the analogous verdicts one could reach about a forecasting model whose long-horizon predictions failed because the world changed.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about most. Which elements of the philosophical dispute map cleanly onto forecasting practice, and which only map with distortion? In particular: is there any legitimate analogue in my vocabulary for the difference between a theory being wrong and a theory's scope conditions having silently lapsed, or is that distinction one that forecasting simply cannot express? Does the Popperian accusation — that "right but early" is a protective maneuver, like adding epicycles — correspond to anything sharper than the familiar warning against overfitting a story to failed backtests, or does something real get lost there? And when you find claims in the Malthus debate that have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all, tell me whether that absence is a defect of the forecasting framework (something it genuinely cannot represent) or a sign that the original philosophical claim was doing less work than it appeared to. I want to know whether my friend's position is secretly the rigorous one, or whether rigor is exactly what it lacks.
