Having spent my career in developmental biology, I now find myself, in retirement, drawn to philosophy of mind, and I would like help thinking through an episode from my own field that seems to bear directly on one of its central questions.

In 1986, after a research program initiated under Sydney Brenner, John White and colleagues published the complete wiring diagram of the nervous system of the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans: 302 neurons and roughly 7,000 connections, essentially invariant from one animal to the next. It remains, to my knowledge, the only animal whose connectome has been fully mapped. The sequel is what interests me. In the decades since, well-funded efforts (the OpenWorm project being the most public) have attempted to reconstruct the worm in silico, and despite possessing the entire wiring diagram and computing resources beyond anything Brenner could have imagined, no simulation has reproduced the animal's behavioral repertoire in a manner the community finds persuasive. Even predicting the output of small circuits from the diagram alone has proven surprisingly difficult, partly because synaptic strengths, neuromodulators, and extrasynaptic signaling are not captured in the diagram, and partly because the animal's behavior depends on its body and environment in ways the diagram does not record.

I would like this episode analyzed through the lens of computationalism, by which I mean the thesis, which I have met in functionalist philosophy of mind, that cognition is computation: that what makes a system minded is its abstract organizational structure, not its material, so that a faithful enough simulation of the right causal organization would itself be (or behave exactly as) the original. The worm seems to me the cleanest possible test case, since here alone the implementational facts are completely known. Please map the episode onto the theory with some discipline: what, precisely, does functionalist computationalism predict a project like OpenWorm should deliver, and is the connectome even the right level of description for that prediction, or does the theory already distinguish the wiring diagram from the functional organization it is supposed to implement? Then consider the shortfall itself. A defender will presumably say the simulations failed for want of synaptic weights and dynamical detail, not because computation is the wrong frame; a critic from the embodied or enactive camp will say the failure shows that no organization abstractable from body and milieu could do the job. Tell me which features of the episode each side explains and which it must explain away, and say honestly where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak. Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: is this episode an anomaly for computationalism, a confirmation of it (on the grounds that the failures are all implementational), or evidence that the thesis is so permissive that no biological result could embarrass it, which would itself be a finding?

I should be grateful if your answer were written as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables of any kind.
