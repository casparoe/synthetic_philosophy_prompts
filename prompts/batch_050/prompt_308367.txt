I am a novelist at work on a book whose chapters run in no consistent order — a woman's life told from the inside, in which certain afternoons recur, slightly altered, in different decades, and the reader is meant to wonder whether these are misremembered scenes or the same moment happening twice. An editor I trust told me the conceit puts me in debt to Borges, so I finally read his essay A New Refutation of Time, where he takes the idealist's demolition and pushes it one step further: Berkeley dissolves matter into perception, Hume dissolves the perceiving self into a bundle of impressions, and Borges argues that once you have granted both, nothing remains to hold the moments in a row — a single perception repeated, whether in one life or across two strangers, breaks the linear sequence, and so time itself goes the way of matter and the self. I found it strangely moving rather than merely clever, and I need to know whether that is because the argument is good or because I wanted it to be.

Here is my request. Give me your credence — an actual number — for the following proposition, stated as precisely as I can manage: there is no single linear succession of moments of the kind Borges denies, such that events become present one after another in an objective order; succession as we experience it is not a deep feature of the world. But I want the number derived, not announced. Decompose the question into the sub-questions your credence actually depends on — I would expect these to include whether the idealist premises Borges inherits (no mind-independent matter, no continuing self beyond a series of impressions) are defensible; whether his inference succeeds even granting those premises, since it is not obvious to me that a repeated perception destroys order rather than merely instantiating it twice; whether considerations he never touches, such as what relativity does to a universal present, reach a similar conclusion by an entirely different route; and whether the felt character of experience — the thing he confesses to in his closing pages, where he seems to surrender to the very time he has just refuted — counts as evidence against the conclusion, is explained away by it, or is simply silent. Assign a rough probability to each with a line of justification, say how they combine into your final figure, and tell me which term the number is most sensitive to.

For calibration: my own credence, sober and in daylight, is around 0.2, though it rises appreciably past midnight; a philosopher friend puts hers at 0.7 and calls mine a failure of nerve. Tell me where our disagreement most likely lives in your decomposition. And one last thing, as a practitioner rather than a student: does it change your assessment if Borges is read not as offering a metaphysical proof but as performing what it feels like to take one seriously — and if so, should it?
