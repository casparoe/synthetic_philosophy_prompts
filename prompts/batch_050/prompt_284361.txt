My paper defends a thesis most historians will find crude: that the longevity of large territorial empires can be forecast actuarially. I assemble a reference class of several dozen empires over the past two and a half millennia, argue that the annual probability of collapse is roughly constant across an empire's lifespan — so that a venerable, apparently stable empire is not, for that very reason, safer than a young one — and I apply the method to a present-day great power, assigning it a quantitative probability of surviving intact for another half-century. Next month I present this to a history department colloquium. The audience: mostly working historians committed to contingency, agency, and the density of the particular, several of whom regard quantitative historical sociology as a discredited fad; two ancient historians who will know my reference-class cases better than I do; one economic historian comfortable with statistics; and one junior colleague sympathetic to cliodynamics who will nonetheless need to test me in public.

Prepare my question-period briefing sheet. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually face from this audience — not the ones a statistics seminar would raise — ordered by the damage each would do if I answered badly, each phrased in the idiom of the person most likely to raise it, with attribution to the relevant corner of the room. For each: what I should concede immediately, the two-minute reply, and the soft spot in that reply I should know about before I give it. Distinguish the objections that are genuinely methodological and answerable from those that express a disciplinary identity commitment, which must be managed rather than refuted, and tell me which is which. One complication I want handled explicitly: this audience will reach for "this time is different" — nuclear weapons, instantaneous communication, international law — which reverses the usual roles, since dismissing that move is normally the forecaster's job; advise me on how to contest it without appearing to claim that nothing about statecraft has changed in two thousand years. State the single concession I should make in my opening remarks to buy the most credibility with this room. Finally, include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and why it is the most dangerous one.
