So I got into a long argument at a pub night with some friends about independence referendums, and I keep circling a problem I can't find a word for. Everyone talks about whether some region has a right to secede, but my issue is one step earlier: who even gets to vote on that. And the specific thing that bugs me is cases where the population of the region was itself shaped, on purpose, by the state the region is trying to leave. Like, imagine a central government spends fifty years encouraging its own people to move into a restless province, and then says sure, hold your referendum -- knowing full well the settlers and their kids will vote to stay. Or the flip version: the region draws its voter rolls to exclude exactly those people, and now the descendants of the settlers say the vote was rigged against them. I've read a bit about real cases that have this flavour -- the way Scotland's 2014 vote gave the franchise to anyone resident in Scotland but not to Scots living in England, the Baltic states tying citizenship to pre-Soviet ancestry after independence, New Caledonia restricting its referendum electorate to long-term residents -- and in each one, whatever rule you pick seems to quietly decide the outcome before a single ballot is cast.

Now, someone told me democratic theorists do have a term for the general who-is-the-people question, the boundary problem or demos problem or something like that. But my sense is that's about the abstract puzzle that you can't democratically decide who the demos is without circularity, any vote on the boundaries needs a prior electorate. What I'm describing seems nastier and more specific: it's not just circularity, it's that one side has already acted in the world to predetermine the composition of the voter pool, so the question of the right franchise is inseparable from judging that past manipulation, and there's no neutral rule left standing. Restoring the "original" population rewards historical claims over present residence; counting everyone currently there rewards the manipulation.

So: does this particular configuration have a name of its own, or is it really just the boundary problem with extra steps and I'm reinventing something? If there's an established concept, tell me what it covers and where my version exceeds it. And if there isn't one, I'd actually love a proposed name plus a definition sharp enough that I can tell which real cases fall under it and which don't -- like, does it apply when the demographic change was an unintended side effect rather than a deliberate strategy? Does it apply to Quebec's indigenous nations, who'd make the same move one level down? I don't need a reading list, just the carving.
