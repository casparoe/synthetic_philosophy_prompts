I'm stuck on a passage in Kant's Critique of Pure Reason (I'm reading the old Meiklejohn translation, from the Antinomy of Pure Reason, Section First, where he's setting up which categories generate cosmological ideas):

"But, secondly, all the categories are not available for this purpose, but only those in which the synthesis constitutes a series—of conditions subordinated to, not co-ordinated with, each other. Absolute totality is required of reason only in so far as concerns the ascending series of the conditions of a conditioned; not, consequently, when the question relates to the descending series of consequences, or to the aggregate of the co-ordinated conditions of these consequences."

Can you help me untangle this? Specifically: what exactly is the difference between the ascending and the descending series, and why does Kant think reason only generates a transcendental idea (and hence an antinomy) for the former? I sort of get the intuition that a cause renders its effect possible but not vice versa, but I dont see why that should stop reason asking about the totality of a series of effects. And second, I'm puzzled about whether the first antinomy even obeys this rule: the question there is whether the world is limited in space, but the parts of space are co-existent and co-ordinated, not subordinated as conditions to a conditioned -- so on Kant's own criterion, shouldnt that antinomy be disqualified? Am I misreading what "condition" means here, or is there a genuine tension? Please answer in British English.
