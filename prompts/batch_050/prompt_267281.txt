Last week I got into a long argument with a friend after our philosophy of physics reading group, and it ended with her claiming that the Everett interpretation, taken seriously, makes rational action impossible. Neither of us managed to land a decisive blow, and I've been turning her argument over since. Here it is as best I can reconstruct it, with her approval of my numbering. (1) If Everett is true, then for any quantum event, every outcome with nonzero amplitude occurs in some branch; there is no fact of the matter about which outcome "will" happen, because all of them do. (2) Probability is a relation among alternatives of which exactly one is realized; where all alternatives are realized, there is nothing for probability to attach to, only actualities of various measures. (3) So Born-rule weights, in an Everettian world, are not probabilities; they are just numbers labeling equally real branches, and calling them "likelihoods" is a confusion inherited from collapse talk. (4) Rational choice requires genuine probabilities: a gamble is rational only if its good outcome is more likely than its bad one, not merely if a good outcome exists somewhere in reality. (5) Therefore, if Everett is true, no one has a rational reason to prefer a quantum gamble whose winning branches carry Born weight 0.9 over one whose winning branches carry weight 0.1; Born-rule-following behavior is a superstition. And here is where she pressed further, in the part I find most disturbing: (6) the very same reasoning applies to my own deliberation, since whatever I choose, there will be branches where things go well and branches where they go badly; my choices cannot make any outcome more likely, only shuffle measure among outcomes that all occur regardless. (7) So Everett implies fatalism about everything, quantum or not. Since fatalism is false, she concluded, either Everett is false or the empirical success of the Born rule is an unexplained coincidence.

I strongly suspect this argument proves too much — a parallel line of thought seems available in a deterministic single world, where the future is likewise "already" settled from the four-dimensional perspective, yet nobody takes that to refute ordinary probabilistic reasoning or deliberation. But I can't locate exactly where the argument goes wrong, and I'd rather abandon almost any single premise than accept (7). Which step do you reject, and why did it look plausible in the first place?
