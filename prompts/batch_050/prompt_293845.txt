You are a professional philosopher with expertise in Buddhist metaphysics, personal identity, phenomenology, and moral responsibility. Think carefully and begin your response with a brief issue-spotting list before giving your structured assessment.

I defend a position I call **Ownerless Center Compatibilism**. It interprets Buddhist no-self as denying that there is an additional entity or further fact that owns experiences, persists unchanged through them, and originates choices from outside the causal order. Nevertheless, it affirms conventional persons: relatively stable, embodied centers of experience and agency constituted by interacting memories, intentions, habits, affective dispositions, social relationships, and self-models. On this view, a person is not a mere illusion, but neither is there a metaphysical owner behind the person-process. Survival is a matter of sufficiently continuous organization rather than strict numerical identity, while responsibility and prudential concern attach to causal continuity, responsiveness to reasons, and participation in an ongoing practical perspective.

What would count against this position? Do not simply ask whether Buddhist no-self is true, and do not provide a general survey of Buddhist schools. Test this specific reconstruction.

Your answer should:

1. **Identify its most consequential commitments.** What must be true about experience, agency, persistence, and responsibility for the view to work? Where does it risk collapsing into a substance theory of self, eliminativism about persons, or an unrestricted bundle theory?

2. **Construct the strongest possible defeaters.** Consider, at minimum:
   - fission, fusion, teleportation, exact replication, and gradual replacement;
   - the apparent unity and “for-me-ness” of present experience;
   - whether deliberation requires treating one’s future self as categorically different from other people;
   - whether responsibility can survive if personal identity is conventional and degree-like;
   - whether the view illegitimately derives a metaphysical conclusion from the absence of an introspectively observable owner;
   - and whether its conventional-person layer is doing explanatory work or merely reintroducing the self under another name.

3. **Distinguish kinds of counterevidence.** Separate metaphysical counterexamples, phenomenological objections, possible results in cognitive science, ethical consequences, and internal tensions within Buddhist thought. For each, explain what observation, argument, or case would count as evidence and why.

4. **Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.** Say which objections a fair-minded defender should concede as genuinely damaging, which can be accommodated without changing the position, and which rely on question-begging intuitions about selfhood.

5. **Specify abandonment conditions.** What single discovery or argument, if accepted, should make me abandon Ownerless Center Compatibilism rather than revise it? Conversely, if the view is insulated against every possible finding because “conventional” can be stretched indefinitely, say so and assess whether that flexibility is a virtue or a defect.

Please reason from concepts and hypothetical cases rather than citing texts, authorities, or bibliographies. I am especially interested in objections that a sophisticated defender would regard as real risks, not only objections made from rival frameworks.
