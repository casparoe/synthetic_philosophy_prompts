The exam question, as the students got it:

"State Fitch's paradox of knowability. Reconstruct the derivation showing that the knowability principle (every truth is knowable) entails that every truth is known, indicating which properties of the knowledge operator you use at each step. Then describe one prominent response to the paradox and say whether you think it succeeds."

The student's answer:

"The knowability principle says that for every proposition p, if p is true then it is possible to know p: p → ◇Kp. This principle is central to semantic anti-realism, since the anti-realist holds that truth cannot outrun what we could in principle come to know.

Fitch's paradox shows that this principle, together with some weak assumptions about knowledge, collapses into the absurd conclusion that every truth is actually known. Take any truth that is in fact unknown: p ∧ ¬Kp. Since it's a truth, the knowability principle applies to it, so ◇K(p ∧ ¬Kp). Now consider what K(p ∧ ¬Kp) involves. Knowledge distributes over conjunction, so K(p ∧ ¬Kp) entails Kp ∧ K¬Kp. By factivity, K¬Kp entails ¬Kp. So the supposition that the conjunction is known yields both Kp and ¬Kp -- a contradiction. A contradiction is impossible, so ◇K(p ∧ ¬Kp) is impossible, contradicting knowability. Hence there is no true but unknown proposition: for every p, p → Kp.

Note that the derivation is entirely constructive; it goes through in intuitionistic logic, so the anti-realist cannot escape the conclusion by retreating to intuitionism.

One prominent response, due to Williamson, is to restrict the knowability principle to 'Cartesian' propositions: propositions p such that Kp is logically consistent. The offending instance p ∧ ¬Kp is not Cartesian, since knowing it leads to contradiction, so the restriction blocks the derivation at the first step while keeping the principle for all the propositions the anti-realist actually cares about. I think this succeeds, since nobody ever meant the knowability principle to apply to self-undermining cases."
