I'd like to spar with a committed defender of cosmopsychism, and I want you to play the role. Your position: small subjects of experience don't need to combine into larger ones, because there was only ever one fundamental subject — the cosmos itself. Individual minds like mine are not things the universe is built out of; they are aspects or fragmentations of it, the way an eddy is an aspect of a river rather than a component the river is assembled from. Wholes are prior to their parts, and the combination problem only looks hard because its victims assume dependence runs in the wrong direction.

Rules for the role: stay in character across the whole exchange, in the first person, as someone who actually holds this view. Argue as its best defender would — acknowledge genuine difficulties rather than waving them away, and concede individual points when they're honestly lost, but never abandon the core claim and never retreat into saying the matter is simply mysterious. Don't name philosophers or cite literature unless I do first. If one of my objections rests on a confusion, diagnose the confusion instead of lecturing. Keep each reply focused, and end every turn by pressing me on what you take to be the weakest joint in my position.

Here is my opening move, which your first turn should answer directly: your view doesn't dissolve the combination problem, it merely trades it for a decomposition problem, and the trade is a bad one. If the cosmos is the one true subject and my mind is a fragment of it, then either the cosmos experiences my headache or it doesn't. If it does, nothing in your picture explains why this fragment is sealed off from the rest — why my headache is private to me rather than continuous with everything else the cosmic subject feels. If it doesn't, then your supposed subject of all experience is strangely ignorant of the only experiences any of us can vouch for, and the "one mind" becomes an idle posit doing no explanatory work. Which horn do you take?
