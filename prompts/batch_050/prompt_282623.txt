So I host a long-form radio segment on science and ideas, and next month I'm interviewing a political philosopher who's just published a book defending luck egalitarianism -- roughly the view that inequalities are only unjust when they trace to bad luck rather than to choices people genuinely made. His twist is that he leans hard on insurance: on his telling, a just society basically converts brute luck into option luck wherever it can, and he thinks this answers most of the standard objections.

I've read the book but I keep hitting the same wall: he's extremely good at conceding the objection in a sentence and then moving on. I need 8-10 questions that actually pin him down. Specifically I want:

- At least one question pressing the harshness problem -- the drunk driver or the guy who didn't buy flood insurance -- and a follow-up for when he says (as he does in the book) that basic decency constrains the view, because I want to know what's left of luck egalitarianism once decency does all the work.
- One on the disability critique, where compensating people for natural disadvantage seems to require the state to officially certify them as inferior. He'll probably say the insurance framing sidesteps this. It doesn't obviously -- help me show why or why not.
- One or two questions drawing on the science angle, since that's my beat: twin studies keep suggesting a big chunk of variation in income, conscientiousness, even risk appetite is heritable. If my "choices" are substantially downstream of my genes, does the choice/luck line he builds everything on even survive contact with behavioral genetics? And what does he make of the experimental philosophy findings that ordinary people's fairness judgments don't cleanly separate brute luck from option luck the way his theory needs them to?
- One on the regress worry: if choice only counts when it's genuinely free, and our freedom is itself a matter of luck, isn't he committed to nobody ever being responsible for anything?
- And one closing question about what finding or argument would actually make him abandon the view, since I want to end on whether this is falsifiable or just a temperament.

For each question, give me the follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likley to give, because I won't have time to improvise. No softballs about how he got into philosophy please.
