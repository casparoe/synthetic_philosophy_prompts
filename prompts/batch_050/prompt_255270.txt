I'm writing a master's thesis in philosophy of mathematics, and the position I've come to defend -- my supervisor calls it "complexity-theoretic epistemic boundedness," though I'd rather not inherit the label -- is roughly this: the limitative results that dominated twentieth-century philosophy of mathematics, Gödel's incompleteness theorems above all, identify the wrong boundary for an epistemology of actual mathematical practice. What really limits mathematical knowledge is not unprovability but infeasibility: the gap between checking a proof and finding one, which I take to be cashed out by the conjecture that P ≠ NP. On my view, verification is mechanical and cheap while discovery is, in the general case, intractable, and this asymmetry -- not any consistency-driven incompleteness -- is what explains why mathematics requires something like insight, why automation has displaced calculation but not proof-writing, and why an adequate epistemology of mathematics should treat feasibly provable, rather than provable simpliciter, as the epistemically relevant notion. I've built the thesis around the claim that this position is philosophically load-bearing: P ≠ NP isn't just a technical conjecture but, if true, a theorem-grade fact about the structure of knowable mathematics.

What I want from you is an adversarial map of my own position: what would count against it, and how badly? I'd like you to distinguish, carefully, between several kinds of threat and to rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. For instance: would a proof that P = NP actually kill the view, or can it survive by retreating to the claim that even polynomial-time discovery might be practically infeasible (and does that retreat render the position toothless or unfalsifiable)? Does the position lean illicitly on worst-case complexity when the epistemically relevant question is whether proof search is hard on average or on the instances mathematicians actually encounter -- and what would it mean for the view if heuristic solvers routinely crack instances that are worst-case hard? Do randomization, quantum computation, interactive proofs, and probabilistically checkable proofs blur the clean verification/discovery asymmetry I'm relying on, or are they red herrings? Is the whole thing hostage to the unproven status of the conjectures it cites, and if so, is that a defeater or just an embarrassment? And is there a deeper objection that I've misidentified the relevant complexity class entirely -- that general proof search in a formal system is far worse than NP, so that P ≠ NP is simply the wrong separation to be doing this philosophical work?

Two further requests. First, for each defeater, tell me whether a committed defender of this position would concede it as counting against the view or would dismiss it as a category error, and why -- I want to stress-test my own instincts about which fights are worth having. Second, end with an honest verdict on whether the position, as I've stated it, is genuinely exposed to evidence or has been quietly insulated by qualifications, and whether that insulation is a virtue of flexibility or a vice of vacuity. If the answer is that the view is really just a metaphor dressed in complexity-theoretic clothing, I'd rather hear it now than at my defense.
