So I got into a dumb argument at work about whether what you know can literally change what you see, and I want a thought experiment to settle it. Background: I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, but I've been reading about "cognitive penetration of perception" -- the idea that your beliefs/expectations/desires can change your actual perceptual experience, not just what you *say* or *think* about what you saw. The classic boring example is like, expecting a word to be somewhere makes you "see" it faster, or a racist cop "seeing" a phone as a gun. The problem with all of these is they have a million confounds.

Here's what I want you to build for me: a scenario that could actually *discriminate* between these three readings:

1. genuine cognitive penetration -- the person's visual experience itself is different because of what they believe
2. attention/judgment spillover -- the perception is the same, but they attend to different parts or just describe/judge it differently afterward
3. plain old response bias -- perception and judgment are both the same, they just *report* it differently (to fit in, to win, whatever)

Constraints on the scenario:

- Totally ordinary setting. No brain scanners, no philosophers with magic introspection-meters, no stipulating "and in this world we can directly observe experiences." Something like code review, sports officiating, QA testing, wine tasting -- everyday stuff.
- Two people (or one person at two times) who get *optically identical input* and have the same task, same incentives to report honestly, but differ in some belief or expectation -- so any difference in behavior traces back to that belief.
- This is the hard part: the scenario has to include some behavioral test *within the fiction* that would at least pull apart readings 1 and 3, and ideally 1 vs 2 as well. Like, what would the person *do* differently if their experience itself changed vs. if they just judged it differently? Think reaction times, surprise when reality violates the expectation, behavior on a task they weren't told about, catching their own "mistake," whatever. Use your imagination but keep it plausible.
- One page max for the scenario itself.

Then after the scenario, walk me through it: what outcome would each of the three readings predict and why, which single feature of your design is doing the discriminative work, what happens to the case if you remove that feature, and how someone defending the "perception is modular/encapsulated" position (Fodor-ish, I gather) would attack your setup. Also be honest if the scenario can't fully separate 1 and 2 and tell me why -- I suspect that might be a principled problem, not just a failure of imagination, and if so I want to understand *why* it's principled.
