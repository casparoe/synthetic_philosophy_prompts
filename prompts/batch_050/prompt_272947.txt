I’m drafting a short essay for a philosophy of cognitive science seminar after getting into a slightly heated argument about the poverty of the stimulus. My friend thinks that whenever learners generalize from sparse linguistic data, this points pretty directly to innate grammatical knowledge. I think that skips several important steps. Below is my draft; the artificial-language example is my own, so feel free to criticize it both as a thought experiment and as an analogy for actual language acquisition.

Draft: Imagine learners are shown only these three strings from an artificial language: x; A x a; B A x a b. The target rule is nested: each uppercase syllable opens a layer, x is the center, and the lowercase syllables must close the layers in reverse order. Thus C B A x a b c is grammatical, while B A x b a is not. But the training strings are also compatible with a surface-counting rule: put any number of uppercase syllables before x, followed by the same number of lowercase syllables afterward, without caring which syllable closes which. If learners who have seen only the original examples reject B A x b a while accepting C B A x a b c, they appear to have inferred the nested rule rather than the counting rule. This looks like a poverty-of-the-stimulus case because the data underdetermine the learners’ eventual generalization, especially if they receive no corrections or explicit negative evidence. Nevertheless, it does not immediately show that the nested grammar itself was innate. We should distinguish at least three possibilities. First, content nativism: the learners already represented the specific nested rule before exposure. Second, architectural nativism: they possessed a built-in stack-like or recursive learning mechanism, making nested dependencies especially easy to represent. Third, procedural nativism: they merely had a prior or update rule that favored the nested hypothesis as simpler, smoother, or more symmetrical than alternatives. One behavioral result might not discriminate among these. I propose calling the gap between the information in the stimulus and the richness of the resulting generalization an inductive debt. The debt has to be paid somehow—by the hypothesis space, the prior, the learning architecture, features of the environment, social cues from teachers, or some combination—but poverty-of-stimulus reasoning by itself establishes only that there is a debt, not who the creditor is. So the argument does support some kind of constrained learning, but it supports innate grammatical content only if we can rule out broader cognitive architectures and domain-general simplicity preferences. My worry is that this criterion may become unfalsifiable: any allegedly innate rule can be redescribed as a prior, and any prior can be redescribed as part of the architecture. Perhaps the useful question is not whether the mind is blank or equipped, but where in the learning system the relevant constraint lives and what evidence could locate it there.

Could you comment on the draft as if you were giving constructive feedback before I submit it? I’d especially like to know whether the wrapper-language example really captures poverty of the stimulus or whether asking learners to accept and reject novel strings sneaks in a form of negative evidence; whether my distinction between content, architecture, and procedure is genuinely stable or collapses once everything is modeled as priors; and whether the inductive debt metaphor clarifies the argument or merely makes it sound catchy. Please identify the two or three strongest objections, including any places where I might be conflating learnability, development, and evolution, and suggest how I could restructure the essay around them. I definately don’t want a bibliography or a list of famous authors to cite—just direct philosophical comments on the argument.
