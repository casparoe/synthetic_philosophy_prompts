Just spent like three hours arguing with my friend about whether our city should take down a statue of some colonial-era figure downtown. His whole case basically rested on one analogy: he said a statue is like a medal the city pinned on the guy's chest -- an honor, not a record -- so revoking it when we stop endorsing the honoree is no different from stripping a disgraced athlete of a title. And he added that nobody learns history from plinths anyway. I countered with the analogy his opponents always reach for: statues are like books in a public library, so removing one is a small-scale book burning, destroying information about the past just because we dislike it.

Audit both analogies for me. Give me the correspondence table for each: what plays the role of the medal committee, the athlete, the stripping of the title, and on my side the librarian, the book, the readers. Then tell me where each mapping breaks -- does a medal imply ongoing endorsement in a way a century-old plinth maybe doesn't? is a one-of-a-kind monument really information the way a reprintable book is? does stripping a medal retroactively change the past the way my side claims removal does? Also what each analogy quietly smuggles in about who "the city" even is and whether it gets to change its mind. And at the end, the part I actually care about: does my friend's medal-stripping conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole arguement?
