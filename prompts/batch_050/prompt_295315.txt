I'm a PhD student who sits awkwardly between a vision science lab and a philosophy department, and for the past year I've run a journal club on the cognitive penetration of perception that has, as far as I can tell, made zero progress. The philosophers keep producing cases and arguments -- Churchland-style theoretical loading, Siegel on how which beliefs you hold allegedly changes what you see, the whole New Look lineage going back to Bruner and Goodman's coin study -- and the vision scientists keep replying that Firestone and Scholl's critique a decade or so back already settled this: the effects are judgment effects, memory effects, attention effects, or response-measure artifacts, and nobody has cleanly shown cognition altering early visual processing itself. Both sides have read the same papers. Nobody has budged.

The impasse crystallized for me in our own lab's work on hill-slant estimation. We ran a preregistered version of the classic finding that a heavy backpack makes hills look steeper, and when we tightened the response measure -- using an action-based measure instead of a visual matching task, partly in response to the demand-characteristics criticisms Durgin and colleagues raised -- the effect shrank dramatically. I thought this was evidence against penetration. The philosophers in the group said it showed nothing of the kind: maybe we had merely washed out a genuine perceptual effect by measuring the wrong thing, since the original claim was about how the hill *looks*, not about what you report or how you act. When I asked what result *would* count against the effect being perceptual, the conversation went in circles, and the vision people made the symmetrical complaint about the philosophers' positive cases -- every apparent penetration effect, they say, is explained away by pre-perceptual attentional selection rather than a change in perception proper, and the philosophers reply that this just relocates the dispute to where perception begins.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really an empirical disagreement at all but a disagreement about individuation: one side uses "perception" for something like encapsulated early vision, the other for the phenomenal character of experience as a whole, so each side's decisive refutation is the other side's change of subject -- possibly compounded by a deeper split over whether perception is even a natural kind that could have a determinate boundary. Tell me whether that diagnosis is right, or whether there's a substantive, resolvable disagreement underneath that I'm missing. Diagnose what kind of dispute this actually is, using what each side characteristically says, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a shared criterion, a particular kind of experiment or neural marker, a conceptual distinction, or nothing at all. Please be explicit about which points are genuinely settled among people who work on this (e.g., that attention and task set affect experience is presumably not the contested part) and which remain live, and don't present either camp's framing as the consensus view.
