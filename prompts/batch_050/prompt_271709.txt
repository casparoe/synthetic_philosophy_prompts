I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a course on free will and moral responsibility, and I'm applying to my university's summer research programme, which funds small independent projects (roughly eight weeks, culminating in a 7,000-word paper). I'd like help turning a half-formed thought into a proper proposal.

The thought is this. The entire debate over Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities seems to be built almost exclusively around blame: the standard case involves an agent who does something wrong, a counterfactual intervener who would have forced the wrongdoing had the agent shown signs of choosing otherwise, and the question of whether the agent is blameworthy despite lacking alternatives. Critics reply with 'flicker of freedom' strategies, and so on. But responsibility runs in both directions, and it strikes me that nobody in this debate, so far as I can tell from the course, starts from praise. Construct the mirror-image case: an agent who does something admirable, alongside a counterfactual device that would have blocked the good action had the agent shown signs of doing the wrong thing. My suspicion is that our intuitions don't transfer symmetrically. Perhaps praise requires alternatives even if blame doesn't (because credit seems to require that the agent could have failed), or perhaps it's the other way round, or perhaps the asymmetry runs along a different axis entirely, such as the difference between alternatives as a control condition and alternatives as evidence about the agent's moral quality. If there is an asymmetry, one side of the standard Frankfurt literature may have been arguing from an unrepresentative half of the phenomenon.

Please write the proposal (around 900-1,000 words) with:

1. A precise statement of the central question, framed so that it has determinate possible answers rather than being an invitation to survey the debate.
2. An explanation of why the existing positions leave the question open — i.e. why neither Frankfurt-style reasoning, nor the flicker-of-freedom responses, nor general reflection on the principle of alternative possibilities settles what should be said about praiseworthiness without alternatives.
3. The method I would actually use over eight weeks, broken into phases. Bear in mind this is a conceptual project: thought-experiment construction and analysis, mapping the logical space of possible asymmetry theses, and testing candidate principles against the case variants. If you think any part would benefit from engagement with experimental work on folk judgements about praise and blame, say so, but the core must be doable by one philosophy student with no lab.
4. What the possible conclusions are and which considerations would decide between them.
5. What would count as the project failing, and an honest note on the most likely failure mode (e.g. the question dissolving into the existing blame-focused debate, or the praise cases turning out to be underdescribed in a way that makes intuitions unreliable).
6. A scope assessment: is eight weeks and 7,000 words realistic, and if not, what should be cut?

Please don't include a bibliography or citations; I'll add references myself from the course materials. Write in British English. Finally, I'd like you to be epistemically open about the proposal itself: indicate how confident you are that the central question is genuinely open rather than already settled somewhere in the literature I haven't seen, and single out the one element of the proposal you are least sure about.
