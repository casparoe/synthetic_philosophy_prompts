I'm trying to work out when a historical "period" is a real thing versus a filing system historians invented afterwards, and the only way I can get at it is by listing my gut verdicts and asking what principle they add up to. Here they are:

- The Middle Ages: pure invention. Nobody in 950 thought of themselves as living in the middle of anything. The whole frame exists so the next bit can be the good part.
- The Renaissance: real, though. The people involved more or less told us so -- Florentines were already talking about a rebirth of something after a long slump, they had a story about a dark interval and their own escape from it. If the people inside a period name it, I want to say it's real.
- The fall of Rome, 476: real for some purposes, but some farmer outside Ravenna wouldn't have noticed anything change that particular year, which makes me think the boundary is way less real than the textbooks imply.
- The Scientific Revolution: real -- something measurably changes in how people argue, mathematize, and experiment over that stretch -- and yet nobody involved thought they were in a revolution, plenty of them thought they were recovering ancient wisdom. So: real thing, misleading name.
- The Great Depression: completely real, even though it was named after the fact and nobody in early 1930 knew they were "in a depression" yet.
- The Sixties: real, even though decades are calendrical accidents. Something about 1963 to the early 70s coheres as a unit and everyone knows roughly what it means.
- The Victorian era: fake, or anyway mere bookkeeping. Its boundaries are one woman's lifespan. Calling 1837 to 1901 a single cultural thing seems like pure convenience.

The obvious problem is that my own criteria contradict each other. If contemporary self-naming is the test -- which is why I keep the Renaissance -- then I have to drop the Depression and the Sixties, and I really don't want to. If measurable discontinuity in ordinary life is the test -- which is why I keep the Depression -- then 476 gets shaky, and honestly so does the Renaissance, since plenty of historians say nothing much changed for most Italians in the 1400s. If the test is whether the period forms a causal unit, where things inside it explain each other better than things outside it, I'm not even sure how you'd check that.

So: is there one principle that gets most of these, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause does which work? Tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up -- I assume I can't keep them all -- and what it predicts for cases I haven't listed, like the "long nineteenth century," 9/11 as the supposed end of an era, or the "early modern period." And the case I keep flip-flopping on: "late antiquity," which was definately coined by modern historians to be kinder to those centuries, but which a lot of people now think tracks something genuinely there. Real or not?

Please don't give me a menu of candidate principles and leave me to choose. Commit to one, defend it, and be upfront about which of my verdicts it overturns.
