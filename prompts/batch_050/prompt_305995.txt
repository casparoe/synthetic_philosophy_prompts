For a graduate seminar on Indian logic, write the list of requirements a philosophical interpretation of Jain anekāntavāda and the sevenfold predication (saptabhaṅgī) must meet to count as an adequate account of many-sided truth, giving the consideration behind each and marking which are non-negotiable and which are commitments of a particular interpretive camp. At minimum, address the following: the account must show how the predicates 'is', 'is not', and 'is inexpressible' can be asserted of one object without formal contradiction, and must say whether it achieves this by relativising predication to standpoints (naya), by the conditionality operator syāt, or by conceding some form of true contradiction; it must explain why the doctrine does not collapse into the trivial thesis that every claim is somewhat true, since the Jains themselves condemned opponents' views as one-sided rather than conditionally acceptable; it must handle the doctrine's self-application, i.e. whether anekāntavāda is itself only conditionally true and, if so, why that is not self-defeating; and it must deliver a verdict on a concrete test case the tradition actually disputed, such as whether a pot is permanent qua substance (dravya) and impermanent qua mode (paryāya). If the desiderata are jointly satisfiable only by adopting a non-classical logic, or only by reading the doctrine as purely epistemic rather than metaphysical, say which trade-off you judge the tradition can least afford, and why. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on cataloguing existing scholarly interpretations.
