Two firms, A and B, each operate an autonomous agent that negotiates daily with the other firm's agent over prices, delivery slots, and penalty terms. The agents have settled into a pattern of brinkmanship that destroys a large share of the available surplus, and engineers at the two firms have proposed a joint modification: retrain each agent to place some positive weight on the other agent's payoff, in the hope of moving the interaction to a more cooperative equilibrium. Each firm knows its own agent's utility function precisely, including its attitudes under risk, but knows only the ordinal structure of the other agent's preferences — which outcomes it ranks above which, inferred from observed play — and not the intensities. The firms now dispute the standard under which they may endorse the modification, and the dispute has been going in circles.

For the purposes of this exercise, use the following working definitions, even if you remember different ones from the literature. A candidate modification is a safe Pareto improvement if, for every assignment of cardinal utility functions to the two agents consistent with everything the firms know, both firms are at least as well off under the modification as under the status quo, and under at least one consistent assignment at least one firm is strictly better off. A candidate modification is an expected Pareto improvement if each firm's expected utility, computed under its own credence distribution over the unknown features of the other agent's utility function, is at least as high under the modification, and at least one firm's is strictly higher.

The robustness camp holds that the safe-Pareto standard is the correct one: endorsement requires improvement under every consistent completion of what is unknown, and anything weaker exposes a firm to losses it cannot rule out. The credal camp holds that the expected-Pareto standard is correct: a rational firm has credences over the unknown utilities and should act on them, and the safe standard is minimax conservatism that predictably blocks deals both firms would welcome.

Write the joint statement these two camps could actually sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, in the following format:

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of at least seven, each substantive and specific to this scenario rather than a platitude about wanting good outcomes. At least one claim must concede a real cost or limitation of the robustness position, at least one must concede a real cost or limitation of the credal position, and at least one must state something both camps accept about the status-quo brinkmanship equilibrium itself.
2. A dissolved disagreement. Identify at least one apparent dispute between the camps that is, on analysis, terminological — plausible candidates include whether expected-Pareto improvements deserve the name "Pareto improvement" at all, and whether "knowing the other agent's preferences" does or does not include knowing intensities. State it as it is usually argued, then dissolve it by introducing precise terms both camps accept.
3. Disagreements table. Columns: the disagreement in neutral wording; the robustness camp's formulation of its own position; the credal camp's formulation of its own position; the crux it reduces to, if any. Each camp's position must be phrased so that camp would accept the wording of its own view.
4. Cruxes. A numbered list of the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. For each: the sub-question stated in wording both camps accept; why settling it would move someone; the case, observation, or argument both camps agree bears on it; and each camp's prediction about that case, recorded in advance. I suspect two of the cruxes are whether credences over another agent's cardinal utility scale are well-founded or even coherent given that such utilities are fixed only up to positive affine transformation, and whether the right standard shifts when the counterparty may strategically shape what you come to believe about its agent — but the drafter may reframe or replace these. At least one crux must be accompanied by a fully worked numerical two-player game, constructed for the purpose, with payoffs and each firm's knowledge state specified, in which the two standards deliver different verdicts on a proposed modification; the example must actually be computed, not gestured at, and each camp's endorsement decision on it recorded.
5. Closing note from the neutral rapporteur. State which of the agreed claims is most fragile and what consideration would overturn it; state, for each crux, what result or argument each camp says in advance would change its mind; and identify where the collaboration's framing of the whole dispute is most likely to have failed — for instance, if both camps turn out to have agreed on an assumption about what the firms actually know that does not survive scrutiny.

Use no citations and no references to specific publications; argue the strongest version of each camp; and wherever a camp's position rests on an empirical conjecture — for example, about how much can be inferred from an agent's observed choices among lotteries — mark it as a conjecture rather than smuggling it in as a premise.
