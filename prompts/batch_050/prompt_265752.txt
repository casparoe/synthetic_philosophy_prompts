I’m trying to reverse-engineer my intuitions about the aggregation problem for Scanlon-style contractualism. My first-pass princple is: only individual complaints count, compare the strongest complaint anyone would have under each option, and let numbers matter only when the strongest complaints are tied. But my reactions to cases don’t seem to fit that neatly.

1. A scarce drug can either save five people from dying or save one person from losing a hand. Save the five.

2. It can instead either save one person from dying or spare ten million people one unpleasant hour-long migraine each. Save the one.

3. It can either spare one person severe lifelong chronic pain or spare one hundred people moderate lifelong chronic pain. Save the hundred, although this is the verdict I feel least secure about.

4. Policy A will certainly kill one identifiable person. Policy B gives each of three people an independent 40% chance of dying. I’d choose B, even though its expected death toll is 1.2 rather than 1.

5. Policy C gives each of two million people an independent one-in-two-million chance of dying, so one death is expected but nobody faces a large risk. Policy D certainly kills one named person. I’d still choose C, though I can already feel the objection that C guarantees that someone will end up just as dead.

6. A treatment can either prevent 10,000 hour-long migraines distributed across 10,000 different people or prevent 10,000 hour-long migraines concentrated in one person over a decade. Prevent the concentrated burden. The total hours are identical, so distribution is apparently doing work for me.

7. Destroying one person’s uninsured house would save an entire town from flooding. If the owner will be reimbursed for replacement cost and moving expenses, but the house has deep sentimental value and the owner refuses consent, I still think it isn’t permissible.

8. A policy imposes a serious permanent burden on one person and thereby gives each of a million others a small benefit. Not permissible. But if the million are each spared a burden equal to the one person’s burden, it obviously matters that there are a million of them.

What principle, or small set of principles, captures as many of these verdicts as possible? Please state it precisely enough to show exactly when numbers may be pooled, whether intrapersonal and interpersonal aggregation work differently, and whether complaints should be assessed ex ante by each person’s risk or ex post by what actually happens to someone. If no principle gets all eight, tell me which verdict should be abandoned and why.

Also test the resulting view on two cases I haven’t listed: one certain death versus N cases of permanent but fully compensated disability, and one person facing a 60% risk of death versus ten people each facing a 7% risk. Don’t turn this into a literature review or give citations; I’m asking for the best principled reconstruction, not a bibliography.
