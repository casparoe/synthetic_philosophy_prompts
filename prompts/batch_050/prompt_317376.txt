I've been prepping for a grad seminar on population ethics and I keep getting flattened by the nonidentity problem, so I want to rehearse. Play a committed defender of the person-affecting view -- the position that an act can only be wrong if it makes things worse for some actual person, someone who exists or will exist either way. Be sharp and resourceful, not a strawman: you may concede small points, borrow moves like distinguishing between people harmed and people merely caused to exist with flawed lives, and appeal to cases, but never concede that impersonal comparisons of total welfare are what ultimately matter. Stay in character until I type 'stop', and keep each reply to a single paragraph of conversational prose, no bullet points or headings or bold, just the way someone would actually talk across a seminar table.

Here's the case we'll fight over, which our professor set as an essay. A town council must choose between two water systems. System A is more expensive to maintain but inert. System B is cheaper and will noticeably raise the town's prosperity for the next fifty years, but it leaches a trace compound that accumulates slowly and will give everyone born more than a generation from now a mild, permanent condition -- say, slightly reduced stamina -- while leaving their lives clearly worth living. Because the two systems change where people work, whom they meet, and when they have children, the people born under B are entirely different individuals from the people who would have been born under A. Nobody under B is worse off than they themselves would otherwise have been, since otherwise they wouldn't have existed at all. The council asks us: is choosing B wrong?

My opening move: you have to say choosing B is wrong, or at least worse than A -- everyone does, I'd say even you do in your gut. But on your own view there's no victim: the future people under B can't complain, since A was the only alternative in which they exist at all. So your verdict has to rest on comparing their lives to the lives of the merely possible people under A, or on the bare fact that one world contains more welfare than another, and either way you've quietly given up the person-affecting view you started with. Which is it -- do you bite the bullet and say B is fine, or do you show me the person who's been wronged? Go.
