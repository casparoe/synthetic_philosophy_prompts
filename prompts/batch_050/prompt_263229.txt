My roommate and I got into it last night over beers about whether there are really any tables, and he produced this argument that I can't accept but also can't seem to break. Here it is, as best I remember it. (1) Assume for reductio that at least one table exists — say the one in our kitchen. (2) A table can survive the loss of a single molecule: nobody would say the table went out of existence because one molecule flaked off the underside; it's still the same table, just imperceptibly lighter. (3) And what goes for one molecule goes for any molecule: if a thing is a table before you remove a molecule, it's still a table after. (4) So by applying (3) over and over, the table is still there even after we've stripped it down to one molecule sitting where the kitchen used to be. (5) But a single molecule is obviously not a table. (6) Contradiction, so the assumption in (1) was false: there are no tables, only a bunch of molecules arranged tablewise that we sloppily call a table.

Then it got worse. When I said fine, maybe there are no tables, he ran the exact same argument with me in place of the table — I can lose one molecule and still be me, so iterate, so eventually I'm just a molecule, so I don't exist. I definately refuse to accept that I don't exist, so the argument must go wrong somewhere, but every step looks innocent in isolation. So: which premise (or step) do I give up? For the one you pick, explain why it seemed so plausible in the first place that reasonable people just nod along to it, and then price out the main escape routes I've seen gestured at — the view that there is some exact molecule whose removal is the fatal one and we simply can't know which; the view that truth or logic gets indeterminate near the cutoff so step (3) isn't straightforwardly true; the bullet-biting view that yes, strictly there are no tables but we can explain why table-talk is still perfectly fine to keep using; and the stubborn view that nothing genuinely survives the loss of any part, so talk of the same table persisting is a convenient fiction from the start. For each, say what it costs and who should be embarassed. And the part I actually care about: does your diagnosis also save me from the parallel argument, or does it need a different story for organisms than for furniture — and if it needs a different story, is that a principled difference or just special pleading to protect ourselves?
