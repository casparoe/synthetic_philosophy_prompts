I keep going back and forth with a friend about Atlantis and I want to settle the structure of the debate rather than the debate itself. Setup: in Plato's Timaeus, before the Atlantis story gets going, the Egyptian priest at Sais explains to Solon why the Greeks have never heard any of this. "You are young in soul, every one of you. For therein you possess not a single belief that is ancient and derived from old tradition, nor yet one science that is hoary with age." And then: "if any event has occurred that is noble or great or in any way conspicuous, whether it be in your country or in ours or in some other place of which we know by report, all such events are recorded from of old and preserved here in our temples; whereas your people and the others are but newly equipped, every time, with letters and all such arts as civilized States require..." (Timaeus 22b–23a, Lamb translation).

Here's my position, which I think is just the standard scholarly one: Plato made Atlantis up, full stop. Not garbled history, not a folk memory of the Thera eruption dressed up -- a fiction built for the dialogue's purposes, and the priest's speech is part of the build.

Now play it straight: what would actually count against that view? Concretely:

1. Notice what the passage does. It supplies, in advance, a complete explanation for the total absence of corroborating evidence: everyone else's records got periodically wiped out by floods and fires, only Egypt kept archives, and conveniently those archives are unavailable to us. Pseudohistory fans love this move. So first question: does this built-in excuse make the *historical* reading unfalsifiable, and if so, does it also make my invention reading unfalsifiable by symmetry -- or are they asymmetric in some way I should spell out?

2. What findings would be *decisive* against invention vs merely awkward? E.g. an Egyptian inscription predating Plato describing a western island power swallowed by the sea; a pre-Platonic Greek text naming Atlantis or the Solon-Dropides-Critias transmission chain; geological/archaeological evidence of something in the Atlantic. For each, say whether it kills the invention view or just dents it. I suspect some things that *feel* like they'd defeat it (say, proof Plato knew about Thera) actually leave it standing, and I want that tested.

3. What do defenders of the invention view themselves concede would count against it -- not what ancient-aliens people say counts against it. If the honest answer is "basically nothing could," is that a vice of the position or just what a well-supported position looks like? Where's the line.

4. Related: what would distinguish a defeater of "Plato invented it wholesale" from a vindication of Atlantis-as-described? These get conflated constantly and I want them pulled apart.

Finally: state where your own reasoning here is most likely to fail -- which consideration, if it turned out differently than you think, would flip your verdict on how exposed the invention view actually is. If you think the answer to one of these questions is load-bearing for everything else, say so.
