I have come to accept the following position on wild animal suffering, and I want a rigorous map of what would count against it. The position: because the vast majority of sentient animals live in the wild, and because the lives of most wild animals -- given r-selected reproductive strategies, starvation, disease, parasitism, predation, and the extreme brevity of most wild lifespans -- plausibly contain more suffering than enjoyment, we have a serious pro tanto moral reason to intervene in nature to reduce wild animal suffering, even where intervention carries real costs to ecological values such as biodiversity, naturalness, or wilderness integrity.

Do not argue for the position. Instead, answer the following questions, in this order:

1. Distinguish the position's separate load-bearing components -- the empirical prevalence claim about net wild animal welfare, the welfarist assumption that animal welfare can be measured and aggregated across individuals and species, the normative inference from badness to a duty or permission to intervene, and the practical claim that effective intervention is achievable without making things worse. For each component, identify the two or three strongest defeaters, taking each objection in its most defensible form (for instance, the strongest version of the ecological-holism objection, not a caricature of it).

2. Rank the defeaters from potentially fatal to merely awkward, and for each, say whether a sophisticated defender of the position would concede it as genuinely counting against them or instead dismiss it as question-begging or empirically unsupported. Where would defenders and critics most deeply disagree about what even counts as evidence?

3. Consider developments that have arguably already occurred: empirical work suggesting some wild animal populations may have net positive welfare, proposed interventions such as large-scale vaccination, contraception, or gene drives, and the broader track record of ecological interventions producing unintended harm. Which of these, if any, should actually move someone who holds the position, and by how much?

4. Finally, assess whether the position's "pro tanto" formulation has insulated it from refutation. Since a pro tanto reason can survive any countervailing consideration simply by being outweighed, is there anything the position actually risks? If it turns out that the view is compatible with every possible finding about wild animal lives, say so plainly and assess whether this is a defect for a view of this kind or merely a consequence of its being a modest claim rather than a bold one.

Conclude with an overall verdict on how exposed the position actually is: which single defeater should worry a holder of it most, and whether the position stands or falls on empirical findings it currently lacks the data to settle.
