i'm running a small decision theory reading group and next week we're doing superrationality, specifically two setups. the first: twenty strangers each get invited to play, and each can either mail in a card with their name on it or not; if exactly one card arrives, that person wins the prize, and if zero or two or twenty arrive, nobody gets anything. the second: a lottery that's free to enter, but the jackpot gets divided by the total number of entries, so the more people who pile in the less it's worth, and if literally everyone enters it rounds to nothing.

i want to open the session by reading a poem out loud that enacts the first dilemma instead of just describing it. here's the constraint i have in mind: give the poem one short stanza per invitee, so 20 stanzas, plus an optional coda, and make the stanzas almost word-for-word identical, because the whole idea of superrational reasoning is that everyone in a perfectly symmetric situation runs the the same calculation and knows everyone else is running it too. the only thing allowed to drift between stanzas is one or two words, and that drift has to do all the emotional work: the slow creep of "but what if *i* just send the card anyway" until the symmetry visibly buckles. the coda (or last stanza) should land on whatever outcome you think the logic actually forces -- twenty identical reasoners and zero prize, or one card and a winner, your call -- but let the form argue it, don't editorialize in the final line.

ideally someone who's never heard of superrationality should figure out why the repetition matters by about stanza five, and someone who knows the dilemma cold should be able to point at the exact word-change where the symmetric equilibrium breaks. the second setup, the self-defeating lottery, can show up in the poem if you find a natural place for it but don't force it. keep stanzas short so the repetition doesn't get tedious.
