I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a philosophy of science seminar on scientific realism, and after our unit on the pessimistic meta-induction I've come out as a committed anti-realist, which has made for some heated arguments with my housemates, most of whom are physics majors. My position, roughly: the history of science is a graveyard of empirically successful theories whose central posits turned out not to exist — phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether — so by induction we should expect our current best theories to be false in the same way, whatever their predictive success. I've read the standard realist replies (the no-miracles argument, Worrall's structural realism, Psillos-style deployment realism), and each strikes me as a retreat: the realist shrinks their commitment to whatever parts of past theories happened to survive, which looks less like a principled defense and more like defining realism after the fact so it can't lose. So I hold that the meta-induction stands and scientific realism is false.

In seminar discussion last week I tried to defend a cleaned-up version of the induction, which I called the referential meta-induction. The idea is to count a historical episode as evidence against realism only if three conditions are met: (a) the discarded theory was genuinely empirically successful by the standards of its own era, including novel prediction; (b) its central theoretical term fails to refer under the successor theory's ontology — genuinely fails, not merely gets reclassified or split; and (c) the theory's successes actually depended on the false posit, in the sense that the derivations producing its novel predictions used that posit essentially, rather than treating it as idle scaffolding. I claimed that even with all three filters in place, the list of qualifying cases stays long enough for the inductive inference to go through. My tutor pushed back but couldn't say exactly what would settle it, and that is what I want help with.

What would count against this position — both the pessimistic meta-induction in general and my filtered version in particular? I want the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be: for instance, would a rigorous case-by-case showing that most of the standard list fails condition (c) be decisive, or could I absorb that by arguing the remaining cases suffice? Would it matter if someone established that success-truth divergence is concentrated in immature sciences, or that theorizing has historically stabilized once a field reaches a certain maturity, so the induction's base rate no longer applies? And I want a committed answer, not a menu: take a definite stand on which considerations would actually defeat the view and which the anti-realist can legitimately absorb, and give an honest verdict on whether my three-condition version has been engineered so tightly that no possible historical finding could count against it — and if it has, whether that kind of insulation is a fatal defect for what is supposed to be an empirical, inductive argument or merely a sign of a well-formulated one.
