I am a novelist, and my current manuscript is narrated by a machine that sorts coins. My writing group is split on whether this conceit is inspired or incoherent, and the split turns on a question I last studied years ago: whether a machine's inner states can be about anything. The dispute sent me back to Searle's Chinese Room, with its claim that only minds mean things in their own right while books, computers, and machines mean only what we take them to mean, and to Dennett's rejoinder involving a coin-sorter — a machine built to accept US quarters and reject all else which, shipped abroad to a country whose standard coin matches the quarter in size and weight, sorts those coins instead. What does it now detect, and is there any deeper fact of the matter than the uses we find for it? I have been polling my own intuitions on cases, and they refuse to settle into a pattern.

Here are my verdicts, such as they are. The transplanted coin-sorter: what it sorts has changed with its surroundings — it now takes the local coins, whatever its makers intended. My finished novel, unread in a drawer: its meaning strikes me as fully fixed already; no future reader would add anything essential. A thermostat: it neither believes nor means anything, and calling its coil a representation of the room's temperature is a courtesy we extend, not a fact about the coil. A fortune-cookie slip: meaningful, but entirely on loan from writer and reader; the paper itself means nothing. A honeybee's waggle dance: genuinely about nectar two hundred meters northeast — no bee intends anything, yet the aboutness seems earned, by natural selection rather than conferred by an interpreter. And a chatbot that tells me it is glad to see me: it does not mean it; whatever meaning the sentence carries is borrowed wholesale from the human writing it was trained on.

What I want is a principle — or, failing that, two candidate principles — that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause each case motivates and what it would say about cases I have not considered, such as a strand of DNA and a sonnet produced by a monkey at a typewriter. I am aware that my verdicts strain against one another: the bee apparently earns its meaning through selection history while the chatbot does not, and I dethrone the machine's makers while enthroning myself as author. If the price of a clean principle is surrendering one of my verdicts, tell me which one and why. Please also say how confident you are in the principle you offer, and single out the one point — a case, a clause, or a consequence — you are least sure of. No more than 400 words.
