Ok so I ran into this argument at a meetup last week and it's been bugging me because every step sounds right but the conclusion is insane. Laying it out how he gave it:

(1) Whether it's ok to take a job depends on the difference your taking it actually makes to the world, not on the job title or how it feels to do it.
(2) In any big labor market, if you turn a job down, the firm hires someone else who does basically the same work. So the harm the job does happens either way, and your taking it causes roughly zero extra harm.
(3) But your salary only exists if you take the job, and donating a chunk of it to effective charities does real good that wouldn't happen otherwise, since the person they'd hire instead of you almost certainly wouldn't donate it.
(4) So taking a legal-but-harmful job (his example was high-frequency trading for a firm that lobbies against financial regulation) and donating a third of the salary is strictly better than turning it down.
(5) Nothing in (1)-(4) mentions the severity of the harm. The same logic works for any job at all, as long as the donations beat the extra harm you personally add, which by (2) is about zero.
(6) So in principle it would be morally fine, even good, to take a job as a lobbyist for something genuinely awful, or in the limit case an enforcer for a criminal org, if the pay and donations were big enough.
(7) That can't be right. So something in (1)-(5) breaks.

I want to keep (1), since I do think consequences are what ultimately matter, and honestly (2) seems empirically true for big firms. But I can't find the exact step where it goes off the rails. Which step do you reject, why does it look plausible at first, and what does rejecting it cost me?
