I'm an undergraduate TA for my university's philosophy of mind course, and I'm helping the professor grade the midterm. One question was: "State Rosenthal's higher-order thought (HOT) theory of consciousness, and explain how it differs from higher-order perception ('inner sense') theories. Then explain the targetless higher-order thought problem -- the apparent possibility of having a HOT about a first-order state that doesn't exist -- and assess whether it is a serious objection to the theory." The question is worth 25 points.

Here is one student's answer:

"Rosenthal's higher-order thought theory holds that a mental state is conscious when and only when the subject has a suitable higher-order thought about it -- a thought representing another mental state of the same subject. So my perception of a coffee cup becomes conscious in virtue of my having a thought with roughly the content 'I am now seeing a coffee cup.' Crucially, on Rosenthal's view the higher-order thought must itself be conscious, since an unconscious thought could no more confer consciousness on its target than an unlit lamp could illuminate a room. The HOT must also be noninferential: if I merely infer from my behavior that I am in pain, the pain does not thereby become conscious.

The theory differs from higher-order perception theories such as Lycan's inner-sense model. Rosenthal posits a dedicated inner scanning mechanism, analogous to ordinary perception, that monitors first-order states, whereas Lycan holds that the higher-order representation is a thought rather than a quasi-perceptual state, avoiding commitment to inner sense organs.

The targetless HOT problem is that it seems possible to have a HOT representing oneself as being in a state S without actually being in S. If consciousness consists in having the HOT, then I could have a conscious experience of red with no first-order visual state at all, which seems absurd, and Rosenthal cannot say what the phenomenal character of such an experience would consist in. However, because the HOT must be noninferential, this problem cannot in fact arise: a noninferential HOT is caused by its target state, so wherever there is a HOT there must be a corresponding first-order state, and misrepresentation is ruled out. The objection therefore fails, though the theory still faces a difficulty about animal consciousness, since many animals plausibly lack the conceptual resources to think about their own mental states."
