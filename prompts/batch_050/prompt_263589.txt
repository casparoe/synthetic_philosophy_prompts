I'm putting together a unit on personal identity for my philosophy of mind seminar and a case keeps nagging at me, so help me think it through.

Every institution that deals with split-brain patients treats them as exactly one person. They sign one consent form before the corpus callosum is cut, they hold one driver's license and one bank account, and if one of them commits a crime we prosecute one defendent, full stop. But in the lab the very same patients are studied as if they were two independent witnesses: flash a word to the right visual field and the patient tells you what it was, flash a different picture to the left field and the left hand points at it while the patient verbally insists they saw nothing, and there are well-known cases where the two sides seem to work against each other -- one hand buttoning a shirt while the other unbuttons it.

Now the wrinkle I want to put to my students. Suppose one of these patients is asked to consent to a risky experimental procedure. You explain it verbally; the patient says yes and signs. Then, on a hunch, you show a yes/no card to the left visual field only and ask the patient to point with the left hand -- and the hand points at NO. The hospital's ethics board asks its token philosopher (me, apparently) whether they have informed consent.

So: what theory of the unity of consciousness, or of persons, does the one-signature practice actually presuppose? That consciousness is phenomenally unified and the lab behavior is mere disunified access to information? Or does it presuppose nothing at all -- is the one-person rule just institutional bookkeeping that no theory of consciousness could touch? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out, and where is it genuinely underdetermined, so that several theories would license the exact same rule? Can the lab practice and the courtroom practice both make sense under a single theory, or is the inconsistency real? And if we took a two-streams reading seriously, what would concretely have to change -- two signatures, a veto for the left hand, new liability rules? Last thing: these patients often develop cross-cueing tricks, sneaking information from one hemisphere to the other through subcortical shortcuts, like the speaking side making subtle head movements the other side can pick up. Does that solve the consent problem or just make it worse?
