I retired from chemistry a few years back and have been falling down the philosophy of science rabbit hole ever since, mostly by arguing over email with my old colleague Dieter. Our latest fight is about when a false theory counts as closer to the truth than another false one. His verdicts so far, as best I can reconstruct them: between "lead melts at 326 degrees" and "lead melts at 400 degrees" he says the first is closer to the truth (it melts at 327.5); between "the Earth is a perfect sphere" and "the Earth is a cube" he says the sphere, obviously, even though both are strictly speaking false; between Newton's inverse-square gravity and some made-up inverse-cube law he says Newton, since the inverse-cube thing couldn't even keep the Moon in its orbit; between "whales are fish" and "whales are reptiles" he says the reptile claim is closer, since whales at least breathe air and bear live young; and between the old caloric theory of heat and a theory that heat is tiny invisible gremlins he says caloric, because it at least got the conservation-ish behavior right. Now the case we've been stuck on all week: two false models of the same reaction kinetics. Model A nails the measured rates to three decimal places but its story about what's happening at the molecular level is flat-out wrong, while Model B is off by a few percent on the rates but its picture of the mechanism (which bonds break, in what order) is basicly right. Predict his verdict, tell me which candidate rules he might be running (closeness of numerical predictions, similarity of underlying structure to the truth, most true consequences preserved, or something else that fits his track record just as well) and what each one predicts here, and name a further case that would actually tell the rules apart. Give me your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, a sentence or two each, no introduction and no conclusion.
