I am a systems engineer working on fault-tolerant avionics. My working vocabulary is observers and state estimators, health-monitoring units that maintain models of other subsystems, fault detection and isolation, and the distinction between a signal merely occurring inside a component and that signal being made available to the wider architecture for control, logging, and arbitration. My knowledge of philosophy of mind is that of an interested amateur.

Here is what motivates my question. On the systems I work on, a monitoring unit keeps a real-time model of, say, the flight-control computer: it estimates what the flight computer is doing, flags discrepancies between expected and actual behavior, and can trigger corrective action. When I first encountered higher-order theories of consciousness — the view, as I understand it, that a mental state is conscious when and because the subject bears a suitable higher-order representation of that state — it sounded to me like the claim that a monitored subsystem thereby acquires a property that an unmonitored one lacks. That struck me as obviously wrong: instrumenting a component does not confer anything on the component itself, and there is certainly no sense in which monitoring makes it like something to be a flight computer. But when I read that first-order theorists take consciousness to be just the state itself, carrying its content and poised to influence the rest of the system, that sounded like the equally flat claim that the flight computer was already doing everything that matters and the monitor adds nothing of relevance. Since both positions cannot be as trivial as they look in my vocabulary, I suspect I am mistranslating at least one of them.

Please do three things. First, restate the debate between higher-order and first-order theories of consciousness in the vocabulary above, as faithfully as you can. The translation should cover the main variants — higher-order thought versus higher-order perception, and self-representational views on which a state represents itself — and the standard objections on each side: the worry that representing something does not thereby make it conscious, the problem of higher-order misrepresentation where the monitoring state depicts the system as being in a state it is not in (including the limiting case where there is no first-order state at all), and the regress worry about whether the monitoring state must itself be monitored. Second, give me a residue report: which moves in the debate translate cleanly into engineering terms, which you had to distort to fit, and which have no counterpart at all. In particular, address whether the notion of what it is like to be in a state, and the distinction between a state's occurring and its being available to the whole system, survive the translation — and where they do not, say whether that reveals a gap in my vocabulary or whether the philosophical debate was trading on something my vocabulary rightly refuses to express. Third, tell me whether my engineer's instinct — that instrumentation cannot confer a new intrinsic property on the instrumented system — amounts to a genuine objection to higher-order theory or is merely an artifact of a bad translation.
