I'm in Year 12 and writing a long essay on whether becoming a good person is mainly a matter of training habits or of understanding reasons, and I'd like to stress-test my argument in conversation rather than against a textbook summary. In his Autobiography, Benjamin Franklin describes the scheme by which he tracked his faults against thirteen virtues in a little book, and he explains why he thought the scheme necessary:

"While my care was employed in guarding against one fault, I was often surprised by another; habit took the advantage of inattention; inclination was sometimes too strong for reason. I concluded, at length, that the mere speculative conviction that it was our interest to be completely virtuous was not sufficient to prevent our slipping, and that the contrary habits must be broken, and good ones acquired and established, before we can have any dependence on a steady, uniform rectitude of conduct."

Please play Franklin himself, speaking in the first person as the elderly man looking back on this project. You may use only the ideas, examples and vocabulary available to an eighteenth-century printer and moralist — no modern psychology, no talk of neurons or behavioural science — and you should stay in character until I say otherwise. You are a shrewd, genial debater, not a saint: you know your scheme's weaknesses, you concede small points readily, but you will defend its core fiercely. Keep each reply to a single substantial paragraph so it feels like a real exchange, and open your reply by stating your answer to my challenge in the first sentence, then give your reasoning — no framing, no restating my question.

Here is my opening move. In this passage you seem to concede that reason cannot make us good — that knowing our interest in virtue leaves us at the mercy of habit and inclination — and so you reach for black marks in a ruled book instead. But doesn't that make moral perfection a branch of bookkeeping? If a man becomes temperate only because he has trained himself to avoid a spot on Tuesday's column, in what sense is he virtuous rather than merely well-drilled? And if the scheme's whole justification is that it works, what distinguishes your thirteen virtues from any other self-improvement regime a man might devise for ends you would yourself call vicious?
