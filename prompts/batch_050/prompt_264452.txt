For the past year, my workplace reading group in applied ethics has hosted a running argument between two colleagues about where legitimate persuasion ends and manipulation begins, and it has become clear that they are talking past each other. One defends what might be called the bypass view: manipulation is influence that works by subverting, exploiting, or circumventing the target's rational decision-making capacities, and its wrongness consists in treating someone's agency as an obstacle to be routed around rather than engaged. The other defends what might be called the hidden self-interest view: manipulation is influence that is covert in its mechanism and primarily serves the influencer's ends rather than the target's own aims, so that open emotional pressure, however crude, cannot be manipulative, while a warmly delivered nudge designed to profit the nudger can be. They keep returning to the same stock cases — candy at the supermarket checkout, a guilt-tripping friend, a surgeon describing a procedure by its survival rate rather than its mortality rate, and a political campaign tailoring its messaging to personality profiles inferred from online behavior — and reaching opposite verdicts on each.

Draft the joint statement that these two could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. I want: (1) a numbered section of claims both would endorse, which I suspect will include some negative claims about what manipulation is not, some paradigm cases they would actually judge identically, and some agreement about which features of an influence attempt are morally relevant even if they weight those features differently; (2) a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated in wording that each side would accept as a fair description of its own position, without either formulation smuggling in the other's premises; (3) their cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one side to revise. My own guess is that one crux is whether exploiting a known, predictable reasoning bias counts as engaging someone's rational capacities or as bypassing them, and another is whether manipulation requires acting against the target's interests at all — that is, whether benevolent or paternalistic manipulation is a coherent category or a contradiction in terms. For each crux, specify a concrete case, argument, or distinction that both parties would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about it. As one test case they have not yet discussed, include this: a public-health app infers from a user's phone usage patterns when the user is tired and willpower-depleted, and deliberately schedules its exercise reminders for those moments, on the grounds that people are most receptive to gentle prompts when their defenses are down. Have both parties render a verdict on this case in terms consistent with their stated positions; if either side's verdict on it turns out to be in tension with its verdict on one of the stock cases, the joint statement should say so. Finally, if you conclude that part of their dispute is merely verbal — for instance, if covertness is doing different work in the two views than either party realizes — identify which part, and explain what non-verbal disagreement, if any, remains once it is cleared away.
