So I'm prepping for a grad reading group on Kant's ethics and I built this argument to motivate the "acting from duty" stuff, but it seems to run away from me and conclude something nuts, and I can't figure out where the leak is. You're a moral philosopher who's taught the Groundwork a dozen times and has seen every version of the cold-hearted-Kant complaint -- think carefully and take it step by step before answering.

Here's the argument:

(1) An action has moral worth only if the agent does it because it's right -- from duty -- and not merely because they feel like it.
(2) If you have an inclination to do something and you do it, then your doing it is (at least partly) motivated by that inclination. Motives don't just sit there inert.
(3) A genuinely warm-hearted, sympathetic person always has an inclination to help when someone needs help. That's just what being sympathetic is.
(4) So when a sympathetic person helps, their helping is motivated by inclination, not (purely) by duty -- so by (1) it has no moral worth, or at least less than the same act done without the inclination.
(5) And the stronger the sympathy, the more the inclination does the motivational work, so the less moral worth the act has. Cultivating warm feelings toward others therefore steadily erodes the moral worth of everything you do for them.
(6) So the moral ideal is someone who feels nothing for others -- or better, finds helping a chore -- but helps anyway. Kant's perfectly moral agent is basically a dutiful grump.
(7) But that's backwards. Someone who helps gladly seems morally better, not worse, than someone who has to drag themselves to it.

A couple of the reading group members think the fix is obvious (just deny that duty and inclination can't coexist), but when I push them on whether an act can be done from duty at all if the inclination would've gotten you there anyway, they get wobbly. Which step do you actually reject, and why does it look so plausible at first? Price the alternatives for me too: what does it cost to reject (1), what does it cost to reject (2), and what does it cost to just bite the bullet on (5)? I'd rather keep (1) if I can, since without some version of it I don't see how to distinguish the grocer who treats customers fairly because it's right from one who does it because it's good for business -- but tell me honestly if that's the premise that has to go.
