I retired from a university physics department three years ago and have been making up for lost time by reading philosophy, mostly through a little reading group at the public library. Last week we read the essay where Kant says you're not allowed to lie even to a murderer at the door who asks whether your friend is hiding in your house, and two members, Henrik and Sofia, got into a long argument about it afterwards. I'd like a verdict.

Henrik's opening move was that the essay is just monstrous and exposes what's wrong with Kant's whole approach: Kant would rather hand a friend over to a killer than dirty his hands with a lie. And Kant's famous bit about consequences — that if you lie, the killer might leave and run into the friend outside precisely because you sent him away — Henrik called obvious special pleading, since the lie is far more likely to save the friend than to doom him.

Sofia said Henrik was missing the point entirely. Kant isn't weighing consequences at all; his claim is that lying is wrong in itself because it can only work by exploiting other people's trust in speech — every lie is parasitic on a practice of truth-telling that the liar's own behavior helps corrode. On that reading the murderer case is a vivid instance of the general rule, not a mad exception, and the bit about the killer bumping into the friend outside isn't a probability estimate, it's an illustration that you can't control what your words do once they're out, so the only thing you can realy be responsible for is whether they're true.

Henrik came back with two points. First, that the parasite arguement proves too much: if lying is out because universal lying would destroy testimony, then flatly refusing to answer should be out too, since if everyone refused to answer whenever the answer might be misused, testimony would be just as useless — yet even Sofia presumably allows that you can simply not open your mouth. Second, that Kant's position has a hole in it you could drive a carriage through: if only literal falsehood is forbidden, you can say something strictly true but misleading, like "he's not in the garden" while the friend hides in the cellar. So either Kant permits deception with extra steps, which makes the rule toothless, or he has to forbid that too, which no Kant text Sofia could point to actually says.

Sofia replied that the silence move fails: Kant's worry is about abusing the trust a statement invites, and staying silent invites no trust, so nothing gets corroded — the universalization worry doesn't arise. On misleading truths, she said that's a real cost of the view and she'll pay it: yes, Kant lets you mislead the murderer with a literally true statement, and yes it looks like hair-splitting, but the alternative — making the duty track your deceptive intentions rather than your words — leaves you with a duty you can't even reliably apply to yourself, because intentions are mushy and words are checkable.

They both turned to me and I honestly couldn't say who'd won. Henrik's "proves too much" charge felt strong but Sofia's answer to it seemed to work, and I can't tell whether the misleading-truth business is a reductio of Kant's rule or just an awkward consequence one should swallow.

Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of Henrik's and Sofia's moves, say whether it lands or fails and why. Then give an overall verdict — who's more right about Kant's position, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. One request: commit. I've read a couple of "on the one hand, on the other hand" summaries of this debate and they left me exactly where I started. Take a definite position on who wins each exchange and who wins overall, and defend it — even if the honest answer is that some move is rhetorically strong but philosophically hollow, or the other way round.
