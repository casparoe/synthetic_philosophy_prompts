A few months ago I won a seat on our county board, and the swearing-in is next week. Part of the ceremony is an oath to uphold our state constitution, and I've been losing sleep over it. The constitution is mostly fine, but it contains one clause I genuinely think is immoral: an old provision barring anyone who doesn't profess a belief in God from holding public office. Courts voided it decades ago, so it's totally unenforceable, a dead letter -- but it's still printed right there in the text, and every attempt to formally repeal it dies in commitee for procedural reasons. It stings extra because I'm not religious myself, so the clause was aimed at people exactly like me.

My sister, who's finishing a PhD in political theory and never lets anything slide, told me flat out that if I take the oath I'm putting my name to the entire document, rotten clause included, and the only honest options are to decline the seat or take the oath while publicly denouncing that clause on the spot. Her view is that quietly working inside a tainted framework mostly just launders the taint, because the outsider's stamp of approval is what keeps the whole thing respectable. Then I ran into my old high school civics teacher at the grocery store, and he gave me the opposite speech right there by the produce. He said a constitution is its text read in light of its stated purposes, not the private motives of the men who drafted it, and that the clause is a deviation from what the document as a whole stands for, not an expression of it -- a tumor on a healthy body, and you don't declare the body diseased because of it. He also pointed out that if I refuse, the seat goes to the runner-up, a guy who openly admires that clause and keeps talking about honoring its spirit.

What I can't sort out: does swearing to uphold a document mean endorsing everything in it, or can the oath coherently mean upholding the document at its best? Is it intellectually honest to read a text against the intentions of the people who wrote it, or is that just motivated reasoning dressed up as principle -- who gets to say what a document really stands for? And there's presumably some clause bad enough that no amount of inside-game fixing justifies the oath, so what determines which side of that line I'm on? Please don't just split the difference and tell me it depends; I want an actual framework I can apply. Structure it like this: a short section called "What the disagreement is really about" (three or four sentences), then "The case for refusing the oath" and "The case for taking it" (roughly two paragraphs each, steelman both), then "Where I'd land and why" (one paragraph). American English please.
