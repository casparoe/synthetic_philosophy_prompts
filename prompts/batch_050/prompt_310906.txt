I'm a novelist (literary fiction, two mid-list books and a third that's fighting me) and twice a year I lead a session at a weekend writers' retreat in Vermont. It's about 12 participants, mixed experience, and I get 90 minutes. I want to do something with a problem I've been chewing on: Newcomb's problem. My way into it is that as a novelist I'm basically the Predictor for my own characters. Once I know a character properly, I know what they'd do in any scene before they "do" it, and yet when the writing is good the choice still feels free from the inside. I want my group to feel that tension in their bodies, not just nod at it.

Design me a game or staged activity that does this. My rough thought: pair people up as "authors" and "characters," authors write a quick sketch of their character and seal a prediction in an envelope before the character faces an actual choice with some small real stakes (I can bring fancy chocolate or bookstore gift cards), and the payoffs should be structured Newcomb-style so taking the guaranteed small thing can be the "rational" move that costs you the big one. But you can redesign from scratch if you've got something better. I need concrete rules, materials, a payoff scheme that works with trivial stakes, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened onto one-boxing vs two-boxing and onto this author-character business about whether a well-written character is unfree. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (someone will definately try to game the prediction phase), and be honest about which feature of the real problem a classroom version can't reproduce -- I'm guessing the predictor's reliability is the weak point but you tell me.

And say how confident you are that this will actually produce the intuition I'm after with a real group, and flag the single point in your design you're least sure of.
