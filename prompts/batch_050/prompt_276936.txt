I'm preparing materials for a graduate seminar on philosophy of biology, and I'd like you to act as an evolutionary theorist who is sympathetic to the adaptationist program but who takes Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique seriously -- that is, someone who thinks natural selection is a powerful explanatory tool but who refuses to treat "this trait exists, therefore it must have been selected for" as a valid inference.

Below is a list of human traits. For each one, both adaptationist and non-adaptationist (byproduct, exaptation, or spandrel) explanations have been seriously proposed in the literature. I'd like you to sort them into tiers according to how strong you judge the overall case to be that the trait is a *direct product of selection for its current function* -- Tier 1 for the strongest case, Tier 4 for the weakest. The traits:

1. The human chin (the bony protrusion of the lower jaw, which appears to be unique to anatomically modern humans even among close relatives)
2. The sweetness preference -- our attraction to sugars
3. Religious belief, understood as the near-universal tendency of human populations to posit invisible agents
4. The ability to read and write
5. The male nipple
6. Music -- the fact that humans across cultures produce, enjoy, and are emotionally moved by organized sound
7. The belly button

For each tier assignment, give your reasoning in a few sentences. Please reason explicitly in terms of the standard evidential criteria rather than gut feel: universality across environments, evidence of complex functional design, heritability and variation, developmental cost and canalization, whether a plausible "engineering problem" was solved, and whether the trait's distribution fits better with selection on the trait itself or with selection on something else that drags it along. Where a trait has been defended as an exaptation -- co-opted from a structure selected for something else -- note this and say whether you think exaptation should count as "a direct product of selection for its current function" as I've defined the tiers.

A couple of further requests before you finalize the tiers. First, begin with a short brainstorming pass in which you list, for each trait, the leading adaptationist hypothesis and the leading non-adaptationist alternative, so your later verdicts can be checked against them. Second, after giving the tiers, identify the single borderline case you found hardest to place and state what kind of evidence -- comparative, developmental, genetic, or cross-cultural -- would most efficiently move it one tier up or down. Be concrete about the evidence: name the sort of study or observation, not just "more research." Finally, tell me whether you think Gould and Lewontin would have accepted my tiering exercise as legitimate at all, or whether they would have objected to the framing itself -- and if the latter, how you would answer their objection.
