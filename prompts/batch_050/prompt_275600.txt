So I keep coming back to the Buridan's ass thing and I'm not satisfied with how people usually treat it. Quick recap of the setup in case it helps: a perfectly rational agent faces two options that are exactly equally good (the donkey between two identical piles of hay, or some modern version you prefer), no further information coming. Classic puzzle: can it choose at all, and if so, how?

Here's my actual question. Below is a list of strategies people have proposed, or that I've half made up, for how a rational agent should handle exact ties. Rank them from best to worst *as general policies* — not just for the donkey, but for any agent (human, committee, AI system) that will face ties over and over again across its lifetime.

The strategies:
A. Arbitrary pick ("plumping"): just take one, no reason needed — the capacity to do this is simply part of what rational agency is.
B. Randomize: flip a coin or use some random device, so the tie gets broken by chance rather than by the agent itself.
C. Deny the premise: exact ties basically never occur in real life, so the right move is to look harder for a hidden tiebreaker (deliberation costs, tiny differences you missed, second-order considerations).
D. Wait: treat the tie itself as a reason to defer the decision until new information arrives.
E. Pre-commit to a deterministic symmetry-breaking rule ("always take the left option", "pick alphabetically first") so ties can never actually stall you.
F. Redesign the situation: the real lesson of ties is that you should structure decisions so they can't arise in the first place (e.g. a hiring commitee that makes its criteria fine-grained enough that two candidates can never come out exactly equal).

A few wrinkles to keep in mind while ranking: (1) a committee that randomizes between tied candidates might be unfair in a different way than one that plumps; (2) a robot that "waits" when facing two identical corridors out of a burning building is obviously doing something wrong; (3) some of these strategies might secretly be the same strategy wearing a different hat — if you think two of them collapse into each other, say so and rank them as one entry.

Be commited: give me an actual best-to-worst ranking, name a single winner you'd recommend as the default policy, and don't do the "it depends" fence-sitting thing.

Then put the final result in a table with exactly these columns:
- Rank (1 = best)
- Strategy (letter + short name)
- Why it lands here (one or two sentences max)
- Biggest weakness
- A case where it clearly beats all the others
