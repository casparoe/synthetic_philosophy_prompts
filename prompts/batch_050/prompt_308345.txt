I'm drafting a paper on decision-making under normative uncertainty, and I've talked myself into an argument whose conclusion would sink the entire project — namely, that there can be no rational procedure for acting when you are unsure which moral theory is correct. I strongly suspect I've proved too much, but I cannot locate the misstep. The argument:

(1) Under empirical uncertainty — when you are unsure how the world is — rationality requires weighting your options by your credences, i.e., maximizing expected value.
(2) Normative uncertainty is relevantly like empirical uncertainty: in both cases you face a decision, you lack knowledge of something decision-relevant, and you must act anyway.
(3) So rationality under normative uncertainty likewise requires maximizing expected choiceworthiness across the moral theories to which you assign credence.
(4) Maximizing expected choiceworthiness requires intertheoretic comparisons: you must be able to say how much better or worse an outcome is by one theory's lights relative to another's, on a common scale.
(5) Any common scale is itself a substantive moral claim — it says how much one theory's units matter relative to another's — and so will be endorsed by some of the theories in your credence distribution and rejected by others.
(6) Hence there is no theory-neutral scale: every candidate scale smuggles in the verdict of some particular theory.
(7) A procedure meant to tell you what to do when you do not know which theory is right cannot itself presuppose that some theory is right, on pain of circularity.
(8) Therefore the procedure in (3) cannot be run from a position of genuine normative uncertainty; to know what maximizing expected choiceworthiness requires of you, you would already have to know which moral theory is true.
(9) Therefore there is no rational way to act under normative uncertainty.

Now, (9) cannot be right. A conscientious person torn between, say, a consequentialism and a deontology with stringent constraints faces a real and apparently tractable question about what to do, and something like "be cautious about what most plausible theories condemn" seems like genuinely good advice rather than a confusion. So something above must go. My own preference would be to keep (1) — expected-value reasoning under empirical uncertainty strikes me as non-negotiable — and I would also hate to surrender (4), since comparing theories on no shared scale looks so much like comparing apples and oranges that (3) would risk becoming mere wordplay. But I am prepared to pay whatever the correct diagnosis costs.

So: which step should I reject, why did it look compelling, and what does rejecting it commit me to? If more than one repair is available, please price them against each other. In particular, I'd like to know whether abandoning (2) — denying the analogy between normative and empirical uncertainty — is cheaper or more expensive than abandoning (5), and what the main families of views in this debate (those that treat normative uncertainty on the model of ordinary uncertainty, and those that deny this) each have to swallow. If the real trouble lies elsewhere — in the inference from (6) to (8), say, or in what (7) demands of a decision procedure — I want that spelled out too.
