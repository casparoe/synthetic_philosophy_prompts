My decision-theory reading group is made up of longtime rationalist-blog people, so the standard material -- Newcomb, the counterfactual mugging, updateless reasoning -- is common background we argue *from*, not about. Last week two members, Elena and Marcus, got into a dispute I was asked to referee, and I found I couldn't settle it cleanly. I'd like an outside adjudication.

First, the common ground. Both accept that you should pay the counterfactual mugger: the coin comes up tails, Omega explains that had it come up heads he would have given you $10,000 on condition of having predicted that you'd hand over $100 in the tails case, and both of them pay, on roughly the grounds that the agent disposed to pay is richer in expectation and that "the coin has already landed" is not an admissible move in that deliberation.

The dispute concerns the blackmail-letter variant. Omega, a highly reliable predictor, monitors your house for termites. He sends you a letter demanding $100 if and only if exactly one of the following holds: your house has termites (which will cost you $1,000,000 in damage if untreated), or he has predicted that you would pay $100 upon receiving the letter. The letter arrives. Elena says pay; Marcus says don't. Here is the exchange as I recorded it:

**Elena:** This is the same problem as the mugging. Compare policies, not in-the-moment inclinations. The agent disposed to pay loses $100 and only ever in worlds where there are no termites. The agent disposed to refuse eats the full termite loss in every termite world. Expected costs aren't close. Refusing, letter in hand, is simply acting out the losing policy at the moment it matters.

**Marcus:** The disanalogy is that in the mugging my disposition was upstream of the $10,000's existence -- Omega's generosity in the heads world depended counterfactually on what I'd do here. Nothing about me or my dispositions affects the termites. The only thing my policy influences is which worlds I receive a letter in. So holding the letter, I reason: given Omega's reliability and given my established disposition to pay, this letter means no termites. And in a no-termite world, paying $100 buys literally nothing. I'm not defecting on the winning policy; I'm following it to its conclusion, and the conclusion is that the payment is dead weight.

**Elena:** That is "the coin has already landed" in a trench coat. In the mugging, everything counterfactual was likewise settled before you chose, and the refuser likewise says "in this branch, paying buys nothing." You cannot use your own disposition as evidence -- "I'm a payer, so no termites" -- and then abandon that very disposition within the same deliberation. An agent who reasons the way you just did is an agent Omega predicts as a refuser, and then the letter means termites again.

**Marcus:** Two genuine differences remain. First, in the mugging, refusing makes me an agent who never had anything to gain; here, the payer's advantage consists entirely in having been the sort of person who wouldn't be *warned* about termites, and that was secured the moment Omega made his prediction, before I act. Second, your dominance argument compares policies, but I'm asking about this action given what I now know, and the two come apart precisely when the disaster is causally independent of me. Note that I'd still one-box in Newcomb -- there the predictor simulated *me*, entangling my disposition with the world's state. Here the entanglement is only between my disposition and my *evidence*. Paying is managing the news, not the house.

**Elena:** The expected-loss arithmetic doesn't care whether you call it news or house. Populations of payers end up richer than populations of refusers, full stop. And your "secured the moment he predicted" move applies equally to the mugging: the $10,000 was also secured or forfeit before you chose. You've drawn a line between a predictor entangling your disposition with the world versus with your evidence, but I don't think that line does work -- the letter's evidential structure *is* the mechanism by which the payer policy wins, just as the counterfactual payout is the mechanism there.

They both looked at me. Please adjudicate round by round: for each of the five speeches, say whether its central claim is right, wrong, or right-but-incomplete, and why. Then address the two cruxes directly: (1) Is Marcus's policy-versus-action distinction sound, or does it collapse into the move both parties already rejected in the mugging? (2) Does the causal independence of the termites genuinely differentiate this case from the counterfactual mugging and from Newcomb, or is it decision-theoretically inert? Finally, say what, if anything, both are missing. End with a last line of exactly this form:

Verdict: [Elena | Marcus | Split] -- [one short clause naming the decisive consideration]
