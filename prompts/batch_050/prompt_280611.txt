*In "Of Tragedy," Hume considers and rejects a fiction-based explanation of why audiences enjoy tragic drama -- roughly, the view that because spectators know the events on stage aren't real, the pain they feel is weakened enough to be outweighed or neutralised. (a) State this view and Hume's reason for rejecting it. (b) Lay out Hume's own positive account of where the pleasure in tragedy comes from. (c) Say whose explanation you find more convincing, and why.*

Here's the student's answer:

*The fiction-based view, which Hume associates with Fontenelle (who developed it a few years after Hume's essay came out), says that tragedy is enjoyable because the audience's belief that the events are unreal keeps the painful emotions weak, so the net experience is pleasant. Hume rejects this because fiction alone can't be the source of the pleasure: a badly written, tedious tragedy involves exactly the same awareness that it's all make-believe, yet it gives us no pleasure at all. So the knowledge that it's fiction is at best a background condition, not the explanation.*

*Hume's own view is that the pleasure comes from the artistry of the representation -- the eloquence of the language, the skill of the imitation, the genius of the author. This pleasure predominates over the painful passions the story arouses and converts them into a stronger, smoother feeling of enjoyment. So on Hume's account, the more sorrow the tragedy produces, the more raw material there is for the artistry to convert, and hence the greater the pleasure -- the whole appeal of the genre is maximising the grief so the conversion has more to work with.*

*I find Hume's account more convincing, since it actually predicts that a sufficiently eloquent presentation of real-world atrocities, like a well-made documentary, would be even more enjoyable than a fictional tragedy, which seems right -- people do enjoy gripping documentaries about terrible events.*
