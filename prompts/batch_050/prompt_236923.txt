I'm a software engineer who ended up, through a weird chain of events, sitting on the grants committee of a small private foundation that funds "unusual quantitative social science." This week we're voting on the biggest grant of the year, and I got voluntold to draft the memo since I'm the only one who's ever debugged a time series. The proposal: a three-year project that wants to expand a historical crisis database (thousands of documented revolutions, civil wars, state breakdowns, etc., coded up from the historical record across a bunch of societies and centuries) and use it to build a forecasting model for political instability in modern states. The PI's approach is basically structural-demographic theory -- the idea popularized by Peter Turchin that societies go through long "secular cycles," and that breakdowns are driven by measurable structural pressures like mass immiseration, state fiscal stress, and especially "elite overproduction" (too many credentialed people chasing too few elite positions, so the losers of that competition turn into counter-elites who destabilize the system). The pitch leans hard on the claim that a model built on these indicators predicted rising instability in the US back around 2010, i.e. before the actual turbulent 2010s/2020s, and the PI says with more data and better ML we could get a real early-warning system.

Here's where the committee is split. The enthusiasts say this is just doing for history what we've already done for weather: lots of cases, careful feature engineering, out-of-sample testing, and you get usable probabilistic forecasts even if you can't predict any single event. The skeptics say the whole thing is astrology with regression: the historical record gives you maybe a few dozen truly independent crises, the "successful" 2010 prediction is one call (and possibly a stopped clock), the indicators are mushy and redefined after the fact, and unlike weather, people react to forecasts -- a widely publicized "instability index" saying a country is about to blow up could itself help blow it up, or get the project banned in exactly the countries where it matters. There's also a fight about whether "history has cycles" is even the kind of claim that can be tested, or whether the cycles are baked in by how the database codes events in the first place.

So: draft the committee memo. It should recommend fund / don't fund / fund with conditions (and if conditions, make them concrete and checkable -- e.g. pre-registered forecasts, holdout procedures, whatever you think actually disciplines this kind of research). Please engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side of whatever you recommend, and specifically take a position on the methodological question underneath the whole fight: is "history can be quantified and predicted" a claim that this proposal can actually test, or does the proposal quietly assume it from the start? I want something the committee can vote on Thursday, not a philosophy lecture, but dont shy away from the conceptual stuff where it's actually load-bearing for the decision.
