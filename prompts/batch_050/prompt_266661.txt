Here's where I keep getting stuck as an amateur archaeology nerd: I have strong gut verdicts about when you can legitimately infer what people believed or how they organized themselves from what they left behind, and I can't figure out what principle (if any) ties them together. So, my verdicts:

1. Rich grave goods buried with a child, when most adults in the same cemetery get almost nothing — I'd say this licenses inferring inherited or ascribed status, some kind of ranked institution, not just "they loved their kid."

2. Same logic, but with deliberately broken objects placed in graves — I want to say this tells us something about beliefs about death or an afterlife. Though honestly I'm less sure here than in case 1, and I'm not sure why.

3. A settlement where every house is the same size and layout: I do NOT think this lets you infer an egalitarian ideology. Maybe it was just expedient, or one builder, or building codes imposed on an unwilling population.

4. But if the identical houses are built over centuries and rebuilt identically after fires, now I DO want to infer something normative — an institution or ideology enforcing conformity, not just convenience. Though I notice this seems to contradict my verdict in 3, since the physical evidence is basically the same, just more of it over time.

5. No weapons in any grave at a site: I refuse to infer a peaceful society. Absence of evidence problem — maybe weapons were inherited, recycled, taboo in graves, whatever.

6. Yet a site with no temples, no palace, no obvious elite architecture for hundreds of years — I DO find myself tempted to infer there was no strong centralized authority, which feels like the exact same "absence of evidence" move I rejected in 5. I can't justify treating them differently but I can't give either one up.

7. Marks on pots that repeat across households: fine, infer ownership or workshop identity. But symbols on the same pots that repeat in ritual contexts — I hesitate to call them "writing" or "religious iconography," even though the evidential situation (repeated pattern, intentional placement) looks structurally identical to the ownership marks.

8. A temple aligned to the solstice sunrise: I'd happily infer astronomical knowledge, since the odds of that alignment by chance are low. But a single alignment could still be coincidence, and I don't know how many alignments it takes before inference is OK.

So: what principle or principles about inferring beliefs and institutions from material remains best captures these verdicts? State it precisely, say which of my verdicts it vindicates and which it forces me to drop (I suspect at least one of 3/4 and one of 5/6 can't both survive), and tell me what it would say about cases I haven't listed — like inferring social hierarchy from differential health in skeletal remains, or inferring trade networks from foreign materials.
