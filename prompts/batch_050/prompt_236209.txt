Since I retired from the chemistry department I've been going to this little philosophy reading group at the library, and last week we got onto probability, which I thought would be the easy week. Instead two members, Diane (a retired maths teacher) and Rob (an engineer), got into a real scrap over Bertrand's paradox and neither would give an inch. I stayed out of it but I keep turning it over. Here's the exchange as best as I remember it.

Diane opened by saying the paradox is a knockdown argument against the prinicple of indifference. If a random chord of a circle has probability 1/2, or 1/3, or 1/4 of being longer than the side of the inscribed triangle, depending on whether you randomize the midpoint, the angle, or the distance from centre, then "spread probability evenly over the possibilities" isn't a rule at all, since the possibilities aren't well-defined. She said this poisons the whole idea of objective Bayesianism.

Rob said that's too hasty. Jaynes handled the chord problem decades ago: you demand the answer be invariant under rotations, translations, and changes of scale of the circle, since nothing in the problem statement picks out any of those, and then only one of the distributions survives and you get 1/2. Indifference isn't dead, it just has to be applied to the symmetries of the physical problem rather than to whatever parameterization you happen to scribble down first.

Diane then wheeled out the wine and water case. A mixture has a wine-to-water ratio somewhere between 1/2 and 2, and that's all you're told. Uniform over that ratio gives one answer for the probability the ratio exceeds 3/2, uniform over the water-to-wine ratio gives a different answer, and the problem here is perfectly symmetric between wine and water, so Rob's "respect the symmetries" fix still leaves a contradiction. She said symmetry can't be the master key when even a fully symmetric setup forks.

Rob's reply was that the wine-water problem is under-specified as a physical problem -- no geometry, no transformation group you actually care about -- so of course pure indifference has nothing to bite on there, whereas the chord problem has real geometric structure. And when I asked him what you do with genuinely structureless problems, he said maximum entropy, and when Diane said maximum entropy needs a background measure so it just inherits the ambiguity, he said the measure itself can be fixed by invariance requirements, like Jeffreys priors, and Diane said that's moving the bump in the carpet, and they were still at it when the library closed.

Can you go through this round by round and tell me who's right at each step -- the chord calculation, whether Jaynes's solution really rescues indifference or redefines it, whether wine-water is a genuine counterexample or just a non-problem, and whether the Jeffreys-prior move at the end answers Diane or concedes her point? And is there something they're both missing?
