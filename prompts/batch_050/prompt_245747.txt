You are a philosopher of history with particular expertise in collective memory, social epistemology, and the ethics of commemoration. I'd like you to adjudicate an argument I overheard between two friends of mine, and I'd ask you to think carefully and even-handedly, since I genuinely can't tell who has the better of it. Before giving your verdicts, please take a moment to reconstruct the strongest version of each person's position, and only then work through the exchange point by point.

Some background. My hometown recently commissioned a review of the story told on the plaque at our old mill, which has always been the centerpiece of the town's founding narrative. The received story — the one I grew up with — is that the mill's founder rebuilt it after a fire at his own expense rather than lay off his workers, and that the town's character of mutual loyalty dates from that act. The reviewers, working from payroll ledgers and court filings, concluded the story is substantially false: the workers were in fact dismissed, and several were prosecuted when they protested. The council now has to decide whether to replace the plaque. My two friends, Marta and Daniel, argued about it at some length. I'll reproduce the exchange as faithfully as I can.

Marta began by saying the plaque has to go, full stop. A plaque is an assertion of fact mounted in public space; if the facts are wrong, the assertion is a standing falsehood, and no amount of local affection changes what it says. She added that the falsehood isn't harmless, since the actual people in the record — the dismissed and prosecuted workers — are precisely the ones the story erases, and keeping the plaque means the town continues to wrong them every day it stands.

Daniel replied that a plaque isn't a footnote in a journal article. Commemorations do a different job from documents: they give a community a usable account of itself, and this particular story carries the value of loyalty in hardship, which he thinks the town genuinely lives by in ways it wouldn't if the story were deleted. Correcting the plaque, he said, treats memory as a failed copy of the archive, when really memory and history answer different questions.

Marta answered that this is special pleading. Once you know a story is false, repeating it to your children isn't preserving meaning, it's just lying with extra ceremony. And she pressed the point that the story's emotional usefulness is not neutral: it flatters the mill-owning side of town and asks the descendants of the fired workers to celebrate their own ancestors' humiliation. A story that does identity-work for some people by doing erasure-work on others doesn't get protected just because the first group finds it inspiring.

Daniel then made what I thought was his strongest move. He said Marta was treating the archive as if it were innocent, but the archive was kept by the mill's owners and the magistrates they appointed. The ledgers and court filings aren't a neutral record that happens to contradict memory; they're the paperwork of the winning side, and oral memory is sometimes the only place the losing side's experience survives at all. So when memory and record conflict, he argued, "correct the memory to fit the record" can in practice mean "defer to whoever had a clerk." He conceded he wasn't claiming this particular story is true — he accepted the review's findings — but said the general principle Marta was relying on, that record trumps memory, deserved less confidence than she gave it, and that the burden should be on the correction, which should also preserve what the old story was doing rather than simply negate it.

Marta's reply was that a biased record calls for better history, not for immunity for myth. Historians know archives are partial and correct for it; that is literally the discipline. But if every community's memory gets to override the documentary record whenever the record is unflattering, then every group keeps its comforting story, there is no shared past left to argue about, and the workers' descendants lose the very tool — evidence — that just vindicated them. She said Daniel was wielding archive-skepticism selectively: he only reached for it when the archive threatened a story he liked.

That is where the conversation stalled. My questions: Who is right at each step? Specifically — is Marta right that a commemorative plaque is an assertion of fact with the obligations that come with that, or is Daniel right that commemoration belongs to a different category of speech where strict accuracy is not the governing norm? Is Daniel's point about the archive being the winners' paperwork a genuine check on the authority of the record, or is Marta right that he's deploying it selectively and that biased records argue for better history rather than for deference to memory? And who is right about what should happen to the plaque itself, given that Daniel concedes the story is false but wants the correction to preserve its function?

Finally, I'd like your honest assessment of what both of them are missing — is there a distinction or a question (perhaps about who gets to decide, or about what memory is evidence for versus what it is for) that neither of them raised, which would have moved the argument forward?
