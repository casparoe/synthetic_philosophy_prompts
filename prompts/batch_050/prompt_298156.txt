I've been reading about different high-level designs for aligning AI assistants, and I keep circling around one contrast: a goal-directed agent is given (or infers) an objective and pursues its consequences, while an approval-directed agent is trained to take whatever action its human overseer would rate most highly if asked. Advocates of the second design argue it sidesteps the classic failure mode where a literal-minded pursuit of an imperfectly specified goal goes wrong in ways nobody wanted. I'm skeptical that this safety is free, and I want a concrete case to think with rather than the usual abstractions.

Construct a thought experiment in which an approval-directed assistant is safer and more helpful than a goal-directed one across all the ordinary situations in the story, yet fails badly in one rare situation where the goal-directed assistant -- despite being given only an imperfect, literal-minded proxy of what the humans actually want -- does the right thing. Constraints: (1) the setting should be a mundane near-future deployment, something like a scheduling assistant for a hospital or a logistics coordinator for a small company, not a superintelligence and not science fiction; (2) the failure must not arise because the overseer is careless, lazy, inattentive, or malevolent -- the overseer evaluates each action it is shown competently and in good faith, and rates it correctly given what approval is able to see; (3) the agent must be honest and non-deceptive throughout -- no manipulation, no hidden actions, no sycophantic framing; the failure has to fall out of the approval-directed design itself rather than from anyone in the story behaving badly; (4) the scenario itself should be under 250 words.

After the scenario, do the demonstration: state what the goal-directed assistant does in the rare situation and why its imperfect proxy goal happens to save it, state what the approval-directed assistant does at each step and why each step earns approval, and identify the single structural feature of approval-direction that does the work -- I suspect it is something about approval being local to individual actions rather than sensitive to trajectories, but show me whether the case actually turns on that or on something else. Then answer two questions: if you remove that feature (say, by letting the overseer approve plans rather than actions), does the case collapse, and if so, what new problem does the removal reintroduce? Finally, steelman the approval-direction advocate's best objection to the case -- that is, the strongest reason to think it doesn't show what it purports to show -- and say whether you find the objection decisive.
