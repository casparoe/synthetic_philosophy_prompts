I teach an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of physics seminar (14 students, one 100-minute session) and want a runnable classroom activity that makes students feel the force of the claim that quantum particles may not be individuals. Almost every treatment I know starts by telling students that permuting two electrons changes nothing, and they nod without feeling anything. I want them to discover, through their own bookkeeping, that the way you count possibilities depends on whether the things counted carry identities -- and then to be genuinely unsettled about which counting is "the real one."

Here is the raw material I imagine using, which you should refine rather than take as fixed. In a first phase, students toss pairs of ordinary coins into cups and tally outcomes; because each coin is in fact this coin and not that one, the split-outcome shows up about twice as often as either all-in-one-cup outcome, and they can verify this by checking the coins' identities after each toss. In a second phase, the rules change: a designated "detector" reports only how many objects landed in each cup, never which ones, and the group must decide whether the old four-way tally was ever tracking something real or was an artifact of labeling. In a third phase, an exclusion rule is imposed (at most one object per cup) and the students see the space of possibilities collapse to a single arrangement. The debrief should then map the three counting regimes onto classical, bosonic, and fermionic statistics, and connect the second phase's discomfort to the philosophical options: that particles lack haecceity or "thisness," that they are individuals discernible only weakly through relational properties, or that the counting question is conventional rather than metaphysical.

Design the full activity for me. I want the philosophical trouble to arise from the tallying rules and the students' own decisions, not from anything I announce beforehand; no lecture up front, and the names "boson," "fermion," and "indistinguishability" should not be spoken until the debrief. Please structure your answer with the following sections, in this order:

- Overview and intended insight (about 150 words): what students should come away seeing, stated without jargon.
- Materials and setup (about 100 words): everything needed, including how the tally sheets should be designed so that phase two's restriction is enforceable.
- Phase one: labeled counting (about 200 words): exact rules, roles, timing, and the tally students should produce.
- Phase two: occupation numbers only (about 250 words): the rule change, how it is enforced physically in the room, and the specific moment where students should be forced to vote on whether the old tally was tracking reality.
- Phase three: exclusion (about 150 words): rules and the quick observation it should provoke.
- Debrief guide (about 300 words): the mapping from each phase to the physics and to the three philosophical positions, including which student utterances from earlier phases to quote back at the group.
- Likely failure modes (about 150 words): what predictably goes wrong when this is run (e.g., students treating the phase-two restriction as mere ignorance of facts that still exist) and how to handle each.
- What the game cannot show (about 100 words): an honest note on which feature of real quantum indistinguishability the activity necessarily fakes, since the facilitator imposes by fiat what physics imposes by nature.

Keep the mechanics concrete enough that I could run the session from your answer alone, and don't pad it with references to the literature -- I'll supply citations myself.
