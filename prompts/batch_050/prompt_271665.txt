I run a grad seminar on formal epistemology, and last week a student tried to end a discussion about undermining evidence with the one-liner 'rational credence just follows the chances.' She meant it as a full stop: once the objective chances are on the table, there's nothing left for belief to figure out. (The case at hand was a fair coin where you've glimpsed the landing through a mirror -- the chances still say 50/50, but you clearly shouldn't stay at 0.5.) I let it slide in the moment, but the slogan has been bugging me. Can you lay out the different precise theses it could be expressing -- from the trivial reading where chance just is whatever ideal credence dictates, so 'follow the chances' says nothing, through a Principal-Principle-style claim about conditional deference given only admissable evidence, to a strong reading where credence must match chance even in the face of inadmissible information? For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, defensible but substantive, or just false, and which reading she would actually need for the slogan to do the work she wanted it to do. Finally, tell me which argument or counterexample would most plausibly change your verdicts, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
