I have no formal training in philosophy, but after thinking about changes in taste and memory I have begun to wonder whether what people call qualia are coherent things to posit at all. The cases I already have are:

1. The two expert coffee tasters. The first says that the coffee tastes exactly as it always did but that the reaction to that same taste has soured; the second says that their standards and reactions have remained fixed while the taste itself has changed. Their discriminations and outward behaviour could apparently be the same.
2. The acquired-taste case. At first beer tastes unpleasant and later it is enjoyed, leaving it unclear whether the flavour has changed or only the drinker’s response to a stable flavour.
3. The rival brain-operation stories. After an operation, one explanation says that present taste processing has been altered, while another says that the memory of how things used to taste has been altered; both fit the subject’s reports.
4. The colour-spectrum case. Two people make all the same colour discriminations and use colour language identically, although one allegedly has the other’s red and green experiences reversed.
5. The gradual-replacement case. Neurons are replaced one by one by functionally identical components, forcing a choice between experience fading without any reportable loss and experience changing abruptly despite functional continuity.

Please construct one additional thought experiment that belongs in this file but is not a variant of these. It should not merely move the problem to another sense, introduce yet another operation, reverse another pair of experiences, or replace another part of the brain. The new case should still produce the central difficulty: two incompatible descriptions agree about every outward fact and every sincere report, but disagree about whether the phenomenal character itself has remained constant while memory, preference, attention, or interpretation has changed, or whether the phenomenal character has changed instead. Please do not settle the matter by stipulating that the subject has infallible access to the answer.

After giving the case, explain exactly what the two descriptions are and what makes the case structurally different from the five above. Then say which conclusion it better supports: that there is a fact about the experience that could remain permanently undetectable, or that the supposed distinction is empty once everything discriminable and reportable has been fixed. Please distinguish those claims carefully rather than moving directly from we could never tell to there is no difference. Finally, give the strongest objection someone might make to your own thought experiment.
