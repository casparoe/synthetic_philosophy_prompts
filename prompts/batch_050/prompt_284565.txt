I've been trying to work out what I think makes something a proof, by checking my verdicts against cases, and I'd like to know what principle they add up to. Here are the verdicts:

(1) The 1976 Appel–Haken proof of the four-colour theorem: I accept it. We know the theorem, even though no human has verified the case enumeration by hand. (2) Hales's proof of the Kepler conjecture: when the journal's referees reported, after years of review, that they were highly confident but could not fully verify the computational parts, I think the community's unease was justified, and I only count the conjecture as settled once the Flyspeck project completed a fully formal, machine-checked proof in 2014. (3) The classification of finite simple groups: tens of thousands of pages by hundreds of authors, never surveyed in full by any one person, with gaps found and repaired over decades. I accept this as knowledge on exactly the same footing as the four-colour theorem. (4) Probabilistic primality testing: if a number passes enough Miller–Rabin rounds that the chance of error is below 2^-100, I say we know it is prime — this feels no worse than trusting an ordinary proof. (5) A hypothetical: a proprietary computer system announces that a long-open conjecture is true, offering only its track record — ten thousand previous conjectures settled correctly — while its code and output remain uninspectable. Here I say we do not know the conjecture.

The tension I can't resolve: in (4) I accept mere probability, in (1) and (3) I accept unsurveyability, yet in (5) I reject a track record stronger than that of most human referees. Is there one principle — about what a proof is, or about what kinds of testimony and computation we may defer to — that delivers all five verdicts? State it precisely, with the case motivating each clause; say which verdict it would sacrifice if it cannot save them all, and why that one; and say what it implies for a case I haven't listed: a proof discovered and written entirely by an AI system that accompanies it with a formally verified certificate.
