I help curate a small fossil collection at a natural history museum, and a pattern keeps coming up in conversations with our staff paleontologist that neither of us can find a settled term for. I would like to know whether biologists or philosophers of biology have named it, and if not, what the nearest concepts are and why none of them quite fits.

The pattern, first in the form we actually encounter it. A lineage changes very slowly over a few million years. Take any two specimens separated by a short stretch of that time and they are similar enough that, had they lived side by side, nothing would have prevented them from forming one breeding population. But compare the oldest and youngest forms in the series and they are unmistakably different kinds of animal: different teeth, different limbs, different proportions. Nowhere in the series is there a generation where it seems right to say the old kind ended and a new one began; every candidate dividing line looks arbitrary, and yet the difference between the two ends is not arbitrary at all.

I am aware there is a well-known spatial analogue of this: populations strung out around some barrier, each exchanging members freely with its neighbors, with the chain's two ends unable to interbreed where they finally meet. I know that case has an established name. What I cannot find out is whether the temporal version has a name of its own, or whether it is treated as the same phenomenon merely rotated from space into time. It does not feel like a mere rotation to us, for two reasons. First, in the spatial case the incompatible endpoints coexist, so their failure to interbreed is observable in principle; in the temporal case the endpoints never coexist, so saying they "could not" have interbred is a counterfactual about animals separated by a million years, and it is unclear what would even make that counterfactual true or false. Second, the spatial case leaves the classification problem local — are the endpoints one kind or two? — whereas the temporal case seems to infect every slice: if no dividing line through the series is privileged, in what sense is the inventory of kinds at any single moment anything more than bookkeeping convenience?

So, concretely: does the temporal chain pattern have an established name distinct from the spatial one? If it does, how is it usually distinguished both from the spatial case and from the broader thesis that the boundaries between kinds are fuzzy? If it does not, say so plainly, and propose a term with a precise definition and a few boundary cases: for instance, does the pattern survive an extinction bottleneck that prunes the middle of the chain; does it require gradual change, or can the same abstract structure arise through steady replacement of one form by another; and does the counterfactual problem about the endpoints mean the temporal case is a different phenomenon altogether rather than an instance of the same one? A bare label will not satisfy me — what I want is the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.

One request about form: please write the answer as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
