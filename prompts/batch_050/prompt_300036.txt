**Examination question**

Two risk analysts, Ada and Bola, begin with the same prior probability distribution over all relevant states of the world. Each receives different private evidence about whether a proposed reservoir will fail within fifty years. Ada’s posterior probability for failure is 0.70; Bola’s is 0.30. Each analyst knows the other’s posterior, knows that the other knows it, and so on without limit, so these posteriors are common knowledge. Both are ideal Bayesians.

An employer proposes that they repeatedly and truthfully announce their current posterior, revise in light of the other’s announcement, and continue until their announced probabilities stop changing.

1. State what Aumann’s agreement theorem implies in this situation.
2. Explain what the theorem does and does not establish about the two analysts reaching the truth.
3. Discuss whether the employer’s proposed announcement procedure adds anything to the theorem’s assumptions, and whether it could make agreement practically achievable.

A good answer should distinguish shared information from common knowledge, posteriors from objective chances or truth, and the static agreement result from a dynamic procedure for reaching it.

**Student’s answer**

Aumann’s theorem applies where two reasoners share a prior and their posterior probabilities for the same proposition are common knowledge. It says that, despite their different private evidence, those common-knowledge posteriors cannot differ. Therefore Ada and Bola cannot rationally remain at 0.70 and 0.30 if both numbers genuinely are common knowledge. At least one of them must have failed to update on everything contained in the situation, including the fact of the other’s judgement.

The theorem is sometimes misread as showing that disagreement is always irrational. Its actual scope is narrower: it does not cover agents with different priors, agents who are not ideal Bayesian reasoners, or cases in which one agent merely knows the other’s estimate without the infinite hierarchy required for common knowledge. Nor does it show that the agreed posterior is true. Even if Ada and Bola both end up assigning 0.52, for example, the reservoir may still fail or remain safe. Their agreement concerns their rational degrees of belief given their shared prior and total information, not the objective chance of failure.

The proposed announcement procedure gives the analysts a practical way to produce the situation assumed by the theorem. Since each announcement reveals new information, they will revise their posteriors, and because they are truthful Bayesians with a common prior the sequence must eventually settle at a common value. Once this happens, their shared posterior will be correct: the announcements ensure that all private evidence has been fully exchanged and that no residual uncertainty can remain. Thus the employer can treat disagreement as merely a failure to continue the conversation for long enough.

The procedure also shows that the original description was impossible. If Ada really had posterior 0.70 while Bola really had posterior 0.30, their posteriors could not have been common knowledge, because Aumann’s theorem says rational agents with a common prior must already agree before any conversation takes place.
