I teach a mixed undergrad seminar on philosophy of language and translation (about 12 students, 80 minute session) and next week we're doing the cluster of ideas from Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot -- basically the claim that in poetry translation, form isn't packaging around the content, it IS part of the content, so "faithful translation" isn't one thing but a bunch of competing dimensions you can't maximize all at once. The book circles around one small French poem by Clément Marot: 28 lines, every line exactly 3 syllables, rhymed couplets all the way through, addressed to a young woman who's ill, teasing her about eating too many sweets, with the poet naming himself inside the poem. Hofstadter spends a big chunk of the book collecting and grading attempts at translating it, and no attempt keeps everything.

Design me an activity that makes the students FEEL that impossibility before anyone names it. Here's roughly what I have in mind, improve freely: split them into teams, give every team the same source material (I'll supply the French text from the book plus a literal interlinear gloss, since almost none of them read French), but give each team a different constraint budget -- one team has to preserve the 3-syllable lines and couplets no matter what, one team can only preserve literal meaning, one team has to preserve the teasing tone and the relationship to the addressee, maybe one team has to preserve the self-reference. Then some kind of structured comparison or vote where they discover that "most faithful" splits the room depending on which dimension people count, and ideally a second round where they have to actively trade constraints against each other under a fixed budget rather than just being assigned them. They should run into the problem through the rules, not through me lecturing.

I need: the exact rules and constraint cards, a timeline for 80 minutes, the debrief questions that map specific moments in the activity onto Hofstadter's claims (form-as-content, translation as choosing which similarities count, why the book's title pun on tombeau is itself a mini demonstration of the problem), what will predictably go wrong when I actually run this (e.g. the meaning-only team's version winning the vote because it's the only readable one) and how to handle it, and an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce -- my guess is the gloss flattens the experience of actually knowing the source language, but tell me if there's more.
