I'm designing a graduate seminar unit on Frege's puzzle about identity statements, and I want the class to slow down before they start reciting the standard Hesperus/Phosphorus material. In *On Sense and Reference*, Gottlob Frege writes:

"The sense of a proper name is grasped by everybody who is sufficiently familiar with the language or totality of designations to which it belongs; but this serves to illuminate only a single aspect of the reference, supposing it to have one. Comprehensive knowledge of the reference would require us to say immediately whether any given sense belongs to it. To such knowledge we never attain."

I've read this paragraph four time now, and I still think the phrase “illuminate only a single aspect” is doing more work than students usually notice. Is “aspect” an epistemic route, part of linguistic meaning, a way of thinking about the object, or just a suggestive metaphor? And why should grasping a sense be compatible with ignorance about which other senses belong to the same reference?

Use that passage as the basis for an adequacy specification for a theory of singular reference and informative identity. The purpose is not to explain everything about objects or metaphysics; it is to explain ordinary linguistic competence, communication, discovery, and rational disagreement. To keep this concrete, use this invented case alongside Frege's: an old harbor survey calls a fixed light “the Beacon”; a newer astronomical database labels what may be the very same thing “K-314.” Competent users can understand both expressions, rationally doubt whether the Beacon is K-314, and later treat the identity as a discovery. Meanwhile, the description “the brightest light visible from Pier 9” might remain meaningful even if the relevant lamp has been removed and it no longer refers to anything. A visitor can also say “that light” while pointing from the pier.

What must an adequate theory get right? Give the requirements with the case or consideration motivating each one: the different cognitive values of “the Beacon is the Beacon” and “the Beacon is K-314”; the possibility of understanding a term without knowing everything about its referent; communication between speakers who associate different information with a term; embedded occurrences in attitude reports; apparently meaningful but empty expressions; demonstratives and context-dependence; and any further requirement I have missed. For each requirement, say whether it is genuinely non-negotiable, a defensible but optional theoretical goal, or merely the prejudice of one semantic camp. Please distinguish sharply between what is relatively settled in philosophy of language and what remains genuinely contested; don't present descriptivism, direct reference, two-dimensionalism, mental-file views, or any other contender as the consensus.

The part I care about most is whether some plausible requirements are jointly unsatisfiable without extra machinery. For example, can a theory simultaneously tie all differences in cognitive significance to differences in semantic content, treat substitution failure in attitude reports as diagnosing semantic difference, preserve simple compositionality, and explain successful communication between speakers with very different information? If not, show exactly where the tension lies and what kinds of repair are available. Also identify the single requirement that excludes the largest number of familiar candidates, and only then give a brief snapshot of how descriptivist, direct-reference, two-dimensionalist, and mental-file accounts fare. Keep the weight on the criteria, not on declaring a winner.
