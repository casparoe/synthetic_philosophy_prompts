I'm a PhD student in history (modern Europe) and, for my sins, I'm the grad rep on our department's centennial exhibit commitee. The design team has proposed a wall called Roots to Routes: a timeline ending in our current faculty, with past approaches arranged as stages that led here. So the old diplomatic-history seminar becomes the root of today's work on transnational networks; a short-lived behavioral-science cluster is included only insofar as it anticipates current data methods; several people who spent careers here are left out because their work does not connect to any present strength. The chair says visitors need a story and donors want one that makes the department look like it has arrived somewhere, not just accumulated stuff.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before we vote: approve, reject, or approve with conditions. The memo needs a clear bottom line and concrete redesign instructions -- what the timeline, captions, omitted-faculty policy, and closing panel should do -- not just a warning against Whig history. Please take seriously the strongest case for the current design: all exhibits select from the standpoint of the present, an anti-Whig display can become shapeless antiquarianism, and it would be dishonest to pretend our current concerns did not guide the selection. Where exactly does legitimate retrospective genealogy turn into writing the past as progress toward us, and how can an exhibit show contingency, defeated alternatives, and paths not taken without lapsing into either celebration or empty even-handedness? End with a recommendation the commitee could actually defend to both donors and historians.
