For several years I have been part of a rationality reading group that circles back, with grim regularity, to the justification of punishment. The debate is now thoroughly stalled, and I would like a diagnosis rather than another round of arguments.

The group's two camps know each other's moves by heart. The consequentialists treat punishment as a tool for reducing crime: they cite deterrence studies, incapacitation effects, rehabilitation programs, and the costs of imprisonment, and they hold that a punishment is justified exactly insofar as it produces more good than harm, all things considered. The retributivists hold that punishment is justified because the guilty deserve it, and that severity should track culpability rather than expected social benefit; they regard punishing the innocent, when it would deter, as a reductio of the opposing view. What strikes me is that nobody disputes the empirical literature. Both sides accept the same deterrence data, the same recidivism figures, the same findings about rehabilitation. Nothing factual would change anyone's mind, and after three years nothing has.

The dispute condensed for me around a passage in Kant's Doctrine of Right, the first part of the Metaphysics of Morals, which we read last spring. Kant argues, in effect, that judicial punishment may never be inflicted merely as a means to some further good, whether for the offender or for society, but only because a crime has been committed; the penal law, he says, has the form of a categorical imperative rather than a piece of social engineering. He then proposes that the measure of punishment is a principle of equality modelled on the lex talionis: whatever one does to another, one does to oneself.

Our consequentialists read the first claim as table-pounding, an ipse dixit dressed as an imperative, and read the equality principle as covert consequentialism, since operationalizing equal harm requires comparing welfare effects anyway. Our retributivists read the first claim as a point about the logic of justification, that consequences are the wrong kind of reason, full stop, and regard the equality principle as the one non-consequentialist currency of proportionality on offer. Nobody moved.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: this is not a factual disagreement or even a normative one, but a disagreement about what a justification is allowed to cite, with Kant's categorical/means distinction marking the exact seam. Is that right? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what the two positions actually say, including whether Kant's equality principle can be made operational without smuggling in a consequentialist metric, and whether his ban on punishing-as-means is an argument or a refusal to argue. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a case both sides must interpret, a formal result, or a reframing of the question. If the honest answer is that this is bedrock and no progress should be expected, say that and explain why.
