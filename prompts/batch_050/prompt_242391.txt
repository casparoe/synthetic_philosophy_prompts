I am a PhD student working on rational choice and the philosophy of action, and I am preparing a dissertation chapter on the Death in Damascus problem. Before comparing particular decision theories, I want a precise specification of what an adequate theory of deliberation under prediction must accomplish.

Take the core case to have this structure: an agent learns that a predictor has already formed an extremely reliable expectation about where the agent will be that night. The predictor will go to the predicted location, and the agent’s encounter there will be fatal. In the limiting version, the prediction is infallible and the agent knows this; in weaker versions, it is merely highly reliable. The agent can remain where they are or travel elsewhere, but their deliberation does not appear to give them ordinary causal control over where the predictor will be. Please treat this as a philosophical decision problem rather than as an exercise in literary interpretation, theology, or the historical details of the story.

Write the desiderata for an adequate account of rational choice in Death-in-Damascus cases. For each requirement, explain the phenomenon or intuition that motivates it, and classify it as one of the following:

1. Non-negotiable: any adequate theory must satisfy it.
2. Substantive but negotiable: a theory may reasonably violate it if it explains the cost.
3. Camp-specific: a requirement imposed by a particular approach to evidence, causation, or rationality rather than by the case itself.

The list should address at least the following issues, though you may reorganize or subdivide them:

- The theory must distinguish an action’s causal influence on an outcome from the evidence that performing the action provides about that outcome.
- It must explain how deliberation can remain practically significant even when every available action leads to the same outcome conditional on an infallible prediction.
- It must distinguish the claim that the agent cannot avoid death from the fatalistic conclusion that deliberation is pointless.
- It must give a coherent account of what the agent learns, if anything, from finding themselves inclined to flee or to remain.
- It must avoid counting the same predictive evidence twice: once as a fixed constraint on the outcome and again as a reason generated by the prospective action.
- It must distinguish three mechanisms that informal discussions often collapse: a prediction made before deliberation, a prediction formed by observing the deliberative process, and a prediction that causally guides the predictor’s later intervention.
- It must handle graded reliability. A theory that gives an answer only for an infallible predictor, without explaining what changes at 99 percent, 80 percent, or 55 percent reliability, is incomplete.
- It must distinguish rational choice from fortunate choice, and rational regret from evidence that the earlier decision was irrational.
- It must say whether rationality requires choosing a lesser probability of death when such a choice is available, and whether the answer changes when attempting to lower that probability is itself evidence that death is more likely.
- It must explain the apparent asymmetry between fleeing because the predictor expects one to flee and fleeing simply because one fears the predictor.
- It must specify whether the rationality of an action can depend on facts about the predictor’s reasoning that are inaccessible to the agent.
- It must be usable by a bounded agent who does not know which abstract decision theory they should be following.

After the main list, identify any desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied. Be precise about whether the incompatibility is logical, depends on the assumption of infallible prediction, or arises only when additional commitments about causation or evidence are added. In particular, discuss possible tensions among action-guidingness, sensitivity to predictive evidence, preservation of causal efficacy, and the avoidance of systematically self-defeating choices. If the apparent conflict merely results from equivocating between different probability assignments or decision points, show where the equivocation occurs.

Also identify the single requirement that would exclude the largest number of familiar approaches to rational choice, and explain why. Then briefly test causal decision theory and evidential decision theory against the finished list, not as a full comparison or scorecard, but as a check on whether the desiderata discriminate among real positions. If either theory needs a more elaborate specification of the case before it can be assessed, state exactly what is missing.

Do not answer by classifying the case as Newcomb-like and stopping there. The important question is what features of prediction, timing, evidence, and control must be represented before that classification would be informative. No bibliography or citations are needed.
