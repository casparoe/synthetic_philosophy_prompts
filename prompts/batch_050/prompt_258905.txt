I'm a novelist, mid-draft on a political thriller set in a fictional country -- a sort of soft-authoritarian state that has kept all the furniture of legality: courts, a penal code, published statutes, lawyers in gowns. The twist of the book is that the regime genuinely does rule through law in some sense, and that's what makes it creepy. My problem is that my beta readers keep telling me the legal system feels like a cartoon: judges getting phone calls from the minister, dissidents simply disappearing. Too crude. I want the state to fail the rule of law in quieter, more plausible ways, the kind a reader almost doesn't notice until chapter thirty, and I realised I don't actually have a working grip on what the rule of law even requires beyond "laws should apply to everyone."

So here's what I want: take what philosophers like Fuller, Raz, and Waldron say about the rule of law as an ideal -- Fuller's stuff about what makes law count as law at all, Raz's more formal checklist-y version, Waldron's insistence that courts and procedure and argument are the heart of it -- and turn it into an audit I can actually run on my fictional state. A sequence of questions I ask about the world I've built, in an order that makes sense (I assume some failures make other questions moot?), what counts as a pass or a fail for each, and which failures would be nearly invisible to an ordinary citizen going about their day, since those are gold for fiction. Where the theorists would disagree about whether something is a genuine rule-of-law failure or just bad policy -- like, does retroactive taxation count, does horrible substantive content count if it's duly enacted -- don't paper over it, just mark it as a judgment call and tell me which way each of them would lean.

Then run the audit on two elements I've already written: first, a statute that criminalises "acts damaging to public confidence," drafted vaguely on purpose, enforced only occasionally and mostly against journalists; second, a system where every statute is public, clear, prospective, and consistently applied -- but the legislature passes a fresh specific law retroactively authorising whatever the security service did last month, every month, like clockwork. My hunch is the first fails noisily and the second fails in some deeper way I can't articulate, but tell me if the audit disagrees. And warn me which boxes my fictional state would pass in a way that might surprise me -- the uncomfortable possibility that it ticks more of Fuller's list than some real democracies do, since that's basically the thesis of the book and I want to get the philosophy right rather then just gesturing at it.
