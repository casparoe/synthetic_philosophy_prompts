So last weekend I got into this huge argument with my friend Maya that started over a video game exhibition and ended with us yelling (friendly yelling) about whether anything can be art. Her position, roughly: if it transmits a feeling from one person to another, it's art, end of story. She'd been reading Tolstoy's What is Art? and kept citing his test -- that art is about infecting an audience with a feeling the artist actually lived through, and that the infectiousness depends on how individual and clear the feeling is, but above all on the artist's sincerity, i.e. that the artist genuinely felt it rather than manufacturing it to manipulate an audience. I kept saying that test makes a sincere advertising jingle art and a cold, calculated masterpiece maybe not art, which seems insane.

Here's the thing though: this isn't just pub philosophy for me. I help run the open-call selection for our city's annual arts festival. We get around 300 submissions, a rotating panel of volunteers screens them, we have maybe 15 minutes per piece, and every single year we have meltdowns over the same question: is this thing even eligible as an artwork? Recent real disputes: a livestream of the artist sleeping for a week, an AI-generated print series where the 'artist' wrote prompts, a stand-up set submitted as performance, a beautifully engineered footbridge design, and found objects with a one-paragraph statement attached.

So: take Tolstoy's sincerity/infection test (I'm paraphrasing, go read the actual chapter if you want -- it's the later part of What is Art?) and turn it into a procedure my screeners could actually run. Questions in order, what counts as a pass or fail at each step, what a screener should do when they literally cannot tell whether the maker 'really felt it' (which is most of the time, since sincerity is not observable and artists lie on their statements), and where the procedure should just stop and hand the decision to a human vote because no test will settle it. Be honest about what you had to drop or distort from Tolstoy to make it usable -- I suspect the sincerity condition is where the whole thing either works or falls apart.

Then run the procedure on three cases: the AI-generated prints, the week-long sleep livestream, and a genuinely moving commercial jingle submitted as 'sound art'. At least one of these should produce an answer the panel (or I) will hate -- if your procedure gives comfortable answers everywhere I won't trust it.

Also, since I'm obviously emotionally invested in winning the argument with Maya: tell me what would change your mind here. What objection or counterexample would make you junk the Tolstoy-based procedure and build on a different approach (institutional theory, cluster definitions, whatever), and where is your translation from his actual view to a usable checklist most likely to have gone wrong? I'd rather have a procedure that knows its own weak points than one that pretends it definately settled a 2000-year-old debate.
