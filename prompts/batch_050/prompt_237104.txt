I take Confucian role ethics, in the form defended by Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont, to be roughly correct: persons are constituted by their roles and relations rather than being autonomous individuals who happen to occupy them, there is no core self to be found underneath the roles, and moral life consists in cultivating these relations well, with ritual and remonstrance providing the resources for criticizing bad practice from within. What would count against it? I don't want generic communitarian objections. I want the specific findings, cases, and collapses the view is exposed to: for instance, whether Mencius's child-at-the-well case, usually invoked in the view's favor, actually commits it to a role-independent humaneness that does the real normative work; whether the fact that unjust roles (say, those imposed on daughters in a patriarchal lineage) can only be criticized by appeal to other roles means the view cannot condemn a role itself, or whether defenders genuinely accept that consequence; whether social-psychological evidence of stable cross-role dispositions, or of early-emerging fairness judgments in children that ignore hierarchy, would trouble the constitutive claim about selfhood or only its motivational story; and whether the view's reliance on ritual flexibility and historical reform (the abandonment of footbinding, say) shows internal critique working or smuggles in an external standard. Consider too a constructed case: a person whose every available role requires complicity in cruelty, with no alternative role to exit into. Tell me which defeaters a committed role ethicist would concede as damaging and which they would wave away, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and whether the view's talk of an aesthetic rather than logical order has quietly insulated it from refutation.
