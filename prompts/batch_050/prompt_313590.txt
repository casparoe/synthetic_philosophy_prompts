I have come to accept a view about moral compromise: when people with genuinely different value systems negotiate, compromise can generate something like economic gains from trade. Each side concedes on the dimension it values less in exchange for movement on the dimension it values more, so that both end up better off by their own lights than they would under either pure outcome or an arbitrary split. On this view, compromise is not capitulation but a way of creating value out of moral disagreement itself, and a society that bargains well across value systems is morally better off, not merely more peaceful. What would count against it? I want the strongest potential defeaters: cases where apparent gains from trade dissolve, for instance where the compromise position turns out to be dominated for both parties by their own evaluative standards; the argument that modelling values as tradeable preferences misdescribes what values are, since values unlike preferences arguably demand rather than weigh; and integrity-based objections holding that conceding on certain matters is wrong whatever is gained in return. Distinguish the defeaters a defender of the view should concede as fatal from those that are merely awkward, note which objections the defender would dismiss as misunderstanding the trading model, and assess whether common qualifications, such as restricting legitimate trade to non-fundamental values, insulate the view from refutation altogether -- and if so, whether that matters for a thesis of this kind.
