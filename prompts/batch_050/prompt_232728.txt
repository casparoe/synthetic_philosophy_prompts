You are a political philosopher specializing in the moral limits of markets, with a reputation for careful reflective equilibrium: extracting precise principles from messy case intuitions and saying plainly when the two cannot be reconciled. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming several candidate principles before committing to one. Do not rush to a single answer; I want to see the live options and why most of them fail.

I sit on a citizens' commission that advises my state legislature on which transactions should be permitted, and I want to know what my own gut reactions actually commit me to. Here are my verdicts on nine cases, as honest as I can make them, including the ones I can't defend well:

1. Kidney sale. A healthy, debt-free, fully informed adult sells a kidney to a stranger for $30,000. Not fine; this should stay illegal.
2. Low-risk trial. A hospital pays me $200 to participate in a vaccine trial carrying a small risk of a week of fever. Fine.
3. High-risk trial. A company pays healthy volunteers $8,000 for a Phase I drug trial with a disclosed risk of lasting side effects; predictably, mostly poor people sign up. Legal and permissible, though the fact about who signs up bothers me in a way I can't articulate.
4. Local gouging. After a hurricane knocks out power, the town's only hardware store triples the price of its generators. Not fine; should be illegal.
5. Traveling seller. An entrepreneur from out of state rents a truck, drives 400 miles with generators bought elsewhere, and sells them in the disaster zone at exactly the tripled price from case 4, to the same desperate buyers. Fine — or at least clearly more acceptable than case 4. I cannot fully justify the difference.
6. Paid turnout. A civic group pays me $50 to vote in a local election I was planning to skip; nobody cares how I vote. Slightly off, but basically fine.
7. Vote buying. Someone pays me $50 to vote for their candidate. Absolutely not fine.
8. Organ futures. A nonprofit offers me $4,000 today for a binding commitment that, should I die young, my kidneys go to transplant patients rather than being buried with me. Notably more acceptable than case 1, even though it is still money for my organs.
9. Two offsets. Paying for carbon offsets so I can fly: fine. Donating $500 to charity instead of apologizing to a colleague I undermined, and calling it an offset: not fine, and framing it as an offset makes it worse.

Here is what I want from you:

First, brainstorm at least three candidate principles that could underlie these verdicts. Make them genuinely different from one another in what they locate as the wrong-maker: the nature of the good being sold, the situation of the seller, the effects on third parties, or what the transaction expresses. Test each against all nine cases.

Second, commit to the single best principle — or a principled conjunction of two clauses — stated precisely enough that I can apply it to new cases without you. Tie every clause to the specific case(s) that motivate it.

Third, tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate. I suspect the 4-versus-5 pair is the trouble spot, but it is possible that 6 versus 7, or 1 versus 3 and 8, is the deeper problem. Where there is a conflict, advise me whether I should abandon the verdict or complicate the principle, and what either move costs.

Fourth, apply your final principle to three cases I did not list and give a confident verdict on each: (a) allowing patients to pay to move up the kidney transplant waitlist; (b) commercial surrogacy contracts; (c) a city selling the naming rights to a public stadium, compared to the same city selling a wealthy resident the right to choose where the new highway goes.

Use clear headings, number your clauses, and where you are uncertain, flag the uncertainty rather than papering over it.
