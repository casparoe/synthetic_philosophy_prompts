In the preface to An Introduction to Comparative Psychology (1894), C. Lloyd Morgan approvingly reports a friend's skepticism about a then-popular claim regarding young turkeys:

"He especially adduced the instinctive knowledge of the cry of a hawk, as such, said to be shown by young turkeys in the absence of individual experience, as in his opinion an unwarrantable assumption."

The methodological principle Morgan is famous for -- roughly, that a behavior must not be explained by a higher psychological faculty if it can be explained by one lower on the psychological scale -- is usually treated as an ally of parsimony. But the two can come apart: "lower on the psychological scale" and "simpler overall" need not coincide, since a low-level associative explanation may require positing an elaborate, coincidentally specific learning history, while the higher-faculty explanation posits one general capacity. Construct a thought experiment that brings this tension out sharply. Specifications:

1. A realistic, presentable observation scenario (one paragraph): a plausible species, a plausible behavior, and no experimenter training regime that settles the interpretation in advance. The hawk-cry case may serve as inspiration but do not reuse it.
2. After the scenario, give two readings: the verdict of a strict application of Morgan's canon, and the verdict of a pure simplicity principle that counts total assumptions rather than ranking faculties on a scale. They must deliver opposite interpretations of the same behavior, and you must show that both readings are genuinely generated by the respective principles rather than stipulated.
3. Identify the single feature of your case that forces the two principles apart, and state what happens to the disagreement if that feature is removed.
4. Say how a defender of Morgan would best respond -- in particular, whether the canon survives if "lower" is read as a claim about evolutionary or developmental priority rather than about simplicity -- and whether, on that reading, the canon still deserves to be called a parsimony principle at all.
