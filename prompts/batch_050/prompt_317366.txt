Every few months the same argument breaks out in an online forum I follow. Someone posts a timeline of predictions, running from Malthus's essay over two centuries ago to the present, each warning that population or consumption was about to outrun some essential supply — food, then farmland, then oil, then water — and each followed by decades in which the catastrophe did not arrive on schedule. Three stock replies always appear, each built on an analogy, and I would like to know which, if any, survives scrutiny.

The first says the whole tradition is the boy who cried wolf: a warning shouted often enough, and wrong often enough, forfeits the right to be heeded, and the villagers were right to stop coming. The second says a Malthusian forecast is like a doctor telling a patient that on his current diet he will be dead by sixty: if the patient is frightened into changing his diet and lives to eighty, the doctor was not wrong — the warning did its work, and the non-arrival of the death is evidence of accuracy rather than error. The third, from a statistically minded friend, says both replies flatter the predictors: declare every year that collapse is coming eventually and you are a stopped clock, right twice a day by construction, since any famine that ever occurs will be claimed as vindication.

Here is what I would like. First, a correspondence table for each analogy — what plays the role of the wolf, the villagers, and the boy's earlier lies; of the diet, the prognosis, and the changed behavior; of the clock's face and its twice-daily correctness. Second, the points where each mapping breaks, with the breaks that matter for the conclusion each analogy is being used to license separated from the ones that are merely cosmetic. I suspect, for instance, that the wolf story concerns present-tense lies rather than forecasts about what happens if nothing changes; that the doctor story assumes both a known mechanism and that the warning caused the cure, neither of which may hold, since synthetic fertilizer and falling birth rates were not adopted out of fear of Malthus; and that the clock story assumes the prediction has no content, whereas these warnings come with mechanisms and rough dates. But I want these suspicions examined, not indulged.

Third, the question the analogies keep circling: what evidence, arriving over the next century, would count as settling that the theory is wrong, and how many repairs — the date postponed, the binding constraint relocated from land to oil to water to something else, the credit for the reprieve claimed by the warning itself — can a tradition absorb before it has made itself incapable of being wrong? The optimists should face the same demand: what would prove them wrong?

Finally: is "right but early" a coherent verdict at all? What would have to be true of the actual past — that the mechanism genuinely engaged and was checked by identifiable brakes, rather than never engaging at all — for that verdict to be earned rather than merely consoling, and could we ever possess evidence distinguishing the two? If all three analogies fail at the crucial joint, I would welcome a better one. I am not asking whether any particular environmental claim is true; I want to know how to grade a track record of long-horizon warnings.
