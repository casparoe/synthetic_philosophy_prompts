I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on the philosophy of history, currently in a unit on pseudohistorical claims — Atlantis, ancient astronaut theories, Fomenko-style compressed chronologies — and I've noticed a dialectical pattern in the literature defending these views that I cannot find a name for, and that doesn't quite match any of the fallacies or demarcation criteria covered in our readings.

The pattern goes like this. The proponent rarely argues directly for the alternative history. Instead, they assemble anomalies: the Piri Reis map and what it allegedly depicts, the tight-fitting stonework at certain Andean sites, medieval chronicles whose dates are hard to square. The orthodox account is then faulted for not fully explaining each item, and the unexplained residue is quietly counted as evidence for the specific alternative on offer — alien visitors, a lost seafaring civilization, centuries inserted by forgers. When a specialist does resolve one of the anomalies — the map's coastline turns out to match a distorted projection of familiar coasts; the stonework shows known tool marks and comes from identified quarries — the proponent does not treat the alternative as weakened. The anomaly is dropped without comment, a new one takes its place, and the evidential weight seems to transfer over intact, as if the case rested on "the anomalies" collectively rather than on any particular one of them. Meanwhile, the alternative hypothesis itself is never asked to explain anything in particular: it predicts no specific artifact and risks no specific failure.

Two features strike me as essential to the pattern. First, the asymmetry: the established account must explain every entry on an open-ended list to remain credible, while the challenger need explain nothing at all. Second, the serial replacement: the argument survives the resolution of any given anomaly because its force is located in whichever anomaly is currently unresolved, rather than in a stable body of evidence.

I recognize that this is adjacent to the argument from ignorance and to gap-based arguments in other domains, and our textbook discusses Popper's criterion and the demarcation problem generally. But the argument from ignorance, as I've seen it defined, is a single inference from "not yet explained" to "therefore my explanation"; it doesn't capture the way the inference migrates from anomaly to anomaly, or the double standard regarding what each side must explain. Nor does unfalsifiability quite fit, since individual anomalies can be and are resolved — it is the case as a whole that is unfalsifiable in practice.

So my question: is there an established name for this configuration — the migrating gap-argument with asymmetric explanatory demands — either in the demarcation literature or in informal logic? If there is, I'd like to know how it is standardly defined and whether the migration and asymmetry are parts of the definition or merely incidental features. If there is no established name, could you propose one along with a precise definition, and mark the boundary cases: how does this differ from ordinary appeals to unexplained data, which are surely sometimes legitimate (as when anomalies genuinely accumulate against a paradigm), and at what point does citing unresolved anomalies shade into the fallacious pattern I'm describing?
