Two researchers at my lab disagree about how to classify the false statements large language models produce. Dr. A argues that such outputs are best understood as bullshit in roughly Harry Frankfurt's sense: the model produces assertions geared toward sounding right rather than being right, is in some relevant sense indifferent to whether they are true, and this indifference — not mere factual falsity — is what matters. Dr. B argues that this classification is a category mistake: bullshit, like lying, is essentially a speech act performed by an agent with particular attitudes toward truth, and since a language model has no beliefs about what is true and no intentions about what its audience comes to believe, its false outputs are neither lies nor bullshit but errors — failures of a system that lacks the attitudes the deceptive categories presuppose. Each regards the other's term as misleading: A thinks calling hallucination mere error obscures what is distinctive about fluent, confident, ungrounded assertion, and B thinks calling it bullshit smuggles in a moralized psychology that the systems do not have.

Draft the joint statement their adversarial collaboration would produce if forced to write one. It should contain:

1. A numbered list of claims both researchers accept — about what Frankfurt's account of bullshit actually requires (indifference to truth rather than intent to deceive, contrasted with lying), about what is and is not known regarding LLM internals and training, and about which observable behaviors of LLMs are not in dispute. Keep these claims substantive rather than vacuous.

2. A disagreements section stating each remaining dispute in neutral wording that both would sign, phrased so that neither side's position is presupposed in the formulation (for example, do not state a disagreement in a way that already assumes LLMs do or do not have beliefs).

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one researcher. I suspect these include whether Frankfurt's account requires a minded speaker at all or can apply to products and processes; whether training objectives that reward plausibility or user satisfaction can ground a functional analogue of indifference to truth; and whether anything of practical or theoretical importance hangs on the classification, or whether the dispute is purely terminological. You may identify different or additional cruxes if you think I've mislocated them.

4. For each crux, a test, case, or consideration both researchers would accept as bearing on it — a hypothetical system, an empirical finding about training dynamics, a thought experiment about corporate speech or automated testimony, or the like — with each side's prediction or verdict recorded in advance, so that the document states what would count as each side being wrong.

The document should end with the single residual disagreement that would remain even if everything else were resolved, stated as precisely as possible.
