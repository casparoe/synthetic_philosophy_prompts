I've been following the alignment literature since the early LessWrong days, and a tension in standard practice has been nagging at me. The dominant technique for making large language models helpful is reinforcement learning from human feedback: contractors compare candidate responses, the model is trained to produce outputs that a learnt reward model -- itself a distillation of those human judgements -- scores highly, and the result is deployed as an assistant. Whatever its practical merits, the whole pipeline is organised around *anticipated approval*: the system is optimised, in effect, to do things evaluators would rate well. Yet the same safety community that builds this pipeline also warns constantly about goal-directed systems that pursue objectives their operators didn't intend -- mesa-optimisers, deceptive alignment, specification gaming. Paul Christiano's old distinction between approval-directed agents (which choose actions because those actions will be approved of) and goal-directed agents (which choose actions because of their consequences in the world) seems to me to be doing quiet load-bearing work here, and I can't tell whether current training practice has actually chosen a side.

So here is my question: what does the RLHF pipeline, as actually practised, *presuppose* about the relationship between approval and good outcomes? Reconstruct the implicit theory. Does it presuppose that human approval tracks real-world value closely enough that optimising the one is optimising the other -- and if so, under what conditions on the evaluators (their knowledge, their time per judgement, their immunity to flattery and confident nonsense) does that hold? Or does it presuppose something weaker and more interesting: that evaluators can *recognise* good behaviour more reliably than they can *specify* it, so that approval is an epistemic instrument rather than the thing of value itself? I'd like you to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with -- presumably several theories of what feedback *is* would license the same pipeline -- and to say where it is philosophically underdetermined.

Then take up the apparent inconsistency: a lab that trains purely on approval but also publishes a behavioural constitution, fine-tunes against sycophancy, and worries about deception seems to be acting on the view that its models might be goal-directed in ways approval can't reach. Is that combination coherent, or does each half of the practice quietly rule out the presuppositions of the other? Finally, tell me what would have to change -- in the training loop, in evaluation, in deployment -- if we abandoned the approval-as-proxy assumption and tried instead to train straightforwardly goal-directed systems toward outcomes we could verify independently of anyone's judgement. Please write your answer in British English.
