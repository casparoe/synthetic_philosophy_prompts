My office mate in the phil department has spent the whole term informally ruling on principles about indicative conditionals, and next week it's my turn to present in our reading group, so I want to predict where she'll land. Her track record so far:

- For plain conditionals with no conditionals inside them, she holds that the probability of "if A then C" just is P(C | A). She's unmovable on this one.
- Modus ponens: fine. If the premises are near certain, the conclusion is near certain.
- Contraposition: rejected. Her example was that "if it rains, it won't rain hard" can be near certain while "if it rains hard, it won't rain" is near certain to be false.
- Transitivity and strengthening the antecedent: both rejected, with counterexamples in the same spirit.
- She's said more than once that what she'd really love is a single connective that obeys the probability equation with no restrictions at all -- you can negate it, conjoin it, embed it inside other conditionals, and the equation still holds for every probability distribution you feed it.

Next week's case is this one: "if the machine is switched on, then if the red light comes on, the alarm will sound." So: what does she say, given her track record? Also: can she keep her dream connective, or do her existing verdicts already rule it out?
