The problem of old evidence has now been running for over four decades: theories confirmed by data already known before their formulation (the precession of Mercury's perihelion and general relativity being the stock case) plainly seem to count as evidence, yet the standard Bayesian machinery assigns old evidence probability one, which threatens to make confirmation impossible. The responses are by now well rehearsed — counterfactual degrees of belief, treating the discovery of the logical relation between theory and data as the new evidence, relaxing logical omniscience — but positions have hardened rather than converged, and each fix seems to generate its own counterexamples. My working hypothesis is that this is not a technical problem awaiting a patch but a stand-off between two conceptions of what confirmation theory is for: a synchronic logic of ideal degrees of belief versus a theory of how historically situated scientists actually acquire reasons. Is that diagnosis right, or is there instead a hidden formal or factual question (about the correct representation of learning, or about which cases are genuinely like the Mercury case) doing the real work? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a new formalism, a re-description of the examples, a distinction that dissolves the impasse, or an argument that the two camps are answering different questions under one name? Reply as exactly five numbered items of one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion.
