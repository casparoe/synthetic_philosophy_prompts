I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy, but a colleague and I started an informal reading group after a long argument about responsibility at work -- specifically about whether we, as rank-and-file employees, bear any blame for a product we both knew was doing harm, even though neither of us decided anything and neither of us broke any rules. That argument led us to Karl Jaspers's The Question of German Guilt, where he distinguishes four kinds of guilt: criminal guilt, which attaches to individuals who violated definite laws and is decided by courts; political guilt, which falls on all citizens of a state simply in virtue of belonging to it and being liable for what it does; moral guilt, which each person must settle with their own conscience; and metaphysical guilt, which arises from failing the solidarity we owe other human beings as such, even where no law or command was breached. We found the taxonomy genuinely illuminating, but we kept running into situations it seemed not to cover cleanly -- guilt for harms one's generation inherited rather than caused, guilt that attaches to a profession or a technical community rather than a state, guilt for what one helped build without ever touching the harmful part.

Here is what I'd like from you: propose a fifth kind of guilt that is not on Jaspers's list and is not merely a rewording, special case, or combination of his four. Test it honestly against them -- if your candidate turns out to be political guilt or metaphysical guilt wearing a new coat, say so and scrap it, and try again. It may help to anchor it in a concrete modern case, such as inherited responsibility for a nation's past wrongs or shared responsibility among engineers for a system's cumulative harms, but the category itself should be general, not tied to one example.

Please structure your answer in exactly four sections, in this order. First, "The proposed fifth kind": name the category and define it in two or three sentences (roughly 75 words). Second, "Why Jaspers's four don't cover it": work through each of the four in turn and explain why the new category isn't reducible to it (roughly 200 words). Third, "The category at work": apply it to one concrete case and show what it lets us say that the original taxonomy cannot (roughly 125 words). Fourth, "Honest verdict": say plainly whether you think the category survives scrutiny or collapses back into Jaspers's scheme, and what, if anything, the exercise shows about the limits of his taxonomy (roughly 100 words).
