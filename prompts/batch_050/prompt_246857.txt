I'm a software engineer with no formal training in economics or philosophy (I build distributed systems for a living), and next month I'm giving a 40-minute talk to my company's internal "decision quality" guild defending a thesis I expect to be unpopular in this room:

Thesis: when an individual makes repeated decisions under multiplicative uncertainty — concentrated equity compensation, a retirement portfolio, a startup bet, career moves with compounding returns — the right optimization target is the time-average growth rate of their single trajectory, not the expected value over possible outcomes, and this claim does not reduce to the textbook advice of "just use logarithmic utility." My planned argument has four parts: (1) expected value is an ensemble average, and it is the right guide only when many independent copies of a decision get pooled together; (2) an individual lives exactly one trajectory, and for multiplicative dynamics with non-ergodic payoffs the time-average growth rate systematically diverges from the ensemble average; (3) absorbing states change everything — once ruin is possible (blowing up an account, a career-derailing failure), a gamble can have positive expected value while the typical trajectory goes to zero; (4) I want to extend this to engineering practice, because I think the same mistake shows up when we reason about retry storms, error budgets, and startup runway as if the ensemble average were what we actually experience. I'll be explicit that I'm not claiming economists are fools — I'm claiming that EV reasoning as practiced by engineers and poker-adjacent decision culture systematically ignores the ensemble/time distinction.

The audience, roughly 35 people: mostly senior engineers and data scientists who treat expected value as close to the definition of rational choice and know it from poker and A/B testing; three ex-quants who will know the Kelly criterion and geometric Brownian motion cold; one postdoc-turned-engineer who has actually read the recent ergodicity-economics literature (Peters and collaborators) and, importantly, its critics; and a couple of product managers who will ask the naive questions that turn out to be the hard ones. Nobody with economics training beyond the quants.

What I need from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not another defense of the thesis:

Be concrete with the math where it matters.
