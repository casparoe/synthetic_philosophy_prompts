I’ve followed rationalist discussions of AI debate, amplification, and obfuscated misalignment for years, and I want a reading-group session that moves beyond the familiar exchange in which one side says cross-examination will expose bad arguments and the other says a sufficiently capable debater can make the bad side look better. Design a classroom activity for 18 people, most of whom know basic Bayes and game theory but have no special expertise in formal debate or AI safety. We have 90 minutes.

The activity should make participants feel a specific failure mode: a conscientious, bounded judge can become more confident in the wrong verdict as an apparently transparent debate proceeds, because the judge can only inspect a small portion of the argument graph. Obfuscation here should not mean lying, making blatant fallacies, or merely talking confidently. A debater should be able to introduce claims that are individually true and locally checkable while strategically directing the judge’s limited attention away from the small number of connections on which the verdict actually depends.

Build the game around a distinction participants should discover through play rather than hear defined in advance: locally auditable support versus globally load-bearing structure. A claim has locally auditable support when its immediate justification can be checked in a few moves. It is globally load-bearing when accepting or rejecting it changes the verdict only after tracing how it constrains distant parts of the case. The activity should contain at least one round in which every inspection the judge chooses is reasonable, every answer is honest, the judge’s confidence justifiably rises after each inspection, and the judge nevertheless ends up endorsing the worse conclusion.

Please provide:

- A complete, playable setup rather than a general template: roles, team sizes, materials, information asymmetries, and one fully specified scenario or argument structure that can be prepared on cards.
- Rules for the two advocates that permit selective presentation, reframing, and the addition of true complications, but forbid fabricated evidence and direct lies.
- A concrete judge protocol, including how many nodes, cards, questions, or minutes of testimony the judge may inspect; when intermediate confidence ratings are recorded; and how the final verdict is made.
- A scoring scheme with small stakes that rewards judges for calibrated verdicts while rewarding advocates for winning under the attention constraints. Avoid real money.
- A sequence of at least three rounds or variants that progressively isolates the intended phenomenon from simpler explanations such as charisma, lying, judge laziness, or ordinary one-sided evidence. At least one variant should let the judge request arbitrary clarifications or cross-examination and still fail.
- A minute-by-minute timeline, including what the facilitator says beforehand. Do not begin with a lecture, and do not let participants hear the terms obfuscation, locally auditable, or globally load-bearing until the debrief.
- A debrief plan that maps particular moments in the game onto the promise and limits of AI safety via debate: bounded verification, adversarial attention-routing, the difference between refuting a claim and understanding its role in a larger argument, and the possibility that greater apparent transparency increases false confidence.
- Instructions for handling what will predictably go wrong the first time: advocates producing incomprehensible sprawl, judges auditing randomly, teams treating the exercise as permission to bullshit, or participants concluding that debate is simply hopeless.
- A frank account of what the activity cannot reproduce. In particular, distinguish deliberate human obfuscation from obfuscation that might emerge through optimization, and say what limitations follow from using ordinary humans as both debaters and judges.

The game should be intellectually serious enough for an experienced rationalist crowd while remaining playable by people who have not read the AI-debate literature. Please do not include readings, citations, or historical background unless they are needed to run the activity.
