I'm a high school student, and my family is stuck in an argument I can't resolve. I'd like advice that goes deeper than "both sides have a point."

Eight years ago, after watching her own mother spend her final years confused and bedridden in a nursing home, my grandmother — at the time completely sharp — signed what she called her living will, with a lawyer as witness. It stated that if she ever developed dementia and could no longer recognize her own children, she did not want any treatment aimed at prolonging her life, explicitly including antibiotics for infections. She talked about it constantly back then. She made each of us promise we'd honor it, and she said the whole point of writing it down was that she wouldn't be able to speak for herself later.

Three years ago she was diagnosed with Alzheimer's. She no longer recognizes any of us. But honestly, she seems content. She lives in a memory-care facility, she paints, she loves when the music therapist visits, she laughs at things we don't understand. She couldn't hold a conversation about her own condition, but by every outward sign she isn't suffering.

Last week she developed pneumonia. Her doctors say a routine course of antibiotics would very likely clear it. Now my uncle says the document governs: she made this decision when she was fully competent, for exactly this situation, and refusing to honor it means her living will was worthless all along. He also argues that if we override hers, no one in our family can ever again trust that their own wishes will be respected once they can't defend them. My mother says the person sitting in that facility painting is the one who matters now; that she's plainly glad to be alive; that the woman who signed the document was picturing misery that never arrived; and that letting a curable infection kill a happy person because of a decade-old fear is grotesque.

What I want to know: Who is right, and why? Is my grandmother's earlier self the real authority over her life, or is her present self the only one whose interests count now? Does it matter that the future she dreaded turned out better than she predicted? Would the answer change if someone had asked her two years ago, in the early stages, whether she still stood by the document? And what does it even mean to respect a person when the person who made the decision and the person living with its consequences feel like two different people?

Please don't just tell me it's complicated. Give me the strongest version of each side, then a verdict with reasons.
