I teach a philosophy of history seminar, mixed third- and fourth-years, about 16 students, and I've got one 75-minute slot to spend on whether contrastive questions in history are well posed -- basically the "why Britain and not China?" version of the Great Divergence debate, the Needham question and all that. My students keep assuming that once you've got the outcome (Britain industrializes, China doesn't), the question of why automatically makes sense, and the only fight is over the answer (coal? institutions? culture? colonies?). I want them to feel, in their bones, that the question itself might be doing smuggling -- that asking "why A and not B?" presupposes a contrast class and a kind of explainable asymmetry that the actual causal story might not supply.

Design an in-class activity where they run into this themselves before I name it. Some kind of small-group simulation or game where outcomes diverge across groups and then the students get asked to explain "why group X and not group Y?" -- and where their confident explanations turn out to outrun what the setup actually supports. I'd love it if at least one group spontaneously invents a "culture of the winning team" style explanation, because that's exactly the historiographic move I want to catch.

Give me: the rules and materials (nothing I have to buy), how many rounds and how long each, what I tell them vs. withhold, the moment where I spring the contrastive question on them, and a debrief that maps what happend onto the Needham question and the well-posedness worry. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong (e.g. a group that refuses to explain because "it's just a game" -- how do I handle that?) and be honest about which feature of the real historical case a classroom sim can't reproduce.
