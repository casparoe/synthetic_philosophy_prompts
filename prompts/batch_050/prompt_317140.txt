I sit on the grants committee of a mid-sized private foundation (~$45M a year in grants, broad mandate of improving human welfare). Last cycle we nearly approved a grant whose entire case rested on a staff calculation: maybe a 1-in-300,000 chance the project helps avert a catastrophe big enough that the expected value swamped everything else on our docket. The meeting got ugly. Half the committee thought the number was fiction wearing a suit; the other half thought refusing to act on it just because the probability is small is a bias against doing math.

Our chair has now drafted what she calls a "fanaticism clause" for the grants charter: no grant may be justified primarily by an expected-value argument in which the estimated probability of the outcome doing the decisive work in the calculation falls below 1 in 10,000, unless two genuinely independent estimation routes converge on a number above that line. Projects that fail the clause can still be funded, but only on ordinary grounds -- track record, near-term benefits, robustness across scenarios.

Write the memo I'll circulate before the vote. It needs a clear bottom line: adopt, reject, or adopt-with-amendments (and if amendments, the actual revised language). Some things I want it to wrestle with:

- Whether the clause regulates grants or merely justifications, and the loophole that creates: staff can just redescribe the same proposal so the tiny probability is no longer "decisive" in the writeup
- Which probability the threshold even attaches to -- the chance the project works, or the chance the outcome matters at all -- since these multiply and honest committee members will disagree about which one sits below the line
- The strongest case against the clause: any cutoff is arbitrary, and tiny-probability, huge-stakes work might be exactly where a private foundation has an edge over governments and markets, so we could be ruling out our best possible grants in advance
- Whether the "two independent routes" proviso actually fixes anything or just invites two correlated guesses dressed up as independent

Whichever way you come down, give the losing side its due rather than a strawman. And please don't hedge into a tie -- the committee needs an actual reccomendation.
