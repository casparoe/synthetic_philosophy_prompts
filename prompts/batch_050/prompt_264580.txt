Okay so I'm a novelist -- literary fiction, mostly, some short stories -- and I keep circling this idea for a book where the main character is a whole village. Not a narrator describing a village, but the village itself as a *subject*, with one unified stream of experience that's somehow made out of all the villagers' inner lives without any of them being aware of it. My writing group thinks this is a fun conceit, but while researching whether anyone's done it before I stumbled into the philosophy literature on panpsychism (the view that consciousness is everywhere, even in fundamental particles) and specifically this thing called the combination problem: even if tiny subjects of experience exist, how do a bunch of them combine into one bigger subject like me, without the little ones just sitting side by side doing their own thing?

Here's what I actually want. Restate the combination problem -- and the main moves people make in response to it, like denying that combination happens, or saying subjects just fuse, or the business about phenomenal bonding -- in the vocabulary I actually think in: POV characters, ensemble casts, omniscient narration, free indirect discourse, the chorus in a Greek tragedy, the moment a crowd scene stops being a bunch of individuals and becomes "the crowd" as a single felt presence, the way a family saga can make the family itself feel like one organism. I've been doing this professionally for fifteen years, so you can assume real fluency with that toolkit -- don't explain what free indirect discourse is.

Then, and this is the part I care about most, give me the residue report. Which parts of the combination problem map cleanly onto stuff novelists already wrestle with (e.g. I suspect the question "when does an ensemble become a single subject" is something I've literally faced at the level of craft)? Which parts only translate with distortion? And is there anything in the philosophical debate that simply has no counterpart in narrative technique -- and if so, is that because my vocabulary is missing something real (like, the novel's "combined subject" only exists in the reader's head and nobody actually has its experiences, which seems like it might matter a lot), or because the philosophers are asking a question that's less coherent than it sounds? I'd love a straight answer on whether my village novel, done right, would actually *solve* anything philosophically or just illustrate the problem prettily.
