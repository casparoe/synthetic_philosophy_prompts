ok so context: I'm drafting a grant proposal on what people call moral trade or moral compromise -- situations where two value systems manage to both do better by their own lights by cooperating or splitting the difference, instead of one side just winning. Could be two people (the animal welfare person and the global poverty person on a committee agreeing to fund some of each charity rather than fighting over the whole pot), or one person torn between two theories (I'm 60/40 between something utilitarian-ish and something deontology-ish and have to actually decide tonight). The literature I know mostly picks a favourite mechanism -- bargaining solutions, expected choiceworthiness, weighted coin flips, parliament-style proportional repsentation of the theories -- and defends it on two or three examples. I want the spec first, before any mechanism: what must ANY theory of gains-from-trade between value systems be able to do to count as adequate for practical decision-making under moral disagreement or moral uncertainty? (adequate for guiding decisions, I mean, not adequate as pure axiology -- fix the purpose as that)

Deliverable: the desiderata as a table with exactly these columns:

Things I suspect have to show up on the list somewhere -- tell me where I'm wrong or double-counting:
1. It has to distinguish a genuine Pareto improvement by both systems' lights from mere capitulation by one side, without assuming a neutral measuring stick between the two systems (the inter-theoretic comparison problem)
2. It has to either treat the within-person case (one agent uncertain between theories) and the between-person case (two people disagreeing) in a unified way, or explain convincingly why they're different problems
3. It has to say something about integrity constraints: the deontologist whose part of the deal involves doing something her theory forbids absolutely, not just disvalues a lot
4. It has to handle fanaticism -- one side cares astronomically more about the contested outcome -- without either letting that side win everything or artificially capping it, or else justify one of those
5. It has to handle asymmetric structure: what happens when one value system lacks the resources to even evaluate the other's outcomes (e.g. a view with no cardinal structure compromising with a utilitarian)
6. Something about stability: does the compromise have to survive re-deliberation, and should the outcome depend on the order in which issues get bundled into the deal?
