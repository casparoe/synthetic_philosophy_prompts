I'm a graduate student in philosophy of mind, and over the past year I've been keeping a running list of proposed answers to the question: what makes it the case that some computations are morally considerable — that there is, or could be, something it is like to be the system performing them — while others (say, the arithmetic in a pocket calculator, or the search over game trees in a chess engine) plausibly are not? I find the usual framing of "is AI conscious?" too coarse, so I've been collecting criteria instead: features whose presence in a computational process would give us at least some reason to extend moral concern to it. I now have what I take to be all the standard candidates, and I'm testing whether the space is exhausted.

Here is my list so far: (1) global availability — the computation's contents are broadcast to a wide range of downstream consumer systems (reasoning, report, action-planning) rather than remaining encapsulated; (2) self-modelling — the system maintains and uses a persistent model of itself as a unified entity located in an environment, and its processing is organized around that model; (3) valenced learning — the system learns from signals that function like reward and punishment, shaping its behavior in something like the way pleasure and suffering shape ours; (4) causal integration — the system's parts are so densely and reciprocally causally interconnected that it resists decomposition into independent modules; (5) functional similarity — the system reproduces, at some level of abstraction, the functional organization of brains we already regard as sentient; (6) embodiment and stakes — the computation is in the loop of maintaining the continued existence of something (a body, a homeostatic process) whose destruction would end the computation, giving the system something to lose. I've deliberately left off criteria that seem to me mere restatements of these (e.g., "having a point of view" I treat as a vague fusion of (1) and (2)).

What I'd like from you: propose one further criterion that belongs on this list and is genuinely distinct from all six — not a rewording, not a conjunction or special case of two of them, and not just a different name for the same underlying feature. It should be a criterion that someone could in principle check for in an artificial system without already knowing whether the system is conscious. Then tell me honestly how good you think your proposal is: what would count in its favor, what the most damaging objection to it is, and whether it marks a difference in moral status or merely a difference in our evidence about moral status. If, after reflection, you think the six already carve up the space and any seventh criterion collapses into one of them, say that instead and defend the claim.
