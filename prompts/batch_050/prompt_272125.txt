You are an expert in decision theory and the logic of rational choice. Think carefully through the dispute below before answering; you may want to begin by isolating each distinct claim made on each side, since the verdict depends on treating them separately.

Two friends of mine, Mara and Jonas, attended a charity fundraiser that ran a variant of the dollar auction: a $100 bill was auctioned off under the rule that both the highest and the second-highest bidder pay their bids, but all proceeds go to a charity both bidders genuinely support. Two attendees ended up in a bidding war, and the second-highest bid eventually reached $85. Mara and Jonas sharply disagreed about how to assess what happened, and I was asked to referee.

Mara's position: escalation past $100 is flatly irrational, and the trap is so predictable that the only defensible move is never to bid at all; the charity twist changes nothing about the bidders' incentives, because the benefit to the charity is a public good the bidders enjoy regardless of who pays. Jonas's position: once you're in, each incremental bid is locally rational — bidding $95 beats losing your $85 bid for nothing — so the mistake, if there is one, lies only in entering; and even entry can be rational when bids are low and the other bidder might fold. He added that the charity twist matters a great deal: since both bidders' payments go to a cause they value, money "lost" in the bidding war isn't fully lost, so escalation here is far less pathological than in the classic setup.

Mara replied that Jonas's step-by-step defense proves too much: the same reasoning justifies bidding at any level without limit, since at every point one more small bid beats forfeiting the current one; rationality must be assessed at the level of policies, and the policy "keep topping the other bid" is dominated by "stay out." She also argued that the charity point is a red herring, because anyone who wants the charity to have another dollar can simply donate one, without a bidding war. Jonas answered that policy-level reasoning smuggles in a power of commitment the bidders don't have — real agents face the auction one bid at a time, and declining a locally good move in deference to a policy you never actually adopted is just leaving money on the table; and on the charity point, he noted that under the both-pay rule, escalation uniquely multiplies the charity's revenue in a way plain donations wouldn't have motivated anyone to do.

Please adjudicate the dispute point by point: say who is right about (i) whether continuing to bid can be rational, (ii) whether the irrationality, if any, is located in entering rather than escalating, (iii) whether rationality should be assessed stepwise or at the level of policies, and (iv) whether the charity twist changes the analysis. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything important that both parties are missing.
