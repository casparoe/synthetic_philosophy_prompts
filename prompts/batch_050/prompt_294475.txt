I retired two years ago after thirty-five years as an experimental condensed matter physicist, and I have spent a good part of my retirement, somewhat to my wife's amusement, reading philosophy. My present fixation is the metaphysics of identity as it applies to quantum particles. For decades I told students that exchanging two electrons makes no observable difference and moved on without ever asking what that fact is a fact about; now, reading about Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles, haecceitism, and bundle theories, I find I cannot tell what I should be demanding of a philosophical account of particle identity before I am in a position to judge any of the contenders, whether that is the view that particles are robust individuals with a primitive thisness, that they are nothing over and above bundles of properties, that they are discernible only through irreflexive relations such as standing in the singlet state, or that quantum field theory dissolves the question because particles were never fundamental in the first place.

So here is what I want from you. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of the identity and individuality of quantum particles must meet: the phenomena it has to get right, the cases it must handle, the distinctions it may not collapse, with the physical or logical consideration behind each requirement. Among the cases I would want the list to cover are these. First, the counting of states: two bosons distributed over two states admit three arrangements, not the four a classical tally predicts, and I want a requirement that a theory explain this rather than merely stipulate it. Second, the exchange splitting in the helium atom, which shows that indistinguishability has measurable dynamical consequences and so cannot be waved away as a mere limitation of our knowledge. Third, a case of my own devising: two electrons prepared in identical spin states in separate traps, the traps then slowly merged into one and separated again with one electron in each; the theory must say whether there is any fact of the matter about whether the electron now in the left trap is the one that began there, and it must say so without smuggling classical trajectories back in. Fourth, the singlet state of two spin-half particles, in which neither particle possesses a definite spin of its own and yet the two stand in an irreflexive relation to one another; the theory must take a stand on whether such relations individuate. Fifth, the passage to quantum field theory, where particle number is not in general a well-defined or conserved notion at all. Distinguish for me which desiderata are non-negotiable because they are fixed by the physics, and which are the preferences of a particular metaphysical camp dressed up as requirements. Flag any subset of the desiderata that you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, since that would itself be the most interesting result, and tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories. Then, briefly, note how the main contenders fare against the finished list, though the list itself is what I care about, not the scorecard.

Please answer as a philosopher of physics who has thought long and carefully about these questions, and do the thinking before the writing: run through the full range of demands one might place on such a theory, test each against the cases above, and discard those that do not survive scrutiny before you compose your answer. I do not want a neutral survey of options from which I am left to choose; commit yourself. Take a definite position on which requirements a theory must meet, on whether the defensible requirements can all be satisfied at once, and, if they cannot, on which one we ought to give up and why. Finally, please write in continuous prose, as one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, however long the answer becomes.
