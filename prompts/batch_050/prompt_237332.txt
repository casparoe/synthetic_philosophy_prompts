I am an economist trained in game theory, fully fluent in the standard partitional model of information: states of the world, possibility correspondences, knowledge operators defined as "true in every state the agent cannot rule out," positive and negative introspection as built-in features, and common knowledge as the meet of partitions. Please restate, in exactly that vocabulary, the debate about luminosity and the KK principle in epistemology: the idea that for some conditions (feeling cold, being in pain, believing that p), whenever the condition obtains one is in a position to know that it obtains; the KK thesis that knowing implies being in a position to know that one knows; and the standard sorites-style argument, associated with Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity reasoning, that luminosity fails because knowledge requires a margin for error that gradually erodes across a series of near-indistinguishable cases. The puzzle I want explored is that in my framework KK seems to hold trivially -- knowledge defined by a partition automatically iterates -- so I suspect the epistemologists must be working with a different notion of knowledge altogether. Give me the residue report: which parts of the debate translate cleanly into partitions and possibility sets, which parts you had to distort or could only represent by weakening the framework itself (non-partitional structures, bounded awareness, probabilistic thresholds), and which parts -- I suspect the margin-for-error or "safety" condition is one -- have no expression in a vocabulary where knowledge is defined by what the agent cannot rule out rather than by how reliably her belief tracks the truth. Conclude by saying whether the residue shows a limitation of the partitional model or shows that the epistemological debate rests on a notion of knowledge that my framework was never trying to capture. Please write your answer as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
