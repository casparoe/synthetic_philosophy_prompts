A friend of mine is skeptical that gradual-replacement mind uploading could work — the procedure where biological neurons are swapped out, one at a time, for silicon components that replicate each neuron's input-output behavior, until the entire brain is synthetic. I built the following argument to convince her, but on reflection it appears to prove too much, and I would like to know exactly where.

(1) A person survives the replacement of any single neuron by a functionally identical silicon substitute. Losing or replacing one neuron makes no difference to anyone's experiences or personality — we lose neurons constantly without noticing, and a substitute with identical causal dispositions cannot matter where the original did not.

(2) Survival is preserved under iteration: if a person survives one change, and the person resulting from that change survives a further change of the same kind, then the original person survives the sequence of changes.

(3) From (1) and (2) by induction: a person survives the gradual replacement of their entire brain by functionally equivalent hardware, with their psychology and consciousness intact throughout. So gradual-replacement uploading works.

So far, so good. But now consider a structurally identical argument:

(1*) A person survives the removal of any single neuron. No one is killed by losing one neuron; brains lose them by the thousand without anyone dying.

(2*) The same iteration principle as (2): survival is preserved across any sequence of changes each of which a person survives.

(3*) From (1*) and (2*) by the same induction: a person survives the gradual removal of all of their neurons, one at a time, with nothing put in their place.

But (3*) is absurd. Having every neuron removed, however slowly, is being killed. So the parallel argument proves something false, and since it shares its entire logical skeleton with my original argument, one of the shared premises or steps must be at fault — which would sink the case for uploading as well. Yet I cannot see where the two arguments come apart. Each individual step of each argument looks locally impeccable: no single removal kills anyone, and no single replacement changes anyone.

Where does the reasoning go wrong, if it does? A satisfactory answer should do three things. First, identify the specific premise or inference that fails, and explain why it looked fine — what makes the failure invisible when each step is inspected in isolation. Second, say whether the diagnosis leaves the uploading argument standing while blocking the removal argument: is there a principled asymmetry between gradual replacement and gradual removal, and if so, does it do the work, or does it merely restate the conclusion I want? Third, price the repair: if I have to give up the unrestricted iteration principle in (2), or treat survival as a matter of degree, or insist that functional equivalence carries a load it cannot obviously bear, what exactly am I conceding? Of everything on the table, I would most like to keep (1), because the whole case for uploading seems to rest on it — but I would rather know the true cost than win the argument with my friend.
