I am a retired entomologist. After forty years in field taxonomy I drifted, in the way these things happen, into philosophy — first philosophy of science, then the psychology of concepts, which I picked up because it seemed to describe my own working life better than it describes anything else. I mention this because I now volunteer two days a week at a regional natural history museum, and my reading has collided with a practical problem I cannot think my way out of alone.

The museum is digitizing its backlog: roughly 30,000 unsorted pinned insects that must be assigned to morphospecies before imaging. The sorting is done by fifteen volunteers, none of them professional taxonomists. Each handles perhaps eighty specimens a session, so no decision can take more than two or three minutes. A collections manager signs off on any new morphospecies and corrects misfiled specimens afterward; each correction costs her about twenty minutes, and errors that slip through propagate into the database. Watching the volunteers, I see all three strategies the psychology literature describes: some match a specimen against the single closest reference specimen in the drawer (exemplar thinking), some against an averaged mental picture of the category (prototype thinking), and some reason from locality, host plant, and life history (theory-based thinking). Disagreements between volunteers using different strategies are common, and we have no principled way to settle them.

Write me a procedure: a decision sequence a volunteer can run at the bench, without any philosophy, that says which strategy to try first for which kind of case, what to do when two strategies deliver conflicting verdicts, when to stop and escalate to the collections manager, and when to consign a specimen to a provisional "unresolved" drawer rather than force a classification. It should be grounded in what the psychology of concepts has actually established about these three modes of categorization, translated into instructions usable by an intelligent layperson.

I have a conjecture of my own that I would like you to take seriously but not to rubber-stamp: the right strategy depends on the kind of unity the target category possesses. Where membership reflects a common cause — a shared lineage or developmental program — theory-based reasoning should override surface similarity; where the category is merely a stable cluster of co-occurring traits, similarity-based strategies should suffice. If you judge this conjecture wrong or in need of qualification, say so plainly and build the procedure on the better basis.

Then run the finished procedure on three cases: (1) a set of beetles visually indistinguishable, half collected on oak and half on willow, where one volunteer insists the host plant is decisive and another insists appearance is all we are licensed to use; (2) a specimen sitting visually between two established morphospecies, matching neither reference specimen well; (3) a specimen matching a reference specimen of a well-known species almost perfectly, but collected 800 kilometers outside that species' recorded range. Let the procedure conclude whatever it concludes, including, if that is where it leads, the verdict that one of these cases cannot be settled at the bench at all or that my conjecture sends us the wrong way. I would rather receive an answer I dislike than one bent to please me.

Two further requests. Where the state of the field bears on the procedure, distinguish what is settled from what remains genuinely contested — I gather, for instance, that whether these three strategies are distinct mechanisms or aspects of a single system is disputed — and do not present a contested position as the consensus; where the procedure must commit to one side of a live dispute, flag that it is doing so and why. And mark honestly the steps where judgment cannot be eliminated and a person must simply decide, rather than disguising those steps as procedure. Close with a candid account of what the procedure cannot do and where it will misfire.

I do not need citations or a bibliography; refer to positions and findings by description and I will track down the sources myself. Please write in American English.
