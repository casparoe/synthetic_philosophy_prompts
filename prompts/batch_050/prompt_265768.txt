I am a novelist revising a chapter in which two philosophers argue about the special composition question: under what conditions do several things compose a further thing? I need a diagnosis of their dispute, not a verdict about which character should win.

One philosopher defends unrestricted composition: whenever there are some things, however scattered or unrelated, there is also something they compose. She argues that every restriction either draws an arbitrary boundary or makes composition vague in an objectionable way. The other philosopher restricts composition to things whose activities jointly sustain a continuing, self-maintaining causal process. He accepts organisms and perhaps some highly integrated machines, but denies that scattered artifacts, disconnected collections, or arbitrarily selected pluralities form further objects.

Their immediate dispute concerns an artwork in the novel. Twelve bronze plates are kept in twelve different cities. They never physically interact, but each bears part of an inscription, and a consortium of curators preserves, studies, and occasionally relocates them as a single collection called the Meridian. The unrestricted composer says that the twelve plates compose a scattered object whether or not anyone calls it an artwork; the curators merely draw attention to one among countless fusions. Her opponent says that there are the plates, the curators, their records, and their coordinated practices, but no further object named the Meridian. Treating institutional attention as sufficient for composition, he argues, confuses a collective description with an additional entity.

The two agree about the plates’ locations, masses, causal histories, inscriptions, and the curators’ intentions and practices. They apparently disagree only about whether something further exists. Their debate later extends to a fictional colony of living cells whose members remain isolated and inactive for decades before responding to a chemical signal, reuniting, and forming a single organism. That case makes the second philosopher’s reliance on one continuing process less straightforward, but it does not obviously vindicate unrestricted composition either.

My working hypothesis is that the dispute has stalled because the characters mean different things by there is: one uses existential claims permissively, while the other takes them to mark real additions to the world’s basic inventory. Assess that hypothesis. Is their disagreement substantive, verbal, or a disagreement about whether facts of composition require nonmereological grounds? Distinguish carefully between disagreeing about which objects exist and disagreeing about which collective descriptions carry ontological commitment. Finally, explain what would count as progress: an independently motivated principle of composition, a formal argument about vagueness or parthood, a translation between the two positions, or a reason to abandon the question. If no empirical discovery could settle the dispute, does that make it non-substantive?
