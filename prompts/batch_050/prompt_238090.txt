I'm reading a popular book on moral skepticism that devotes a chapter to the ring of Gyges story in Book II of Plato's Republic. Here is its summary of the passage:

"Glaucon tells Socrates that justice is a social contract invented by the weak to protect themselves: in Plato's words, 'it is a mean or compromise, between the best of all, which is to do injustice and not be punished, and the worst of all, which is to suffer injustice without the power of retaliation.' Glaucon's point, driven home by the tale of a shepherd who finds a ring of invisibility and proceeds to seduce the queen, murder the king, and seize the throne, is that nobody is just willingly -- we behave decently only because we fear punishment, and anyone with the ring would do the same. Plato's own view, voiced through Socrates, is that this cynical account is correct about human psychology but that crime nonetheless does not pay, since the unjust man ends up miserable, so that in the long run justice is the more profitable policy."

I have only a passing acquaintance with the Republic and can't tell how much of this to trust. The quoted sentence is real, but I suspect the framing around it distorts what Glaucon is actually doing in the dialogue. Could you audit the summary: which parts a careful reader of Book II would accept, which parts misrepresent Glaucon's speech or Plato's project, and whether the book's closing claim -- that the Republic answers the challenge by showing justice pays better in the long run -- follows from anything in the accurate remainder?
