I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a long-form piece about the concept of truth for a general but intellectually curious readership. I've hit a technical dispute in the philosophy of language that I need to get right, and I'd like your help working through it.

Deflationary theories of truth hold that the truth predicate adds no substantive content: calling a sentence true is roughly equivalent to simply asserting the sentence, and truth is best understood as a logical device that lets us agree with claims, generalize over them, or endorse statements we cannot or do not wish to restate one by one. In my reporting I've encountered a cluster of objections, sometimes called the generalization problem, targeting this picture. Consider statements like "Everything the witness said under oath is true" or "Most of what the theory predicts is true," where the speaker cannot list the statements in question and may not even know what they are.

Please explain the following, in terms a careful non-specialist could follow:

1. Why such generalizations put pressure on the deflationary account. Specifically, explain why the two standard deflationary maneuvers run into trouble: (a) treating the truth predicate as shorthand for an infinite conjunction or disjunction of the relevant statements, and (b) analyzing the generalization using substitutional quantification, so that "Everything the witness said is true" comes out as roughly "for every way of filling in a sentence, if the witness said that, then that."

2. The specific difficulty that arises for the substitutional-quantification strategy when some of the relevant statements contain terms that fail to refer to anything — and why it matters whether the truth of the quantified claim should depend on whether those substitution instances are themselves true, false, or neither.

3. What this objection is and is not supposed to show. Does the generalization problem, if it succeeds, establish that truth must be a substantive property with explanatory work to do, or is there a plausible deflationary reply available? Where do you think the burden of proof lies, and why?

Keep the answer conceptual rather than bibliographic — I need the structure of the ideas, not a reading list.
