So I've been rewatching The Grand Budapest Hotel and I keep thinking about it alongside the nationalism stuff I read in college -- Benedict Anderson on nations as "imagined communities" and Hobsbawm on invented traditions, countries basically cranking out ceremonies, anthems, and folk costumes in the late 1800s to make themselves feel ancient. Here's the thing that's bugging me: Zubrowka, the country the movie is set in, literally does not exist. It's not an imagined community in Anderson's sense, it's an imaginary one. And yet the whole movie runs on nostalgia for it -- the hotel with its rituals and uniforms and codes, M. Gustave's whole way of life, Zero grieving it decades later.

So here's what I want: read the film through Anderson and Hobsbawm seriously. Does the movie basically prove their point -- that all national nostalgia works like this, that "real" nations are also built out of invented ceremony and print and storytelling, and Zubrowka just makes the construction visible because there's nothing underneath? Or does the film actually break their framework, since Anderson is pretty careful that "imagined" doesn't mean fake or fabricated -- communities are imagined but the attachment is real, and he's explicitly not saying nations are lies? Where does Gustave's famous line about the hotel being the last shred of civilization fit -- is he defending a real thing, or performing a tradition he knows is theater?

I also want you to be honest about where the reading strains. Like, Anderson puts a lot of weight on print capitalism and the novel/newspaper making simultaneity possible, and the movie is obsessed with books and narration and writers, but does it care about simultaneity at all, or is its imagined community held together by something else (service? taste? grief?)? And if a nation that never existed can produce real mourning, what does that do to Hobsbawm's distinction between invented traditions and genuine custom -- does the film suggest the distinction was never doing much work, or does it accidentally show why it matters?

Don't just use the movie as a prop for the theory or vice versa. If the film dramatizes something Anderson and Hobsbawm miss about how inventedness and sincerity can coexist, say what that is.
