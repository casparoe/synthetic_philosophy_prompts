I'm in a reading group on early modern philosophy and we got properly stuck on Berkeley's master argument in the Three Dialogues Between Hylas and Philonous -- the bit where Philonous challenges Hylas to conceive of something existing unconceived, and Hylas ties himself in knots because whatever he thinks of is, of course, being thought of by him. One of my friends, who is definately too pleased with himself, has spent the week insisting that this argument proves far more than Berkeley wants, and I can't find where he cheats. His version goes like this. (1) To conceive of an object existing unconceived, you must conceive of that object. (2) Anything you conceive of is, whilst you conceive of it, conceived by you. (3) So the supposedly unconceived object is conceived, and the idea of an unconceived object is a contradiction. (4) Therefore nothing can be so much as thought of as mind-independent, which is what Berkeley needs for esse est percipi. (5) But now run the same machinery on me rather than on matter: to conceive of an object existing unperceived by me, I must represent it to myself, so nothing can be thought of as existing unperceived by me, which is just solipsism. (6) Worse, to conceive of another mind entertaining ideas I don't have, I must in some sense have those very ideas before my mind, so there can't be other minds either -- including God, so Berkeley can't use the divine mind to keep the furniture of the world going when nobody's looking. (7) Berkeley plainly isn't a solipsist and thinks other spirits are amongst the easiest things to establish. (8) So somewhere between (1) and (6) the master argument either goes wrong or eats its own author.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the answer is that conceiving and perceiving come apart, or that there's a slide between the act of thinking and the object thought about, I'd like that spelled out slowly, because my friend insists any such distinction is one Berkeley himself isn't entitled to once he's reduced objects to ideas. And whatever fix you propose, can you say whether Berkeley can still use the master argument against mind-independent matter afterwards, or whether the fix defangs it -- i.e. is my friend right that the argument either proves solipsism or proves nothing? If the cleanest rescue means giving up something Berkeley says elsewhere in the Dialogues, tell me which bit has to go.

Please answer in American English. I'd also like you to be explicit about which parts of your diagnosis are settled amongst people who work on Berkeley and which are genuinely contested -- for instance, whether the master argument is even load-bearing for his idealism or just a rhetorical flourish, and whether the standard objection I've rehearsed actually lands -- rather than presenting one scholarly reading as if it were the consensus view.
