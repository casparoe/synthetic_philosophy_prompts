I teach a required technology-and-society module in an engineering school, and next term I have a single ninety-minute session with second-year mechanical and civil engineering students to introduce the claim associated with Langdon Winner's 1980 essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" -- roughly, that technical artifacts and systems can themselves embody political properties, rather than being neutral instruments whose only moral relevance lies in how people choose to use them. My students will arrive holding the neutrality view almost as a professional creed, and I don't want a lecture that argues at them; I want an explanation whose pieces fall into place in the right order. Please don't give me the explanation itself. Give me the plan for it: which component ideas must be established before the thesis is even intelligible to this audience (I'm guessing they first need the distinction between an artifact's intended use and its settled patterns of effect, but tell me if something else comes earlier), and why that ordering is forced rather than merely convenient.

I also need you to settle my choice of carrying example. The obvious candidate is the one Winner himself made famous -- the low-clearance parkway overpasses on Long Island, allegedly designed so buses could not reach the beaches -- but I suspect its reliance on a possibly bigoted designer's intent makes it pedagogically dangerous, since it invites the class to hear the whole thesis as a claim about villains. The alternative I've considered is an agricultural mechanization case, where the political consequences seem to flow from the machinery's requirements rather than from anyone's scheme. Tell me which example should carry the session and exactly what it must have and lack to do the job, and if neither is right, propose one. Then give me the misconception map: the misreadings students like mine predictably form (I expect at least three -- that the thesis says artifacts have intentions, that any unequal impact automatically counts as politics, and that accepting it commits you to a strong technological determinism in which design choices don't matter), where in the sequence each of these will first take root, and the specific point in the plan where each should be headed off rather than rebutted after the fact.

Finally, tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so out loud -- I assume the scholarly dispute over whether the bridge story is even historically accurate belongs in that category, but I want a principled reason, not just a time-saving excuse -- and give me one check for understanding I can run in the last ten minutes: a question or small task whose answer would show that a student has grasped the thesis as a claim about the structure of artifacts rather than as the slogan that technology isn't neutral, which they'll all be able to repeat whether or not they've understood anything.
