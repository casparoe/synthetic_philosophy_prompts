Some friends and I got into an argument over dinner about whether voting is a duty, and I realized we were mostly trading intuitions about cases rather than defending principles. So here are my gut verdicts, and I want to know what principle, if any, underlies them. First: someone who stays home from a close, competitive national election in a swing district because they'd rather watch television has, I think, done something mildly wrong. Second: someone who stays home from the same election in a district where the outcome is a foregone conclusion has done nothing wrong at all -- at most they've missed something worthwhile, the way one might miss a good concert. Third: someone who votes for a third-party candidate they genuinely believe in, in a district where the outcome is settled, is doing something perfectly fine, even admirable. Fourth: someone who casts that same third-party vote in a razor-thin district, fully aware it may tip the result toward the candidate they regard as clearly the worse of the two viable options, is blameworthy -- though I admit I'd judge them less harshly than the television-watcher. Fifth: of two people who abstain from the same close election, the one who abstains as a considered protest against both candidates strikes me as less blameworthy than the one who abstains out of sheer indifference, even though the causal footprint of their abstentions is identical. Sixth: a thoroughly uninformed voter who shows up and votes essentially at random seems to me to act worse than if they'd stayed home; ignorance doesn't excuse the act, it condemns it. Seventh: the obligation feels strongest of all in a small local election -- say, a school board race that was decided by eleven votes last cycle -- even though the stakes are objectively smaller.

I can already see two tensions in my own list, and I'd like them handled honestly rather than papered over. My first and second verdicts seem to demand that blameworthiness tracks the expected marginal impact of one's vote, which varies with the competitiveness of the race. But my fifth verdict assigns different blame to two abstentions with identical expected impact, which means something besides consequences -- intention, or what the abstention expresses, or the quality of the agent's deliberation -- is doing work. Meanwhile my sixth verdict introduces a third dimension, since the random voter in a close race might have just as much expected impact as the informed voter, only randomly directed, and yet I condemn the one and not the other. So: is there a single principle, or a small set of jointly sufficient principles, that delivers all seven verdicts? If the candidates on offer each have to sacrifice one of my verdicts, tell me which verdict is the cheapest to give up and why -- my suspicion is that the fifth one is the most expendable, but I'm not confident. And once you have your best principle in hand, please tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't sorted out: a referendum on a highly technical policy question where the asker knows most voters, including themselves, cannot competently evaluate the options, and a runoff election in which the asker regards both candidates as genuinely awful but one as marginally worse -- is voting for the lesser evil there obligatory, permissible, or an impermissible complicity?
