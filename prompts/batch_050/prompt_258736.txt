I've been drafting a short paper defending an algorithmic account of what it is for an individual finite sequence to be random, and before I commit I want to know whether the package of views I'm building on is jointly tenable. My commitments: (a) randomness can be a property of a sequence itself, not merely of the process that produced it -- a sequence can be random or not on its own merits, whatever its provenance; (b) an n-bit string is random just in case it is incompressible, i.e., no program substantially shorter than n bits outputs it; (c) whether a given string counts as random should not depend on which universal Turing machine we happen to fix as the reference machine, since that choice is arbitrary; (d) a string generated by a genuinely chancy quantum process could nonetheless fail to be random; (e) the first ten million digits of pi, considered simply as a digit string, are intuitively non-random -- they're the wrong kind of thing to call random, however well they perform on statistical test suites.

Two worries I'd like adjudicated. First, which of these are in real tension and which only appear to be? I suspect (a) and (d) look incompatible but aren't, and that the genuine problems are elsewhere. Second, for each real tension: how severe is it, and what is the cheapest revision that restores consistency? Please order the tensions by how much damage they do to the overall project of defining randomness for finite sequences, and if one single revision would repair most of the damage, identify it and state its cost honestly. I'm not attached to any particular commitment, so don't assume I'd rather drop one than another.
