I am a second-year PhD student working on nineteenth-century German philosophy, and next month I am presenting a chapter of my thesis to our department's Hegel reading group. I would like help preparing for the question period.

My chapter defends a heterodox reading of the master-slave dialectic in the Phenomenology of Spirit. The standard story I am attacking runs as follows: the slave, through formative labour, achieves a kind of selfhood and freedom unavailable to the master, because in shaping objects the slave finds his own mind mirrored back at him in durable form, whereas the master's recognition comes from a dependent consciousness and so is worthless. Against this, I argue three things. First, that the labour reading mistakes a necessary condition for a sufficient one: finding oneself in one's product presupposes that one already takes oneself to be a persisting agent, and the dialectic gives us no account of where that prior self-certainty comes from, since the fear of death is a one-off confrontation with negation that cannot be iterated into a stable self-conception. Second, that the transition to stoicism is therefore not a retreat from a half-achieved freedom but the dialectic's honest admission that work cannot deliver recognition at all, because recognition is an interpersonal achievement and labour is, at bottom, a relation between one consciousness and things. Third, that this is a feature rather than a flaw: Hegel's point is that no amount of individual self-cultivation substitutes for mutual recognition, which is why the dialectic must pass through the unhappy consciousness and beyond before recognition becomes possible between equals. So on my reading the chapter on lordship and bondage is deliberately a failure narrative, and commentators who treat the slave's labour as an embryonic freedom are reading a dead end as a destination.

The audience: about ten people. Two are committed to the labour-as-liberation reading and know the secondary literature on Bildung well; one of them thinks the fear of death is precisely what supplies the self-certainty I claim is missing, so she will target my first move directly. One is a broadly Kojève-influenced reader who cares about the struggle for recognition as the motor of history and will resist anything that de-centres the life-and-death struggle. Two are analytically trained philosophers of action who are polite but sceptical of the whole idiom and will push me on whether talk of consciousness finding itself in objects is even a coherent claim, let alone a false one. The rest are graduate students like me who will follow the strongest objection.

Please give me a preparation sheet: the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as these particular audience members would phrase them, in their vocabulary; for each, how damaging it is, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and a reply I can actually deliver in two or three minutes. I also want the objection I am privately hoping nobody raises, stated as sharply as my strongest critic would state it, and an honest note on the weakest point in my own position so that I can decide whether to defend it, hedge it, or quietly drop the third claim.
