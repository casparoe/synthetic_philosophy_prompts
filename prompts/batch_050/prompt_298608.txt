Ok so I got into a dumb argument with my brother over christmas that I've been chewing on since. He was visiting me here in the US (we grew up somewhere with no tipping culture) and he refused to tip at a diner, like flat out left nothing, and his defense was basically: conventions are just arbitrary coordination games, nobody actually owes anyone anything, it's like which side of the road you drive on. If everyone in this town drove on the left I'd drive on the left while I'm here, fine, but tipping isn't like that, it's just a tax on people too polite to defect.

The annoying thing is I found this old bit from Hume's Treatise of Human Nature (Book 3, the section on the origin of justice and property) that kind of supports the coordination reading. He's talking about how rules of justice get established without anyone ever signing a contract, and he says: "[L]anguages [are] gradually establish’d by human conventions without any explicit promise. In like manner do gold and silver become the common measures of exchange, and are esteem’d sufficient payment for what is of a hundred times their value." So the picture is: these things are made-up, nobody promised anything, but they only work because we all pile in, and piling in is what makes them valuable.

My brother's move is to run this in reverse: money and language are conventions where conforming costs you nothing and defecting gets you nothing (a language only you speak is useless), so the fact that they're conventional doesn't make them optional. Tipping, queueing, not jaywalking — same structure, so his refusal was just free-riding dressed up as philosophy. My gut says he's equivocating somewhere. With money the convention does the work FOR you; with tipping it seems like the convention is mostly a way of making me pay a stranger's wages. But I can't quite pin down whether that's a real disanalogy or just me not wanting to tip either.

Can you audit the analogy properly? Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the two sides of the road, the shared benefit, the "everyone piles in" bit — for tipping, queueing, and jaywalking separately, since I suspect they're actually three different structures wearing one coat. Then tell me where the money/language picture breaks for each one: where conforming is costless vs costly, whether defection is self-defeating or actually profitable, whether it matters that the waiter sees you and the drivers at the jaywalking intersection don't. And the real question: does Hume's story about why conventions are binding even when "made up" actually deliver the conclusion that a visitor owes the locals compliance, or was that conclusion being smuggled in by the analogy the whole time? If there's a better analogy for what tipping actually is, I'd take that too.
