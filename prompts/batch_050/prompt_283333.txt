Having spent forty years as an experimental physicist, I came to political philosophy late, and I keep noticing a difference in disciplinary habit. When two groups of physicists disagree, they sometimes run an adversarial collaboration: before any new measurement, both camps co-author a document stating what they agree on, precisely where they diverge, and which outcome would count against each side. I would like you to produce such a document for the dispute over whether taxation is theft.

The two parties: first, a libertarian in the entitlement-theory tradition, who holds that individuals own themselves and their legitimately acquired holdings, that there are pre-institutional moral entitlements to one's earnings, and that coercively seizing a portion of those earnings is morally on a par with forced labor. Second, a liberal egalitarian, who holds that property in external goods is defined by legal convention rather than prior to it, that no one has a moral entitlement to a specific pre-tax income, and that taxation is part of the background rules that make legitimate holdings possible at all. Restrict the discussion throughout to income taxation levied by a roughly just, democratically legitimate state; neither side's case should rest on kleptocracies or on a zero-tax night-watchman fantasy.

The document should contain the following, in this order:

1. A stipulation section fixing at least two senses of the word theft — a legal-institutional sense and a moralized sense (roughly: taking, by force and without consent, what its owner is morally entitled to) — and a statement of whether the slogan is trivially false, substantive, or differently substantive under each sense. If some part of the dispute dissolves once the senses are separated, say which part and why.

2. A numbered list of agreed claims: statements both parties can sign as written. These might concern, for example, what coercion does and does not by itself establish, the disanalogies between a tax on earnings and the literal conscription of labor hours, the state's role in constituting the currency and markets in which income is earned, and hypothetical tax rates both sides would agree are wrongful.

3. A disagreements table with one row per residual dispute. Columns: the disagreement stated neutrally; the libertarian's own position, worded so a libertarian would sign it; the egalitarian's own position, worded so an egalitarian would sign it; whether the row is a genuine crux (i.e., whether settling it would move at least one party); the thought experiment or consideration both accept as bearing on it; and each side's prediction about that case, recorded in advance.

4. A short closing statement from each party: the strongest version of the opposing view as that party understands it, and one sentence stating what would change its author's mind.

The formulations matter more than the verdict. A signed agreement built on vacuous claims, or disagreements phrased so that one side is strawmanned, would be a failed collaboration by the standards of my old field, and I will read it as one.
