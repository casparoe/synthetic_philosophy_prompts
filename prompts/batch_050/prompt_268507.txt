I run a small forecasting club with some friends from work -- we make predictions about elections, tech product launches, box office numbers, whatever, and track who's calibrated. So the question of what to do when you and someone you respect land on wildly different numbers is not abstract for me, it's like a weekly occurrence. Anyway I fell down an internet rabbit hole and found out philosophers have been arguing about exactly this for years, under names like peer disagreement, the equal weight view, and the steadfast view. Roughly: one camp says when a genuine epistemic peer disagrees with you, you should move substantially toward their position, and the other camp says you can rationally hold your ground if you did your own reasoning right. What struck me is that the debate seems totally stalled. Every argument I could find on either side seemed to have been around forever, with standard replies and counter-replies, and nobody conceding anything. As far as I can tell the field has been circling the same three or four moves for a decade or more.

I have a hypothesis about why, and I want you to stress-test it. My guess is that the two camps are secretly answering different questions under one label. The conciliationists sound like they're giving you a *policy* -- something like a rule for how to update over time when new social evidence comes in, the way my club has a house rule about revising forecasts. The steadfast people sound like they're making a point about what your total evidence *right now* supports, including your own first-order reasoning, which the other person can't just vote away. If that's right, the impasse isn't a substantive disagreement at all, it's two theses that can both be true talking past each other, and the whole debate is at least partly verbal. But maybe I'm wrong and there's a real disagreement underneath -- about what rationality even is, or about whether other people's conclusions are evidence in the same way data is, or about whether the notion of an "epistemic peer" is doing all the work and is basically stipulative. There's also this wrinkle I keep noticing: the two camps disagree about conciliationism itself, so if conciliationism is true it seems to tell its own defenders to become less confident in it, which the steadfast crowd treats as checkmate and the conciliationists treat as a technicality. I'd like to know whether that self-application problem is a genuine structural issue or just a gotcha.

So here's what I want from you: a diagnosis, not a survey. Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is -- verbal, or substantive, or two different questions sharing a name, or a clash of bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for -- using what each side actually says as your evidence. Then tell me, given your diagnosis, what would even count as progress here: a sharper distinction, a formal result from epistemology or statistics, an empirical finding about how disagreement actually affects accuracy (which I'd love, since it's testable in my club), or nothing at all. And be honest if you think the debate is stalled because it's hard rather than because it's confused, and tell me which side you think the self-application problem actually hurts. One format thing: please write your answer as continuous prose, like an email or a letter -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. I find I actually read arguments when they're written out that way.
