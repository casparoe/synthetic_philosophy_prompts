I TA on a big second-year epistemology course (about 120 students) and the lecturer has decided that essays lose points for committing what Wilfrid Sellars called the "myth of the given". Problem: the students haven't read Sellars, the grading load is heavy (40ish essays each, maybe 20 minutes per essay), and there are four of us grading who know Sellars to wildly different degress. One grader failed a paper last term for relying on "the given" when, as far as I can tell, it was just an ordinary appeal to perceptual evidence, so we clearly need to calibrate before the next batch comes in.

Can you turn the critique of the given into a grading procedure I can hand to the other TAs? Concretely:

- A set of tests, in order, that a grader can apply to a paragraph of a student essay and get a verdict: commits the myth / doesn't / borderline. The tests need to bite on the actual moves students make, e.g. "I can't be wrong about how things seem to me, so seemings are the foundation of knowledge", "all justification terminates in raw sense data", "pain is self-intimating so my beliefs about it are certain".
- It has to respect the distinctions that actually matter in Sellars -- like the difference between experience causing or prompting a belief (fine, presumably) and experience justifying a belief all by itself without any conceptual abilities on the subject's part (the myth) -- without requiring graders to have read Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind.
- Tell me which distinctions have to survive the simplification and which we can safely drop for undergrad grading. I suspect some of the machinery (the regress argument, the stuff about observation reports and how they get their authority) can't all fit in a one-page rubric, so I need to know what to keep.
- Work through two or three sample essay moves, including one where the procedure gives a verdict the grader (or student) will hate -- e.g. a case where the student's argument is genuinely bad but not for Sellarsian reasons, so we can't dock points on these grounds.
- Flag where the procedure will probabaly misfire: kinds of essays where it gives false positives (flagging something that's actually fine) or false negatives, and the spots where a grader just has to use judgment and no checklist will save them.

Also, please be honest about confidence here. What's the weakest link in the procedure -- where is it most likely to break down in practice? And what would change your mind: is there an argument or example that would show the checklist misreads Sellars, or that "the given" isn't actually a coherent thing to police in student essays at all? If the whole exercise rests on a contested reading of him, say so plainly rather than hiding it.

Please write the answer in British English.
