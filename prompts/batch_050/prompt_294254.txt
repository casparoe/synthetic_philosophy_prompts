In the posthumous Chapters on Socialism (1879), Mill treats the case against socialism as serious but contingent rather than principled. He presses three objections in particular: the incentive objection, that ordinary people will not exert themselves without differential reward; the population objection, that prudential restraint on family size will not survive communal provision for children; and the blueprint objection, that a fixed scheme adopted once and for all cannot accommodate the experimentation through which social arrangements actually improve. Yet he also denies that the existing competitive order enjoys any presumption of innocence — its distribution of property descends from conquest and violence, and its rewards bear little relation to exertion — and he frames the ultimate question as which system is consistent with the greatest amount of human liberty and spontaneity. In his Autobiography he had already declared that the social problem of the future was how to unite the greatest individual liberty of action with common ownership of the raw material of the globe and an equal participation of all in the benefits of combined labour.
