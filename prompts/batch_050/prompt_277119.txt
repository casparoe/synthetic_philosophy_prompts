So next month I'm presenting at our department's grad philosophy colloquium, and I'm defending the line that in Parfit's hitchhiker case the genuinely rational person is the one who is disposed to act irrationally: you pay the driver once you're safe in town even though at that exact moment paying is pointless for you, because having that disposition is the only thing that gets you rescued in the first place. My twist (this came out of an argument with friends after a game theory reading group, and I think it's the contentious bit) is that this isn't a special case about dying in deserts or mind-reading drivers -- I want to claim that commitment-style "rational irrationality" generalizes to ordinary life, so it's often rational to make yourself into the kind of person who keeps promises, follows through on threats, and keeps agreements even when you'd get away with defecting, and that the supposed problem of "why should I pay now that I'm already safe" dissolves once you evaluate dispositions rather than individual acts.
