I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I'm applying for a semester-long residency at a university humanities center and they want a two-page proposal, so here I am. My half-baked idea: Moore's paradox -- "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining" sounds unhinged when said out loud, even though the content is perfectly possible, since people fail to believe true things all the time. My hunch is that first-person fiction is a good stress test for the standard explanations of this. If the absurdity is really about assertion, then a character just *thinking* the sentence, in stream of consciousness, shouldn't feel weird -- but I think it still does. And if it's about belief instead, then I shouldn't be able to write a convincing character who actually lives in that state, though I suspect I can, with the right machinery (self-deception, compartmentalization, dissociation). So the project is a novella whose narrator's inner monologue keeps sliding into Moore-paradoxical territory, plus a short essay on what worked and what didn't and what that shows about where the paradox actually lives -- speech, belief, or self-knowledge.

Turn this into the proposal: the question stated precisely enough that it could come out either way, why the usual accounts (the assertion-based ones, the belief-based ones, the self-knowledge ones) leave it open, what I'd concretely do month by month, why this needs a novella rather than just an essay arguing the same thing, and what would count as the project failing -- e.g., what result would mean fiction has nothing to tell philosophy here. Also, be honest with me about the soft spots: tell me which philosophical argument, if it turned out to be right, would sink the whole premise, and where the framing above is most likely to be confused, since a residency committee full of philosophers will notice even if I dont.
