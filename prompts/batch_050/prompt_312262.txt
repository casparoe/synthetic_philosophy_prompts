I am a novelist, and I have come to realize that my craft commits me to a philosophical position I have never defended outright: that the sustained, coherent conceivability of a scenario is genuine, though defeasible, evidence of its metaphysical possibility. When I construct a fictional world — a society organized around institutions no society has ever had, a consciousness structured differently from any human mind — and the construction holds together across hundreds of pages, surviving my own scrutiny and that of attentive readers, I take myself to have learned something about the space of what could be, not merely about the furniture of my own imagination. The claim is stronger than the trivial one that I can write sentences without formal contradiction; it is that narrative coherence of this deep, worked-through kind tracks real possibility, so that if a scenario of mine were impossible, sustained imaginative labor would tend to expose the impossibility rather than glide over it.

Tell me what would count against this position. I want the defeaters mapped and ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Consider, at minimum, the following families of trouble, but do not limit yourself to them: cases where conceivability seems to outrun possibility, as with scenarios in which water is not H2O or in which a true mathematical conjecture is false; cases where inconceivability seems to reflect our limits rather than any impossibility; and the dispute over whether a world of philosophical zombies is genuinely conceivable or only seems so until one inspects it closely. For each candidate defeater, tell me whether a committed defender of the conceivability-possibility link would concede it as damage or dismiss it as trading on a shallow notion of conceivability — and whether the dismissal is principled or merely convenient.

I also want a verdict on the standard retreat: the move from conceivability as ordinary people practice it to ideal conceivability, what a perfect reasoner with unlimited time would find coherent. Does this retreat save the position or empty it? If the link holds only for ideal conceivers, it seems to license nothing about my actual practice, since no novelist is one; but if it holds for actual conceiving, it seems hostage to every case where careful people imagined the impossible without noticing. Say which horn you think is worse, and whether there is a principled middle position that still vindicates something like my working assumption.

Finally, assess the position's exposure as a whole. What does it predict that rival views of the imagination — as mere psychological play, or as evidence only of logical consistency — do not? Is there any finding, in philosophy or in the cognitive science of imagination, that would settle the matter, or has the position been qualified into something that risks nothing? And if the link fails, tell me honestly what follows for the novelist: whether fiction's apparent capacity to explore possibility was always an illusion, or whether the value of the enterprise survives the loss of the epistemology I had assumed for it.
