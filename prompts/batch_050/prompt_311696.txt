I am a PhD student in epistemology with a strong secondary interest in metaethics, and a colleague in my reading group has been pressing an argument that evolutionary debunking arguments are self-defeating. I find her conclusion intolerable but cannot locate the misstep, so I am writing for a diagnosis.

Her argument runs as follows:

(1) If the best explanation of why we hold a class of beliefs makes no reference to their truth, and we would hold those beliefs even if they were false, then those beliefs are unjustified.
(2) The best explanation of our moral beliefs is evolutionary: they were favored by selection because they made us cooperative and group-cohesive, not because they were true; the moral facts, if there are any, do no work in the explanation.
(3) Hence we would hold our moral beliefs even if the moral facts had been entirely different.
(4) Therefore our moral beliefs are unjustified.
(5) Our beliefs about epistemic norms — that beliefs should be responsive to evidence, that one should distrust belief-forming processes that are insensitive to truth — have exactly the same kind of explanation: they were favored because creatures holding them outcompeted creatures that did not, not because they were correct. So we would hold them even if the true epistemic norms had been different.
(6) Therefore our beliefs about epistemic norms are unjustified.
(7) The debunking argument in (1)–(4) essentially relies on beliefs about epistemic norms — most obviously, on the norm that (1) itself expresses.
(8) An argument whose premises the arguer is unjustified in accepting cannot justify its conclusion for that arguer.
(9) So the conclusion at (4) is unjustified for anyone who runs the argument: evolutionary debunking defeats itself.
(10) Since every debunking argument has this shape, no belief is ever undermined by showing that its origins are truth-insensitive.

She wields (10) cheerfully: it would equally protect beliefs formed by reading tea leaves or by wishful thinking, since any attempt to debunk them will invoke epistemic norms with evolutionary origins. I take that to be a reductio of the argument rather than a discovery about debunking, but I have been unable to say, in a way she accepts, which step is the culprit. I am fairly committed to something in the neighborhood of (1) — that purely contingent, truth-insensitive etiology defeats justification — and I would rather not give up the force of (2)–(4) against our moral beliefs either, though I will if the cost analysis demands it.

Please tell me which single premise or step you reject, explain why that step looked innocent, and price the main alternatives — in particular, what it costs to reject (5) instead of (8), and vice versa, and whether either rejection leaves my colleague a way to rerun the argument in a weakened form. If you think the argument is sound after all, say so and defend (10) against the tea-leaves objection.

State the diagnosis in a fixed form as the final line of your answer: \answer{reject (n): short label for the flaw}, with n the number of the premise or inference step you reject.
