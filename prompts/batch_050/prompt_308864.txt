You've heard the standard debate about whether artifacts have politics — the suspiciously low parkway bridges, the tomato harvester, all the greatest hits. I want a fresh case, built to order, that seperates three ways of cashing the slogan out. Here are the readings I mean (I'm stipulating these, so work with them as given, not with whatever some author said):

1. **Blueprint**: an artifact has politics when somebody's political aims or values are baked into its design, whether or not the design actually achieves them.
2. **Straitjacket**: an artifact has politics when adopting it more or less forces a particular kind of social or political arrangement — you can't really have the thing without the polity.
3. **Foothold**: an artifact has politics when, given the background conditions people actually live under, it systematically and predictably advantages some groups while burdening others — even if nobody meant it to, and even if different background conditions would erase the pattern entirely.

Construct a thought experiment where the three readings come apart in exactly this pattern: Blueprint says not political, Straitjacket says not political, Foothold says political. Constraints:

- One paragraph, and the artifact has to be mundane and current — a consumer product, a piece of workplace software, a building feature, an app. No weapons, no surveillance systems, nothing speculative or sci-fi.
- The burden on the burdened group must be measurable and not empirically contested — no appeals to contested psychology, and no harms that a character in the story could just flatly deny are real.
- No villain. Nobody in the story intends the burden, notices it, or profits from it. If there's any character whose fingerprints are on the design in a way that flips Blueprint to yes, the case fails.
- The political effect must depend on scale or on other people's uptake: the artifact is harmless for a lone user, and the pattern only shows up once alot of people adopt it (or once some background convention sets in). State explicitly what the load-bearing background condition is.
- The burdened people have to keep using the thing voluntarily, and it has to genuinely help them along some dimension — the burden can't just be that it's a bad product they should abandon.

After the scenario: give the verdict of each of the three readings, with the reasoning, in a few sentences each. Then name the single feature of your case that makes Foothold peel away from the other two, and say what happens to each verdict if you delete that feature — does the case collapse into a genuinely apolitical artifact, or into one that's political on all three readings? Finish with the strongest objection from someone who thinks Foothold is way too permissive — that on it, everything from forks to Tuesdays counts as political, so the thesis goes vacuous — and say whether your case survives by tightening "systematically and predictably."

For the record, the point of the exercise is to test whether artifacts can have politics when the politics is entirely relational — nothing in the object, everything in the world around it. So don't stipulate that the artifact embodies exclusion or encodes values; the pattern has to be *generated* by features the three readings can each see and react to, not asserted.
