I'm in an environmental ethics seminar and after class two people in my cohort got into a long argument about wild animal suffering that the rest of us couldn't settle. It went roughly like this:

(1) Maya said that if you take wild animal suffering seriously at all, habitat preservation is plausibly bad: most wild animals are r-selected, almost all offspring die young and horribly, so the average wild life is probably net negative, and preserving habitat just means manufacturing more suffering. Developing land is the compassionate option.

(2) Jordan said the net-negative claim is way shakier than Maya treats it -- it depends on how you weigh a huge number of very short invertebrate lives against a few longer vertebrate ones, on guesses about insect sentience, and on a comparison against nonexistence that nobody knows how to do. He also said habitat destruction doesn't cleanly reduce populations, it kills the current residents through starvation and displacement, which are exactly the bad deaths Maya claims to care about.

(3) Maya replied that uncertainty isn't a pass -- you still have to act on your best guess, and if the probability that wild lives are net negative is over 50%, expected value favors destruction. And the killing-current-residents point just means destruction should be fast and permanent (parking lots, agriculture), not slow and reversible, which her view handles fine.

(4) Jordan said her view handles it "fine" only by biting a bullet nobody can bite: if her argument works it doesn't just favor development, it favors deliberately exterminating wild animal populations everywhere as quickly as possible, and a moral view with that conclusion has gone wrong somewhere upstream, probably by treating "creating fewer animals" as straightforwardly good in a way we'd never accept for humans.

Can you adjudicate this move by move -- who's right at each step and why, whether Maya's expected-value reply in (3) actually answers Jordan's uncertainty point, whether Jordan's bullet-biting objection in (4) is a real refutation or just an appeal to intuition, and what (if anything) both of them are missing? Also say who you think is more right overall.
