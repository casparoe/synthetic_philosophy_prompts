So I'm a PhD student in historical sociology and I've been going back and forth on this question of how moral norms actually die, and I want to figure out what I actually think by laying out my gut reactions on cases. Background: there's the Williams thesis that British abolition of the slave trade happened because slavery had become economically marginal for the empire, so abolition was cheap; the revisionists say West Indian slavery was still profitable and Britain ate real costs. And Appiah argues that in cases like dueling and footbinding the moral arguments were all made decades before the practice collapsed, and what actually killed them was a shift in where honor attached. Here's where I land on each:

- British abolition: I think Williams is basically right that interests did the heavy lifting, and the abolitionist arguments mostly provided cover. The campaign had been around for generations without effect.
- BUT Britain compensated slaveholders to the tune of a fortune (the loan was only paid off in my lifetime), and Parliament spent serious naval resources suppressing the trade for decades. That seems like a society paying real costs for a moral conviction, which shouldn't happen on the Williams story. So I also feel the abolitionist argument did genuine causal work. These two obviously pull against each other and I can't tell which to give up.
- Dueling: dead arguments for centuries, everyone conceded it was irrational and unchristian, and then it vanished within a generation or two once it started looking ridiculous rather than honorable. I think Appiah's diagnosis is clearly right here.
- Footbinding: I'm less sure. Yes it collapsed fast once national shame got involved, but there was also state coercion and the natural-foot societies enforcing pledges. I don't think pure honor-shifting explains it; it feels like honor plus a state that could actually punish people.
- Apartheid: I think this was mostly interests -- sanctions, divestment, the economy strangling -- and the moral argument had been available and ignored for decades. Though the Afrikaans churches flipping seems like it mattered too, which is awkward for me since that's more like argument/honor than interests.
- One I'm genuinely torn on: meat eating and factory farming today. Every argument against it has been made, thoroughly, for fifty years, and consumption keeps rising. My framework should predict something here and I don't know what.

Is there a single principle about what kills entrenched norms -- something like "arguments are necessary but never sufficient; practices die when the people whose identity is bound up with them can exit without losing face, or when the practice stops paying" -- that captures all my verdicts, or do I need to abandon one? State the principle precisely, say which clause each case motivates, and tell me what it predicts for the factory farming case and for one case I haven't mentioned, like dueling's modern analogues or corporal punishment in schools. If the principle has to sacrifice one of my verdicts, tell me which one and why.
