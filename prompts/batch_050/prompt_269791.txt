I'm the chair of the acquisitions committee at a small modern art museum, and we're stuck. We've been offered, cheap, an indie video game about grief: the player decides which memories of a dead spouse the protagonist gets to keep, the endings contradict each other, and no single playthrough shows you the whole piece. The artist refuses to designate a 'canonical' playthrough, says the work is the rule system rather than any session of it, and reserves the right to patch the rules forever as players figure out how to 'win.'

Half the committee wants to buy it like we'd buy a film. Our registrar is digging in her heals: you can't conserve something with no fixed form, and one trustee argues that anything the audience can win or lose puts the player's agency where the artist's expression should be, so it can't do what art does. The curators counter that the player's agency is exactly the medium here -- the queasiness of choosing which memories to keep is the content, and a recorded playthrough of it is just documentation, not the work.

Draft the committee's decision memo: acquire, decline, or acquire with conditions (preserving the source code, commissioning recorded playthroughs, contracting around future updates, whatever). Give us a bottom line we can actually vote on, the reasoning behind it, and the strongest version of the side that loses. Then say how confident you are in the recommendation and flag the single step of the argument where you're least sure of yourself.
