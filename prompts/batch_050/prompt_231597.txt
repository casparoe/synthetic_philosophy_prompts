I have been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade, and I have absorbed the standard critiques of democracy that circulate there: voters are rationally ignorant, tribal signalling crowds out deliberation, and a well-designed prediction market would outperform an electorate on almost any factual question. Part of me half-believes all of that. Another part remains stubbornly attached to the idea that legitimate authority flows from the consent of the governed. Where I keep getting stuck is direct democracy. I have confident gut verdicts on a run of real cases, but I cannot find the principle that generates them. Here are the cases, with my verdicts; please treat the verdicts as data to be explained, not conclusions to be defended.

1. A Swiss canton puts its budget, including funding for a new school building, to an obligatory referendum as a matter of routine. Fine. This seems to me direct democracy working as intended, and I would happily live under it.

2. California, 1978: Proposition 13 caps property taxes and imposes supermajority requirements for raising revenue, passed by simple-majority initiative, still constraining the state's fiscal policy nearly half a century later. Not fine. One day's majority has handcuffed every subsequent legislature. But I notice I cannot say whether my objection is to the subject matter (constitutionalising fiscal rules) or simply to the entrenchment (a simple majority binding all future majorities).

3. The 2016 Brexit referendum: advisory, binary, decided by a narrow margin, on a question whose operative content — what leaving would actually consist in — was not on the ballot. Not fine. Yet the 2014 Scottish independence referendum felt fine to me: clear question, long campaign, engaged electorate. The two look structurally similar, so either my principle must separate them or one of these verdicts has to go.

4. Ireland, 2015: a referendum approving same-sex marriage. Fine — better than fine; it seemed like a society taking collective ownership of a reform rather than having it handed down by judges. But Switzerland, 2009: a popular initiative banning the construction of minarets. Not fine. In both cases a majority voted directly on the standing of a small minority, and my verdicts point in opposite directions. This is the pair I cannot reconcile.

5. California, 2008: Proposition 8, by which voters removed a marriage right the courts had recognised. Not fine. Note that this sits awkwardly beside my verdict in 4, and I do not think "expanding rights good, removing rights bad" quite works, since from the losing side's point of view the Irish vote was also a restriction — of the institution of marriage as they understood it.

6. Until the federal courts intervened, some Swiss communes decided individual citizenship applications by popular vote, in places by secret ballot, and applicants with the wrong surnames fared conspicuously worse. Not fine — indeed the most clearly not fine of all these cases — though I cannot articulate precisely what makes it worse than the minaret vote, which is also a majority passing judgement on a small, identifiable group.

7. I would not want a referendum on whether the central bank should raise interest rates next month, or on whether a proposed particle collider justifies its cost. Not fine, and this one feels easy — but every principle I sketch that explains it either swallows cases I approved in 1, 3 and 4, or collapses into "questions I personally think are hard", which is obviously no principle at all.

The case I keep going back and forth on: a citizens' initiative, itself passed by simple majority, that raises the threshold for all future initiatives to two thirds — a referendum whose sole content is to make future referendums harder. Every general view I try delivers a verdict here that I immediately want to disown.

What I want: a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply them to new cases without you in the room. For each clause, tell me which of my verdicts motivates it. Then apply your preferred principle to three cases I have not considered: the 2011 UK referendum on the alternative vote; the 2015 Greek referendum on the creditors' bailout terms; and a hypothetical Swiss-style popular vote on joining the European Economic Area. Finally — and this is the part I care most about — be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate. I would rather be told that verdict 4 or verdict 6 has to go than be handed a contorted principle that saves everything by distinguishing nothing. I would guess this needs 800–1,200 words.

Three constraints on form. First, please write in British English. Second, no bibliography or citations — I am asking for philosophical work, not a literature review. Third, somewhere in the answer, address my lingering suspicion that the real distinction is between the referendum as a standing institution, embedded in a political culture that expects and prepares for it (Switzerland), and the referendum as a one-off weapon deployed by a government that wants to outsource a decision (elsewhere) — because if that suspicion is right, several of my verdicts are tracking the wrong variable entirely, and I would like to know that too.
