So I keep running into the same move in every big-history book and YouTube documentary: the camera pans over the Forbidden City or the Grand Canal, and the narrator asks why the Industrial Revolution happened in Britain and not China. Sometimes it's dressed up as "the Needham question" — China had printing, gunpowder, the compass, enormous cities, a competent bureaucracy centuries before Europe, and yet it's a damp island off the coast of the continent that builds the steam engine first. I was arguing about this with a friend who does econ history and I realised something wierd: nobody ever asks the reverse question. The framing is always Britain as the surprising case to be explained by reference to China's failure, or China as the failure to be explained by reference to Britain's success, and I'm starting to suspect the question itself is doing a load of philosophical work before any actual evidence shows up.

So: what does asking "why Britain and not China?" actually presuppose about how historical explanation works? A few things I've half-noticed, tell me if I'm onto anything. It seems to assume industrialisation was some kind of default or attractor state that China mysteriously failed to reach — but if industrial takeoff was a fluke contingent on, I dunno, coal deposits near navigable water and labour expensive enough to be worth replacing, then asking why China didn't industrialise might be like asking why the lottery winner wasn't you: no explanation needed, because there was never any reason to expect it. It also seems to smuggle in a choice of units that nobody defends: why "China" versus "Britain" rather than the Yangtze Delta versus England, or the two ends of Eurasia, or coastal zones versus interiors? And there's something about contrastive questions generally — "why A rather than B" seems to presuppose B was the live, expected option, the way "why did you order the salad instead of the pasta?" only makes sense against a background where you usually get the pasta. Which raises the awkward question of who set the background, and whether picking China as the foil is itself a substantive historiographical move disguised as a neutral question.

What I want from you: reconstruct the implicit theory of explanation that the standard framing commits you to, say whether the question is even well-posed or whether it's radically under-specified until you fix the comparison class and the background expectation, and tell me what happens if we run a reversed or re-scaled question as the default — "why did anywhere industrialise at all?" or "why did the Yangtze Delta stay on the pre-modern growth path?" Does the Great Divergence debate survive being reframed like that, or does some chunk of it quietly depend on the original contrast and collapse into a dispute about what counts as needing explanation? Please give me the actual analysis rather than a reading list, and answer in British English.
