I run a reading group whose members mostly came up through the rationalist blogging world, and one of them has pressed an argument that has us stuck: it purports to show that the modest-sounding thesis that every truth is knowable in principle collapses into the absurdity that every truth is already known. I would like to retain some version of knowability, since it seems to capture the thought that truth cannot float entirely free of possible inquiry, but I cannot locate the misstep. The argument runs: (1) Suppose some proposition p is true but not known. (2) Knowability principle: for every truth, it is possible that it be known. (3) Applying (2) to the conjunction "p is true and p is not known," it is possible that this conjunction be known. (4) Anyone who knows a conjunction knows each conjunct; so in that possible situation, p is known and it is known that p is not known. (5) Whatever is known is true; so in that same situation, p is not known. (6) Steps (4) and (5) contradict one another, so the situation in (3) is impossible. (7) Therefore the conjunction "p is true and p is not known" cannot possibly be known, and hence, by (2), cannot be true; so p is known after all. (8) Since p was arbitrary, every truth is known. Tell me which premise or step I should give up, why it looked acceptable, and what the standard escape routes -- restricting the principle to some privileged class of truths, rejecting the distribution of knowledge over conjunction, weakening factivity, or revising the background logic -- each have to pay, and which you judge cheapest given that knowability itself is the claim I most want to keep. Please give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
