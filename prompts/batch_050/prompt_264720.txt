I cover biology and the philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine, and last month I moderated a panel on biological individuality that went completely off the rails in the best way. A philosopher argued that a Portuguese man o' war isn't one organism but a colony of specialized zooids, a biologist shot back that by the same lights a human is a holobiont whose cells are outnumbered by microbes and whose mitochondria are domesticated bacteria, and someone in the audience asked why slime mold cells that fuse into a crawling slug and sacrifice themselves as a stalk don't count as one thing. It got me thinking about the actual criteria people wheel out -- physiological unity, shared germline bottlenecks, immune systems policing cheaters, cooperation without conflict -- and how every single one seems to exlcude something it shouldn't or include something absurd.

So here's what I want you to write for my notebook (and maybe a column): a set of mock form letters, maybe 600-700 words total, from something like the Admissions Committee for the Society of Certified Organisms, responding to membership applications. Give me three or four letters. The applicants should include a siphonophore, an ant colony applying as a single superorganism, a lichen, and a human being. The committee should be unfailingly polite and bureaucratic, citing its bylaws and criteria with complete confidence, and the comedy should come from the fact that its criteria are real ones from the actual debate -- so the siphonophore gets rejected on grounds that a careful reader notices would also disqualify the human, the ant colony gets dinged for lacking a Weismann barrier or a germline bottleneck in a way that quietly indicts half of metazoan reproduction, and the human's application turns out to be on shakier ground than anyone assumed. Deadpan institutional voice throughout, no winking, no jokey asides.

The thing I care most about is that the satire is earned: a philosopher of biology reading it should recognize exactly which positions and which arguments each rejection letter is parodying, and feel that the committee is being skewered fairly rather than set up as a straw man. Ideally the last letter lands the real sting -- that the category of organism might be a bureaucratic fiction all the way down, or at least that the committee's letterhead is doing more work than its criteria. Don't break character at the end to explain the joke; trust the reader to see what's being ironized.
