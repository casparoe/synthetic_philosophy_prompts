I’ve spent more than a decade reading rationalist blogs and forums, and next month I’m presenting a paper at a rationalist-adjacent philosophy workshop on whether the simulation argument should dampen “fanaticism” about the far future. By fanaticism, I mean treating a tiny probability of affecting an astronomically large population as sufficient to dominate ordinary expected-value comparisons. My thesis is not that the simulation argument is irrelevant to longtermism. It is that the argument does not, by itself, justify a uniform discount on far-future stakes. Moving from “we may be simulated” to “our far-future impact matters less” requires further assumptions about which observers are simulated, what the simulators value, whether simulated welfare counts, and whether our choices can influence or correlate with outcomes outside our immediate environment. My positive claim is that the argument should primarily widen our model uncertainty: it can reduce confidence in some concrete far-future mechanisms while also opening unusual channels of influence, without establishing a general presumption against fanatical tradeoffs.

The audience will contain about fifteen people: a philosopher specializing in anthropic reasoning, a formal epistemologist, a decision theorist, an AI-governance researcher, two committed longtermists from the effective-altruism community, and several technically literate graduate students who are skeptical of simulation reasoning. Nearly everyone will be familiar with the standard simulation trilemma, self-sampling versus self-indication assumptions, Pascalian wagers, substrate independence, and acausal or evidential correlations. The longtermists will care about whether my view smuggles astronomical value back in under another description; the anthropic specialist will care about observer reference classes; the decision theorist will care about whether correlation without causal control is doing illegitimate work; and the skeptics will attack any move that makes the simulation hypothesis unfalsifiable or infinitely flexible.

Prepare me for the question period. Please give the objections roughly in the order I am most likely to receive them, ranked by both likelihood and potential damage. For each objection:

- identify who is most likely to raise it;
- phrase it in that person’s natural vocabulary, as a pointed seminar question rather than as a textbook summary;
- explain the strongest version of the concern rather than a strawman;
- state what I should concede immediately;
- give me a concise reply suitable for delivery in under two minutes;
- identify the weakest step in my reply and how I should respond if challenged on that step;
- distinguish whether the objection threatens my narrow claim, my broader compatibility with longtermism, or only some optional illustration I could drop.

Please make sure the prep sheet covers at least these pressure points:

1. the difference between the probability that our world is simulated and the probability that our actions have morally relevant effects;
2. whether assigning substantial probability to being simulated should already change the expected value of influencing base reality;
3. whether simulated persons count morally, without assuming that substrate alone determines moral status;
4. whether simulation reasoning undermines the empirical premises behind familiar far-future scenarios more than it undermines fanaticism in general;
5. whether my appeal to “correlation” is just acausal bargaining dressed up in respectable language;
6. whether anthropic selection lets me preserve the possibility of enormous impact no matter what evidence I receive;
7. whether my view creates a double standard by distrusting speculative longtermist mechanisms while trusting speculative simulation mechanisms;
8. whether the simulation hypothesis should reduce confidence specifically in claims about the far future, because simulations may be terminated, truncated, manipulated, or rerun;
9. whether a sufficiently large number of simulations reverses the discount rather than creating one; and
10. whether my conclusion is merely the platitude that expected value is sensitive to empirical assumptions.

Also include the objection I should most hope nobody asks—the one that exposes a genuine vulnerability rather than merely being rhetorically difficult—and give me the least evasive answer I can offer. If there is an initial concession that would make the whole presentation more credible without surrendering the thesis, identify it.

The answer should be a practical prep sheet rather than an essay, should not include citations, a bibliography, or a reading list, and should be written in American English.
