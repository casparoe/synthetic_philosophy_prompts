I'm part of a small graduate reading group working through the classical pragmatists, and this month we did C. S. Peirce's "The Fixation of Belief" (1877). Almost all of our discussion went where it usually goes -- the irritation-of-doubt psychology and the four methods of fixing belief -- but I got stuck much earlier in the essay, on a remark Peirce makes while describing Lavoisier's practice: "his way was to carry his mind into his laboratory, and literally to make of his alembics and cucurbits instruments of thought, giving a new conception of reasoning as something which was to be done with one's eyes open, in manipulating real things instead of words and fancies." I'd like a careful explanation of what conception of reasoning this passage actually articulates and what argumentative work it is doing in the essay as a whole, because the people in my group gave me three incompatible answers and I suspect at least two of us are wrong.

Here is where I most need help. First, does the image of reasoning as "manipulating real things" amount to an early version of the pragmatist idea that thought is essentially bound up with action and experiment, and if so, how does it sit with the argument Peirce gives later in the same essay that the method of science is distinguished by its appeal to something whose character is independent of anyone's opinion? One member of my group read the Lavoisier passage as a realist commitment -- the apparatus is a mind-independent recalcitrant that forces belief -- while another read it as nearly verificationist, as though the meaning of a chemical claim just is the laboratory routine. Can the passage bear both readings, and does the rest of the essay discriminate between them? Second, does the example overfit chemistry: if reasoning is essentially something done with one's eyes open on real things, what becomes of mathematics and purely theoretical inquiry, where Peirce himself spent much of his career? Is there a coherent extension of the picture, or a genuine strain between this passage and the essay's more general account of inquiry? Third, I'm puzzled about how this portrait of inquiry -- deliberate, self-controlled, experimental -- fits with the essay's framing of belief and doubt as something closer to a homeostatic response, in which inquiry begins only when belief is disturbed. Are these two different models of inquiry coexisting in one essay, or is one of them doing merely rhetorical work?

Please treat this as an exercise in interpretation rather than a summary of the essay: build your answer around the quoted sentence and its place in the surrounding argument. Where the text genuinely settles a question, say so plainly; where Peirce scholarship is genuinely divided -- for instance, over how continuous this 1877 essay is with the 1878 statement of the pragmatic maxim and with Peirce's later, more explicitly realist revisions -- flag the disagreement as a disagreement rather than presenting one reading as the consensus, and tell me which features of the passage itself leave the question underdetermined.
