So I've been reading about the "live and let live" system in WWI trench warfare -- the thing where units on opposite sides of no man's land would more or less stop trying to kill each other, snipers would fire at the same spots every day, artillery would shell the same empty field on schedule, both sides would sometimes even let supply wagons through, until the high commands got wind of it and forced the system to collapse by ordering trench raids.

I want an analysis of this through the lens of the iterated prisoner's dilemma and the evolution of cooperation literature (Axelrod and co). Run it both directions.

From theory to episode: map the case onto the formal machinery carefully. Who exactly are the players -- individual soldiers, units, armies? What's the payoff matrix, and is it actually a PD or something else (stag hunt? assurance game? a PD only for some actors)? What does the static front line do to the shadow of the future and why does that matter. How does the system handle noise -- a stray shell that kills someone, was it defection or an accident, and how would a TFT-ish strategy cope with that. And the part I find most interesting: the raids. The commanders basically couldn't observe restraint directly but raids created observable, unambiguous confrontation and forced retaliation. What does that do to the game -- does it change payoffs, change monitoring, or just reset the iteration? Also why rotating troops would matter on the theory.

From episode back to theory: what does the case confirm, and where does it strain the account? E.g. the theory explains spontaneous cooperation between units but does it explain why the norm held within each side -- soldiers restraining each other, punishing hotheads on their own team? Is that cooperation between enemies or enforcement within a group, and can the standard model tell the difference? And the endgame question: why didn't everything unravel as the war neared its end, the way backward induction says finite iterated games should? Does the case show TFT is the right model, or is something else (norms, identity, leadership, plain exhaustion) doing the real work and game theory just redescribing it?

Two requests: keep the mapping disciplined -- if the theory is silent on some feature, say so instead of forcing a prediction out of it. And be honest about what's settled vs contested here, both on the history side (how widespread this actually was is debated, I think?) and on the theory side -- dont present the Axelrod-style reading of the truces as if historians and game theorists all agree it's the right one.
