Two colleagues in my philosophy of physics reading group argued over Pierre Curie's 1894 paper "Sur la symétrie dans les phénomènes physiques" (Journal de Physique), and they asked me to referee. The contested passage, in my rough translation, runs as follows: when several phenomena are superposed in one system, their dissymmetries add together; the only symmetry elements that can survive in the combined system are those common to each phenomenon considered separately; and when certain effects reveal a certain dissymmetry, that dissymmetry must be found among the causes that produced them.

(1) Marta argued that this passage licenses "explanation by symmetry": if the symmetry of a whole is fixed as the common symmetry of its contributing causes, then citing a cause's symmetry genuinely explains the effect's symmetry -- the way we explain why the field of a spherically symmetric charge distribution is itself spherically symmetric.

(2) Daniel replied that she has the inferential direction wrong. On his reading, the passage supports only the contrapositive claim -- dissymmetry in an effect must trace back to dissymmetry somewhere in the causes -- plus a bookkeeping rule about intersecting symmetry groups. Bookkeeping constrains what effects are possible; it does not explain why this effect occurred, and it is silent whenever the phenomena at issue are not related by superposition at all.

(3) Marta answered that the intersection rule is precisely what makes symmetry explanatory, because it supplies counterfactual support: perturb the setup in any way that preserves the relevant symmetry and the effect's symmetry survives. Invariance under a range of perturbations, she said, is the mark of an explanation, not of mere bookkeeping.

(4) Daniel countered that this proves too much. The rule is symmetric between "cause" and "effect" as relata: read backward, the effects' symmetry would equally "explain" the causes' symmetry. Since explanation is asymmetric, whatever does the real explanatory work must come from outside Curie's principle -- for instance, from dynamics, in the way Noether's theorem ties symmetries of the laws to conserved quantities.

Please adjudicate move by move: assess each of the four moves, saying who is right about what Curie's text actually licenses, whether the superposition rule delivers the counterfactual support Marta claims, and whether the asymmetry of explanation forces Daniel's appeal to dynamics. Conclude with who is more right overall and what, if anything, both are missing.
