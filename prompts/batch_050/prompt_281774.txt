I teach an introductory philosophy of science seminar — 18 students, one 80-minute session, no prior exposure to confirmation theory. Design an in-class card game that makes students feel the force of Hempel's raven paradox from the inside: each card represents an object (bird or not, black or not), and small groups test the hypothesis that all ravens are black by requesting cards one at a time, choosing on each request whether to be shown a raven or a non-black object, with their score tied to how accurately they eventually wager on the hypothesis. Crucially, I want you to rig the decks so that the same observation — a non-black non-raven — carries different evidential weight in different groups' decks, so that students discover for themselves that confirmation depends on background composition rather than on logical form alone. Specify the deck compositions, the exact request rules and payoff scheme (no real money), a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto Nicod's criterion and the equivalence condition — but no lecture up front, and students should not hear the name of the paradox until afterwards. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (for instance, groups concluding that non-raven evidence is simply worthless) and which feature of the actual paradox the game cannot reproduce.
