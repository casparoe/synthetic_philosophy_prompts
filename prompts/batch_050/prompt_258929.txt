I teach a third-year philosophy of science module, and in a single two-hour seminar I have to introduce my students to the problem of verisimilitude. They arrive knowing Popper's falsificationism and the rough shape of the pessimistic induction, but nothing about the post-1970s literature on truthlikeness. I do not want you to explain verisimilitude to me; I want a plan for how I should explain it to them.

Map the order in which the components must be introduced and what each depends on. My instinct is: first the motivation (why a fallibilist needs an ordering of false theories by closeness to the truth, and why neither probability nor content alone supplies one); then Popper's own proposal and its failure on false theories; then the likeness program as the natural successor; then Miller's language-dependence objection; then the responses that concede some but not all of what Miller showed. Tell me whether that dependency structure is right or whether some piece has to come earlier or later to be intelligible.

I plan to carry the whole thing on Tichý-style weather cases: a small set of independent claims (it is hot, it is windy, it is raining), a known truth, and rival theories that each get some claims right and some wrong, so that rankings can be computed and then destabilized by redefinition of the primitives. Say what such a carrying example must have and must lack to do this job, why it beats opening with Newton versus Einstein (which they will all want to reach for), and at what point in the session real theories should be allowed back in.

Give me the misconception map: I expect at least conflation of verisimilitude with probability, the assumption that greater content always means greater truthlikeness, the reading of Miller's argument as proving the concept incoherent rather than proving measures language-relative, and the thought that closeness to the truth just is numerical accuracy of predictions. For each, tell me where in the sequence it predictably forms and where I should head it off rather than let it harden.

Finally, give me one end-of-seminar task that would show the idea has landed rather than been memorized (I am considering handing them a miniature theory-ranking case and asking them to construct a Miller-style relabeling of it themselves), and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, with a sentence I can actually say to the room when someone asks.
