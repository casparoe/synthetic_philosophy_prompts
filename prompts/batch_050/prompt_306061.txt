The seminar text is J. R. Lucas's "Minds, Machines and Gödel" (Philosophy, 1961). The bit we focused on:

"Gödel's theorem must apply to cybernetical machines, because it is of the essence of being a machine, that it should be a concrete instantiation of a formal system. It follows that given any machine which is consistent and capable of doing simple arithmetic, there is a formula which it is incapable of producing as being true — i.e., the formula is unprovable-in-the-system — but which we can see to be true."

One of my students then presented this souped-up version, and neither of us can find the leak:

(1) Any consistent machine that proves arithmetical truths has its output representable as a formal system F.
(2) By Gödel, if F is consistent, its Gödel sentence G(F) is true but unprovable in F.
(3) But we can see that G(F) is true, so human mathematicians can come to know a truth F cannot prove. (This is just Lucas's move.)
(4) Nothing about F was special; the trick works for every consistent finitely presented system.
(5) So for every consistent finitely presented F, humans can in principle come to know G(F).
(6) Since G(F) is equivalent to Con(F) over a weak base theory, that means humans can in principle know, of every finitely presented system, that it's consistent.
(7) But no algorithm can sort the consistent finitely presented systems from the inconsistent ones — that's undecidable, tied to the halting problem. So human mathematicians are, in principle, hypercomputers: minds with capacities no Turing machine has.
(8) There are definately no hypercomputers sitting in my seminar room. So something above fails — and since the only non-mechanical step is (3), which is Lucas's whole argument, Lucas must fail too.

I feel like the slip is between (2) and (5) rather than in (8)'s modus tollens, and everything seems to hang on what "see" means in that last clause of the quote — who the "we" is and what they actually have access to.
