So last night I got into this huge argument with my roommate about whether it's ever okay to just... say stuff you're not sure of. It started because she told our friend group that a restaurant had closed down, it turned out it hadn't (it was just renovating), and when someone corrected her she went "oh crap, forget I said anything." And I was like, why are you apologizing? You read it on some local blog, you had perfectly good reason to believe it, you weren't lying or being reckless. She insisted she still shouldn't have said it, that when something you assert turns out false you've done something wrong even if you did everything right. That seems insane to me. If I can only ever say things that turn out true, I'd basically never open my mouth, since any of us could be wrong about almost anything.

But then she turned it around on me and it's been bugging me. She said: fine, suppose I claim the lottery ticket I just bought is going to lose. Statistically I'm way, way more justified in that than she was about the restaurant. But everyone would say I don't actually *know* the ticket will lose, and it would be weird to flat-out assert it. And she said something like, if I made some wild guess with no evidence at all and it happened to be true, nobody would congratulate me for a proper assertion, they'd say I got lucky and had no business saying it. So apparently we blame people for true-but-groundless assertions AND for justified-but-false assertions. How can both of those be the rule?

So here's what I want to understand. Ordinary talk seems to have all these built-in norms -- we retract stuff that turns out false, we ask "how do you know that?" when someone states something confidently, we mock lucky guesses, we hedge with "I think" when we're shaky. What picture of the rules of asserting does all this actually presuppose? Is there one consistent norm underneath (like, you should only say what you know, or only what's true, or only what you reasonably believe), and if so which parts of the practice does it explain and which does it fail to explain? Or is everyday assertion-talk just a mess of incompatible rules, like maybe we presuppose a truth-rule when we retract but a justification-rule when we criticize guessers? And if the practice really is inconsistent, which part would have to give way -- should we stop apologizing for false-but-reasonable claims, or stop blaming true-but-baseless ones? Please don't just name a famous paper's answer, I want to know which norm the practice *actually* commits us to and whether that commitment survives scruitiny.
