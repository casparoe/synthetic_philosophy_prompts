I am a final-year undergraduate in a jurisprudence seminar where each theoretical text is paired with a novel, and I am writing my long essay on Kafka's The Trial alongside Fuller, Raz, and Waldron on the rule of law. I want a reading of the novel that treats the three accounts as competing diagnostic instruments, faithful in both directions rather than using Kafka as decoration.

The questions I want answered are these. First, run Fuller's eight desiderata over the Court that prosecutes Josef K.: which fail, and in which scenes does the failure become visible? Two puzzles interest me especially. One is whether the congruence requirement, fidelity between declared rule and official action, can even be assessed in a system where nothing is ever promulgated, and what Fuller should say about a legal order whose informality is so complete; Titorelli's explanation of the three possible outcomes of a trial seems relevant, as does the Advocate's account of how cases actually move. The other is the flogging scene: the Court appears to discipline its own warders because K. complained, yet the proceedings are as opaque as the main trial, and the scene stands unchanged when K. returns. Is that a grotesque parody of legality, or evidence that the system has a legality of its own which K. simply cannot access, and does the difference matter for Fuller?

Second, Raz holds that the rule of law is one virtue of law among others, instrumental to the law's capacity to guide conduct, and in principle compatible with great evil; his comparison is to the sharpness of a knife, an excellence usable for good or ill. Does The Trial vindicate or trouble this? Everyone in the novel, K. included at the very end, treats the Court as law. If Fuller is right that wholesale failure of the desiderata means there is no legal system at all, the novel's central sociological fact becomes hard to state. If Raz is right, the Court is a legal system lacking one particular virtue, and much of the horror lies elsewhere. Which diagnosis fits the novel, and is there a third option?

Third, Waldron locates the essence of the rule of law in the procedural dignity of courts: being given a hearing, being addressed as an agent capable of reasons. Read K.'s trajectory through this lens: his attempt to argue at the first interrogation, the way his speech is treated as symptom or evidence rather than as reasons, the absence of any verdict or reasoned judgment even at the quarry. Is the deepest horror, on Waldron's account, that K. is never once treated as a bearer of dignity before a tribunal, and does the novel support Waldron's claim that this procedural dimension is the core of the ideal rather than one item on a checklist?

Finally, I want an honest account of where the three-lens reading breaks down. The doorkeeper parable in the cathedral suggests the Law's inaccessibility is not a design flaw that better institutions could fix but something constitutive, which has invited theological readings of the Court as divine law or authority as such. If so, where should the critic concede that the jurisprudential reading distorts the novel? And conversely, what does the novel show about the rule of law that the arguments of Fuller, Raz, and Waldron do not capture, for instance the phenomenology of living under a law that refuses to speak to you, or the way unofficial practice colonizes the space where declared law should be?
