A few friends and I run a small forecasting group -- we make probabilistic predictions about elections, product launches, that sort of thing, and we keep score. At our last meeting two members gave sharply different probabilities for the same event and, after hearing each other's reasoning, barely moved. A third member intervened with what he clearly took to be a knockdown point: rational people cannot agree to disagree. If both of them were really reasoning correctly and each knew the other was reasoning correctly, he said, their estimates would have to converge; so the fact that they still disagree proves that at least one of them is being irrational, and they should keep talking until the gap closes. He attributed this to a famous result in game theory, though he couldn't reproduce the argument when pressed.

I have heard this slogan in other places too -- used against people who "agree to disagree" about politics, used to argue that two honest experts who share their evidence must end up with the same view, and once used to suggest that persistent disagreement in science is a sign of someone's bad faith. I suspect the sentence is doing different work in each of these cases, and I want to understand what precisely it could mean before I let it referee our group's arguments. Could you lay out the distinct claims it might be making? I am especially interested in what has to be assumed for each version: Does it require that the two people started from the same prior beliefs, or is that optional? Does it require that each person's conclusion be common knowledge between them, and is that an ordinary thing to have or an extraordinary one? Does it say anything about people who weigh the same evidence differently because they reason by different but defensible methods? And is it a claim about what real people do, what idealized agents would do, or merely about what consistent betting behavior would look like?

For each reading you distinguish, please say whether it is close to trivial, substantive but defensible, or probably false, and whether it is the sort of thing that could actually be proved rather than merely asserted. Then tell me which reading, if any, my group member would need in order to draw his conclusion that our two forecasters' continuing disagreement shows irrationality on someone's part -- and whether that reading is one anyone should accept. Finally, I would like to know what the strongest version of the slogan would license an outsider to conclude from observing two informed, sincere people who still disagree after full discussion: that one is irrational, that the preconditions of the result fail, or something else entirely.
