Last month, in a tenure-file discussion I participated in, a colleague offered a thirty-second hypothetical that reversed the room's tentative verdict, and I have been uneasy ever since about how easily one well-told story out-argued an hour of documented evidence. I want to understand the mechanics of that kind of persuasion, so I would like one built to order.

Construct an intuition pump to the following specifications. First, the setting must be realistic and professional — a hospital, a courtroom, a hiring committee, a school board — with no trolleys, no brain transplants, and no perfect predictors. Second, on first presentation the scenario must reliably push a reflective reader toward a definite verdict (for instance, that a policy is fair, or that a decision was justified), and that verdict must be at least arguably wrong: the pump should mislead, not merely persuade. Third, the misleading force must trace to exactly one load-bearing feature of the telling — a single identifiable device such as the order in which information is revealed, the point of view from which the events are narrated, the naming or labeling of the parties, a default option smuggled in as the baseline, one vivid case standing in for a base rate, or a quiet slide between two senses of a key term. Fourth, the scenario may not lie by omission: every fact it asserts must be accurate, so the manipulation has to live in the presentation rather than in suppressed facts. Fifth, when that one feature is surfaced and neutralized — the story retold with the device removed and everything else held fixed — the reader's intuition should flip or at least weaken markedly.

After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it meets each specification: state the predicted first-read verdict and explain why the device produces it; give the decontaminated control version and explain why the verdict changes; say which device is doing the work and how you ruled out the alternatives; identify one well-known thought experiment from the philosophical literature that runs on the same device, and say whether the parallel vindicates or embarrasses it; and give the strongest defense a proponent of your pump could offer — the argument that the alleged sleight is actually a morally or epistemically relevant feature and that the decontaminated version is the one that distorts. Close with what, if anything, this exercise implies about when thought experiments may legitimately be used in real deliberation of the kind I described.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single device you chose and the scenario in one sentence — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of this request.
