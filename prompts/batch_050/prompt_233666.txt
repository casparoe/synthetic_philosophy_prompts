I teach a second-year philosophy of biology module, about 18 students, and I've got one 75-minute session to spend on signaling before we get to the reading. I want an in-class game that makes them feel the logic of the handicap principle in their gut before anyone names it -- specifically the part that everyone glosses over, which is that a signal isn't convincing because it's expensive, it's convincing because it's expensive *for the weak and affordable for the strong*, and that cheap talk collapses the moment lying is free.

So: design a game with actual rules, materials, and a payoff scheme that doesn't use real money (points or tokens are fine, maybe a small prize for the top scorer if you think that helps incentives). Ideally it runs in rounds. In the early rounds senders should be able to claim anything they want for free, and I want the students to watch trust evaporate in real time as receivers learn that the claims carry no information. Then later rounds introduce some kind of costly display -- burning tokens, paying points to send -- and I want them to discover, not be told, that communication only restarts once the cost is *differential*, i.e. genuinely cheaper for the "strong" type than the "weak" type. If you set the game up so the display costs everyone the same flat amount, I'd like you to flag that as the classic design mistake, because it is one.

Then give me a debrief plan that maps what happened in each phase onto the handicap principle and onto signaling theory more generally -- where the peacock's tail, the stotting gazelle, and the degree-on-the-CV fit in. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing: students colluding, someone gaming the token economy, the strong types not realising why they should bother signaling). And be honest about what the game can't reproduce -- my worry is that real biological signals evolve by selection rather than being chosen by strategic agents, and my students might walk away thinking gazelles are running cost-benefit analyses, so tell me how to close that gap in ten minutes at the end.
