I sit on the acquisitions committee of a mid-sized contemporary art museum, and we've got a vote coming up on a piece that's split us down the middle. The artist wants to install two polished steel spheres in a perfectly square, all-white room, one on each side, exactly equidistant from the center. The foundry has machined them to be as identical as physically possible -- same alloy, same finish, same weight to within a hair. No markings, no serial numbers, nothing. The artist's statement says the whole point is that there's no way, even in principle, of telling them apart, and that the work "is" the pair, not either sphere on its own.

Half the comittee loves it. The other half, led by a trustee who did some philosophy in college and has not let this go for three meetings straight, argues the piece is conceptually broken. His line is basically the Leibniz thing: if two objects share literally every property, then there's nothing that makes them two rather than one, so the artist is promising something he can't deliver. He keeps bringing up Max Black's two-spheres scenario, though honestly I think he's got the dialectic backwards -- wasn't Black using the spheres against the principle rather than for it? Anyway, the trustee wants us to either reject the acquisition or condition it on the foundry engraving a tiny distinguishing mark on the underside of each sphere, which the artist flatly refuses.

There's also a practical wrinkle that I suspect is secretly the philosophical crux: the install crew keeps asking which sphere goes on the left pedestal, and the artist says it doesn't matter and also that it matters enormously -- the work has to be genuinely two spheres, not one sphere shown twice or some mirror trick.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote: acquire, reject, or acquire with conditions, with an actual bottom-line recommendation. It needs to say whether the trustee's objection holds up (I suspect not, but I can't fully articulate why), whether "which one goes on the left" is a real question about the work or just a confusion, and what the museum would actually be buying if we say yes -- one object, two objects, an arrangement, or something else. Don't just side with me though: if the trustee has a genuine point buried in there, give it its due. And at the end, say how confident you are in the recommendation overall and flag the single claim in the memo you're least sure of.
