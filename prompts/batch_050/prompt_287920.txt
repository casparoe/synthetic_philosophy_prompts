Had a long argument with a friend about the centipede game last night and we got nowhere, so I want an outside verdict. Her argument, reconstructed as best I can:

(1) In the final round of any game with a known finite number of rounds, a rational player just takes the payoff-maximizing move — take in the centipede, defect in the last round of a finite prisoner's dilemma. There's no future left to protect, so this is uncontroversial.
(2) Both players know (1), so whatever happens in the second-to-last round can't change what happens in the last one — the future is already pinned down. That makes the second-to-last round effectively the last round, and the same reasoning applies there.
(3) Iterate all the way back: rational play means defecting (or taking) from move one.
(4) Nothing in this reasoning used anything special about lab games, just rationality plus mutual knowledge of it, so it applies to every finite interaction.
(5) But that proves way too much: it says cooperation is never rational in any relationship with a known end date — a final deal before retirment, a last group project, an endgame alliance — and tons of that cooperation is obviously smart.
(6) So some step in the chain is wrong.

She says the only weak point is (5)'s "obviously" and we should just bite the bullet. I feel like something slippery happens between (1) and (2) — the pinning-down move — but I can't articulate what's actually wrong with it, since (1) really does look untouchable. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? And if the argument is actually sound, tell me which premise I'm cheapest off dropping.
