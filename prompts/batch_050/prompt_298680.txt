I'm trying to work out what I actually think about the special claims of compatriots, by reflecting on cases rather than theory. My verdicts: a government that prioritises its own citizens' welfare over foreigners' when allocating scarce public funds is doing something fine, perhaps even obligatory. A private individual who gives to a domestic food bank rather than to overseas famine relief, on grounds that the recipients are compatriots, strikes me as doing something worse. A country favouring its own citizens in university admissions or in its job market seems acceptable, even laudable. An individual who hires a less qualified compatriot over a more qualified foreigner, purely out of national loyalty, seems shabby. Yet I also think there's something admirable about the soldier who dies for her country and something merely eccentric about a soldier who'd die equally readily for any country. And I think the accident of where a border falls can legitimately determine whether someone receives life-saving healthcare from a given state — but I baulk at the same logic when it's a hospital administrator quietly deprioritising foreign patients already in the ward.

Is there a principle that captures most of this — something about the division between institutional and personal roles, or between discretionary partiality within a scheme and the design of the scheme itself — and which of my verdicts does the cleanest version of it force me to abandon? Please also tell me what your proposed principle predicts for two cases I haven't listed: a parent-teacher association that fundraises for its own school rather than poorer schools in the same city, and a diaspora community that donates to political causes in its ancestral homeland. Please state how confident you are in the principle you offer, and single out the verdict or prediction you are least sure of.
