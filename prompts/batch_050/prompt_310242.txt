I teach the second-year survey course in ancient philosophy at my university, and next term I must cover Aristotle's treatment of money and exchange — the discussions in Politics I.8–10 and Nicomachean Ethics V.5 — in a single ninety-minute lecture. My students will have read some Plato and a little of the Ethics, but their economic intuitions come from half-remembered notions of supply, demand, and just deserts. I do not want the lecture written for me; I want the pedagogy planned.

Plan the explanatory arc with the following in view. First, the dependency structure: which pieces of the argument must be in place before subsequent ones can even be intelligible. My instinct is that the account of the household and its telos, and the distinction between the two arts of acquisition, have to precede anything about coinage or lending, but I would like the full ordering laid out with reasons, including where the account of reciprocal justice in exchange belongs relative to the Politics material. Second, the carrying example. I want one sustained exchange scenario — I am considering a cobbler and a farmer who need each other's goods, first bartering and later using coin — that can bear the weight of the whole lecture: natural versus unlimited acquisition, the conventional character of money, and the usury objection. Tell me what such an example must contain and must lack to serve this purpose, and whether my choice is the right one or whether something else (e.g., starting with usury because it is vivid) would be better, with reasons. Third, the misconception map. I expect at least the following: that Aristotle's objection to interest is simply that lending rates are unfair; that his talk of money as conventional means he thinks money is a mere fiction with no real function; and that he is working with some proto-labor theory of value, since the students know Marx and assume every theory of exchange must measure by labor. For each, tell me where in the sequence it will predictably form and exactly where I should head it off. Fourth, a single closing question I can pose that will reveal whether the students grasped the structural character of Aristotle's objection to usury — that it concerns the use of money as a generator of money rather than any defect of particular lenders or rates — rather than having merely absorbed a moralism about greed. Finally, tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and say that I am refusing it: I am inclined to set aside the scholarly dispute over whether Aristotle has anything properly called a theory of economic value at all, and I would like to know whether that deferral, and any others you would recommend, is defensible.
