You are an experienced university examiner and a professional philosopher specializing in action theory and practical rationality, setting the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on intention and rational choice. I need one essay question on Kavka's toxin puzzle that a student cannot answer by reciting the standard setup (the billionaire, the midnight intention, the painless-but-miserable day of illness, the payment made before any drinking is required). Think carefully before you write: first, privately brainstorm the three or four most common ways students go wrong on this puzzle, then design the question so that each of those confusions is exposed rather than hidden.

To force engagement beyond summary, build the question around the following variant, which the students will not have seen. In the Double-or-Nothing version, everything is as in the original case except that the billionaire pays ten million rather than one million, and only on condition that you both sincerely intend at midnight to drink the toxin and actually drink it at noon. The exam question should have three parts: (a) ask students to state, as precisely as they can, why on the standard diagnosis a rational agent cannot simply form the midnight intention in the original case, making explicit which premise does the work -- whether it is a claim that intending requires believing one will act, a claim that reasons to intend cannot float free of reasons to act, or something else; (b) ask students to analyze the Double-or-Nothing variant and to argue what it reveals about where the original puzzle's difficulty is really located -- specifically, whether the variant's tractability (if it is tractable) shows that the original problem was never about intention as such but about a mismatch between reasons for intending and reasons for acting; (c) ask students to apply their answer to the case that motivated the puzzle: a deterrent policy that requires sincerely intending to do something (say, retaliate) that, when the moment came, one would have no reason to do. They must say whether the toxin puzzle shows such a policy to be irrational, impossible, or merely hard, and whether the deterrent case differs from the toxin case in any way that matters. Allocate marks across the three parts (say 12, 14, and 14 of 40) and specify a 90-minute time limit.

Then provide a model answer of roughly 650 words that takes definite positions rather than surveying the terrain: it should commit on which premise makes the original case hard, on what the variant shows, and on the deterrence application, while acknowledging the strongest point on the other side of each.

Finally, provide a marking scheme that does real discriminative work. Name at least four specific moves an A answer must contain that a B answer will lack (for example, distinguishing the impossibility of forming the intention from its irrationality, or noticing that in the variant the reason to drink at noon is itself a product of the midnight intention, which raises the question whether bootstrapped reasons are genuine reasons). Also name at least three specific, identifiable mistakes that should cost marks regardless of the answer's overall conclusion (for example, treating the puzzle as merely about willpower or weakness of will, or assuming without argument that the money is a reason to drink rather than a reason to intend). The scheme should be usable by a grader who has not thought about the puzzle before reading it.
