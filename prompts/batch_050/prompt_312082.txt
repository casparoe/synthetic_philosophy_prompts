Last weekend I had a two-hour argument with a friend that I now want to stage in my classroom rather than summarize. The argument was about whether a constitution — or any rule system — can genuinely bind itself. My friend claimed that entrenchment settles the matter: a legal order can declare certain provisions unamendable, and if it also declares the entrenchment clause itself unamendable, those provisions are locked forever. I argued that this is an illusion. Any rule purporting to make itself unamendable can be attacked by a later change that first targets the entrenchment, and at that point the question of which rule really governs has no answer internal to the system. We went in circles about eternity clauses, self-entrenched rules, and whether the procedure for changing rules can itself be changed by that very procedure, and neither of us conceded.

I teach an upper-level philosophy of law seminar (16 students, one 80-minute session). Design an in-class activity — a deliberately stripped-down rule-changing game in the spirit of Nomic — that puts students inside this dispute before they ever hear it formulated. Requirements:

- An initial rule set of no more than six rules, including a scoring rule, a proposal-and-vote procedure, and at least one rule whose wording invites someone to attempt entrenchment or self-amendment. Include a win condition or small non-monetary stakes so that students actually care about outcomes rather than treating rule changes as idle.
- A mechanism by which the facilitator seeds the problem — roles, planted proposals, or a rule that guarantees someone tries to lock a rule, or to amend the amendment procedure itself, within the first twenty minutes. The activity must not depend on a student spontaneously inventing the paradox.
- An explicit protocol for the moment a proposed change conflicts with a rule that claims to govern its own amendment: the students, not the facilitator, should have to issue a ruling, and the ruling should leave a written record the class can inspect in the debrief.
- A timeline for the full 80 minutes, including a hard stop to play even if the game is mid-crisis.
- A debrief plan that maps specific moments of the session onto (a) the paradox of self-entrenchment, (b) the question whether the amendment procedure can amend itself, and (c) whatever in the game played the role of an ultimate standard for which rules count — and whether that standard could itself be captured by the rules or had to remain outside them.
- An honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (students lawyering ambiguities in the initial rules, one player hijacking the session, play bogging down in procedure before the paradox ever surfaces), with countermeasures for each.
- A frank note on which feature of real constitutional self-reference the game cannot reproduce and I will have to supply by lecture afterwards — for instance, whether my authority as facilitator quietly does work that, in a real legal system, nothing can do.

Conclude with a one-paragraph judgment: whose side of the original argument — my friend's or mine — the activity will leave students on, and whether that outcome is a fair test of the dispute or an artifact of the game's design.
