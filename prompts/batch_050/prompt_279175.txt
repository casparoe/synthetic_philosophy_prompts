So my reading group has been going around in circles for like two months on the non-identity problem and climate ethics, and I want a diagnosis of why, not another round of the same arguments.

Quick setup in case it helps: the problem (Parfit's, roughly) is that the people who will exist in 2100 depend on which policies we pick now. A green transition changes who gets born -- different people meet, different kids, different everything down the line. So when the future person in the flooded city says "you wronged me," the reply is: had we decarbonized, *you* specifically wouldn't exist at all. Some different person would be living your life instead. Unless your existence is so bad it's not worth living, you can't say you were harmed by the choice that brought you about. We all get the logic. The fight is over what follows.

Half the group says this shows the real case for climate action can't be about harming future *people*, and that if person-affecting morality is the only kind that makes sense, climate obligations get way weaker than everyone assumes. The other half says that's insane, obviously we can wrong future generations even if we can't wrong identifiable future individuals, and they keep reaching for impersonal principles (make the world go better, whoever's in it) or for group-level harms (we harmed *future people* as a class even if no particular one of them can complain).

What's driving me nuts is that nobody on either side has updated in two months, even though we all agree on the logic of the non-identity argument itself. The person-affecting side keeps saying "harm requires a victim who's worse off than they'd otherwise be" like it's just what the word means, and the impersonal side keeps saying "if your principle implies that rendering the planet worse is morally fine as long as you scramble the identities, your principle is broken" like *that's* just obvious.

My working hypothesis: this isn't a disagreement about climate or about future people at all, it's a disagreement about whether morality has to be cashed out in terms of things that happen to particular people, and neither side can argue for that starting point -- it's just an intuition they're each treating as a definition. So the debate is kind of verbal and kind of bedrock at the same time?

Is that right? Use what each side actually says (including the moves I might have misstated) and tell me what kind of disagreement this really is. And concretely: is there anything that would actually move it -- a distinction that dissolves it, an argument form that could settle the person-affecting question itself, some way of reframing so both sides are answering the same question -- or is this just a place where philosophy runs out and you pick your starting intuition?
