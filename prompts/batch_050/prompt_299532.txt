A friend and I have been arguing for weeks, ever since she read a book defending the view that virtual worlds and the things in them are perfectly real, about which virtual objects and virtual events genuinely exist or genuinely matter. I've been quietly tallying her verdicts, and I want to test whether she has a consistent rule. So far: the table in our VR meeting room is, she says, a real thing -- a real virtual table -- though not a real table, since you can't stub your toe on it. When someone hijacked her account and sold a rare in-game item she could have sold for real money, she insists she was literally robbed, not merely inconvenienced. When another player destroys her avatar in consensual player-versus-player combat, she says no one has been harmed at all. When a coordinated harassment campaign drove a teenager out of her guild, she says real harm was done even though nothing physical happened. And when players exploited a duplication glitch that flooded the in-game economy and wiped out the value of items others had spent hundreds of hours earning, she calls that a real harm too -- her reason being that their invested time was devalued, even though no real-world money changed hands.

Here is the new case. A player spent years building an elaborate virtual rose garden inside a game, then died. Almost no one else plays on that server anymore, the garden is essentially never visited, her family has no interest in the game, and the company is now deleting inactive accounts in accordance with its terms of service. Is deleting the garden the destruction of something real and valuable -- a genuine loss, even if it harms no one -- or is it morally on par with wiping a disused hard drive? Please predict my friend's verdict, identify which general rule her earlier verdicts suggest she is running, and note that I suspect her verdicts are consistent with more than one rule: for instance, a view on which virtual events matter exactly insofar as they set back the interests of real persons, versus a view on which virtual artifacts are real and valuable in virtue of their own structure and the roles they play in their world, regardless of whether anyone currently cares about them, versus a view on which virtual goods are socially constituted kinds whose reality and value depend on an active community's ongoing recognition. Tell me what each of these rules predicts about the rose garden, which is the best fit for her track record overall, and which single further hypothetical case would most efficiently discriminate among the rules that survive.

I'd also like you to be explicit about how confident you are in the prediction you give -- whether you think her verdicts genuinely underdetermine this case -- and to single out the one step in your reasoning where you are least sure of yourself.
