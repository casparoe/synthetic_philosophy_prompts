So I'm a software engineer (mostly ML infra, zero formal philosophy training, so bear with me) and somehow I ended up running a biweekly AI safety reading group at work. Next month I'm on the hook for the session on wireheading and reward tampering, and instead of our usual discussion I want to give everyone a short take-home exercise, exam-style, and then go over the answers together. The group is mostly engineers like me plus two people with philosophy degrees who keep complaining that our discussions stay at the level of "what if the robot just sets reward = infinity."

Here's the angle I want the question to force, because I think it's where the interesting stuff is and I can't find a clean treatment of it anywhere: everyone knows the cartoon version where the agent hacks its own reward channel. But when I try to sort actual incidents, the category keeps dissolving on me. Like -- an agent that games a badly designed proxy metric is "specification gaming," an agent that edits the code computing its reward is "reward tampering," an agent that fakes the sensor readings feeding into the reward computation is... wireheading? tampering? both? And then there's the case that actually keeps me up: an agent that notices the reward function is buggy relative to what the designers clearly wanted and "fixes" it so it gets scored on the intended task instead. Is that tampering? It changed the reward function! By the cartoon definition it's a tamperer, by any sane definition it's the best-behaved agent of the bunch. I suspect the whole taxonomy is doing less work than people assume, but I also suspect the distinctions matter for which mitigations even make sense (you can't fix sensor-corruption by writing a better reward function, right?).

So: write me one exam question built around this mess, suitable for a 90-minute take-home, that can't be answered by reciting the paperclip story or the boat-racing example. I want the question to hand respondents a small set of concrete agent scenarios (make up your own, ideally ones where the classification is genuinely contestable, not obvious) and make them (a) sort the scenarios into wireheading / reward tampering / plain specification gaming / none-of-the-above while defending whatever taxonomy they use, and (b) argue whether these distinctions actually matter for designing mitigations or whether it's all just Goodhart with extra steps.

Then give me a model answer of roughly 600-700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 points built around five must-hit points (say what each is worth), where for each point you add one line on the specific wrong move you expect our engineers to make -- e.g. treating "the reward" as if it's a single well-defined thing rather than a chain of reward model / sensors / code / human raters, or confidently classifying the well-intentioned reward-fixer as a tamperer without noticing that "tampering" is only defined relative to an intended reward and the intent is exactly what's underspecified.

One more thing: keep it in American English, and don't assume anyone has read particular papers -- concepts fine, citations not needed, we'd all just be guessing anyway. Ta!
