I've been reading around the literature on invented traditions and imagined communities — Hobsbawm and Ranger's edited volume The Invention of Tradition, Anderson's Imagined Communities — and a friend helped me distill what seemed like the core debunking insight into an argument. The problem is that when we wrote it down carefully, it seemed to prove that nothing inherited can have any authority at all, which neither of us believes. Here it is:

(1) A tradition claims authority over us by presenting itself as the way "we" have done things since time immemorial: the practice binds us because it is immemorial, and it would not bind us if we did not take it to be.

(2) Historical inquiry shows that most practices making this claim were in fact constructed at specific, often quite recent dates, frequently by identifiable people with identifiable motives — clan tartans and the kilt as a badge of ancient Highland identity, much of the pageantry of constitutional monarchies, national anthems, folk costumes, public holidays.

(3) A practice deliberately constructed at a known date by known agents is not immemorial; the claim in (1) is, for these practices, simply false.

(4) Therefore the authority these traditions claim is illusory: they bind us, if at all, only on some other ground than the one they offer.

(5) But the reasoning does not stop at the famous cases. Every practice had a first performance. Every rule of grammar, every legal norm, every table manner, every moral principle — including the principle that invented traditions are suspect — was at some point introduced by someone for some reason, and before that introduction it was not "how things were done."

(6) So by the same argument, no inherited practice whatsoever can bind us in virtue of its pedigree, since every pedigree begins at a datable act of introduction.

(7) Conclusion: either nothing inherited has any authority — language, law, manners, morals included — or the argument must go wrong somewhere before (6).

I am fairly sure I want to keep the debunking upshot for the nationalist cases in (2)–(4); what I cannot accept is (6), since it would drain authority from norms I have no wish to give up, including the norms I would use to criticize the nationalists. What I cannot see is which step does the damage. Does the argument contain a subtle flaw — an equivocation, a shift in what "immemorial" or "invented" must mean between (2) and (5), a slide in what kind of authority is at issue — or is it valid, so that one of (1) through (4) has to be abandoned? If a premise must go, tell me which rejection is cheapest, what it costs, and why it looked plausible in the first place. And please say what would change your mind: what consideration or counterexample would lead you to locate the fault at a different step, and where your own diagnosis is most likely to fail.
