After forty years as a research geophysicist, I have in retirement been reading formal epistemology, and I keep returning to the family of Bayesian convergence results — the theorems to the effect that agents who begin with different prior credences, but update on a shared and growing body of evidence, must eventually come to agree, their priors being washed out in the limit. I would like you to test this picture against an episode I lived through the tail end of: the plate tectonics revolution. The facts, as I understand them to be common knowledge, are these. Wegener proposed continental drift in the early twentieth century, and the idea was rejected by most geologists and geophysicists for decades, partly on the ground that no known mechanism could plausibly move continents through oceanic crust. Then, in the 1950s and 1960s, paleomagnetic data, the mapping of mid-ocean ridges and seafloor spreading, and the global distribution of earthquake zones produced a remarkably rapid consensus, so that by the end of the 1960s drift had gone from heterodoxy to orthodoxy.

Please analyze this episode as a case study in the washing out of priors, and address the following, in whatever order suits the analysis. First, is the initial resistance to drift best modeled as rational agents updating from low priors — reasonable ones, given the missing mechanism — or as a failure of rationality that the convergence theorems cannot excuse? Second, the theorems guarantee agreement only in the limit, yet the actual community converged within roughly a decade. Does that speed vindicate the Bayesian picture, or does it suggest that the dispute was never really about priors at all, but about likelihoods — that is, about which data were trustworthy, with geophysicists long dismissing the geologists' qualitative evidence and the geologists distrusting instrumental geophysics? Convergence results typically assume the parties agree on what the evidence means; if that assumption is what actually failed and then got repaired, what is left of the washing-out story? Third, the theorems describe a fixed agent updating over time, whereas much of the real shift came through generational turnover — young scientists entering the field with no stake in the old view — which is not the washing out of priors but their replacement. Does that distinction matter to the moral we should draw?

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction, from case to theory. Does the tectonics episode confirm the convergence picture, expose a load-bearing assumption it requires, or show that the theorems, being asymptotic, are simply silent about the timescales on which actual science is decided? I would rather you say plainly where the model does not apply than force it to speak.
