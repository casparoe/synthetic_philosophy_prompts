EXAM QUESTION:
Expressivists hold that, at least in the first instance, sincere utterances of moral sentences give voice to desire-like or conative states of mind rather than to beliefs that the world is a certain way. State the Frege-Geach problem for expressivism, and explain why it poses a distinctive difficulty for expressivist semantics -- that is, why it is not just the general worry that moral sentences, on the expressivist view, fail to be truth-apt. Then sketch one way an expressivist might try to answer the problem (you may draw on proposals associated with Blackburn or Gibbard, or construct your own) and raise one objection to that response.

STUDENT'S ANSWER:
The Frege-Geach problem was first formulated by Gottlob Frege as an objection to expressivist theories of moral language, and was later refined by Peter Geach in his articles in the 1960s. The problem is that moral sentences embed in logically complex contexts. Consider the argument: (1) If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong. (2) Lying is wrong. (3) So, getting your little brother to lie is wrong. This argument is valid in virtue of its form -- it is modus ponens. But on the expressivist view, in premise (2) the phrase "lying is wrong" is asserted and expresses disapproval of lying, whereas in premise (1) it occurs unasserted, as the antecedent of a conditional, and no disapproval is expressed there at all. So the phrase apparently means something different in the two premises, and the argument commits a fallacy of equivocation. The expressivist owes us an account of what "lying is wrong" contributes to premise (1) that keeps it univocal with premise (2) and thereby explains the argument's validity.

This is a deeper difficulty than the mere point that moral sentences are not truth-apt on the expressivist picture, because it concerns the preservation of meaning and inferential form across asserted and unasserted occurrences, not the status of moral sentences considered on their own.

Expressivists have a natural reply available. Once we adopt a deflationary or minimalist conception of truth, there is no obstacle to calling moral sentences true, and once they count as true in the deflated sense, the embedding problem dissolves: the conditional in premise (1) can be understood in the ordinary truth-conditional way, just with a thin notion of truth. Blackburn's quasi-realism, which shows how we can earn the right to truth talk in ethics on an expressivist basis, thereby also resolves Frege-Geach.

Alternatively, for negation, the expressivist can simply say that "lying is not wrong" expresses disapproval of not lying.

However, one might object that accounts like Gibbard's, on which moral judgments express acceptance of norms and a complex moral sentence expresses a state of being "tied together" in certain attitudes, merely stipulate a formal structure of attitudes without explaining why reasoning that respects that structure counts as genuine inference rather than mere causal transitions between sentiments.
