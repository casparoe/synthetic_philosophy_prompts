I serve on my university's licensing committee, which is deciding whether to renew a contract with an apparel supplier whose garments are sewn in factories in South Asia paying wages that, while legal and above local alternatives, are a small fraction of what comparable work commands here. Two fellow committee members got into an extended argument at our last meeting, and it never resolved. Elena opened with the claim that the whole debate rests on a mistake: the workers take these jobs voluntarily and prefer them to subsistence farming or informal scavenging, so both they and the supplier benefit from the arrangement, and any deal that leaves both parties better off than they were cannot count as mistreatment; canceling the contract would punish the workers by removing their best option. Marcus replied that mutual benefit proves nothing by itself, since a transaction can improve both parties' positions and still be exploitative -- a destitute person selling a kidney cheaply to the only interested buyer is made better off relative to having no deal, yet the buyer has plainly taken unfair advantage of her desperation, and what matters morally is how the surplus from the cooperation is divided, not merely whether both sides gain. Elena came back with two points: first, that fairness requires a baseline, and since the supplier did not cause the workers' poverty, it is incoherent to hold the supplier responsible for terms that are unfair only relative to a background it inherited rather than created; second, that demanding wages far above the local market simply pushes production toward automation or toward factories elsewhere, so the "fair division" standard Marcus wants is self-defeating from the workers' own point of view, and besides, refusing to trade with people whose choices you disapprove of amounts to treating their agency as worthless. Marcus answered that consent given under desperate circumstances does not do the justificatory work Elena assumes, and that her efficiency argument quietly changes the subject from justice to beneficence -- the question was never whether he could improve the workers' lives but whether he is permitted to participate in terms he would regard as abusive if the positions were reversed, and obligations of justice bind even when honoring them is not Pareto-improving. Elena's last move was to turn that point around: if the issue is justice rather than beneficence, then justice condemns everyone who fails to help, including Marcus, not just the party willing to deal, and it is arbitrary to single out the one participant who actually offers the workers something while all the bystanders offering nothing get a pass.

I genuinely cannot tell who has the better of this, and the committee votes in three weeks. Please adjudicate the dispute as it actually unfolded: tell me, point by point, who is right about whether mutual benefit suffices for permissibility, who is right about the fairness-of-division standard and the baseline problem, whose position on consent under desperation survives scrutiny, and whether Elena's final tu quoque lands or misses. I would also like to know what, if anything, both of them are overlooking -- some consideration neither raised that would change how at least one of these sub-questions should be answered. Where a point is a genuine draw, say so rather than forcing a verdict, and be specific about why.
