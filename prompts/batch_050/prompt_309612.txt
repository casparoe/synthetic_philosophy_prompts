I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a long feature on a question philosophers of mathematics keep circling: whether mathematics is, in some defensible sense, an empirical or quasi-empirical enterprise. The centerpiece of the piece is Imre Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations, the classroom dialogue in which successive proofs and counterexamples batter Euler's conjecture that for any polyhedron, vertices minus edges plus faces equals two. To keep the article honest, I am trying to map the full repertoire of responses a proof-centered community can have when a cherished theorem meets a counterexample.

From the dialogue, I count five such responses:

1. Surrender — treat the counterexample as decisive and abandon the conjecture altogether, on the naive-falsificationist model of how refutation is supposed to work in the natural sciences.

2. Monster-barring — protect the theorem by contracting the definition of its central term, so that the offending object (the hollow cube, the star polyhedra) is simply no longer counted as a "polyhedron." The theorem survives, but only by stipulation and with a shrunken domain.

3. Exception-barring — keep the original definitions but cordon off the exceptions, restricting the conjecture to whatever class of polyhedra happens to be safe, without examining the proof to learn why the exceptions arise.

4. Monster-adjustment — reinterpret the counterexample itself, recounting its vertices, edges, and faces or reclassifying the object, until it no longer refutes the conjecture, as with the pair of tetrahedra joined at a single vertex or edge.

5. Lemma incorporation — the response Lakatos endorses: subject the proof to step-by-step analysis, use global counterexamples to hunt for the hidden or "guilty" lemma on which the proof tacitly relies, and rebuild the conjecture with that condition made explicit, so that counterexamples stop being monsters and become instruments for improving the theorem; in its full form this yields genuinely new, proof-generated concepts, such as the notion of a polyhedron that can be stretched onto the surface of a sphere.

As far as I can tell, these exhaust the responses Lakatos stages, but the article would be stronger if I could say whether the space of possible responses is actually larger. So: can you propose a sixth response to the refutation of a mathematical conjecture that appears nowhere in Proofs and Refutations and is not a rewording, special case, or combination of the five above? Please describe it concretely — ideally by showing how a community practicing it would have handled the counterexamples to the Euler conjecture — and explain how it relates to the five responses on my list. Then give me your honest judgment of whether it is a respectable way for mathematics to proceed, and what adopting it would imply for the quasi-empirical picture of mathematics that the dialogue is usually taken to support.

Please write the answer in American English.
