Then you basically multiply the three scores and fund whatever comes out on top.

What I want is for you to build me a thought experiment that stress-tests this. Specifically: construct one scenario where a cause scores *honestly* high on all three factors, but funding it is still obviously a mistake. Constraints:

2. The high scores can't be fake. No lying advocates, no measurement error, no "the charity turns out to be corrupt." The problem really is that big, the intervention really does reliably deliver the outcome per dollar, and almost nobody really is working on it.
3. The reason not to fund it can't be about the funder personally (like "you'd be bad at executing it" or "your comparative advantage is elsewhere"). It has to be a reason that no one should fund it, full stop.
4. The scenario has to generate the verdict, not stipulate it. Don't just write "and funding it backfires" — the reader should be able to see the mechanism for themselves.
5. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph.

For context on what I'm using this for: I'm trying to figure out whether the framework needs a fourth factor (timing? feedback effects? something else?) or whether the three already cover everything if you apply them carefully enough. So a case that only works because someone scored the factors sloppily would be cheating — it has to break even when scored honestly and competently.

After the scenario, walk me through:
- which feature of the case is doing the actual work,
- what happens if you remove that feature — does it collapse back into a normal cause worth funding?,
