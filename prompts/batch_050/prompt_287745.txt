I'm finishing an undergraduate degree split between philosophy and cognitive science, and for my capstone paper I need to commit to a position on the structure of concepts: prototypes, exemplars, the theory theory, or some principled hybrid. The trouble is that every textbook presentation sounds convincing while I'm reading it, so I've tried to force my actual commitments into the open by polling my own verdicts on cases. I'd like you to tell me what single account (or tightly constrained hybrid) best captures the pattern, which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and what it predicts about cases I haven't considered.

My verdicts: (1) Penguins are every bit as much birds as robins are; the fact that people are slower to classify them is a fact about recognition heuristics, not about the concept itself. (2) A child who has encountered exactly two dogs, both poodles, and consequently calls a wolf a dog while refusing to call a German shepherd one, nonetheless possesses a genuine dog-concept — badly calibrated, but hers. (3) When a botanist says a tomato is a fruit and a cook insists it's a vegetable, they are genuinely disagreeing rather than talking past each other, and I side with the botanist: the cook is wrong, not merely speaking another dialect. (4) 'Things to rescue from a burning house' strikes me as a real concept, formed on the spot, with clear members and clear non-members, even though no one could have acquired it from stored instances. (5) If everything we call a cat turned out to be a radio-controlled automaton planted from somewhere else, the word 'cat' would still refer to those things; we would have learned something shocking about cats, not lost the word's referent. (6) The Pope is not a bachelor, and neither is a man in a decades-long unmarried partnership, whatever the eligibility gloss says. (7) Here is where I suspect I'm in trouble: on the one hand, I want to say that possessing the concept grandmother requires grasping the genealogical structure, so that someone who thinks grandmothers are simply kind old women who bake lacks the concept entirely; on the other hand, I think a five-year-old who believes that all grandmothers wear glasses and bake cookies, though hers does neither, still refers to her grandmother with the word and plainly knows who her grandmother is — she has the concept. I can't see how to hold both halves of (7), and I suspect one of my other verdicts is quietly inconsistent with (3) as well.

My own tentative principle, which you're welcome to demolish, is that each concept is governed by whichever component the surrounding practice treats as least revisable — statistical information where the practice tolerates surprises, causal or functional structure where it doesn't — with childhood possession being a kind of deferential loan. Test that against my cases, repair it if it's repairable, and if it isn't, give me the strongest alternative that sacrifices as few verdicts as possible. In each case, note which specific verdict motivates each clause of your account. Finally, tell me what your account predicts about two cases I haven't listed: a child who uses 'money' only for coins, and a speaker who is convinced, against all evidence, that whales are fish — does she have our concept of a whale, or hers?
