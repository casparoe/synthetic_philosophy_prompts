I work on a small team that evaluates language models before release, and a disagreement keeps recurring in our internal reviews for which I lack a name. Suppose we test a model's refusals against a large, fixed collection of harmful requests and find that it fails on one request in ten thousand; we then report that number as evidence that the system is safe. A colleague objects that the figure is nearly meaningless: the moment the system is deployed, the people seeking harmful outputs will not sample from our fixed collection. They will probe, share what works, build on one another's partial successes, and eventually converge on the small region of inputs where the system fails, so the effective failure rate against real users will be far higher than the measured one, no matter how large our test set was. What strikes me is that both sides are doing legitimate statistics; they disagree only about what the relevant population of attempts is, and specifically about whether that population should be treated as fixed independently of the defense or as something that reorganizes itself in response to the defense.

Once I noticed the pattern here, I began seeing it elsewhere. Antibiotics are trialed against bacteria as they currently exist, but prescribing them changes which bacteria exist. A tax authority audits returns, finds a low evasion rate, publishes its audit criteria, and watches evasion migrate to wherever the criteria do not look. Bridge engineers, by contrast, seem to me genuinely entitled to a fixed failure probability, because storms do not study the bridge. So the underlying distinction appears to be something like: failures whose frequency one can estimate from past data versus failures whose frequency is produced by the very act of deploying and publicizing the countermeasure, because the source of the challenge adapts to it.

My question is whether this gap — between reliability measured against a fixed population of challenges and reliability against a population of challenges that is generated or reshaped by the countermeasure itself — has an established name, and in which literature it lives. I am aware of adjacent ideas: computer science has worst-case versus average-case analysis, statistics has distribution shift, economics has the complaint that one cannot extrapolate from data gathered under one policy regime to behavior under another, and there is the familiar saying about a measure ceasing to be a good measure once it becomes a target. But each of these seems to capture only part of what I mean: distribution shift need not be caused by the defense, worst-case analysis concerns guarantees rather than the process by which failures come to be concentrated, and the measure-becomes-target idea concerns proxies collapsing rather than challenges adapting. If there is a precise term for the specific phenomenon I describe, I would like to know it, how it relates to each of those neighbors, and where its boundaries lie — does it require a literally intelligent adversary, or do evolving bacteria and strategically shifting taxpayers qualify? And if no established term exists, what would you call it, and how would you define it so that the bridge case falls clearly outside? Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as though writing a letter.
