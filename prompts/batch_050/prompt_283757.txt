I am preparing a seventy-minute session for mathematically capable first-year undergraduates on undefined expected values, using a Pasadena-style lottery. The students understand finite expectation, elementary probability, and basic infinite series, but they have no measure theory and are inclined to assume that every infinite sum has a definite answer if its terms are listed carefully enough. I do not want an explanation of the paradox itself; I want a plan for how I should explain it.

The carrying example will be an invented Meridian lottery. A fair coin is tossed until the first tail appears. If that happens on toss \(n\), the player receives \((-1)^{n+1}2^n/n\) ducats. Thus the possible payoffs grow explosively while alternating in sign, and their probability-weighted contributions form the alternating harmonic series. Design the explanatory arc around this example: what must be established before the game is introduced, whether the payoff formula should appear before or after its probability-weighted contributions, how to move from finite truncation to infinite expectation, and why the distinction between conditional and absolute convergence must precede any discussion of a fair price.

Include a misconception map in the order the misconceptions are likely to arise. At minimum, address the beliefs that the lottery’s expectation is \(\ln 2\) because the alternating series sums to \(\ln 2\), that rearranging terms cannot change the value of an infinite sum, that an undefined expectation means the probability distribution is defective, that the positive and negative payoffs simply cancel, and that this is merely the St. Petersburg game with signs added. For each misconception, identify where in the lesson it should be anticipated or corrected, and give the intervention I should use rather than merely saying that it is wrong.

Finish with a check for understanding that would show the students have grasped why the expectation is undefined rather than infinite, conditionally valued, or simply controversial. Also specify what I should deliberately leave out at this level—such as measure-theoretic integration or rival proposals for assigning the game a value—and how I should mark those omissions without making the central conclusion seem provisional.
