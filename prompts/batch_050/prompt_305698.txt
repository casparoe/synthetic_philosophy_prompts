Write a deadpan military field circular, roughly 600 words, from the general staff of an army that has resolved a scandal over scouts making overconfident reports by decreeing that all intelligence must henceforth be communicated strictly as conditionals: never "the enemy is probably in the north," always "if you are in the blue sector, the enemy is probably in the north." The circular records the consequences in numbered sections: the quartermaster's office complaining that soldiers' credences now drift in ways nobody ordered or can justify; a court of inquiry into a private whose probability of being shelled rose substantially after hearing a conditional that everyone at the inquiry agrees told him nothing about shelling; rival memos from two staff mathematicians proposing different bookkeeping rules for updating on such reports, each demonstrating that the other's rule gives absurd results in some corner case; and a closing recommendation that conditionals be banned, undermined by the observation that the recommendation itself can only be stated conditionally. The humor should come from the actual shape of the problem — the two natural update rules disagreeing, the learned conditional seeming evidentially irrelevant to the proposition whose probability nonetheless shifts, the staff's inability to say which rule is correct — so that a reader who knows the Judy Benjamin problem recognizes it instantly and finds the satire fair, while a reader who doesn't still gets a funny piece of bureaucratic absurdism. The voice should stay flat and official throughout, with no winking, and the generals should never quite understand what has gone wrong.
