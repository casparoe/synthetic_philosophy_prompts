So I co-run a decision theory reading group at my uni (mix of philosophy and cs undergrads, plus a couple of very keen postdocs who mostly lurk). Everyone has done Newcomb's problem and can roughly say what CDT and EDT are, but nobody has touched anything updateless. Next session is on me, 75 minutes, topic: counterfactual mugging and the XOR blackmail letter. I want to teach these as one idea, not two weird puzzles that happen to rhyme.

Don't explain the two cases to me -- plan how I should run the session. Specifically:

- The dependency order. What has to be in their heads before the mugging even parses as a problem rather than a scam (my guess: they need some grip on choosing a policy rather than an act, but maybe that's wrong and something else is load-bearing, e.g. the distinction between "Omega predicted accurately" and "Omega controls the outcome"). Tell me what to assume from their Newcomb knowledge and what has to be rebuilt from scratch.
- Which case goes first and why. My instinct is the counterfactual mugging first because it has no blackmail vibes, then XOR blackmail as the harder sell where paying feels like rewarding extortion. Argue with me if the order should be reversed.
- The one running example to carry the session. Should I anchor on the coin-flip mugging with concrete dollar amounts, or build my own scenario (I've considered a version where Omega offers the deal by email and you only see one branch)? Say what the carrying example must have and must NOT have -- e.g. does letting Omega be a perfect predictor smuggle in too much, and does it matter for the pedagogy whether the coin is flipped before or after you're asked?
- The misconception map, in the order it'll show up in the room. For each one, where exactly in the sequence should I head it off, and is there a misconception I'll get that I haven't listed?
- One question or mini-case to end on that tells me whether they got the shared structure of the two puzzles rather than just memorized that "LDT says pay." Ideally something where the wrong answer is diagnostic -- i.e., their wrong answer tells me which misconception they still hold.
- What to openly refuse to cover at this level (logical counterfactuals? proof-based UDT? the asymptotic-decision-theory stuff?) and how to say "that exists and we're skipping it" without losing the room.
