Got into it with my housemate last night and it's still bugging me. I'd said the proposed gene-drive releases against malaria mosquitoes in West Africa seemed obviously worth trialling, and he laid out this:

(1) It's wrong to deliberately make an irreversible change to a shared ecosystem when you can't foresee the downstream effects.
(2) A self-propagating gene drive is effectively irreversible once released — you can't recall it — and its ecological knock-on effects are precisely the kind we can't foresee.
(3) So releasing the drive is wrong.
(4) But (1) also convicts smallpox eradication: we deliberately drove an entire species out of the shared ecosystem for good, without knowing what that would do to, say, cross-immunity against related poxviruses.
(5) And if (1) convicts smallpox it convicts half of civilisation — antibiotics (resistance is forever), introduced crops — and going the other way it bans de-extinction too, since releasing a revived passenger pigeon is just as irreversable.
(6) Smallpox eradication was plainly right, so (1) is false and the irreversibility objection collapses.

He cheerfully bit the bullet and said maybe smallpox eradication really was wrong and we just got lucky, which I can't take seriously. I tried arguing smallpox was never literally irreversible since samples were kept frozen; he called that a technicality and said the ecological removal was permanent either way. I've got a nagging feeling something slides between (1) and (4) — smallpox felt different from a gene drive — but when he asked me to state the difference as a general principle, I couldn't.

So which step do I give up, and what does each exit cost? If forced I'd rather ditch (1) than accept that smallpox eradication was wrong, but I want to know what I'm paying, because I still have the intuition that there's something recklessly different about a self-propagating drive. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble, and don't restate the argument back at me.
