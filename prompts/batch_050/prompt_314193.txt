I run the weekly discussion sections for an undergraduate course on rational choice. Next Friday, in one fifty-minute section with about fifteen second-years who have just been through the axioms of expected utility theory, I have to teach the money pump argument for the transitivity of preference -- what it shows, and equally what it does not show, about someone whose preferences run in a cycle. Don't rehearse the argument for me; plan how I should teach it.

What has to be in place before the pump makes sense at all (I'm guessing: the idea of a pairwise acceptable trade, the permission to keep trading, and the distinction between a preference ordering and a pattern of actual choices across time), and in what order, given that the students arrive fresh from the axioms and will assume transitivity was simply assumed and then forgotten? Which example should carry the whole session -- some concrete three-option cycle the students can feel in their gut -- and what must it have and lack to do that work? In particular, tell me whether to use small explicit transaction fees or just the trades themselves, since I suspect that choice determines which objections I get later. Then the misconceptions, in the order this cohort will actually produce them: that the argument merely shows the cyclic agent is imprudent rather than irrational; that no real person would keep accepting the trades, so nothing follows; that transitivity is as secure as modus ponens; and the retreat to redescribing the options so that the cycle dissolves. For each, say exactly where in the sequence I should head it off, and which of them I should let a student voice aloud before dismantling. End with one question I can put to the room -- something like asking them to find the smallest change to the setup that blocks the pump while keeping the cycle, and to say whether the friend of intransitivity can adopt that change for free -- and tell me what a good answer sounds like. Also tell me what to bracket at this level and say I'm bracketing: I'm inclined to skip resolute versus sophisticated choice and the diachronic Dutch book entirely, and I'd like to know if that's the right cut.
