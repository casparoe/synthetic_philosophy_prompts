Question:

Does computation by itself make a system sentient? Your answer should explain the mapping or pancomputationalism worry: if “implementing a computation” only means that some observer can map physical events onto computational states, almost anything might count as running almost any program. Distinguish three kinds of similarity:

1. producing the same observable inputs and outputs;
2. instantiating the same abstract state-transition structure; and
3. having the same underlying physical causal and counterfactual organization.

Apply the distinction to a rock, an ordinary computer running a detailed whole-brain emulation, and a room-sized system that answers every question by consulting a prerecorded lookup table. Finally, explain what all this does and does not imply about moral status.

Student answer:

Computational functionalism holds that mentality depends on causal organization rather than biological material. On this view, carbon neurons are not intrinsically special: if silicon components reproduce the right functional relationships, the resulting system could in principle have experiences. So the moral question is not whether something is made of meat, but whether it has the kind of organization associated with consciousness.

The mapping objection challenges that picture by saying that computation is partly in the eye of the beholder. Given a permissive enough code, the changing charge distributions in a rock could be mapped onto the states of a simulated brain. Pancomputationalism is the claim that every physical object literally runs every possible computation. Since that would make rocks and weather systems just as sentient as brains, the bare appeal to computation cannot be enough.

A better functionalism should therefore require a non-gerrymandered connection between physical states and computational states. The mapping has to track stable causal mechanisms, not merely be invented after the fact. It should also support counterfactuals: had a different signal entered the system, the relevant internal mechanisms would have taken a different path. This gives us a principled reason to distinguish an organized brain from a rock whose random molecular motions can be redescribed as a computation.

The whole-brain emulation running on an ordinary computer is the strongest candidate for sentience. If it reproduces the causal organization of a brain at the right level of detail, then a computational functionalist should probably count it as conscious. The fact that the program is “only a simulation” does not settle the issue. Searle’s Chinese Room shows that formal symbol manipulation by itself cannot create understanding, and therefore proves that no digital simulation can be conscious, but it leaves open whether a sufficiently detailed emulation might be.

The lookup-table system is more complicated. Externally it may behave exactly like the emulation for every tested input. Still, it does not compute its answers in the way a brain does. It merely searches a giant list of prerecorded conversations. However, if the lookup table has the same abstract input-output transition graph as the brain emulation, then it necessarily possesses the same causal organization. Any differences are just differences of speed or engineering, so the counterfactual worry is ultimately beside the point.

The rock is the weakest candidate because the mapping from its physical states to mental states would be wildly arbitrary and would not support the right counterfactuals. Rejecting rock consciousness therefore does not require rejecting machine consciousness.

The upshot is that computation is relevant to moral status only when it is connected to sentience. Intelligence or fluent conversation alone would not be enough, since a system might be useful or behaviorally convincing without experiencing anything. Given our uncertainty, we should extend some moral caution to systems that plausibly instantiate consciousness-supporting organization, while remembering that behavioral imitation by itself is weak evidence.

I’m not looking for a general reaction or a thumbs-up/thumbs-down. Please make the assessment point by point, especially where the answer sounds sophisticated but may be glossing over an important distinction.
