I'm a novelist, and the book I'm working on has a minor character modeled pretty loosely on Kim Davis -- the Kentucky county clerk who in 2015, right after Obergefell legalized same-sex marriage, refused to issue any marriage licenses at all rather than sign them for gay couples, and spent a few days in jail for contempt before deputies started issuing them. I want the character to be philosophically interesting rather than a cartoon villain or a cartoon martyr, so here's what I need from you: analyze that episode through Rawls's framework on religion in public life. Public reason, the duty of civility, the proviso that lets comprehensive doctrines in provided public reasons get offered in due course, his distinction between what you owe as a citizen versus what you owe when you're the one exercising coercive state power. What does the framework say Davis should have done? And does it say the same thing about the ordinary citizens who rallied to her side and saw her as a conscientious objector, or is their position actually different under the theory?

Then run it the other direction, because that's the part that matters for my novel. What does the episode show about the theory? A few things I keep circling: whether public reason as Rawls sets it up is even psychologically available to someone whose deepest commitments are religious -- whether the demand to translate your real reasons into reasons "others could reasonably accept" isn't itself a kind of burden the theory quietly refuses to count; whether Davis's move of shutting the window for everyone, straight couples included, changes the Rawlsian verdict at all or is a red herring; and whether her situation is better handled by the liberal tradition of conscientious objection, which I gather Rawls has things to say about too, or whether that category is reserved for citizens defying the law and can't stretch to cover the officials who are supposed to be the law. If Rawls is just silent on some of this, tell me where, rather than forcing him to speak.
