I am a novelist, three books into a career, and I have become preoccupied with something I first noticed in my own drafts. Early on I invented a private category I call the threshold scene: a character pausing at a doorway, gate, or checkpoint before crossing. Over successive novels the category has slipped. A chapter in which a woman lets a phone ring out became, in my notes, a threshold scene; so did a border crossing that never happens, and a pause before a confession. My editor insists some of these are not really threshold scenes at all, and when I try to state the rule that would settle which ones count, I find I cannot produce necessary and sufficient conditions that include everything my intuition admits and exclude everything it rejects. Something analogous happens at the level of the book as a whole: my publisher has successively labeled my current manuscript literary fiction, speculative fiction, and a thriller, and the shelving decision seems to be made not by checking the book against a definition of any of these but by resemblance to particular neighboring books — it is a thriller insofar as it sits well next to certain other titles, and the center of gravity of the category itself shifts as new titles appear.

From this I have arrived at a position, roughly the one running from Wittgenstein on family resemblance through prototype research in psychology to Douglas Hofstadter's claim that analogy is the engine of thought: concepts are essentially fluid. Membership in a category, on my view, is governed by analogical pressure — a situation is drawn into a concept the way a planet is drawn by gravity, and the concept is deformed in turn by what it captures — and the slippage I observe in my threshold scenes is not a defect of fuzzy everyday categories, to be engineered away with better definitions, but the core mechanism by which all concepts, including apparently crisp ones, work and grow. Even a word like "mother" or "chair" or "number" is, I want to say, the same kind of thing as my threshold scene, only older and more settled. I take this view to be vindicated daily in my practice, since the slippage is precisely where the creative work happens.

What I would like to know is what would count against it. What evidence — from developmental or cognitive psychology, from linguistics, from how experts categorize in mature disciplines, or from reflection on microdomains like my own — would show that some concepts really are rule-governed, or that slippage is a peripheral phenomenon riding on a stable definitional core? Please distinguish defeaters that would be fatal from ones that would merely be awkward. I am particularly interested in what a committed fluidity theorist would themselves concede as counting against the view, as opposed to what its critics allege: would it be damaged if mathematical and legal concepts turned out to be genuinely rule-based, or would the theorist simply quarantine those as degenerate cases, and is that quarantine legitimate? Finally, I worry the view may have been qualified into unfalsifiability, since any apparently rule-governed concept can be redescribed as analogy that has temporarily frozen — so please tell me whether the position risks anything at all, and if it does not, whether that is a vice in a thesis of this generality. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which of them my own little archive of threshold scenes could in principle supply.
