Last term I took a seminar on the philosophy of pain, and the reading that has stayed with me is not a piece of analytic philosophy but a short Buddhist discourse. In the Sallatha Sutta (Saṃyutta Nikāya 36.6, translated from the Pali by Thanissaro Bhikkhu), the Buddha says: "Just as if they were to shoot a man with an arrow and, right afterward, were to shoot him with another one, so that he would feel the pains of two arrows; in the same way, when touched with a feeling of pain, the uninstructed run-of-the-mill person sorrows, grieves, & laments, beats his breast, becomes distraught. So he feels two pains, physical & mental. ... Now, the well-instructed disciple of the noble ones, when touched with a feeling of pain, does not sorrow, grieve, or lament, does not beat his breast or become distraught. So he feels one pain: physical, but not mental." A friend who has done long meditation retreats insists this gets the badness of pain exactly right: the arrow wound is mere sensation, and everything bad lives in the second, reactive dart. I want to test that claim against my own case intuitions, because when I actually run through examples, my verdicts refuse to line up.

Here they are. (1) My tension headache last week was bad, and its badness seemed to shrink and swell in proportion to how much I minded it. (2) A patient with pain asymbolia after a stroke still feels the burn when her hand rests on a hot pan: she rates the intensity as before, calls it pain, but smiles, does not withdraw her hand, and sincerely says it does not trouble her. My verdict is that nothing bad is happening to her in the pain itself, whatever is bad about the tissue damage. (3) A postoperative patient on morphine reports that the pain is exactly as it was but no longer bothers him; his pain strikes me as much less bad than before the drip, though the sensation is unchanged. (4) My neighbor who swims in ice water genuinely relishes the burn and would feel cheated without it; his pain is not bad, and if anything is good for him. (5) I can imagine a drug producing pure anguish — overwhelming distress and an urge to escape — with no painful sensation at all; that state seems terrible, worse than many pains. (6) But now: a kidnapped woman is injected against her will with something that leaves the felt pain of what follows fully intact while abolishing all caring. She feels everything and minds none of it; stipulate that the drug leaves no memory and no after-effects. Setting aside the kidnapper's wrongdoing and asking only about the pain itself, my verdict flips: her hours of un-mind­ed pain seem to me dreadfully bad. Yet I cannot find what separates her from the patient in (2). Both women's indifference was imposed from outside — one by a lesion, one by a needle — and both, beforehand, would have minded exactly as much as each other. So the difference cannot be what either would have felt, and apparently cannot be whether her condition was voluntarily acquired. Does it matter simply that one indifference was induced by someone with evil intent?

What I would like: a principle, stated precisely enough that each clause is motivated by one of my verdicts above, that captures as many of (1) through (6) as coherently possible, and an honest statement of which verdict the simplest such principle forces me to surrender and at what cost. I also want the principle's deliverances on two cases I cannot settle for myself: first, an animal that withdraws from the stimulus, guards the injured limb, and learns to avoid it, but in which nothing sorrows or dreads — no second dart of the reflective sort the sutta describes, so that the dart picture seems committed to saying its pain is not bad, which I cannot accept but also cannot refute; and second, a person indifferent to pain from birth, who has never had a self that would have minded, so that no appeal to what she would have felt can get any grip. Finally, tell me what the best principle implies about the passage: does it vindicate the Buddha's suggestion that the badness of pain resides in the reactive dart rather than the first arrow, refine it — say, by relocating the second dart below the level of reflective sorrow — or refute it outright?
