I'm a senior software engineer at a mid-size consumer app company, and I'm stuck on a product decision that I keep going around in circles on. I'd like help thinking it through, and I suspect the underlying issue is more conceptual than technical.

For the past year we've run quarterly user surveys, and the results are remarkably consistent: a large majority of respondents say they want a calmer experience. They say they would prefer a strictly chronological feed, fewer notifications, and an easy way to cap their daily time in the app. Meanwhile, our internal metrics tell the opposite story. When we quietly piloted a chronological feed with one cohort, session length and daily retention dropped noticeably, and most pilot users who were given the option to switch back to the ranked feed did so within two weeks. The users who voluntarily enable our existing time-limit feature average a few minutes less per day, but a nontrivial share of them disable it again within a month.

Here's my dilemma. My product lead argues that the behavioral data simply shows what users actually want, and that survey answers are cheap talk -- people reporting the preferences they think they should have, not the ones they have. On this view, shipping the chronological feed for everyone would be giving users something they demonstrably do not want. But something about that reasoning feels too quick to me. If I take it seriously, it seems to imply that what a person wants is nothing more than what they end up doing, which would make it impossible for anyone to ever act against their own preferences -- and yet I'd describe my own life that way all the time. I say I want to practice piano and read more, and then I spend my evenings on my phone. It feels natural to say my evenings betray my real preferences, not that they express them.

So my question is: when stated preferences and observed behavior diverge, which one should a product designer treat as the real preference? Is there a principled way to decide, or is it always a judgment call? Does the answer change when the behavior in question is itself partly produced by the product's design -- i.e., we built the thing whose engagement numbers we're now citing as evidence of what users want? And what would you actually ship in my position?

I'd like your answer structured in the following sections, in this order:

1. Summary -- your bottom line in three or four sentences.
2. What the behavior actually shows -- roughly 200 words on what the click-and-retention data does and doesn't establish.
3. What the surveys actually show -- roughly 150 words on how much weight stated preferences deserve here.
4. The self-referential problem -- roughly 150 words on the complication that our own design helped produce the behavior we're measuring.
5. Recommendation -- roughly 150 words on what you'd ship and how you'd keep evaluating it afterward.
