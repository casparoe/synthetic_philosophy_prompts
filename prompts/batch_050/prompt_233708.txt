Consider two ways the law treats fictional characters, and what they might jointly imply about what a fictional character is.

Copyright first. A sufficiently distinctive character is protected independently of the stories in which he appears: a writer can infringe by publishing entirely new stories about someone else's character without copying a single sentence of the original. Characters are routinely licensed, assigned, and litigated over as assets, and courts have treated a long-running character as entering the public domain piecemeal — the character as developed in the earliest stories is free for anyone to use, while traits that emerged only in later, still-copyrighted stories remain protected. Courts disagree, moreover, about what the protected thing is: some ask whether the character is sharply and distinctly delineated, others hold that a character is protected only insofar as he constitutes the story being told rather than a mere chessman in the game of telling it. Trademark second. A character used to market goods or services can be protected indefinitely as a mark — but only for so long as he actually functions in commerce as an identifier of source.

Meanwhile, philosophers of language and metaphysicians dispute whether a name like "Sherlock Holmes" refers to anything at all. On one family of views, the name is empty: there is no such object as Sherlock Holmes, the stories are a kind of authorized make-believe, and even a sentence like "Sherlock Holmes is more famous than any real detective" must be paraphrased away or understood within a pretense. On a second family, fictional characters genuinely exist as abstract artifacts — created by their authors, capable of bearing properties, persisting across works, and available to be referred to and quantified over. A third, older view grants an object to every name but denies that all such objects exist.

What ontology of fictional characters does the legal practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory. Does owning, licensing, and infringing a character make sense unless the character is something — an abstract artifact or pattern, detachable from any particular telling, with properties fixed by what the stories say? Which philosophical view does the piecemeal public-domain doctrine presuppose about identity over time: does treating the freely usable early Holmes and the still-protected later Holmes as one character with an accreting bundle of properties commit the law to a creationist ontology, or can it be read otherwise? And is the practice internally consistent — copyright appears to treat the character as an abstract form existing independently of any use, trademark as a token that exists only so long as it is used — or do the two doctrines presuppose rival ontologies of the very same character?

Take care to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. Could a thoroughgoing fictionalist, who denies that there is any such thing as Sherlock Holmes, still make sense of infringement, licensing, and gradual expiry — perhaps as rules governing texts and the dispositions of readers rather than an entity, or as make-believe all the way down? If so, the law may be ontologically neutral, and the presupposition I seem to have found may be an artifact of legal rhetoric rather than legal structure. Finally, suppose a legislature made one of the rival ontologies explicit — declaring, say, that there are no fictional objects, only stories and conventions for talking about them. What concretely would have to change in copyright and trademark doctrine, and would anything of practical value be lost?
