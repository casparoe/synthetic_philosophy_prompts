Hey, I'm in a decision theory seminar this semester and we just did the classic money pump argument against intransitive preferences — the one where if you prefer A to B, B to C, but C to A, a trader can keep charging you a penny to swap along the cycle until you're broke with nothing to show for it. My professor basically treats this as a knock-down proof that your preferences have to be transitive, full stop.

But the more I think about it, the weirder the whole argumentative practice seems. Each individual trade the agent accepts is, by their own lights, a strict improvement — they give up something plus a penny for something they prefer. So the irrationality can't be located in any single choice. Which makes me think the money pump only works as an argument if you already buy a bunch of stuff that's doing quiet work in the background: that rationality can evaluate whole sequences of choices and not just choices one at a time, that the agent's preferences stay put across the sequence, that willingness-to-trade-for-small-sums actually tracks preference, that the agent doesn't see the pump coming (a "sophisticated" agent who knows the trader's plan apparently just declines the first trade, so does the argument only apply to naive agents?). It kind of seems like the money pump presupposes a diachronic norm — something like "don't end up strictly worse off than where you started for no gain" — that can't be reduced to any synchronic rule about individual choices, and then gets cited as evidence *for* the synchronic rule (transitivity). Is that circular, or is the idea that transitivity is just the only way to guarantee compliance with the diachronic norm?

So my actual question: what exactly does relying on the money pump as a test of rational preference commit you to philosophically? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of rationality underneath it, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what just makes it rhetorically smooth, and say what happens to the argument if you deny that there are any genuinely diachronic norms of rationality at all — does the case for transitivity collapse, or is there a version that survives? Also curious whether the same commitments show up in other diachronic-rationality arguments (like Dutch books) or if money pumps need something extra.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning — no preamble, no restating my question back at me.
