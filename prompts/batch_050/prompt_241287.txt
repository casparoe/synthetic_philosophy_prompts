Next term I am teaching a philosophy of science unit on long-run prediction, and a tutorial last week stalled on a question I would like to turn into something more rigorous: when Malthus's 1798 forecast of population outrunning food supply failed to materialise in industrialising Britain, was his theory refuted, or merely early? My students keep sorting themselves by gut feeling — one camp insists that a prediction with no stated deadline can never fail, the other that every failed doomsayer hides behind "not yet" — so I want an instrument that forces them to locate their intuitions in the logical space before they learn any labels.

Please construct a decision tree that does this. It should begin from the shared commitment that a specific long-run prediction (Malthus's own, or Ehrlich's late-1960s forecasts of imminent mass famine) has not come true on the timescale its audience reasonably assumed, and lead through branching diagnostic questions to named positions as leaves. The leaves must include at least: naïve falsificationism (the theory is simply refuted); a Lakatosian verdict (the programme is degenerating but not yet dead); the "right but early" defence (the underlying dynamics still bind and only the timing was off); the cornucopian view (technology reliably outruns scarcity, so the theory erred in kind, not in timing); the regime-conditional view (the theory held for the agrarian regime, but the regime changed); and the warning view (the prediction succeeded precisely by failing, because it provoked the response that averted it). Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the names: each branch node should be a concrete case or claim on which the positions genuinely come apart — for instance, whether the Simon–Ehrlich wager on metal prices counts as evidence against scarcity theories generally, whether a prediction with no deadline is falsifiable at all, or whether the Green Revolution refutes the famine forecasts or merely postponed them — phrased so that a student can answer honestly without knowing which leaf they are headed towards. Note any combination of answers that no actual position occupies, with a sentence on whether the gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect, and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate.

Present the result as exactly seven numbered items, with no introduction or conclusion: items one to five the diagnostic questions in branching order, each in a sentence or two with an indication of which answers lead where; item six the resulting map from answer combinations to the named leaves, including the empty cells; and item seven a note stating how confident you are that the tree covers the actual space of defensible positions, singling out the one distinction or leaf placement you are least sure of.
