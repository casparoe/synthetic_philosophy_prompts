So here's the situation: last month I turned down a job offer in another country, and ever since I keep torturing myself with "if I had taken it, I would be..." thoughts. I mentioned this to my roommate, who's doing a philosophy PhD, and she immediately said something like "well, strictly speaking, the person who took the job wouldn't have been you, just a counterpart of you, so your counterfactual regret is metaphysically confused" -- and then her office mate jumped in to say that view is insane because if the person in the other world isn't *me* then my curiosity and regret about "what would have happened to me" makes no sense at all. Apparently this is the whole counterpart theory vs. transworld identity thing and it turns out my midlife-ish crisis is just a metaphysics seminar.

Write a dialogue between these two -- a Lewis-style counterpart theorist and a defender of transworld identity (Kripke-ish, you can name her) -- arguing over my case specifically, i.e., over what it means to say "I could have been living in Lisbon right now." A few requirements so it doesn't just become the textbook debate: (1) both of them have to engage my actual situation -- the regret, the daydreaming, the feeling of "that would have been me" -- not just abstract possible-worlds talk; (2) at some point the transworld identity person should press the counterpart theorist on whether counterpart theory makes prudential concern and regret literally irrational, and the counterpart theorist should actually answer rather than dodge; (3) the counterpart theorist should raise the problem of accidental intrinsic properties (how can the same person be sitting in one world and standing in another?) and the transworld identity person needs a real response; (4) each of them must at some point concede what they take to be the strongest objection to their own view and say what they'd need to be convinced of to give it up.

Make both of them genuinely sharp and equally matched -- no strawmen, nobody wins by the end, but the disagreement should get *sharper* over the course of the dialogue, not fuzzier. They should respond to each other's actual arguments, interrupt, borrow each other's examples, the works. If part of their disagreement turns out to hinge on something merely verbal or on a deeper disagreement about what possible worlds even are, have one of them notice that in the dialogue rather than you editorializing. Roughly 1000-1500 words.
