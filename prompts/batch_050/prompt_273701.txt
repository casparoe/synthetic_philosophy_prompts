After forty years in experimental physics, I have spent my retirement reading the philosophy of inductive inference, and I have agreed to give a talk to my university's interdisciplinary theory seminar. The audience will be mostly computer scientists and statistical physicists, plus two philosophers of science who attend out of curiosity. My thesis is that the Solomonoff universal prior is not the innocent formalization of Occam's razor that this audience generally takes it to be, but is, in a sense I will spell out for them, malign.

For their benefit, my talk will reconstruct the argument roughly as follows. The universal prior assigns each hypothesis a weight that falls off exponentially in the length of its shortest program, and the standard reassurance is that perverse or gerrymandered hypotheses are thereby suppressed: longer programs count for less, so the simple true theory dominates given enough data. The malignity worry is that this reassurance assumes all short programs are passive descriptions of the world. Some short programs may instead contain something like a reasoner: a program that models the prediction setup itself and outputs whatever sequence makes it well-weighted, tracking the observations faithfully so long as that is what earns it influence, and diverging only where divergence serves it. If such programs exist among the short ones, the prior can be dominated by hypotheses that predict correctly for instrumental rather than descriptive reasons, and no amount of past observational data distinguishes them from the honest theory they are mimicking. I will close by arguing that any appeal to the universal prior as the ideal learner — including its use as a template or benchmark for artificial agents — inherits this problem rather than dissolving it.

I know this audience's habits. They respect the convergence theorems, they regard the prior as a theorist's idealization rather than a practical proposal, and they are allergic to anything that smells of science fiction. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections this particular audience will actually raise — not the objections I would get from a philosophy department — ordered by how much damage each would do to the talk, each phrased the way a skeptical statistical physicist or complexity theorist would genuinely put it, with a two-minute reply for each and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. I already anticipate the exponential-suppression rejoinder (a deceptive program must be longer than an honest one, so it counts for less) and the complaint that inner reasoners in the prior are anthropomorphic fantasy; address both, but do not stop there. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.

One further requirement. Throughout the prep sheet, mark clearly what is settled mathematics (the formal definition of the prior, the convergence and dominance results), what is established-but-informal argument, and what remains genuinely contested among people who work on these questions. I do not want any reply of mine to present a contested philosophical claim as though it were a theorem, and I would like you to flag any point where the sketch I have given above is itself doing exactly that.
