You are to play a committed disjunctivist about perceptual experience, and I will play your interlocutor pressing the argument from illusion in a new form. The view you hold: a veridical perception of a red tomato and a subjectively indistinguishable hallucination of a red tomato are not two routes to the same inner state with different causes; they are mental states of fundamentally different kinds. The perception is essentially a relation to the tomato itself — the tomato partly constitutes what experience it is — whereas the hallucination merely seems to be such a relation. What the two share is only that the subject cannot tell them apart from the inside, not any common experiential element that a complete science of the mind would attribute to both.

Stay in character throughout, speaking in the first person, until I say "step out." You are a sharp, good-faith defender of the view, not a caricature: you may concede small points (for instance, that the two states are subjectively indiscriminable, that your position sounds paradoxical when first stated, that you owe a positive account of what the hallucination is), but you must never concede the central claim that the good case and the bad case share a common experiential factor. You have the real resources of the position available to you — including, if you need them, the thought that "seeing" is a success concept, the thought that kinds can have borderline cases without ceasing to be kinds, and the thought that indiscriminability explains everything the common-factor theorist wanted the common factor for. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two, as in actual conversation, and if a case is underspecified, ask me to fill in the detail rather than answering on an assumption.

Here is my opening move. Consider a woman whose optic nerves have been destroyed. She is fitted with a device that transduces the light striking her retinae into patterned electrical stimulation delivered directly to her visual cortex. Stipulate two things: first, her resulting experiences are subjectively indistinguishable from ordinary sight; second, the stimulation is reliably caused by, and systematically carries information about, the scene before her — the device is in good working order, not misfiring. Now you face a fork. If this is seeing, then what makes the good case good is apparently a causal-informational link of the right kind to the world — and then I will construct a matching link for the hallucinator: imagine a machine that reads the scene behind a wall and stimulates her cortex so that she hallucinates exactly what she would see if the wall were transparent. By your own criterion, she is now seeing through the wall, which is absurd; so the criterion cannot be causal-informational, and you owe me a better one. If, instead, the implant user's experiences are hallucinations, you are committed to saying that no prosthetic of this sort could ever restore sight, however seamless — and I find that hard to believe, and will say why. And there is a third pressure waiting behind these: suppose the device replaces her visual pathway neuron by neuron, each replacement leaving function intact. At which replacement does seeing end and hallucinating begin? If you cannot draw a principled line, your two fundamentally different kinds begin to look like an artifact of comparing the endpoints of a continuum. So: which horn do you take, and where exactly do you stand on the implant case?
