A colleague of mine works on the situationist challenge to virtue ethics, and over several years of conversation I have collected her verdicts on whether particular people possess the virtues they are commonly credited with. I want to predict how she will rule on a new case, and to understand the principle she is actually applying. Her record so far:

1. A financial manager who is scrupulously honest in every professional matter — reports, audits, taxes — but lies to his spouse freely about small daily things, and defends the lies as kindness. She says he is not honest, full stop; compartmentalization does not qualify as the virtue.

2. A woman who helps strangers in need whenever she registers the need, even at real cost to herself, but whose attention is unreliable: when preoccupied or absorbed in her own affairs she has walked past obvious distress without a flicker. Once she notices, she never refuses. My colleague calls her compassionate, though imperfectly so; the failures are failures of perception, not of the will.

3. A man who has given substantial sums to charity for twenty years, but only when asked in person, and never once on his own initiative. She denies that he is generous; his giving is triggered by social pressure rather than by his own recognition of need.

4. A soldier with a distinguished record of physical courage in combat who will not report his superior's serious misconduct because doing so would end his career. She will call him brave in battle but refuses to call him courageous without qualification.

The new case: an employee who is scrupulously honest in every domain of his life — with colleagues, family, strangers, and institutions, even when honesty costs him — but who freely explains that his motive is purely prudential. He keeps what he calls an integrity ledger: he has calculated that a reputation for perfect honesty is his most valuable asset, that any discovered lie would destroy it, and that honesty is therefore always the profitable policy. He reports feeling no pull toward truth for its own sake and says he would lie without hesitation in any case where detection was genuinely impossible.

Please do the following. First, reconstruct at least two distinct rules that are each consistent with all four of her prior verdicts, and state precisely what each rule predicts about the ledger-keeper. Second, give your best guess as to her actual verdict, with reasons — including which of the four prior cases is the closest analog and why. Third, if her record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say what further case, and what verdict on it, would best distinguish the candidate rules you find most plausible. I am less interested in whether the ledger-keeper is *really* honest than in whether my colleague must say he is, given what she has already said.
