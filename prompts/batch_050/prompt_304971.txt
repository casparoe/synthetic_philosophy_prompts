I am drafting a novel whose protagonist has undergone corpus callosum surgery, and in working out whether to write him as one narrator or two I talked myself into an argument I now cannot escape, though its conclusion strikes me as absurd. It runs as follows. (1) After the surgery, the two hemispheres display genuinely divided mental lives: one can name an object the other selected, confabulate explanations for actions it did not initiate, and pursue purposes the other hemisphere actively resists. (2) A subject of experience just is whatever integrates perception, memory, and intention into coordinated behavior over time. (3) Each hemisphere in these patients does exactly that, albeit with truncated inputs. (4) So the patient houses two subjects. (5) But nothing about the criterion in (2) is special to fully severed brains: partial disconnections, and even the divided attention and mutually ignorant subsystems of an ordinary intact brain, satisfy it to a lesser degree. (6) Therefore every ordinary skull houses many subjects, differing only in degree of integration. (7) That is absurd, so something above must go. I would like to keep (1) and (6)'s descriptive claim if at all possible, and I care most about keeping a workable notion of a single persisting narrator. Tell me which step you would reject, why it looked plausible, and what it costs to reject it rather than one of its neighbors. Please end with your answer in the form \answer{reject step N} with N the single number of the step.
