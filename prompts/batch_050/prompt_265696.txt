I am a novelist by trade, and I keep returning, with a mixture of admiration and irritation, to Tolstoy's account of art in What Is Art?: art as the deliberate transmission, by external signs, of feelings the artist has lived through, such that the audience comes to feel them too; good art distinguished from bad by the value of the feeling transmitted, ultimately by its service to what he takes to be the religious consciousness of the age; and counterfeit art produced by professionals borrowing, imitating, striking, and embellishing rather than sincerely infecting. Because this theory, if sound, would condemn most of what I do for a living, I have spent some years collecting objections to it. The ones I already have, which I do not need repeated, are these:

(1) The verdict objection: his criteria oblige him to dismiss Shakespeare, Beethoven, Michelangelo, most opera, and his own great novels, while praising simple folk tales and didactic stories; any theory whose outputs diverge this wildly from the cases it was meant to systematize refutes itself.

(2) The formalist objection: a great deal of art — instrumental music, ornament, much lyric poetry — has its value in formal organization rather than in the transmission of any identifiable feeling, so infection is at best one function of art, not its definition.

(3) The circularity objection: his positive standard for the content of good art is simply his own heterodox Christianity, so "good art" collapses into "art Tolstoy's theology approves of," and the theory gives no independent way to settle disputes between competing moral-religious outlooks.

(4) The sincerity objection: the artist's sincerity is neither necessary nor sufficient for artistic value, is frequently undetectable from the work itself, and would, if taken seriously, make criticism a branch of biography rather than of aesthetics.

(5) The passivity objection: the infection model treats audiences as vessels into which the artist's feeling is poured, ignoring that reception is interpretive and active — that different competent audiences take different things from the same work, and that this plurality is often the point.

What I want is an objection to Tolstoy's theory that is not on this list and not a rewording, special case, or combination of anything on it. I would be glad if it engaged the transmission mechanism itself, or the account of counterfeiting, rather than merely piling on further unwelcome verdicts. State the objection precisely, say where exactly it bites in the structure I sketched above, and then tell me honestly whether you think it succeeds — I am not looking for ammunition so much as for a fair test of whether my profession has a defence.
