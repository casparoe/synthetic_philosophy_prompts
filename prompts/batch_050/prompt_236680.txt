I'm trying to work out when, if ever, the fact that someone converts resources into welfare more efficiently than others gives them a claim on more of the resources. Here are my gut verdicts on cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, lies behind them. (1) The classic utility monster, who gets vastly more pleasure than anyone else per unit of good but derives it from watching others suffer: he is not entitled to everything, and indeed I feel his pleasure shouldn't count for much at all. (2) A person who is simply sunnier than everyone else and gets far more enjoyment out of any good: I think a spare concert ticket should go to her rather than to a bored friend, but I balk at letting this scale into a general principle of distribution. (3) A disabled person for whom resources yield less welfare than they would for others, because her medication is expensive and buys only modest functioning: I think she deserves priority, which is the opposite of what conversion efficiency recommends. (4) Someone who has cultivated refined tastes and now gets enormous pleasure from cheap things: no special claim, though I can't say why this differs from (2). (5) A digital mind that verifiably experiences bliss orders of magnitude more intense than any human's per unit of energy: I want to say it does not thereby inherit the world's power supply, but I am noticeably less confident here than in (1), and less sure of my reason. Is there a principle that captures all or most of these -- in particular one that reconciles (1) with (2), and (1) with (3)? Please state it precisely, say which verdict it forces me to abandon if it can't save them all, and tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed (say, an animal species with an extraordinary capacity for enjoyment). If you think my verdicts are simply incoherent, or that the request rests on a confused premise -- for instance, that 'counts toward distribution' and 'counts as welfare' are being run together -- say so plainly rather than forcing a principle onto them.
