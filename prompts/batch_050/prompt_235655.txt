Last weekend I spent an entire evening arguing with a friend about Buridan's ass. Her view was that the case proves rationality simply runs out: when two options are exactly on a par, no reason can guide the choice, so whatever the ass (or we) end up doing is arational — a coin flip dressed up as agency. I came away thinking the real puzzle lies elsewhere, not in which option to take but in when to stop deliberating. The ass starves because deliberation continues past the point where it could possibly be informative, and the standard treatments I know either treat picking as instantaneous and costless or assume well-ordered preferences from the start.

I'm applying for a three-month summer research fellowship and would like you to draft the one-page proposal (I'll supply all citations myself, so refer to positions and arguments rather than authors and titles). The central question: what makes it rational to terminate deliberation between options one judges to be equally good, and is the act of picking itself governed by reasons or exempt from them? The proposal should state the question precisely; explain why the obvious existing answers — the picking/choosing distinction, satisficing, and the idea that options can be "on a par" — leave it open; defend a specific approach over the main alternatives; say what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and keep the scope realistic for the time available.

Finally, end the proposal with a candid note on what would change your mind: which consideration would show the question is ill-posed or already settled, and where the case for its tractability is most likely to collapse.
