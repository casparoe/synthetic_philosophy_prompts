I have no formal training in economics or philosophy, but I have been thinking about the sunk-cost fallacy after restoring an old boat. I have spent five years and a large amount of money on it. To finish would cost another $12,000, while the completed boat would be worth only $8,000 to me, whether measured in resale value or use. Assume that the work has not been enjoyable, has taught me nothing useful, and will have no effect on my reputation. The usual advice is therefore to stop.

A friend argues that this advice overlooks something. Her argument is:

1. A rational decision should compare the complete histories that would result from the available choices, not merely the portions of those histories that remain after the decision.

2. A person may rationally care about whether earlier sacrifices were redeemed, even when that concern produces no further practical benefit.

3. Whether earlier effort was wasted is not always fully settled when the effort is made. If the boat is completed, those five years were part of completing it; if it is abandoned, they were not.

4. Therefore, my present choice can make a difference to the evaluative status of the past, even though it cannot change the past events themselves.

5. Consequently, sunk effort can provide a reason to continue a project without being treated as a future benefit.

6. The greater the effort already invested, the greater the waste avoided by completing the project.

7. There is no objective limit to how strongly a person may disvalue wasted effort.

8. Therefore, for any losing project, a sufficiently strong aversion to waste could make continuation rational.

9. Indeed, the more damage a losing project has already done, the stronger the rational pressure to continue may become.

10. Hence persistence is never irrational merely because it honors sunk costs. What is called the sunk-cost fallacy is really just a disagreement about how much people happen to care about waste.

This seems to prove too much. It would allow a gambler, a business owner, or a government to make continued escalation rational simply by becoming distressed enough at the thought that previous losses might have been for nothing. Yet I am reluctant to reject premise 1 or 2, since it also seems rational to care about the shape of one’s life as a whole rather than only about what happens next.

Which premise or inference should be rejected first, and why did it initially seem plausible? If there is a legitimate distinction here, please separate three kinds of case: past investment that changes future payoffs or information, rational concern with the meaning or coherence of one’s life, and the genuine sunk-cost fallacy. Also explain what is lost by rejecting each of the more plausible premises.
