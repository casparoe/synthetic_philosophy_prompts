My oldest friend is getting married in six weeks, and as his best man I owe the reception a toast. The couple met in a graduate seminar on bargaining theory; he became an economist, she became a philosopher of action, and their former cohort will occupy two long tables at the dinner. Their parents, siblings, and childhood friends will occupy the rest, and most of that second group has never encountered a payoff matrix in the wild. I want a toast of about five minutes built, from the first sentence to the last, around a single idea: the commitment race.

By a commitment race I mean a strategic situation in which the players can bind themselves -- genuinely restrict their own future options -- and the binding is the whole game. Sometimes the advantage goes to whoever commits first, since everyone else must then plan around the commitment; sometimes the race runs backwards, each player holding back because the first to commit reveals need or surrenders leverage; and sometimes two players race to commit to incompatible demands and collide. He explained all this to me over drinks years ago, and I thought it was about trade negotiations. Watching his engagement unfold this past year, I realized he had been describing his own life. A wedding, I now believe, is the technology a couple builds to end a backwards race: a way for two people to commit simultaneously, in public, at deliberately great cost to ever undoing it.

The material you have to work with: for nearly two years, each of them privately wanted to get engaged and each waited -- I know this from both sides, since each separately confessed to me a fear of being the one who wanted it more. The stalemate broke by accident, when each, unknown to the other, bought a ring and proposed on the same evening. Within a month they had merged their savings and signed nonrefundable deposits on a venue neither could have afforded alone.

Constraints. Explain the idea once, early, plainly enough for the grandparents and precisely enough that the seminar tables find nothing to object to. The concept must do structural work, not decorate: every element of the wedding you mention -- the rings, the deposits, the hundred and forty witnesses, the vows, the legal apparatus -- must be presented as commitment technology, and the conclusion must be earned from that framing. Ban the phrases "meant to be," "soulmate," and "when you know, you know" outright; the entire point of the theory is that commitment devices exist because feelings and incentives fluctuate, so the toast may not smuggle in the sentimental premise it is designed to replace. It may aim at something better -- the thought that wanting to be bound by another person is itself the deepest declaration available -- but it must earn that line rather than assume it. Include exactly one joke, built on the double proposal as a simultaneous-move game in which both players were terrified the other would wait, and make it land for both halves of the room. Above all, keep it warm: this is a wedding, not a colloquium, and the analysis should read as the highest compliment the groom has ever been paid.

After the toast, append a final line beginning "Summary:" that states, in one sentence, the claim about commitment the entire speech rests on.
