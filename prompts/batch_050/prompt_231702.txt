Most constitutions draw a level distinction: ordinary laws can be changed by the legislature, but the rules for changing the constitution itself are harder to invoke — supermajorities, referendums, ratification by regions. Some constitutions go further and contain eternity clauses: provisions declaring certain fundamentals (human dignity, democracy, the federal structure) unamendable even by the full amendment procedure. Some courts have gone further still and claimed the power to strike down constitutional amendments that conflict with a constitution's basic structure, even where the text contains no eternity clause. Yet the machinery this creates is strangely looped. The amending power is itself created and bounded by the constitution; courts that police the amending power derive their own authority from the same constitution; and the eternity clause is, on its face, just another provision that the amendment procedure is barred from reaching — a bar that seems to hold only because the clause says it does. Meanwhile, in systems without such clauses, the amendment rules appear to apply to themselves: nothing obviously prevents amending the amendment procedure to make future amendments easier.

Please reconstruct the philosophical commitments this family of practices presupposes about the structure of legal authority. Does the eternity clause presuppose that any coherent hierarchy of norms must have an inviolate level — a highest rung that the machinery it governs cannot reach — or does it actually presuppose that an authority can bind its own future exercises, a kind of self-reference that a strict hierarchy would forbid? Does the judicial power to invalidate amendments presuppose that courts stand above the constitution, or merely that they speak for a deeper layer within it? Which parts of the practice are strictly committed to one picture, and which are underdetermined — compatible both with a strict-levels view and with a tangled, self-amending one? And what would concretely have to change in constitutional design and judicial practice if we instead adopted the opposite picture: a fully self-applicable amendment power with no inviolate level, or alternatively a strict hierarchy enforced by some authority outside the constitution altogether?
