Here's a passage from Augustine's De Mendacio (On Lying), an early treatise of his (around 395 AD) where he tries to nail down what a lie even is. He writes: "that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind" — the heart of the liar is "double" — and a few lines later he sums up: "from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie."

Ok so here's my question. Imagine Augustine were shown a modern AI chatbot. It confidently outputs falsehoods all the time (hallucinations), but there's a real case that it has no inner mind at all — nothing it "holds" as true while producing something else. On Augustines definition, does the chatbot lie? If not, does the lie live anywhere — in the engineers who trained it, the company that deployed it knowing it errs, the user who passes on its output as fact? And what about the flip side, where the bot says something true but the user ends up misled anyway — does that count as anything for him? His account makes lying turn entirely on the speaker's inner state, so machine speech seems like a genuinely hard case for it... or maybe it's actually an easy one and I'm missing something obvious.
