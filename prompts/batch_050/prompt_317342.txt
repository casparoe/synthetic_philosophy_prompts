I'm writing the final for my upper-level seminar on population ethics and I want one essay question on negative utilitarianism that can't be answered by reciting "minimize suffering, full stop" plus a couple of canned objections. The specific target is the lexical threshold version of the view -- roughly, the idea that agony has lexical priority over everything else (no amount of pleasure, or of mild discomfort averted, outweighs it), but that below some threshold of mildness, pains and pleasures trade off against each other more or less the way a classical utilitarian would say.

Here's the crux I want students actually pressed on, so build the question around it. Give them a made-up policy case: a health authority can fund exactly one of (a) a program preventing a single two-hour episode of the worst pain anyone in the region will ever experience, (b) a program preventing a million one-day moderate headaches, or (c) a program that prevents no suffering at all but gives ten thousand people a year of elevated mood and extra enjoyments. Then the twist I really care about: require them to construct the spectrum case *themselves* -- a chain of conditions running from the agony down to the mild headache in increments too small to notice from the inside -- and to say where in that chain the lexical priority is supposed to kick in, whether a vague or fuzzy threshold rescues the view or just hides the arbitrariness one level down, and whether the "pleasures compensate below the threshold" clause makes the whole thing a classical utilitarianism with a suffering surcharge in disguise (and whether that would be a bug or a feature). Question should be answerable in 75 minutes.

Deliverables, in this order:

**The question** -- ~150 words including the case description, worded so that a student who memorized the taxonomy of NU variants but never thought about where the threshold has to sit will flounder.

**Model answer** -- about 600 words. It has to hit the trilemma (deny that mild harms aggregate across the threshold and bite the million-headaches bullet; fix a sharp threshold and eat the arbitrariness; make the threshold vague and risk breaking the transitivity of "at least as bad as"), and it should actually take a position on the vagueness escape rather than just listing the options and shrugging.

**Marking scheme** -- 20 marks total, split across five must-hit moves, with the marks each is worth and, for each, a one-line note on the way students typically fumble it (e.g. wheeling out the spectrum argument as decisive without noticing it only works if betterness is transitive).

**A vs B seperator** -- one short paragraph saying what distinguishes an A answer from a B one, framed in terms of the specific moves an A answer has to contain.
