I'm trying to work out when I'm morally complicit in wrongdoing I don't cause, by consulting my gut verdicts on cases. Buying a chicken sandwich at a fast-food chain whose supplier mistreats animals: complicit, mildly, even though my purchase changes nothing at the margin. Buying a used gasoline car rather than a new electric one: not complicit in climate change in any way that keeps me up at night, even though the marginal effect is about the same size. Buying a cheap t-shirt I have reason to believe was sewn in a sweatshop: complicit, and worse than the sandwich. Knowingly buying a stolen bike at a flea market: complicit, and this feels like the worst of them, though the bike would have been sold to someone else anyway. Voting for a candidate whose platform includes a policy I take to be unjust, because her opponent is worse overall: not complicit at all, even if she wins and enacts it. Working as an accountant for a company whose defense contracts I object to, doing work unrelated to those contracts: complicit, somewhat — but a janitor at the same company isn't. Investing in an index fund that holds shares in that company: not complicit, or barely. And the case I can't settle: my friend is going to shoplift regardless of what I do, and he asks to borrow my car, which makes the trip easier but not the theft more likely; driving him feels wrong in a way that buying the sandwich doesn't, yet by the causal standard I seem to be using elsewhere, both are equally inert. Is there a principle that captures most of these verdicts — what it tracks (causal contribution, participation, benefit, endorsement, something else) and why that thing matters — and which of my verdicts would it force me to give up? If no single principle fits, I'd rather have two candidate principles, stated precisely, each with the verdicts it saves and the ones it sacrifices, and what each says about the car case.
