Next month I am delivering the opening remarks at the retrospective of the fictional documentary photographer Tomas Reyes at the small photography museum I direct, and I need help writing them. Here is the situation.

Reyes, who died last year, built a fifty-year reputation on street photography that appeared to catch the world unguarded: a fisherman asleep upright against his nets, three children sharing one raincoat. He always maintained that he never directed his subjects. His archive, opened last month, tells a different story. Roughly a third of the celebrated images were staged -- the fisherman was paid to pose and was not a fisherman. A smaller number were composites, assembled in the darkroom from multiple negatives. The discovery will be public knowledge by opening night; the museum has decided to show the contact sheets alongside the prints rather than cancel the exhibition, and I have to stand up and say something honest about why anyone should still walk through the door.

The philosophical complication I want built into the remarks is the transparency thesis associated with Kendall Walton: the claim that photographs, unlike paintings, are transparent, so that when we look at a photograph of something we are, in some literal sense, seeing that thing itself, because the image's dependence on the scene runs through a mechanical-causal process rather than through the artist's beliefs about the scene. The thesis has standard consequences and standard problems that the remarks need to reckon with: staging leaves the causal chain intact but defeats the epistemic point of documentary photography; compositing severs the chain itself; and it is not obvious that transparency was ever what made the pictures worth looking at, since nobody frames a painting by Vermeer as evidence of anything. The speech has to take an actual position on what the staging and the compositing each do to the status of the work -- whether all the images are damaged equally, or differently, or whether the scandal reveals that we had misdescribed our reasons for valuing the pictures all along.

The audience is split. Half will be the general public, including Reyes's widow and daughter, people who loved the images and feel betrayed, and the local press. The other half will be a conference of aestheticians visiting for the opening, who know the transparency literature cold and will notice instantly if I misstate the thesis, dodge the distinction between staging and compositing, or reach for a banality like "art is complicated." The remarks therefore cannot contain anything false or glib for the specialists, anything cruel or evasive for the family, and cannot pretend the archive does not exist -- but they also should not be a lecture with no warmth in it. The transparency thesis has to do real structural work: it should determine which defenses of Reyes are still available and which the discovery forecloses.

Structure your response as follows. First, "The remarks": the speech itself, roughly 700 words, written to be spoken aloud. Second, "The argument underneath": about 250 words explaining the position the remarks commit to on staged versus composited photographs, how the transparency thesis constrained what could honestly be said, and what you deliberately left out of the speech and why.
