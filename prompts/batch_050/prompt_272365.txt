I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on Marx's early writings (about 15 students), and I'm putting together the final exam. I want one essay question that students answer in 75 minutes, centered on the following passage from the "Estranged Labour" section of Marx's Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844:

"Do not let us go back to a fictitious primordial condition as the political economist does, when he tries to explain. Such a primordial condition explains nothing; it merely pushes the question away into a grey nebulous distance. The economist assumes in the form of a fact, of an event, what he is supposed to deduce – namely, the necessary relationship between two things – between, for example, division of labor and exchange. Thus the theologian explains the origin of evil by the fall of Man – that is, he assumes as a fact, in historical form, what has to be explained."

(The passage is printed on the exam and does not count toward the answer length.)

The question should not ask students to describe the four aspects of alienation -- they have all memorized that taxonomy and I don't want it recited back at me. Instead, the question should turn on the methodological charge in this passage: what exactly Marx is accusing the political economists of doing when they "start with the fact of private property" without explaining it, what the analogy with the theologian's fall of Man is meant to establish (and where the analogy might break down), and how Marx's own declared alternative -- "we proceed from an actual economic fact," namely the worker's impoverishment amid growing production, and ultimately the concept of estranged labor -- is supposed to avoid the same sin of assuming what must be deduced. The best version of the question should then push students to evaluate whether Marx succeeds: does the concept of estranged labor genuinely explain private property rather than presuppose it, or does it smuggle in its own unargued premise (say, a picture of unalienated human productive activity) that functions the way the economist's primordial condition does? Students should be required to take and defend a position on that last point rather than hedge.

Please give me:

1. The exam question itself, worded precisely enough that a student cannot earn full marks without engaging the quoted passage directly, with a target answer length of 600-700 words.
2. A model answer of roughly that length, written the way your strongest student would write it under time pressure -- so it should have a clear thesis, reconstruct the methodological argument from the passage before evaluating it, and commit to a verdict on whether Marx's starting point is less question-begging than the economist's.
3. A marking scheme out of 20, built around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth. For each point, add a one-line note on the characteristic way weaker answers fumble it (e.g., treating the theology analogy as a mere rhetorical jab rather than a diagnosis of a specific explanatory failure, or summarizing alienation descriptively without ever saying what it is supposed to explain). Finally, state in two or three sentences what specifically separates an A answer from a B answer on this question.
