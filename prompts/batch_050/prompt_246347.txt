I want to build a reading seminar around the question of how moral revolutions actually happen -- why norms like Atlantic slavery, dueling among European elites, and footbinding in China ended when and how they did. But before we argue about which explanation is right, I want a specification of what an adequate theory of moral revolutions would have to do in the first place. Write me that specification: the desiderata any theory of the causes of moral revolutions must meet, each stated as a requirement together with the case or consideration that motivates it, and an explicit judgment about which requirements are genuine constraints and which are merely the prejudices of one research tradition.

The list must at minimum grapple with the following, though you should add requirements of your own where the standard demands it:

1. The arguments-were-available problem. Antislavery arguments of recognizable force existed for centuries before abolition; anti-footbinding campaigns failed repeatedly in the nineteenth century before succeeding within roughly a generation. A theory must explain the timing, not just the outcome -- why arguments or pressures that were on the table for a long time suddenly worked. Tell me whether this is a genuine desideratum or an artifact of hindsight bias in how we date "availability."

2. The counterfactual of interests. The Williams thesis holds that slavery ended because it stopped paying, not because conscience won. Any theory must say what evidence could discriminate an interests explanation from an ideas explanation when the two coincide -- and must handle cases where they pulled in opposite directions and the norm still collapsed, if such cases exist.

3. The individual-norm gap. A moral revolution is not just many people changing their minds; plenty of norms persist while being privately disbelieved, and some collapse while still being privately endorsed. A theory must explain the mechanism by which private conviction and public convention come apart and rejoin -- honor, shame, respectability, threshold effects, or something else -- rather than treating mass opinion change as sufficient.

4. Failed and stalled revolutions. The theory must not only retrodict successes; it must explain contemporaneous campaigns that went nowhere (or regressed), using the same machinery that explains the wins, without ad hoc adjustments.

5. Scope discipline. It must mark which features of its explanation are general and which are specific to honor cultures, to market-integrated norms, or to practices with identifiable victims, and it must not silently smuggle the specificity of its parade cases into its general claims.

For each desideratum, give the motivating case and a sentence on what a theory forfeits if it abandons that requirement. Then: identify the single requirement that disqualifies the most existing contenders, and flag any pair of requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied (if the list is unsatisfiable as written, that is itself a finding I want stated plainly). Finally, score three broad families briefly against the list -- an economic-interests account in the style of the Williams thesis, an honor-and-respectability account in the style of Appiah's work on dueling and footbinding, and a moral-argument-first account on which reasons accumulate until they tip -- but keep this scoring short; the criteria are the deliverable, not the verdicts.

If you think this request rests on a confused premise -- for instance, that asking for theory-neutral desiderata in advance is impossible because every requirement smuggles in a theory, or that "moral revolution" is too heterogeneous a category to theorize about at all -- say so directly and explain why, rather than politely complying with a task you judge ill-posed. I'd rather be told the seminar is misconceived than receive a spec that conceals the fact.
