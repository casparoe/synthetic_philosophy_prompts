A popular book on the limits of mathematics that I have been reading summarizes the situation concerning the consistency of arithmetic as follows: "Gödel's second incompleteness theorem shows that no consistent mathematical system can prove its own consistency. It follows that we can have no genuine reason to believe that Peano arithmetic -- the basic axioms of whole-number arithmetic on which the rest of mathematics rests -- is consistent. Gentzen's celebrated consistency proof of 1936 changed nothing, since it secretly presupposes the very consistency it purports to establish, as must every consistency proof. Working mathematicians are therefore in the position of gamblers: they stake everything on the bet that no one will ever derive a contradiction from the axioms, and if someone does, all of mathematics built on those axioms collapses overnight, because in classical logic a single contradiction entails every statement."

The book goes on to conclude that the mathematician's confidence in arithmetic is therefore ultimately an article of faith, epistemically on a par with religious belief. I have the sense that the summary is broadly pointing at something real but is sloppy at several joints -- the parenthetical about classical logic, for instance, seems to be doing more work than it should. Could you go through the summary claim by claim and tell me: which parts would a logician accept as stated; which are defensible simplifications whose qualifications matter here; and which are simply wrong or conflate distinct claims (I am especially unsure about the flat assertion that no consistent system can prove its own consistency, and about the charge that Gentzen's proof is circular rather than merely reliant on principles outside Peano arithmetic)? Finally, does the book's concluding comparison to religious faith actually follow from the parts of the summary that survive correction, or does it depend precisely on the parts that do not?
