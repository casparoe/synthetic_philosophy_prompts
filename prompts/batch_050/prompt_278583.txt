I've been circling a problem in decision theory that a colleague insists is a toy and that I can't stop thinking about: the Pasadena game and its relatives. As I understand it, the game pays out on successive coin flips a prize whose magnitude grows as 2^n/n, with the sign alternating or determined by the flip, so that term by term the contributions to expected value form a conditionally convergent series. By the Riemann rearrangement theorem, reordering the terms lets you make the expectation come out to any value you like, or none at all. My working position is stronger than the usual this-is-puzzling reaction: I think games like this show that unbounded utilities, ordinary countable additivity, and expected value maximization cannot jointly serve as a complete norm of rational choice, and that the intellectually honest response is to concede that expected value theory has a restricted domain of application rather than to engineer a patch that assigns the game a value anyway.

I've also been toying with a fix of my own and want it stress-tested: take the value of a gamble to be defined relative to the natural presentation order of its payoffs, and treat a permutation of the payoff structure as a strictly different gamble. My worry is that this covertly abandons the idea that only the probability-and-payoff profile matters, but I'm not sure whether that is a fatal cost or a cheap one, and I don't know how it interacts with dominance reasoning or with comparisons between two Pasadena-style games.

So: what would count against my position? Please survey the escape routes a committed expected value theorist could take -- bounding utility, relaxing additivity, declaring the expectation undefined but rescuing comparisons via dominance or other partial orderings, non-standard analysis, and anything else I've missed -- and for each, say whether my position actually has an answer to it or whether it genuinely defuses the paradox. Distinguish objections that would sink the restricted-domain view from ones that would merely embarrass it, and note where defenders of expected value maximization would themselves concede that Pasadena-type cases are a real cost rather than a curiosity. If the right conclusion is that the paradox is less threatening than I think, say so and argue for it. I'd also like you to indicate how confident you are overall in your assessment, and to single out explicitly the one claim in your response you're least sure of and why.
