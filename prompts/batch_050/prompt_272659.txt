So a few months ago I got talked into giving a short talk at my local current-affairs discussion group (like 10 minutes plus questions, nothing fancy) and now I'm regretting it. My position: Catalonia should be legally allowed to hold a binding referendum on independence and to leave Spain if a clear majority votes for it. I have no academic background in any of this, I've just read a lot of articles over the years and argued about it alot with my brother-in-law.

The problem is that while prepping I stumbled on Abraham Lincoln's First Inaugural Address (1861), and one part of it kind of floored me. He writes:

"I hold that in contemplation of universal law and of the Constitution the Union of these States is perpetual."

and then a bit later:

"One party to a contract may violate it--break it, so to speak--but does it not require all to lawfully rescind it?"

He's obviously talking about the American South, not Catalonia, but the argument seems to transfer uncomfortably well: a country is like a contract, so no part can just walk out on its own, everyone has to agree. Which is basically the Spanish government's position dressed up in nicer language.

So I need two things.

First, help me actually understand the quote before anyone throws it at me. What exactly is the argument here? Is "country = contract" even a good analogy? Contracts get dissolved or renegotiated all the time, and marriages end even when one side wants to stay, so why should a political union be the one contract that's eternal? Also, is there a difference between breaking a contract (just declaring independence unilaterally) and asking to leave through a vote? And does it matter that Lincoln is smuggling in a constitution (which you can't leave) while calling it a contract (which you can)? If the analogy is weak I want to know precisely where it breaks, and if it's actually strong I need to know that too, in advance.

Second, prep me for the Q&A. The audience will be maybe 25 people: a retired constitutional law lecturer who loves quoting documents at people; a history teacher who I know for a fact idolizes Lincoln and will definately have this exact passage ready to go; a couple of small business owners who mostly care about what independence does to their bottom line; and a handful of younger independence supporters who are on my side but will get annoyed if I concede too much. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way these people would actually put them (not in academic-speak), with what I should concede up front, what I should hold firm on, and a reply I can realistically deliver in about a minute each. Flag which objections have real force versus which ones just sound scary. Someone will probably bring up Scotland, where London agreed to the 2014 vote, and say that proves you need the central state's permission, i.e. Lincoln was right, so include that one.

And don't just arm me with comebacks: tell me where the weakest point in my own position is, and include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
