I live in Oslo with my Swedish partner, and we host this running dinner with a mixed Swedish-Norwegian crowd plus one American couple, and without fail, every third dinner or so decends into the same fight: which country is actually more liberal. The Norwegians say Sweden is the ultimate nanny state -- you can't buy a bottle of wine without going to the state monopoly store, taxes are suffocating, and the Swedish state has a long history of micromanaging its citizens' lives. The Swedes fire back that Norway is literally an oil state: the government owns enormous chunks of the economy and sits on the world's biggest sovereign wealth fund, the alcohol monopoly is even more restricted and overpriced than Systembolaget, and at least Sweden has had real school choice and vouchers since the early 90s while Norwegian schooling is way more statist. Then someone brings up immigration (Sweden took in more refugees per capita than almost anyone in Europe around 2015, then swung hard the other way), someone else mentions that both countries are wierdly strict on drugs by Western European standards, someone mentions Norway conscripting women, and the American couple just sits there confused, because in their mouths "liberal" means basically left-wing and half the examples being thrown around sound to them like the opposite of liberal. Some of these details are half-remembered from the news so don't fact-check me on specifics -- the exact policies matter less to me than what happens next in the argument.

Which is: nothing. In years of these dinners nobody has ever changed sides, and what strikes me is HOW the argument absorbs new information. When a Norwegian concedes, say, the school-choice point, the concession is always "okay, but that's not the kind of liberal that counts" -- and the Swedes do the exact same move with state ownership of the economy. So my working hypothesis is that the fight is running on at least two hidden fault lines at once: a semantic one (the American sense of liberal vs the older European market-liberal sense are both in play even within Scandinavia -- Sweden's Liberalerna is a centre-right party, after all), and a philosophical one (whether freedom is mostly non-interference, in which case high taxes and alcohol monopolies are paradigm illiberalisms, or whether it's the real capability to live your own life, in which case a generous welfare state might count as freedom-ENHANCING and the whole scoreboard flips). Maybe there's a third fault line too: are we comparing the countries' current policy bundles or their direction of travel. But I could be wrong about the whole diagnosis -- maybe there IS a fact of the matter here and someone at my table is just misinformed.

So here's what I want from you. Take the role of a political philosopher who specializes in conceptual analysis and also genuinely knows Nordic politics. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming: lay out at least three candidate diagnoses of what kind of disagreement this is -- verbal (a cluster concept where everyone weights the dimensions differently), factual (someone's just wrong about the policies), philosophical (rival conceptions of liberty that can't both be right), or something I haven't thought of, e.g. the question being ill-posed because "liberal" isn't one dimension at all -- and argue the candidates against each other using the specific moves my dinner guests actually make, not a generic lecture on the meanings of liberalism. Then commit to a diagnosis and answer the follow-up: given your diagnosis, what would SETTLE this? Would a well-constructed freedom index do it, or does any index just smuggle the contested weights back in? If facts can't settle it, what would count as progress -- splitting the question, agreeing on a stipulation, dropping the word? And if your honest answer is that this argument can never end, say that, explain why, and tell me what my dinner table should be arguing about instead.
