In teaching Quine's doctrine of the indeterminacy of translation, I have long relied on the standard classroom analogy: rival translation manuals, each equally compatible with all of a speaker's linguistic dispositions, stand to one another as Fahrenheit and Celsius stand to one another — alternative schemes for describing a single underlying reality, with no fact of the matter as to which is "correct." I would now like to know how far this comparison can actually be pushed. Give the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the underlying thermal facts, of the numbers assigned, of the choice of scale, of the formula that interconverts the scales? Then identify where the analogy strains. In the thermal case the two scales are trivially interconvertible and preserve an invariant structure — orderings, ratios, physical laws — that is wholly untouched by the choice between them. Do rival manuals (rabbit, rabbit-stage, undetached rabbit part) preserve anything comparable, given that they attribute genuinely different beliefs and ontologies to the native speaker? If they do not, does that show the doctrine is more radical than the benign analogy suggests, or does it rather expose a disanalogy that the doctrine cannot survive? Finally, the comparison makes indeterminacy look like a mere notational convenience. Does it thereby conceal what is actually unsettling about Quine's claim, or does it reveal that the claim was never threatening to meaning in the first place? I want an audit of what work the analogy can and cannot do, and, if there is one, a proposal for a better analogy that captures the doctrine without either domesticating or overstating it.
