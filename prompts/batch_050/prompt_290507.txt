Having retired a few years ago from a career in experimental biology, I have belatedly developed a serious interest in moral philosophy, and I now convene a fortnightly reading group of fellow retirees, most of us scientists by training. Next year I should like to devote our sessions to a cluster of problems that has nagged at me ever since I first encountered the literature on evolutionary and psychological debunking arguments: the difficulty of distinguishing, within our own moral judgements, between what one might call a preference and what one might call a bias. The distinction matters enormously in practice — debunking arguments stand or fall on whether a judgement's causal history is of the discrediting sort — yet the more I read, the less confident I am that anyone has drawn it in a way that is not circular. We seem able to identify an influence as corrupting only by reference to judgements we already trust, and to certify those judgements as trustworthy only by reference to influences we already regard as benign. In the laboratory, one separates signal from systematic error by running controls; here, there appears to be no control group.

What I want from you is not a survey of the debate but a defended agenda. Suppose our group has roughly twenty sessions — say, two hundred hours of collective attention, none of us specialists, all of us comfortable with statistics and causal reasoning but not with formal semantics. Which open questions in this area should we spend those hours on, and in what order? Candidate questions that occur to me include: whether the preference/bias distinction can be drawn without presupposing the moral theory it is meant to defend; whether cross-cultural or cross-temporal variation in a judgement is genuine evidence of bias or merely of contingency, and how one would tell the difference; whether debunking arguments are empirically testable at all, or whether the psychological premises function as unfalsifiable scaffolding; and whether the whole problem dissolves once one gives up the ambition of theory-neutral vindication. Please do not feel bound by my list — reorder it, prune it, and add what I have missed — but do defend your ordering by the criteria you use (how much hangs on the question, how tractable it is for amateurs of our sort, how many other questions are downstream of it), and state what would change the ranking.

I should also like your verdict on a device I have been toying with as a possible session structure, which I call the twin-histories test. For a given firm moral conviction, one imagines two possible selves who hold it with equal confidence: one whose conviction arose from a history we are antecedently inclined to respect (say, extended reflection and exposure to suffering), and one whose identical conviction arose from a history we are antecedently inclined to dismiss (say, parochial upbringing and social pressure). The test asks whether our assessment of the judgement tracks anything about the judgement itself, or only our prior ranking of histories. Is this a useful instrument, does it merely restate the circularity worry in costume, and where in your proposed agenda, if anywhere, would it earn its keep?
