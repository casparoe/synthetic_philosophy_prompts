I am a novelist, and my current book follows a woman slowly emerging from a minimally conscious state after a brain injury; long stretches are narrated from inside that condition, where she can neither speak nor signal. The book's moral architecture rests on a set of views I assembled while reading consciousness science and philosophy of mind, and before I commit the final draft I would like to know whether the set hangs together. I hold all of the following:

(1) What gives the patient's condition its moral weight — what makes the subject worth a novel at all — is phenomenal consciousness, what it is like for her, as distinct from access consciousness, the mere availability of information for reasoning, report, and deliberate action. Her state matters even if none of her experience is ever accessed by anyone, including her.

(2) Global workspace theory is the best scientific theory of consciousness we have: a mental content is conscious roughly when it is globally broadcast across the brain, made available to the systems responsible for report, inference, and decision.

(3) Conscious experience can outrun a subject's capacity to access, integrate, or report it. This holds for my character, for actual minimally conscious patients, and for ordinary life — the face glimpsed in a crowd and gone before one can describe it, the grief one cannot put into words. The unarticulated inner life is real, not a retrospective confabulation.

(4) The explanatory gap is real and permanent: no completed neuroscience will explain why a given physical process is like something from the inside. This is part of why I believe fiction has cognitive work to do that science cannot.

(5) Fiction can do that work: a reader of my novel can come to know, at least partially, what it is like to be my character.

Please audit this package. Go through the commitments in whatever grouping is most illuminating, distinguish genuine tensions from merely apparent ones, and rank the genuine ones by severity rather than treating every friction as equal. For each genuine tension, state which commitment would have to be revised or abandoned and what that revision costs — intellectually and, where relevant, for the book's premise. Then name the single revision that would restore the most consistency at the least cost. I would rather be told that two of my beliefs cannot both be true than receive a diplomatic verdict that everything coheres.
