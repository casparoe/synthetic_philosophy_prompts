So a bit of background: I spent just over forty years as a comparative physiologist, mostly fish and some invertebrate work, and since retiring I've fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole in a big way. I'm trying to write a short essay, maybe for a blog or one of those little magazines aimed at working scientists, and I'd like comments on the draft below. Not on the prose, I know the prose is rough. What I want is: where does the reasoning go wrong, am I making beginner philosophical mistakes, is the argument in the second section actually valid or is it a false trichotomy, and is the third point genuinely novel or something everyone in the field already knows and I'm about to embarass myself. Be blunt, I'm too old for polite.

DRAFT — "Coming round slowly"

When I was still working I anaesthetised more animals than I can count. Trout, crabs, the odd octopus. What always stuck with me wasn't putting them under, it was watching them come round. A zebrafish just starts swimming again, like a switch got flipped. An octopus doesn't. It comes back in stages: first the suckers start gripping, then it flashes strange colours, then it moves but badly, drunkenly, and only after a while is it plainly itself again. A ramp, not a switch. Different animals seem to have different ramps.

Here's my first point. Most of the debate I read about animal consciousness circles the same handful of questions: does the animal have the right brain bits, does it do some clever behaviour, does it weigh one goal against another. All of that is looking for consciousness in the steady state. I think the transition is more informative. If you want to know what's inside a machine you don't watch it idling, you watch it boot up. The recovery profile — switch versus ramp, and what kind of ramp — might tell us something the idle animal never does.

Second point, and this is the one I keep going back and forth on. The same few anaesthetic compounds work on mammals, birds, fish, insects, cephalopods. I've even read that some plants stop responding under ether, which people apparently already noticed in the 1800s. So it seems to me there are only three options. Either anaesthetics knock out consciousness wherever they work, in which case consciousness is staggeringly widespread. Or they only ever knock out responsiveness and movement, in which case they never told us anything about consciousness at all, including in ourselves — and that can't be right, because we know from the inside what they do to us. Or there's some middle position where ether abolishes experience in a rat but only movement in an octopus, and I can't see how you hold that without baking the conclusion into your definition of consciousness from the start. I'd especially like to know whether that trichotomy is as forced as I think it is, because if it isn't, the whole section collapses.

Third point, the one I think might actually be new. If consciousness can fade in and out — and anaesthesia suggests it can — then maybe the shape of the recovery curve is a rough, comparative measure of how much consciousness there is to recover. A creature with a switch has something like an on/off light. A creature with a long structured ramp, with distinguishable intermediate states, has something with more depth to it, more layers that have to come back online in order. I realise "more layers" isn't the same as "more experience," and this is probably where the argument is weakest, so tell me if there's any way to cash it out empirically or whether it's just a nice image pretending to be a hypothesis.

END OF DRAFT

A few specific questions beyond the general comments. Am I conflating wakefulness or responsiveness with phenomenal consciousness throughout, and if so where exactly does the conflation do the damage? Is general anaesthesia in an octopus even the same phenomenon as in a rat, or am I treating a shared drug effect as a shared mechanism when it might not be? And the plant business genuinely worries me — if ether stops a flytrap closing, does my second argument prove too much? If you think the essay should be cut down to just one of the three points, tell me which one keeps.
