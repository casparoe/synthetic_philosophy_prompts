I'm a senior taking a seminar on the epistemology of financial markets, and for my term paper I tried to extend the no-trade results we covered (Aumann-style agreement arguments and the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem) into a general thesis about rational commerce. I've ended up proving something absurd, and I can't locate the failure. Here is the argument as I wrote it:

(1) Suppose two agents share a common prior, are both expected-utility maximizers, and it is common knowledge between them that both are rational and that the trade in question is purely speculative (i.e., zero-sum, with no hedging or consumption motive on either side). Then neither can rationally agree to the trade: the other party's willingness to accept the deal at a given price is itself information, and once each side updates on that willingness, the deal no longer looks favorable to either. Any proposed trade unravels through successive rounds of updating before it can be executed.

(2) The conditions in (1) are approximately satisfied in modern financial markets: participants are highly sophisticated, each knows the counterparties are sophisticated, everyone has access to the same public price history, and a large share of trading volume is speculative rather than driven by hedging.

(3) Therefore, real financial markets should exhibit almost no speculative trading volume. (In fact, daily volume is in the trillions of dollars, so at least one of (1) or (2) must already be false — but grant me the idealization for the sake of argument.)

(4) Nothing in the reasoning of (1) is specific to financial assets. It turns only on the fact that both parties act on private information and know the other is doing so. So it applies equally to buying a used car from a stranger, accepting a bet with a well-informed friend, or hiring a job candidate whom a better-informed employer chose not to retain.

(5) Therefore, rational agents should almost never transact with informed counterparties at all. We should expect something close to universal autarky: no speculative trade, no betting, no secondhand markets, deep suspicion of any offer extended to us.

(6) But (5) is absurd. People transact constantly, and in many of these cases transacting is plainly rational.

Something has gone wrong, but I genuinely can't say where. I'd most like to keep (1), since it rests on an actual theorem, and (6), since its denial is ridiculous. My TA glanced at the draft and said the flaw is in (1) or (2) themselves, which I find hard to believe given that (1) has a proof. If I have to surrender a premise, which one is cheapest to give up, and why did it look plausible? Please tell me what rejecting it actually costs — I don't want a label like "the idealization fails" without an account of which specific clause of the idealization does the work and why the argument needs it.
