I'm a paralegal with no science or philosophy training, but I read a lot of popular science, and I keep coming back to one magazine article about the neuroscience of free will. I want to know how much of it I should trust. The article summarizes the research like this: "In the early 1980s, the physiologist Benjamin Libet had people flex their wrist whenever they felt like it while watching a fast clock, and he found that a buildup of brain activity known as the readiness potential begins roughly a third of a second before people report feeling the urge to move. In other words, the brain is already preparing the movement before the person consciously decides anything. Decades later, brain-scanning experiments by John-Dylan Haynes and his colleagues pushed this further: activity in frontal and parietal regions of the brain could be used to predict which of two buttons a person would press as much as ten seconds before the person felt they had made up their mind, at accuracy well above chance. The conclusion is hard to escape. What we call deciding is something the brain finishes unconsciously, and conscious awareness is an after-the-fact announcement of a result it had no part in producing. Free will, understood as the sense that we author our own actions, is an illusion the brain generates for its own purposes. Libet himself conceded only one loophole: consciousness cannot start the act, but it can veto it in the last fraction of a second, so even if we lack free will we may keep what he called 'free won't.'"

My instinct is that the experiments are real but that the article is moving faster than they do, and I can't locate exactly where. So here's what I'd like. First, audit the summary: which claims about Libet and about the later scanning studies are accurate, which are technically accurate but carry implications the studies don't support, and which a researcher in this field would flatly object to? I'd especially like to know whether "the brain has already decided" is the only reading of activity that precedes awareness, or whether there are rival interpretations of what that early buildup of activity even is. Second, separate the science from the metaphysics: does anything in these results actually bear on free will as philosophers argue about it, or only on a particular picture of how conscious decisions work? Third, and most important to me, don't leave me with a menu of options. Tell me which reading you think is best supported and commit to it: either these experiments genuinely threaten some notion of free will worth caring about, in which case say which notion and why, or they don't, in which case say what would have to be true of the brain for experiments like these to threaten it, and why the actual findings fall short.
