In my history-of-science reading group everyone reaches for travel metaphors without noticing: this guy was ahead of his time, that theory was a dead end, she anticipated Darwin by fifty years, medieval astronomy was stuck, the idea whose time had come. The tacit picture is that ideas are travellers on a road that ends at us, and the historians job is to measure how far along each one got. Last week someone objected that this is just Whig history with a map and we should quit it. Can you audit the analogy? Give me the correspondences explicitly -- what plays the role of the road, the destination, being ahead, the wrong turns -- and then where it breaks: real roads have their endpoints fixed in advance, travellers know which way they're facing, the road doesn't get redrawn by whoever arrives last. Which of these disanalogies actually matters for the way we use the metaphor? In particular: does talk of "anticipation" and "dead ends" really commit you to treating the present as the terminus, or can you keep the harmless kernel (ideas genuinely have descendants) without the teleology? And if the travel picture is quietly smuggling in the progress story, what better analogy should the group default to when describing where an old theory stood relative to what came after?
