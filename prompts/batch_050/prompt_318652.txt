I'm a graduate student in ancient philosophy, and in two weeks I'm presenting a paper to our department's work-in-progress seminar on Aristotle's account of natural slavery in Book I of the Politics. My thesis is that the definition Aristotle offers in I.4 already contains the seeds of the doctrine's failure on Aristotle's own terms. The passage my argument turns on is this, from 1254a in the Loeb/Rackham translation: "one who is a human being belonging by nature not to himself but to another is by nature a slave, and a person is a human being belonging to another if being a man he is an article of property, and an article of property is an instrument for action separable from its owner." My claim is that "belonging to another" is a property relation, and property relations are matters of convention and law rather than of nature, so the definition smuggles the very institution it is meant to ground into the allegedly natural criterion for who fits it. I argue that Aristotle cannot patch this just by pointing to the psychology of the deliberatively deficient slave later in I.5 and at 1260a, because that psychology does no work unless the ownership relation is already doing the definitional lifting.

The audience will be tough in a specific way. There are two senior specialists in Aristotle's political philosophy, one of whom is sympathetic to the revisionist line that Aristotle's criteria for the natural slave are so restrictive that the theory actually delegitimizes slavery as practiced in the Greek world rather than justifying it, and who will push back hard on my claim that the definition is doing justificatory work at all; there is a scholar of Greek law who will care about whether "belonging" and "article of property" can bear the conventionalist weight I put on them, given how ownership and status actually worked in classical Athens; and there are several generalists who will ask whether I am just faulting Aristotle for not anticipating a distinction between nature and convention that he himself draws explicitly in I.6 when he separates natural slavery from slavery by convention and force. Prepare me for the question period.
