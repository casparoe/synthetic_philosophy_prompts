Two researchers who have long disagreed about the Neolithic transition have agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and I want you to draft the joint statement they could both sign. The first, whom you may call the welfare-revisionist, holds that the adoption of agriculture was, for the people who lived through it and for many generations afterward, a decline in the median individual's lifetime welfare and a sharp increase in hierarchy and coercion, and that describing the transition as progress is a retrospective illusion sustained by survivorship bias and by our own position as its beneficiaries. The second, whom you may call the agricultural realist, denies that the transition was a welfare decline on net: either the relevant comparisons are underdetermined by the evidence, or the gains in food security, infant survival, and carrying capacity outweigh the documented costs, or the shift was a demographic ratchet that, once population had grown, no individual band was in a position to reject, so that calling it a mistake confuses an evaluative judgment with a description of an unavoidable process.

The collaboration must organize its question around three distinct claims, and the statement should keep them separate throughout: first, the ex post welfare claim, that median lifetime welfare fell in the millennia following adoption; second, the ex ante choiceworthiness claim, that a well-informed forager band, knowing what farming would bring, would still have had reason to adopt it; third, the counterfactual claim, that there existed a feasible alternative trajectory that was neither foraging-as-it-was nor agriculture-as-it-came. Require the statement to note explicitly that the two researchers may agree on the first claim while disagreeing on the second and third.

Structure the statement in five sections, in this order. Section 1, Framing and Definitions (roughly 150 words): define the three claims above and stipulate what welfare means for the purposes of the document, covering at minimum health, leisure, autonomy, and exposure to violence. Section 2, Agreed Claims (numbered, roughly 250 to 350 words): at least eight claims both sides can sign, which should include some substantive concessions from each side rather than only truisms, and at least one agreement about how to interpret skeletal and paleopathological evidence. Section 3, Residual Disagreements (a table, four to six rows): each row states a disagreement, phrased so each side would accept the wording of its own position, and flags whether the disagreement is empirical, normative, or semantic. Section 4, Cruxes and Agreed Tests (roughly 350 to 450 words): identify the three or four sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party, and for each, describe a finding, body of evidence, or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Section 5, Signed Caveats (roughly 100 words): each researcher names the agreed claim they found hardest to sign and why they signed it anyway.

The total statement should run between 900 and 1,300 words. Phrase everything so that a careful reader could not tell which side you, the drafter, sympathize with.
