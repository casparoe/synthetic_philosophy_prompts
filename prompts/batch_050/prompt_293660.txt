I'm in a philosophy of science seminar and we just did Hempel's "Studies in the Logic of Confirmation" — the raven paradox part. There's a bit near the end where Hempel says the whole paradoxical feeling is basically a psychological illusion: it only feels absurd that a white shoe should confirm "all ravens are black" because we quietly smuggle in background knowledge we already have (like that there are way more non-black things than ravens), and if you imagine knowing nothing else, the shoe really does confirm the hypothesis, just by some tiny amount.

For my term paper I want to push on that move, and I need a thought experiment built to order. Here's what I want:

Construct a scenario where Hempel's "it's all just background knowledge" explanation does NOT dissolve the weirdness — that is, a case where even after you explicitly strip out or fix the background information, an instance that Nicod's criterion counts as confirming the hypothesis still seems epistemically irrelevant, or an instance it counts as irrelevant still seems genuinely confirming. So I want confirmation and intuition to come apart in a way Hempel's diagnosis can't mop up.

Constraints:
- Realistic setting, not science fiction. Something like a factory quality-control line, a birdwatching survey, a library audit — a normal person could actually be in this situation.
- Finite, known population of objects. No "infinitely many non-black things" hand-waving.
- The background knowledge has to be stated explicitly in the setup (what the observer knows about how many objects there are, how they were sampled, etc.), so that nobody can accuse the case of working by smuggling in extra assumptions — that's the whole point.
- One scenario, or two tightly paired ones where flipping a single variable flips the verdict. If two, tell me which variable is doing the work.

After the scenario, show it meets the spec: state the hypothesis, the observed instance, what Nicod's criterion says about it, what a reasonable person's confirmation intuition says, and why the gap survives even with the background knowledge fully on the table. Then the honest part: say how a defender of Hempel would try to rescue the psychological-illusion diagnosis anyway (e.g., by arguing I've snuck background info back in through the sampling setup), and whether their rescue works or just relocates the problem.
