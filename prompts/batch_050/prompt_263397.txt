Another friend said the mid-level product guy who designed that flow is genuinely evil, and a third friend jumped in with "nah, read Arendt -- it's the banality of evil, he wasn't evil at all, that's the whole point." But the more I think about it the less I know what that phrase even commits you to. It seems like it could mean a bunch of different things: that terrible outcomes are usually produced by ordinary, non-demonic people rather than monsters; that the people involved often aren't motivated by hatred or ideology but by careerism, boredom, obedience, whatever; that the perpetrators literally don't think about what they're doing; that "just following orders" somehow lessens their guilt; or maybe even something stronger like evil as such never has deep motives. And presumably what Arendt was claiming about Eichmann specifically is yet another thing, which might be more or less defensible than the bumper-sticker version.

So: lay out the distinct precise claims "the banality of evil" could be making, from the near-platitude end to the genuinely radical end. For each one say whether it's trivially true, substantive but defensible, or actually false (or at least empirically shaky -- I gather historians have pushed back on Arendt's picture of Eichmann himself, but don't need a bibliography, just the shape of the objection). Then tell me which of those readings my friend would actually need for his move to work -- i.e., for the phrase to show that the dark-patterns guy is NOT evil rather than just being a different flavor of evil. My suspicion is that the reading that's true doesn't get him his conclusion and the reading that gets him his conclusion isn't true, but maybe I'm missing one.
