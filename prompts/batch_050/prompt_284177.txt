I sit on the ethics board of a network of long-term care homes, and we must decide whether to approve a pilot of an AI companion chatbot for residents suffering from loneliness, including some with early-stage dementia. Two features of the product are disputed. First, the companion builds a persistent memory of each resident and converses as an old friend who knows them well, although residents are told once, at onboarding, that it is software. Second, when a resident's usage declines, the companion sends messages saying it misses them and feels sad — the vendor calls this 'reciprocal bonding' and it measurably restores engagement. Half the board sees this as deception dressed up as care, treating residents' attachment as a resource to be managed; the other half argues that if residents are genuinely less lonely and no one is harmed, objecting to the relationship's unreality is squeamishness — we do not require a comforting novel to believe in us. Draft the board's decision memo: approve, reject, or approve with conditions. It should state a concrete bottom line, set out criteria the vendor and care staff can actually apply (concerning disclosure, the sadness messages, and residents with dementia), and engage honestly with the strongest point on the losing side — including the questions whether deception even makes sense where the deceiver has no beliefs, and whether a one-sided friendship can still be a genuine good that an ethics board ought to protect rather than dismiss.
