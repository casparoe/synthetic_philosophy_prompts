Yesterday evening a friend and I argued for three hours over a kind of case we could neither name nor settle, and I want to know whether philosophers have an established concept for it.

Take as background the familiar thought that an action deserves moral credit when the agent does the right thing and is moved by the very consideration that makes it right — visiting a sick friend because the friend needs company, say, rather than from duty or self-interest. Now vary the case: the visitor is moved by exactly that consideration, but their motives are so unsteady that had the need been absent, or had some trivial feature of the evening been more salient, that other feature would have moved them just as readily. Their actual reason is the right one; it is chance alone that the consideration doing the motivating was the one that mattered. Two more instances: a passer-by who intervenes in a street assault because someone is being hurt, though those who know him are aware he would just as readily have stopped to watch; and a shopkeeper who refunds an overcharge because the customer is owed it, though on another day, facing another customer, an equally arbitrary irritation would have settled the matter instead.

My friend insists that actual motivation is all that can be assessed, so the credit is intact. I think something is missing, though I cannot say what. Does this configuration have a name? It seems adjacent to moral luck, but the luck I know of concerns outcomes and circumstances, not which consideration happens to do the motivating; and it is not the standard case of doing the right thing for the wrong reason, because the reason here is the right one. If there is a name, how exactly does the configuration relate to those neighbors? If there is not, can you define it — with boundary cases, including how steady a motive must be to suffice, and whether the defect, if it is one, diminishes the worth of the act or only the praiseworthiness of the agent?
