After forty years working as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, I have spent my retirement catching up on the philosophy I never had time for, and this past year I have been a member of a small study circle that runs itself rather like an upper-level undergraduate seminar. We have just finished a unit on decision theory and population ethics, and the convener has asked me -- as the group's token numbers person -- to set the final assessment. I would like your help designing it. The topic is what philosophers call fanaticism about expected value: the view that, at least in principle, a sufficiently tiny probability of an astronomically large payoff can rationally outweigh a certainty of a modest good, so that maximizing expected value can commit one to choices that seem frankly deranged -- handing one's wallet to a stranger who claims he can save a googolplex of lives, or steering all of public policy toward vanishingly unlikely but unimaginably vast futures. What I found striking, coming from physics, is that one apparently cannot simply dismiss this: rejecting the fanatical conclusion seems to require giving up something else one wants, and I want the exam to make students locate exactly where that pressure lands rather than merely express distaste for the conclusion.

Concretely, I would like a single essay-style exam question, answerable in 90 minutes and worth 50 marks, built around a concrete decision scenario you should construct: say, a government choosing between a programme certain to provide a moderate, well-understood benefit to a known population and an alternative intervention whose success is very improbable but which would, if it worked, affect an enormous number of future lives. The question must not be answerable by reciting the standard objections to expected-value maximization, nor by gesturing at Pascal's wager. It should force students to confront the structural dilemma at the heart of the issue: that any principled refusal to let arbitrarily small probabilities of arbitrarily large values dominate one's choices appears to require either abandoning some otherwise attractive axiom of rational choice (transitivity, continuity, separability across cases, or respect for stochastic dominance), or else accepting that there is some probability threshold below which no magnitude of stakes can compensate -- and that either horn carries costs the student must actually articulate rather than merely name. Please state explicitly in the question that students are being assessed on the quality of their reasoning about this trade-off, not on which horn they prefer.

Along with the exam question, please supply two further items. First, a model answer of roughly 600 to 800 words that demonstrates what an excellent response looks like: it should set up the scenario crisply, state the fanatical conclusion and why expected-value reasoning generates it, identify at least two distinct escape routes (for instance, capping or discounting value at extreme scales versus restricting the probabilities one is willing to take seriously, or rejecting continuity) and trace a genuine cost of each, and end by defending a position with an explicit acknowledgment of its weakest point. Second, a marking scheme allocating the 50 marks across components, which names the specific moves a top answer must make to earn full credit and -- this matters to me -- the specific confusions that should cost marks. Among these, please include at least: conflating fanaticism with ordinary risk aversion; treating the problem as solved by simply rounding very small probabilities down to zero without addressing why that move is principled or what it costs; and confusing this issue with the separate problem of the repugnant conclusion in population ethics. If there are other confusions you would expect from students at this level, please build them in as well.
