I'm in a philosophy of religion seminar, and last week a friend who recently served on a jury derailed our discussion of Hume's essay on miracles with a complaint: the judge's instructions about weighing conflicting witness testimony sounded to him like arithmetic, and the arithmetic didn't obviously work. That sent me back to a passage in Section X ("Of Miracles") of David Hume's An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, which I admit I had skimmed past on my first reading. Hume writes:

"All probability, then, supposes an opposition of experiments and observations, where the one side is found to overbalance the other, and to produce a degree of evidence, proportioned to the superiority. A hundred instances or experiments on one side, and fifty on another, afford a doubtful expectation of any event; though a hundred uniform experiments, with only one that is contradictory, reasonably beget a pretty strong degree of assurance. In all cases, we must balance the opposite experiments, where they are opposite, and deduct the smaller number from the greater, in order to know the exact force of the superior evidence."

Write an essay (roughly 900 words) assessing this "subtraction rule" as a general account of how a rational person should resolve conflicting evidence, and work out what its fate means for Hume's famous conclusion that testimony could never establish a miracle. On the critical side, consider whether evidence is really fungible in the way the rule requires: a single unimpeachable video recording does not seem to be "cancelled" by a hundred hearsay reports to the contrary, the independence or coordination of witnesses changes what their agreement is worth, and one solid counter-instance against a supposedly uniform regularity (as with black swans and the claim that all swans are white) seems to refute rather than merely subtract. On the sympathetic side, consider readings on which Hume is offering a deliberately rough heuristic for proportioning belief, or an early intuitive gesture toward the kind of likelihood-weighting later formalized in probability theory, where "deducting" smaller evidence from greater is a tolerable stand-in for comparing net support.

The essay should test the rule against both of the cases that framed my question: my friend's jury room, where two credible eyewitnesses flatly contradict three others, and Hume's own target case, where the uniform experience of mankind that the dead stay dead confronts testimony that someone has risen. Note the wrinkle that Hume himself, later in the same section, refuses to believe a hypothetical resurrection of Queen Elizabeth even on the agreement of all historians — and ask whether that refusal is consistent with the subtraction rule or quietly abandons it. Weigh the strongest considerations on both sides, but do not end on a shrug: reach and defend a verdict on whether the subtraction rule is a crude but reparable shorthand for sound probabilistic reasoning that ultimately supports Hume's skepticism about miracle testimony, or whether its failure as a general account of evidence shows that his case against miracles rests on a broken scale.
