So here's something I've been chewing on for a seminar I'm putting together on early computing and philosophy of mind. Von Neumann died in 1957, and The Computer and the Brain (the Silliman lectures, published posthumously in 1958) is basically his last word on how brains and machines differ. Quick refresher of his main moves, in case it's been a while: he argued the nervous system is a hybrid of digital pulse-signaling and analog/statistical processing, that the message-system of the brain is "essentially statistical" in character rather than exact, that individual neurons are slow and imprecise compared to vacuum-tube elements, and — the part I find most interesting — that because neurons switch so slowly, the brain simply cannot execute long chains of sequential logical steps in a lifetime, the way even a 1950s computer can in an afternoon. So he concluded the brain must get its results through massive parallelism, shallow "logical depth," and probably an internal coding scheme that looks nothing like the notations of logic or mathematics — his famous speculation about the language of the brain not being the language of math.

Now the counterfactual. Say von Neumann gets another seventy years and is sitting in front of a modern LLM in 2024. Transformers are trained by a purely statistical procedure (gradient descent on next-token prediction), do their forward pass in a small fixed number of parallel layers — very shallow logical depth per token, suspiciously close to his picture — and yet, with chain-of-thought prompting, the same systems will grind through multi-step proofs and long sequential reasoning on digital hardware with floating-point rounding noise everywhere. Meanwhile the brain does the apparently-sequential stuff (chess, arithmetic, planning) on wetware he insisted couldn't support deep serial computation.

Here's what I want from you, and please actually commit rather than surveying possibilities:

1. Which single claim of his would he concede is dead? My guess is it's the logical-depth argument — LLMs suggest you can bootstrap long serial reasoning out of repeated shallow parallel steps, so "slow components => no deep chains" looks wrong. But maybe he'd say the brain still can't do it without external scaffolding (writing, scratchpads) and that LLMs leaning on generated text-as-scratchpad proves his point. Where do you land?

2. Would he count LLMs as vindicating his statistical-message-system picture, or as an embarrassment to it? The weights are digital and exact, the behavior is statistical; he predicted the reverse division of labor for brains, roughly.

3. His "language of the brain is not the language of mathematics" line — does the fact that LLMs learn fluent language as pure geometry/linear algebra support him (linguistic competence falls out of a non-linguistic internal code) or refute him (the internal code turns out to be... math)?

4. The one question I'd want answered above all: would he revise his estimate of how *digital* the brain is, given that a fully digital system trained statistically turns out to be this brain-like? Or would he dig in and say the residual gaps (sample inefficiency, energy, embodiment) show the brain is doing something transformer-like systems fundamentally aren't?

Give me your best reconstruction of his actual position on each, with the textual/structural reasons — don't just tell me what's true, tell me what *he'd* say and why, and flag where his own assumptions (e.g., treating neurons as the relevant computing elements) would be doing the heavy lifting. If you think I'm misreading the logical-depth argument somewhere, say so bluntly.
