A colleague pointed me to Good's theorem: as I understand it, it says that in an idealized Bayesian decision setup, information that is free and arrives before you decide can never lower your expected utility, so you should always accept it. That makes the theorem sound like a blanket license to measure everything, which is obviously wrong in practice — the costs, delays, and behavioral side effects are real.

Write me the list of requirements that an adequate policy for information-gathering decisions must satisfy, with the concrete consideration behind each requirement. Since the theorem is presumably correct on its own terms, I want the requirements organized around exactly which of its premises fail in real engineering settings and why each failure mode demands its own criterion. Mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable versus which reflect one camp's preferences, and say if any subset is jointly unsatisfiable. End by taking a definite position on which single premise's failure does the most practical work — give your reasons rather than leaving the trade-offs for me to weigh.
