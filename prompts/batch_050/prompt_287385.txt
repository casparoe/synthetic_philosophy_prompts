I serve as a law clerk to the Appeals Chamber of the International Tribunal for the South Atlantic, a body whose statute reproduces Article 6 of the 1945 Nuremberg Charter verbatim. By practice direction, the Chamber treats the published holdings of the International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg and of the International Military Tribunal for the Far East as binding precedent. In a recent interlocutory decision, the Chamber endorsed the following passage from the Nuremberg judgment of 30 September–1 October 1946:

In the first place, it is to be observed that the maxim nullum crimen sine lege is not a limitation of sovereignty, but is in general a principle of justice. To assert that it is unjust to punish those who in defiance of treaties and assurances have attacked neighbouring states without warning is obviously untrue, for in such circumstances the attacker must know that he is doing wrong, and so far from it being unjust to punish him, it would be unjust if his wrong were allowed to go unpunished.

The precedents the Chamber has adopted include the following:

1. It rejected the objection that punishing the planning and waging of aggressive war violated nullum crimen sine lege, and convicted leaders whose state was bound by the Kellogg-Briand Pact.
2. It refused to declare that atrocities committed by a government against its own nationals before the outbreak of war were crimes against humanity within its jurisdiction, holding — however revolting the acts — that a proven connection to the war or to aggression was required and had not been shown.
3. It convicted a publisher of crimes against humanity for sustained incitement to persecution and extermination in his newspaper, although he had played no part in planning or executing the killings and was acquitted of the conspiracy count.
4. It acquitted a subordinate broadcasting official on all counts, emphasizing that he had neither made policy nor attained a rank implicating him in any criminal plan.
5. It acquitted an economics minister on the aggression counts despite his central role in rearmament, because participation in the inner planning of particular aggressive wars had not been proved beyond reasonable doubt.
6. It convicted an admiral of war crimes but declined to rest any part of his sentence on his orders for unrestricted submarine warfare, because the navies arrayed against him had done the same.
7. Its sister tribunal entered convictions for conspiracy to wage aggressive war over a dissent arguing that aggressive war was not criminal under international law when the acts occurred, and that the charge was therefore ex post facto.

The Prosecutor now charges Defence Minister R— of the Republic of M— with crimes against peace. M— never adhered to the Kellogg-Briand Pact or to any treaty renouncing war, and its constitution expressly reserves armed force as an instrument of national policy. On R—'s direction, M—'s forces, without warning or declaration, disabled a neighbour's air defences through covert cyber operations and then annexed a resource-rich province. R— publicly claimed pre-emptive self-defence; internal memoranda in evidence show him privately describing the operation as unprovoked conquest. The defence moves to dismiss, arguing that no pre-existing law binding on M— criminalized aggressive war.

Please address the following. (i) Formulate two rules, each consistent with every adopted precedent above, that diverge on R—'s case: one reading the quoted passage as announcing a self-sufficient principle of justice, under which punishment is just wherever the attacker must have known he was doing wrong; the other treating the clause "in defiance of treaties and assurances" as the operative condition, so that criminal liability for aggression presupposes a renunciation binding the defendant's state. If a third rule also fits the record, state it. (ii) Say what each rule predicts for R—, and identify which adopted precedent is the closest analog to his case. (iii) Give your best prediction of how a chamber faithful to its own track record would rule, with reasons — including how much weight the dissent in precedent 7 should carry, and whether endorsing the broader rule would commit the Chamber to the very retroactivity the Nuremberg judgment claimed to avoid. (iv) Describe a further hypothetical case, absent from the track record, whose resolution would have forced a choice between your two rules, and say how each rule would decide it.
