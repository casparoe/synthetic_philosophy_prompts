I teach a philosophy of probability module -- 16 undergrads, one 90-minute seminar -- and I want them to feel the Dutch book argument in their wallets rather than nod along to it on the page. Design an in-class game around this setup: I fill an urn with red and black balls behind a screen, say nothing about how I chose the mix, and students have to post prices at which they'd buy OR sell a £1 bet on various draws and combos of draws, with me as the bank allowed to take whichever side of their price I like. Work out the actual mechanics: how to force genuinely two-sided prices so they can't hide behind "I'd never take that bet", how to handle the kid who quotes 50p for literally everything (I believe he's still bookable but tell me how), how to run it without real money, and a scripted round where I get someone's prices on a draw and its negation to drift apart and then book them in front of everyone. Then give me the debrief: map each way of leaking guaranteed money onto the specific probability axiom it violates, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (I expect at least one student to insist the game is rigged and one to refuse to post prices -- both are teachable moments, but how?), and be honest about which step of the argument the game can't produce on its own. I suspect it's the jump from "you'd lose money for sure" to "your degrees of belief are irrational", since losing money to a croupier who knows your prices feels different from being epistemically broken -- but tell me if you think the real gap is somewhere else.
