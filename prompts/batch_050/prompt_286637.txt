For a second-year undergraduate course in philosophy of language — 18 students, 80 minutes, a seminar room with movable furniture, meeting before the class has read any Wittgenstein — design a structured game that makes participants feel the force of the notion of family resemblance from the inside, before the term is ever mentioned. The specific thing I want them to feel is the combination that seems barely coherent on the page: that a concept can be applied competently, with genuine right and wrong answers to borderline cases, even though no one — not even the person who introduced the concept — possesses a defining rule for it.

The core mechanic should be this: one subgroup introduces a novel category purely by presenting exemplars (objects, cards, or described cases — never a stated rule); the other participants must extend the category to new items and defend contested classifications. Around that mechanic, please provide:

- A within-session contrast: at least one round run with a category given by an explicit definition and at least one run by exemplars only, so that students can compare the phenomenology of rule-application and resemblance-matching in their own reasoning. Specify whether the difference between rounds should be concealed until the debrief, and why.
- An item pool engineered so that, at a planned moment in each exemplar round, one item defeats the single-feature rule the room has most likely converged on, and one borderline item exists that even the inventing subgroup cannot settle without improvising — the moment where the inventors discover they had no hidden rule either.
- A challenge-and-adjudication procedure that forces justifications to be spoken aloud, so the room can hear justifications terminate in appeals to similarity rather than in a rule.
- Complete rules, roles, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a scoring scheme that does not use money.

Then the debrief: map specific predictable moments of the game onto the philosophical points — resemblance without a common essence; normativity without definitions, and why the lazy "anything goes" reading of family resemblance is wrong; the introducer's lack of privileged access to the rule of their own concept. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first two times I run it (for instance: teams secretly adopting a simple rule and the round degenerating; students optimizing for points instead of classifying honestly; adjudication collapsing into majority vote, conflating agreement with correctness) and how to handle each failure. Finally, an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce: real family-resemblance concepts are acquired unreflectively and woven into a form of life, whereas my students will know they are playing a game — say what I must supply in lecture to close that gap.
