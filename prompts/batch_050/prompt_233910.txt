I teach an upper-division seminar in classical Chinese political philosophy: 14 students, one 80-minute meeting on Han Feizi, coming after sessions on Confucius and Mencius. Design an in-class simulation in which the students alternately govern and are governed over the same simple production task under two regimes: first a Confucian minister, who may lead only by moral exhortation, personal example, and appeals to the group's shared good; then a Legalist minister, who may not exhort at all but is armed with publicly posted standards (fa), the two handles of reward and punishment, and the practice of holding each officeholder strictly accountable to the stated duties of their role (xingming). I want the design to make the students discover the core Legalist claims themselves rather than hear them from me: that incentives scale to large populations in a way virtue does not, that clear impersonal standards allow even a mediocre ruler to govern, and that any announced metric of performance immediately invites gaming and concealment. Give me the production task, the payoff scheme (no real money), the constraints or scripts for each minister, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the simulation onto the fa/shu/shi triad and onto Han Feizi's arguments against rule by virtue. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which features of Legalism the simulation cannot reproduce — genuine fear, monopoly on punishment, long time horizons — and what I must supply in discussion instead.
