I am preparing for a qualifying exam in philosophy of medicine, and I keep returning to an objection against naturalism about disease that seems to me decisive, yet the literature does not treat it as decisive, which makes me suspect I have missed something. Please act as an expert philosopher of medicine, well versed in the naturalism–normativism debate, and think carefully through the strongest available naturalist position before answering.

My worry runs as follows. On Christopher Boorse's biostatistical account, disease is defined as a departure from species-typical functioning, where what is typical is fixed by statistical reference classes of age and sex. The whole point of the view is that judgments of disease are purely empirical and value-free, settled by biology rather than by social attitudes. But if that is so, then it seems the view is committed to saying that when psychiatry reclassified homosexuality in the early 1970s, this must either have been a scientific discovery about sexual functioning — which it plainly was not, since no new biological findings drove it — or else the concept of disease was always tracking social values after all, which is precisely the normativist position. In other words, either naturalism makes institutional reclassification impossible as a correction, or it concedes the debate to its opponents. Moreover, since the account rests on statistical typicality, it seems equally committed to counting statistically rare but harmless conditions as diseases whenever the relevant reference class makes them atypical.

Given this, why do sophisticated naturalists not simply abandon the view? I want a response in continuous prose only, like a letter or an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables. Begin with your bottom-line assessment of whether my objection succeeds, and then reconstruct what I may have conflated, taking care to show exactly where the reasoning goes wrong rather than merely asserting that it does.
