A colleague of mine, a statistical physicist, holds firm views about when it is legitimate to convert ignorance into a probability distribution, and over the years I have kept a record of her verdicts. They run as follows.

1. A coin of unknown bias, no tosses yet observed. She assigns the bias θ a prior proportional to 1/√(θ(1−θ)), which piles mass near 0 and 1, and she explicitly rejects the uniform prior. Her stated reason is that a prior over a chance should not change merely because the problem is reparameterized, and this is the one that survives that demand.

2. Bertrand's chord problem, as classically stated: a chord of a unit circle is chosen at random, and one asks the probability that it exceeds √3, the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. She refuses to answer. The three classical constructions give 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4, and she maintains the question has no answer until the procedure generating the chord is specified.

3. A workshop produces cubes whose edge length is known only to lie between one and two meters. Asked for the probability that the edge exceeds 1.5 meters, she refuses again: uniformity over the edge yields 1/2, uniformity over the volume yields about 2/3, and nothing in the setup privileges either description.

4. The decay rate λ of a radioactive isotope, before any counts are taken. She uses a prior proportional to 1/λ, on the grounds that the rate and the half-life are two descriptions of a single fact and must receive consistent distributions.

5. A dust grain rests somewhere along a ten-meter rail, nothing else known. She uses a flat prior over position: the answer must not depend on where one chooses to mark the zero.

6. Long straws are dropped from a height onto a floor on which a circle is chalked, each straw's center landing uniformly over the floor with uniform orientation, and one considers the chords their lines cut on the circle. Here she computes without complaint, obtaining 1/2, since the perpendicular distance from a chord to the center comes out uniform.

Yesterday she put a new case to me. A laboratory owns a machine that draws chords on a target circle. The documentation has been lost except for a single line stating that the machine's output statistics are unaffected by rotating, resizing, or repositioning the target circle. She asked what probability I would assign to such a chord exceeding √3. I would rather predict her verdict before giving my own.

Here is what I would like from you. First, predict her verdict on the machine case, with your best reconstruction of the rule she is actually running. Second, say which of the six precedents is the closest analog, and defend the choice. Third, set out at least two general rules, each consistent with all six verdicts, that diverge on the machine case, and state what each predicts. In doing so, address the following worry: the straw mechanism of verdict 6 is itself invariant in just the sense the documentation describes and yields 1/2, yet a mechanism that chose midpoints uniformly over the disk's area would be invariant in the same sense and yield 1/4 — so does the machine's invariance settle the question, or only appear to? Fourth, describe the single further case whose verdict would best discriminate between your candidate rules.

One request about calibration. Commit to a prediction where the record supports one, but indicate how confident you are and what would change your mind. And if you conclude that her six verdicts are not jointly consistent, or that the machine case as I have posed it is too underdescribed to admit a principled prediction, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a verdict to be agreeable.
