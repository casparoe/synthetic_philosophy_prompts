Two colleagues in my department have co-taught a seminar on moral responsibility for three years and have finally agreed to write up what their endless arguments have actually established. One is a defender of the principle of alternative possibilities -- roughly, that a person is morally responsible for what they did only if they could have done otherwise -- and so takes Frankfurt-style cases to trade on some subtle confusion. The other accepts that Frankfurt cases succeed and concludes that responsibility never required alternatives in the first place. Acting as a neutral metaphysician of agency with experience drafting adversarial collaborations, write the joint statement they should produce. I want: a numbered section of claims both can genuinely sign (about what the original Frankfurt setup establishes, about what it was designed to show versus what it merely suggests, about the distinction between an agent choosing on their own and the mechanism that would have stopped them otherwise); a disagreements section in which each residual dispute is stated in wording that each side would accept as a fair description of its own position; the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that resolving them would force at least one party to concede ground; and for each crux, a test case or argument both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that case written down in advance so that neither can move the goalposts afterward.

To keep the document from merely re-litigating the literature's greatest hits, their collaboration must also cover two newer wrinkles they invented together in seminar. First, the adaptive regulator: a device that does not wait for a sign of the agent's decision but continuously nudges the agent's deliberation, applying pressure in proportion to how far the agent drifts from the target choice, so that in the actual sequence the agent reaches the decision apparently unaided while the device has silently done most of the work -- does the argument for responsibility surviving Frankfurt cases require the intervener to remain wholly inactive, and if the PAP defender says this case is just manipulation, what principle distinguishes it? Second, the flicker question sharpened into a dilemma: grant that some trace alternative possibility always survives in Frankfurt cases (the agent could have shown the prior sign, could have begun to decide otherwise), and ask whether such a thin alternative is the right kind to ground responsibility. I'd like the document to record, for each side, what they would count as a decisive result here -- for instance, whether the Frankfurt defender would concede anything if it turned out that every robust case smuggles in a causally relevant prior sign, and whether the PAP defender would concede anything if thin flickers turned out never to do the moral work required of them.

Format matters: begin with a short brainstorming pass in which you list candidate agreements and candidate cruxes before selecting the ones that survive scrutiny (mark this clearly as scratch work, not part of the statement), then give the joint statement proper -- agreed claims first, disagreements second, cruxes with pre-registered predictions third. Throughout, resist two failure modes I've seen in drafts of this kind: agreements so hedged they are vacuous (both sides "agree that responsibility is complicated" is not a finding), and disagreement statements slanted toward one side's vocabulary (if one camp's position can only be stated using the other's loaded terms, reword it). Finally, if at any point their dispute is partly verbal -- for example, if each side is using "could have done otherwise" in a different sense, or if "responsibility" is doing different work in their mouths -- the statement should say so explicitly, identify which portion of the disagreement survives once the equivocation is removed, and note whether the surviving portion is smaller than either of them had assumed.
