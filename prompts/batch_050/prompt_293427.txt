I'm preparing a seminar session that pairs Camus's The Fall with recent philosophical work on standing to blame, and I'd like a careful reading of the novel through that lens. The debate I have in mind goes roughly like this: blaming someone seems to require a kind of moral standing that hypocrites arguably forfeit -- the person who condemns others for what he himself does seems to be doing something objectionable beyond the original wrong, and philosophers have disagreed about whether he loses the right to blame, whether his blame simply rings hollow, and whether acknowledging one's own guilt restores standing or is beside the point. Clamence is an unusually pointed test case for all of this, because his self-described vocation as judge-penitent looks like an engineered solution to the standing problem: confess everything about yourself first, relentlessly and publicly, and thereby secure a perch from which you can judge everyone else without ever being answerable to the same tribunal.

Read the novel through this frame and tell me where it illuminates the debate and where it complicates it. Some questions to organize the reading: Does Clamence's strategy of preemptive self-accusation actually grant him standing to judge others, on any of the theories in play, or does the novel present confession as a con -- a way of converting humility into authority? How should the scene on the bridge over the Seine, where he fails to act, figure into the assessment: is his later penitence a response to that failure or a performance layered on top of it? Does the novel suggest that self-knowledge of one's hypocrisy makes any moral difference at all, or is knowing-what-you-are just a more refined hypocrisy? And since the book is a monologue addressed to a silent listener who gradually becomes implicated, what does the form itself add to the philosophical discussion -- is Camus making a claim about standing, about the audience's standing, that a journal article on hypocritical blame can't easily make? I'd also like you to say honestly where the reading strains: the novel is arguably about guilt, bad faith, and the loss of innocence more than about the ethics of blame as such, and if forcing it into the standing debate distorts either the book or the debate, say so and identify the point of strain rather than smoothing it over.

A note on how I'd like the answer pitched: be willing to take positions -- tell me which theory of standing best survives contact with the novel, or whether the novel dissolves the debate rather than adjudicating it -- but flag your confidence as you go, distinguishing what the text clearly supports from what is one defensible reading among several. And if any premise of my request is off -- if the judge-penitent strategy isn't really a bid for standing, or if I've mischaracterized the philosophical terrain, or if the pairing itself is a mismatch -- I'd rather you push back directly than build a reading on a faulty foundation.
