You are a philosopher of language who has spent several years advising courts and drafting bodies on questions of legal interpretation; you are equally at home in the philosophy of definite descriptions and in the law of wills and contracts. Think carefully and methodically. Before settling on an answer, brainstorm at least two rival reconstructions of the practice described below, and only then commit to one.

I am a law student, and I have been nagged by the feeling that the doctrines I am taught about how legal language refers rest on a philosophy of language nobody states out loud. Consider three things our courts routinely do:

1. A testator leaves property "to the eldest son of my brother," but the brother never had a son. The gift simply fails. The court does not redirect it to the brother's daughter, or to his widow, or to whoever comes closest to satisfying the description. A definite description that picks out nothing produces, legally, nothing.

2. A testator leaves "my house" to a niece, and it turns out he owned two houses. Here the court does not let the gift fail at once: it admits extrinsic evidence about the testator's circumstances and intentions to work out which house was meant, and only declares the clause void for uncertainty if no such evidence resolves the matter.

3. Under the doctrine sometimes stated by the maxim that a false description does not vitiate, a gift "to my nephew John" is upheld in favor of John even when John is in fact the testator's cousin, so long as John is otherwise identified beyond doubt. The description is wrong, the intended recipient exists, and the description is treated as disposable surplus rather than as a failed referring device.

While puzzling over this I came across the following in Bertrand Russell's "On Denoting" (Mind, 1905), where he first sets out his theory:

"A phrase may be denoting, and yet not denote anything; e.g., 'the present King of France'."

"Now the, when it is strictly used, involves uniqueness; we do, it is true, speak of 'the son of So-and-so' even when So-and-so has several sons, but it would be more correct to say 'a son of So-and-so'."

My questions, which I would like you to answer in a continuous essay of roughly 1,500 words (the quoted passage does not count toward that):

First, reconstruct the implicit semantic theory of definite descriptions that the three doctrines above, taken together, presuppose. Does the practice commit the law to Russell's picture, on which a definite description contributes quantificational structure to the whole proposition and succeeds only if exactly one object satisfies it? Or is it better read as Strawsonian, treating existence and uniqueness as presuppositions whose failure voids the clause rather than falsifying it? Or is the third doctrine evidence that the law runs on something like a referential/attributive distinction, sometimes honoring the object the drafter had in mind over the words used?

Second, test the practice against the passage. Russell concedes that ordinary speakers use "the" loosely where strictness would demand "a," and he sets that looseness aside as incorrect. The second doctrine seems to do the opposite: it institutionalizes the loose use and repairs it with extrinsic evidence. Does that show the law presupposes a contextual-completion semantics Russell would reject, or can a Russellian explain the second doctrine as a pragmatic repair layered over a strict semantics? Say which reading you find more forced, and why.

Third, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with. Which of the three doctrines would have to change if the law adopted, wholesale, a pure Russellian semantics with no pragmatic repair? Which would survive a Strawsonian or Donnellanian reform untouched? Where is the practice genuinely underdetermined, compatible with several theories at once?

Finally, give a verdict on coherence: is the law's split treatment of empty, incomplete, and false descriptions a principled triangulation of a single underlying view of legal reference, or an accident of doctrinal history that no single semantics can rationalize? If the latter, say which doctrine you would sacrifice first and what theory the surviving two would then presuppose.
