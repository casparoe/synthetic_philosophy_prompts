So I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm putting together a humor sidebar to run alongside a review of some philosophy-of-mind book. We keep going back and forth on concepts and my editor pitched doing something on Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument -- the whole thing where if every physical event already has a sufficient physical cause, and effects aren't systematically overdetermined, then mental properties get muscled out of doing any actual causal work, and your wanting coffee doesn't move your arm, the neurons do. I think there's a great parody in here but I keep botching it, so I want you to write it: a deadpan workplace document, like an HR complaint or a grievance filing, submitted by Mental Causation (employee) against Physical Causation (coworker / rival department), alleging that the physical has been taking credit for every single piece of work the mental ever did.

The humor should come from the complaint being *correct about the argument* -- the mental's case should rest on the real premises, dressed up as HR language: causal closure becomes a company policy that every deliverable must have a fully documented physical cause, the exclusion principle becomes a rule against two employees claiming credit for the same outcome, systematic overdetermination gets floated and shot down the way it always is (the boss doesn't believe in two people both fully finishing the same task), and the threatened outcome is the mental being reclassified as purely decorative staff, i.e. epiphenomenalism as a job title. Ideally the complaint also has the mental quietly undermining itself somewhere, since the filing itself is full of intentions and desires doing things -- the reader should catch that wink without the document ever acknowledging it. Maybe end with the HR rep's preliminary ruling or a note that the case has been pending since Descartes.

Roughly 500-600 words, formatted like an actual internal memo or complaint form with fields if you want (employee name, department, nature of grievance, prior incidents). It should be funny to a philosopher who's read Kim and still parseable to my readers who haven't -- no jargon that isn't explained by the joke itself. And please don't have the physical department get its own rebuttal memo, one document only, the funniness is in how one-sided and self-defeating the filing is.
