The exam question was: "Philosophers often distinguish lying from merely misleading. (a) Explain the distinction and the standard definition of lying it rests on. (b) Give your own example of misleading someone without lying to them. (c) Is lying morally worse than misleading? Give one argument for thinking it is, one for thinking it isn't, and say where you come down."

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"Lying is standardly defined as asserting a proposition you believe to be false with the intention of deceiving your audience. Misleading, by contrast, is getting someone to form a false belief without asserting anything false -- for example through a conversational implicature. My example: my roommate asks where I was last night and I say 'I was out with friends,' which is literally true, but I omit that we were at the casino, knowing he'll assume we were at the bar. Grice would say I exploited the maxim of quantity, since implicatures arise from what a cooperative speaker would be expected to say.

On the moral question: Kant famously argued in his essay on the murderer at the door that lying is wrong because it uses the deceived person merely as a means, which is why even lies told for good ends are forbidden. Augustine, similarly, held that a lie is only actually wrong if it succeeds in deceiving the victim, since an unsuccessful lie harms no one. An argument that lying is worse: when you lie you violate the norm of assertion itself, you make a kind of public commitment to something you know is false, whereas the misleader only lets the listener draw their own bad inference, so the listener shares responsibility. An argument that lying isn't worse: the harm is the same either way, since in both cases the victim ends up with a false belief they act on, and the misleader may even be more cowardly because they're trying to keep plausible deniability. My own view is that because lying violates a norm of assertion, lying is therefore always morally worse than misleading, since the means matter more than the outcome."
