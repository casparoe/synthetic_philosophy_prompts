I host a small history reading group, and last week a session on the origins of the First World War turned into a two-person standoff that I stayed out of; I'd like a referee now. Anna argued that the Fischer controversy is essentially settled in Fischer's favor: the Septemberprogramm, she said, shows that what Germany sought in 1914 was continental hegemony and sweeping annexations, and that this reveals the war was not a tragic accident but a bid for world power that German leaders had contemplated for years. Ben pushed back that the Septemberprogramm was drafted in September 1914, after the fighting had started, and that war aims drawn up six weeks into a war cannot be evidence of what anyone intended in July; every belligerent's aims ballooned once the blood price was paid. Anna then shifted to pre-war evidence: the unconditional support Germany gave Austria-Hungary in early July 1914 despite knowing it might trigger a continental war, and the December 1912 meeting at which the Kaiser and his military leadership discussed a coming war. Ben answered with the more recent sleepwalkers picture: Russia began general mobilization first, France gave Russia its own loose assurances, Austria-Hungary issued the ultimatum, and Britain's ambiguity encouraged everyone to gamble — so responsibility is distributed, and singling out Germany mistakes causal centrality for moral guilt. Anna's final move was that a state can be held responsible as a continuing agent the way reparations practice assumes, and that the Kaiser's constitutionally entrenched control over foreign and military policy makes attributing elite intent to the German state more legitimate, not less. Ben's final move was that Article 231 was written by victors, that conscripts and socialists who opposed the war were part of the "nation" being blamed, and that assigning national guilt is itself a political act rather than a historical finding — at which point the evening ended.

Please adjudicate the exchange point by point rather than crowning an overall winner.
