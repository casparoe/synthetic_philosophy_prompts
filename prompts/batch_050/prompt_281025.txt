I'm drafting the background section of a grant proposal on mental privacy and neurotechnology, and before surveying the literature I want the specification first: what any adequate account of mental privacy would have to deliver. Write as a philosopher who works on the ethics of emerging technology, and reason through the cases below before fixing the criteria, since the cases are meant to do the work.

The deliverable is a desiderata list: the cases an account must classify correctly, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the empirical facts about current neurotechnology it cannot contradict, each requirement paired with the consideration that makes it a requirement. Mark which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are the commitments of particular camps, and flag any subset of desiderata you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable -- if the list itself rules out every option, say so, since that is a result, not a failure. Build the list around these four cases and let the requirements fall out of them: (1) a consumer EEG headset that infers fatigue and attention from aggregate signal patterns and sells the resulting profile to advertisers, the manufacturer protesting that no thoughts were read; (2) a forensic decoder that reconstructs, from brain scans, a coarse image a suspect was picturing -- accurate enough to place them at a scene but incapable of recovering words or propositions; (3) a device that predicts, seconds before the subject feels any urge, that they are about to reach for their phone, a reach the subject experiences as spontaneous; (4) a skilled interrogator who, from face and voice alone, correctly judges that a suspect is lying about where they were on Tuesday.

The point of the set is to force a mental-privacy account to come apart from a generic data-privacy account: cases 1 and 4 should test whether your requirements turn on the source of the information (neural versus behavioral) or on something else, and cases 2 and 3 should test whether detection of a mental occurrence, extraction of its content, and prediction of a state the subject does not yet experience are one wrong or three. If your requirements deliver different verdicts on these, name the feature of the cases that drives the difference. Close by briefly scoring three broad families of view against the list: privacy as control over access to information, privacy as a special status for neural data, and privacy as protection against inference. Lead with a single sentence stating what you take the core protected interest to be, then give the list and the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the brief.
