Before I submit an article manuscript on distributive ethics, I want to stress-test the position it defends for internal coherence. Here is the full set of views I am committed to:

(a) Prioritarianism: the moral value of a benefit to a person is greater the worse off that person is, and distributions should be assessed by the sum of priority-weighted well-being rather than by total well-being alone.

(b) The separateness of persons objection is a decisive reason to reject utilitarianism. It is not merely one consideration to be weighed against efficiency: a theory that adds benefits and burdens across distinct lives the way a prudential agent adds them across the stages of a single life has made a mistake in kind, and this mistake is fatal.

(c) The leveling-down objection shows that equality has no intrinsic value, but it has no force against my view. Prioritarianism assigns positive weight to everyone's well-being, so it can never prefer making the better-off worse off when no one gains; the objection only touches views that value equality as such.

(d) "Worse off" in (a) should be understood in terms of how a person's whole life goes, not their momentary state. Justice tracks lifetime well-being, so when allocating scarce resources the relevant question is whose life is going worse overall, not who is suffering most right now.

(e) Case verdict: suppose a hospital can either fund palliative care that greatly relieves the final months of a patient whose life overall has gone and will go much better than average, or fund a modest but lasting benefit for a patient whose life is going much worse overall. I hold that we should fund the palliative care, because the urgency of intense suffering at the end of life outweighs the rival's stronger priority-weighted claim.

(f) A person's claim to assistance does not rest on comparisons with other people. How badly off someone is gives them a claim directly; the strength of their claim does not depend on how anyone else's life is going.

My questions: which of these commitments are actually in tension with one another, and which only appear to be? For each genuine tension, state exactly what follows from what, and identify the least costly revision available to me — including what that revision would force me to give up elsewhere in my argument. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, and say explicitly which commitments you think stand firm. I would rather be told that one commitment must go than receive a list of minor discomforts.
