I'm writing an essay for a political theory seminar and would like a reading of Shakespeare's Measure for Measure through Han Feizi's Legalism — specifically the contrast between rule by virtue (the Confucian idea that a ruler's moral example transforms the people) and rule by incentives (clear, publicized laws plus the two handles of reward and punishment, wielded impersonally and without exception).

Set the problem up this way: Vienna's strict statutes against sexual license have gone unenforced for years; Duke Vincentio, rather than enforce them himself or repeal them, hands power to Angelo, a man of reputed austerity, and stays in the city disguised as a friar to watch what follows. Angelo revives the dormant law and applies it without exception to Claudio, then proves willing to trade Claudio's life for Isabella's body. The Duke ends the crisis through covert surveillance, deception, and a substitution trick, and closes the play by pardoning or punishing each character according to his own discretion rather than any statute.

Work through at least these cruxes, using the Legalist vocabulary where it genuinely fits (fa, shi, shu, the two handles, the matching of claimed performance to actual conduct) rather than as decoration:

First, the dormant statute. Han Feizi's view is that law functions only when it is publicized and applied consistently, so that subjects can predict consequences. From that standpoint, is Angelo's revival of an unenforced law after years of desuetude the correct Legalist move — restoring the law's credibility — or precisely the arbitrariness Legalism exists to prevent? And does the play itself take a position: whom are we made to sympathize with, and does that sympathy track a principle or merely a person?

Second, Angelo as the case against appointment by reputation. Han Feizi warns that the ruler's central error is trusting men rather than institutions, and that reputed virtue is exactly what should never be trusted. Does the play simply confirm this — the austere-seeming minister is the corrupt one — or does it complicate it? Angelo's fall comes not through greed or faction but through a desire he seems not to have known he possessed. Is that a failure Legalist techniques of verification could have caught in advance, or does the play depict a kind of corruption that no incentive structure addresses?

Third, the Duke as closet Legalist. A ruler who conceals his identity, gathers information in disguise, and steers everyone through deception looks, on paper, like a practitioner of shu, the arts of secrecy and control. But Han Feizi's ideal ruler is supposed to remain still and inscrutable, letting law and office do the work; the Duke's method is the opposite — personal, theatrical, improvisational — and the finale makes every outcome flow from his whim. Read the closing scene — the pardons, the engineered marriages, the punishment of Lucio — as a judgment on this hybrid: does the play show rule-by-technique collapsing into rule-by-personality, and would Han Feizi regard the Duke's arbitrary mercy as worse than Angelo's arbitrary severity, since mercy and cruelty dispensed at whim are equally unpredictable?

Then step back. The play is standardly read as being about mercy and equity tempering strict justice. What does a Legalist reading do to that consensus — does it reveal that both the harsh and the merciful options on offer are personal rule in different costumes, making the play, despite itself, an argument for impersonal law? Finally, say what the play adds to the Legalism–Confucianism debate that the treatises do not. I have in mind that Han Feizi treats human badness as predictable and therefore manageable, whereas the play's central surprise is self-opacity: Angelo does not know himself. Does the Legalist system quietly depend on the premise that people, including rulers and ministers, know what they want?

Be faithful in both directions: do not flatten the play into a Legalist allegory, and where the play resists the reading, say so.
