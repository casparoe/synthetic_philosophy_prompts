My roommate and I have been arguing about Mill's harm principle for basically the entire semester -- he's a hardline Millian who thinks the only legitimate reason to restrict speech is direct harm to others, and offense, hurt feelings, and "corrosive effects on public discourse" don't count, full stop. I lean toward the view (roughly the democratic-self-government camp) that some speech can be restricted when it systematically undermines the preconditions of free and equal citizenship -- so certain kinds of targeted harassment or coordinated disinformation might be fair game even if no single instance causes a neat, countable harm. We keep talking past each other, and our TA suggested we try doing an adversarial collaboration and write up a joint statement instead of just rehashing the same fight.

Here's the thing that makes it tricky: we both actually read On Liberty and we both think Mill was right about a lot, so this isn't "Millian vs. censor." The disagreement is more like: does the harm principle scale to diffuse, statistical, system-level harms (a million small speech acts each of which is harmless-ish, but which together degrade the information environment or drive some group out of public life), or does Mill's framework only make sense case-by-case, where you can point to a specific victim? Also we disagree about whether Mill's whole epistemological argument for free speech (the dead dogma / suppressed truth stuff) still works in an environment where speech is amplified by engagement-optimizing algorithms rather than distributed by pamphlet, which Mill obvously never had to think about.

So please draft the joint statement we'd produce if we did this properly. Structure it as follows, in this order: "Agreed Claims" -- a numbered list of maybe 8-12 claims both of us could sign, including some about what Mill actually argued and some about the modern cases; they should be substantive, not platitudes like "speech matters." Then "Residual Disagreements" -- 3 or 4 disagreements, each stated twice, once in wording my Millian roommate would accept as describing his view and once in wording I'd accept for mine, no strawmanning either side. Then "Cruxes" -- the sub-questions where settling them would actually move one of us; for each crux, give a concrete hypothetical case or observation we could both agree bears on it, and write down in advance what each of us predicts about that case (like, what he'd say about it and what I'd say), so we can check later whether the evidence goes where we expect. Aim for maybe 400-500 words on the agreed claims, 300 or so on the disagreements, and 400-500 on the cruxes.
