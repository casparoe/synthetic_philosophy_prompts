I write novels for a living, and at a dinner last week two of my friends got into an argument about a character in my draft, and it's been bugging me ever since because I genuinely can't tell who won. The character: a bloke who decides, completely sincerely and after alot of thought, that he is done with gambling. He knows the maths, he hates what it's done to his marriage, he means it. That same evening he walks past the bookies and goes in. In the morning he's disgusted with himself, and by lunchtime he has decided again, just as sincerely, that he's done. This loop repeats.

My friend Dana (clinical psychologist) said this is straightforward irrationality: he judges, all things considered, that he shouldn't gamble, and then does it anyway, so he's acting against his own better judgement, and that's the textbook case of weakness of will. My friend Marcus (economist, unfortunately) said there's no irrationality anywhere in the story. His preferences just change as the reward gets closer: at noon, staying clean matters more to him than a flutter, at 6pm outside the bookies it doesn't, and a preference isn't the kind of thing that can be irrational, it just is what it is. Dana said that can't be the whole story, because the man himself doesn't treat his 6pm self as equally authoritative -- he regrets it, he asks his wife to hold the bank cards, he routes his walk home to avoid the high street. If the two selves were equally legitimate, taking sides between them would make no sense. Marcus replied that one self outmanoeuvring another is just intrapersonal strategy, like Ulysses and the mast -- both selves are fully real, one of them happens to get its way by tying the other's hands, and nothing in that makes either one irrational. Dana's last move was to say the preference flip is completely predictable and doesn't track any new information about what's actually good for him -- nothing about gambling got better between noon and 6pm -- so the flip looks like a glitch in the machinery rather than a genuine change of mind. Marcus said that's just smuggling in one particular shape of time-preference (the tidy exponential kind) as the definition of rationality, when every actual human discounts steeply near-term, and you can't convict the whole species of irrationality for not matching a model.

So, as referee: who's right about which specific point here? In particular, is acting against your own settled judgement automatically irrational? Do the bank-card and walk-rerouting manoeuvres actually show what Dana thinks they show? And does the "no new information" observation do the work she needs it to do? Tell me what both of them are missing, and who's more right overall. Please answer in British English.
