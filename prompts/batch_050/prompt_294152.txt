A colleague in my metaphysics seminar has been defending an argument that seems to convert an insight about rigid designation into a wholesale elimination of contingent identity. He asks us to consider a close election in which, as a matter of fact, exactly one candidate received the most votes and exactly one candidate submitted the first registration form, and these were the same person, Aurelia. His argument runs as follows. (1) A term rigidly designates an object if, once its actual referent is fixed, it designates that same object in every possible world in which the object exists. (2) Ordinary proper names are normally rigid in this sense, which is why an empirically discovered identity between two names can be necessary even though it is knowable only a posteriori. (3) Any uniquely satisfied description can be made rigid by prefixing it with “actually”: thus “the actual candidate who received the most votes” designates Aurelia even when we are describing a possible world in which someone else received the most votes. (4) In the election case, “the candidate who received the most votes is the candidate who submitted the first registration form” is true but plainly contingent. (5) Its rigidified counterpart, “the actual candidate who received the most votes is the actual candidate who submitted the first registration form,” is also true. (6) Because both terms in that counterpart are rigid, its truth is necessary. (7) Hence every apparently contingent true identity has a necessary counterpart produced merely by making the reference of its terms explicit. (8) But making reference explicit should not change the proposition expressed. (9) Therefore, the apparent contingency of the original identity statement was an artifact of nonrigid wording; strictly speaking, no true identity statement is contingent.

The argument strikes me as proving too much, since it would make the contingency of the election identity nothing more than a defect in how we happened to formulate it. Yet I can also see why each step initially looks respectable: step (3) does seem to produce a rigid term, and step (6) appears to follow directly from the standard treatment of identities between rigid designators. Which step should be rejected or qualified, and why does it appear plausible in the first place? In answering, please distinguish the necessity of the rigidified sentence from the status of the original proposition, explain what work “actually” is doing, and say what theoretical cost would be incurred by rejecting each of the most plausible alternatives.
