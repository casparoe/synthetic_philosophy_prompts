I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a short piece about what fiction can do that philosophy papers can't. My editor suggested pairing Hemingway's "Hills Like White Elephants" with the classic abortion-ethics literature, and at first I thought it was a gimmick, but the more I sit with it the weirder and more interesting the fit seems. Help me think it through.

Here's the angle I want tested. The story famously never says what the operation is. Meanwhile Thomson's violinist argument works by granting the thing her opponents want most -- fine, suppose it's a person from conception -- and then arguing that even so, you aren't required to stay plugged in, because nobody has a right to use your body without your ongoing consent. And Marquis goes the other direction entirely: he sets the personhood question aside and says what makes killing wrong is that it robs someone of a future like ours, experiences and projects and all that, which is why it covers fetuses whether or not they count as persons yet.

Now here's what strikes me about the story. Jig never makes anything like Thomson's move. She doesn't talk about her body or her rights at all -- she talks about the world, about how they could have everything, and how once it's done things won't be theirs anymore. And the man makes this strange mirror-image of the Marquis point: he keeps framing the operation as restoring their future, getting back to how things were, as if the thing at risk of being killed off is the couple's old life. So the story seems to stage two competing futures -- the fetus's hypothetical one and the couple's remembered one -- without ever naming either.

So: read the story through both lenses seriously, not as a cartoon. Where does Thomson's framework actually apply, given that Jig isn't a stranger kidnapped in a hospital bed but someone in a relationship, and the pressure on her is emotional rather than legal? Where does the man's rhetoric look like a corrupted future-like-ours argument? And be honest about where the reading breaks down -- does the story even take a side, or is it doing something the papers can't, like showing that the real moral action is in how people talk (and refuse to talk) rather than in the argument itself? If you think the pairing doesn't survive scrutiny, say so and tell me which lens cracks first. I'd rather kill the piece than file something that flattens either the story or the philosophy, and my deadline is definately not helping.
