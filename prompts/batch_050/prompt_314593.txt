Epicurus held that death is nothing to us: as long as we exist, death is not yet present, and once death is present, we no longer exist to be harmed. Now consider a speculative idea arising from the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, sometimes called quantum immortality: if every event with a nonzero probability of killing you produces branches in which outcomes differ, then in some branch you always survive — the truck swerves, the bullet jams, the tumor turns out benign — and since you can only ever find yourself in a branch where you still exist, your subjective trajectory might be one of never dying, however astronomically improbable your continued survival becomes.

Suppose Epicurus were presented with this idea. Would he take it to vindicate his counsel not to fear death, to refute it, or to leave it untouched? In answering, address at least the following: whether Epicurus's argument depends on death actually befalling the person, and what "befalling" even means when every outcome occurs in some branch; whether his parallel aim of freeing people from the fear of an eternal afterlife gives him reason to dread quantum immortality rather than welcome it, given that surviving forever may mean surviving in steadily worse conditions; and whether an argument about what is bad for a person can even engage a claim about which branches a subject's experience continues into. Ground your answer in Epicurus's actual commitments — about pleasure, the completeness of a finite life, and the sources of fear — rather than in a generic caricature of Epicureanism.
