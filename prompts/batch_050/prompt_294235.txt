I am a PhD student in philosophy of science writing a dissertation chapter on diagnostic classification in psychiatry, and I am stuck on a question I would like help with: what, exactly, makes a human kind a "looping" kind in the relevant sense, as opposed to a kind that merely changes after it is classified? Rather than argue from definitions, I want to start from my verdicts on a set of cases and ask what principle, if any, lies behind them. The cases:

(1) A new diagnosis enters widespread use. Patients join support groups, read first-person accounts, and begin reorganizing their autobiographical memories to fit the diagnosis's published prototype; over several years, their reported symptoms drift toward that prototype. My verdict: paradigm case of looping.

(2) A diagnosis leads insurers to reimburse only one particular treatment, so nearly everyone with the label ends up on the same drug, and the clinical picture of the population shifts as a result. My verdict: the classification changed the people, but this does not feel like looping in the sense that matters philosophically — nobody's self-understanding was involved.

(3) Researchers tighten a biomarker threshold, so the population carrying the label changes overnight, though no patient's self-understanding, behavior, or symptoms change at all. My verdict: clearly not looping; just re-sorting.

(4) A diagnosis becomes heavily stigmatized. Patients learn to conceal the relevant symptoms from clinicians, measured prevalence drops sharply, but (let us stipulate) nothing changes in the patients except how they present under observation. My verdict: I want to say this is not looping either — the kind itself did not change, only our evidence about it did — but I notice the concealment is entirely mediated by patients' awareness of the label, which makes it uncomfortably similar to case (1).

(5) Patients receive a diagnosis and, disliking the stereotype it carries, deliberately cultivate traits opposed to the prototype; the population drifts away from the published description until clinicians revise it. My verdict: this is looping, full stop — even though the causal arrow points away from the classification rather than toward it.

(6) A diagnosis is removed from the manual, and within a decade the syndrome itself becomes rare, not merely unreported. My verdict: retrospective evidence that the kind was loop-dependent all along.

Three questions. First, is there a single coherent principle behind these verdicts — perhaps one that distinguishes routes of influence (self-ascription, institutional incentive, measurement artifact) — or do I need several? Second, I suspect (4) and (5) are in tension: if awareness-mediated change counts as looping even when it only alters self-presentation, then (4) should count too; if it does not, my treatment of (5) needs justification beyond the direction of drift. Tell me which verdict a well-motivated principle forces me to abandon, and state the principle precisely enough that each clause can be traced to the case motivating it. Third, what does your candidate principle predict for cases I have not considered — say, diagnoses assigned to young children before they can understand them, or a machine-learning risk score that is disclosed to the people it scores?
