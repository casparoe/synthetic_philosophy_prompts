Last night I got into a long and somewhat heated argument with a friend about Newcomb's problem, and we reached an impasse I keep turning over. My friend insisted that one-boxing is simply correct, full stop: the predictor is stipulated to be highly accurate, so taking only the opaque box dominates in expected value, and anyone who two-boxes is leaving money on the table. I replied that two-boxing is simply correct: the money is already placed by the time you choose, nothing you do now can change the contents of the boxes, and dominance reasoning settles it. What struck me afterward is that we were both reasoning confidently from the same one-paragraph description of the case, and I have come to suspect that the description itself is where the problem lives.

So here is what I would like you to argue for me: the thesis that Newcomb's problem, as standardly stated, has no determinate answer, because the stipulation that the predictor is "highly accurate" radically under-specifies the case. In particular, I want you to argue that whether one-boxing or two-boxing is rationally mandatory depends on facts the standard setup leaves open -- most importantly, how the predictor achieves its accuracy. A predictor that runs a fine-grained simulation of your deliberation, a predictor that exploits some stable upstream cause of both your deliberation and its prediction, and a predictor that has merely observed a statistical regularity about people like you are three very different mechanisms, and I suspect the rational verdict flips across them. If that is right, then the endless one-boxer/two-boxer standoff is mostly an artifact of people quietly importing different precisifications of the same vague scenario, and my friend's confident verdict -- and mine -- were both premature. Make the strongest version of this case: spell out at least two precisifications, show how the rational verdict changes across them, and explain why the intuition that there must be a single right answer survives anyway.

Please be intellectually honest about the argument's limits rather than just performing conviction. In particular, end by telling me what would change your mind: which consideration -- a specific precisification where the verdict does not actually flip, a principled reason to treat the under-specification as irrelevant to rational choice, or something else -- would show that the problem does have a determinate answer after all, and where in your own reasoning you think the argument is most likely to break down.
