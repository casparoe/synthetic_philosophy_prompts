Construct a thought experiment that stress-tests the standard epiphenomenalist reply to the paradox of phenomenal judgment. Background: the paradox is that if phenomenal experiences are causally inert, our judgments about them cannot be caused by them, so their striking accuracy seems miraculous; the standard reply is that at least some phenomenal judgments are partly *constituted* by the experiences they concern, so no causal link is required to explain the fit. I want a case, built from ordinary-life materials (no zombies, no inverted spectra, no omniscient neurosurgeons), in which the constitution route and the causal route come apart: a case where a subject's self-ascription of an experience is partly constituted by that very experience, so the epiphenomenalist reply applies, yet where there is nonetheless intuitive pressure to deny that the subject knows what her experience is like — or, if you judge that direction impossible, the reverse: constitution absent, knowledge intuitively present. Constraints: the scenario itself must be at most 250 words; it must not stipulate the verdicts (the judgments about knowledge must follow from the setup, not be declared); and it must isolate the causation/constitution variable while holding the subject's reliability, truth, and belief-fixation method as fixed as you can.

After the scenario, demonstrate that the specifications are met, using a table with these columns: Specification | How the case meets it | Verdict under the causal theory of phenomenal knowledge | Verdict under the constitution reply | Reasoning behind each verdict | Strongest objection available to the side the case embarrasses. Below the table, add two short paragraphs: one identifying the single feature of the case that does the work and what happens to the verdicts if it is removed, and one stating what the case would show, if anything, about whether the paradox of phenomenal judgment can be dissolved rather than answered.
