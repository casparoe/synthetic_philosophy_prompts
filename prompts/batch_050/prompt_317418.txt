I'm in a grad reading group working through Bradley's Appearance and Reality (the 1893 one), and the regress argument from the chapters on relation and quality has been living rent free in my head for weeks. As I understand it, roughly paraphrasing: take a quality and a relation that's supposed to connect it to something else -- well, now you have two items, the quality and the relation, and you need to explain how THEY hang together, which seems to demand a further relation, and so on without end. Bradley's own escape hatch is some non-relational Absolute that absorbs everything, which honestly strikes me as giving the problem a name rather than solving it. But when I try to state my own view, I keep contradicting myself across cases, so I want to know if there's actually a principle behind my gut verdicts or if I just have a mood.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. (1) A list of words -- "cat," "the," "mat," "sat," "on" -- versus the sentence "the cat sat on the mat": the sentence has a unity the list plainly lacks, and I think something extra (syntax, the copula, whatever) genuinely has to be added; a pile of ingredients isn't a proposition. (2) Two red patches resembling each other: here I think the resemblance just relates them, full stop -- demanding a third thing to connect the resemblance to the patches is exactly the regress trap, and I refuse to step in. (3) A pile of stones versus a dry stone wall: nothing extra needed, arrangement alone does the work, talk of "glue" is just more arrangement. (4) The same notes played simultaneously versus played in sequence as a melody: something extra is needed (temporal order), the chord is not the tune. (5) A molecule versus the same atoms drifting near each other: I want to say bonds are just more physical stuff and arrangement, so no glue needed -- but I notice this sits uncomfortably with (1) and (4), where I demanded something extra. (6) A rose being red, i.e. the particular instantiating the universal: I don't think instantiation itself needs a further instantiation-relation to bind it to the rose, that's another regress trap. (7) Bradley's Absolute as the answer to all this: cheating, dissolves the question instead of answering it. And the pair that really bugs me: (1) and (2). If resemblance can relate its relata "for free" without generating a regress, why can't word-order or the copula unify the sentence for free -- and if the sentence genuinely needs a unifier, why doesn't resemblance?

So: what principle, if any, captures these verdicts? State it precisely -- something like a criterion for when a whole needs a further binding element beyond its parts and their relations, and when the binding comes for free -- and note which case motivates each clause of it. Test it against cases I haven't considered: a soccer team's unity over a season, the pixels of a digital photo forming an image, a promise binding two people, a fact versus the things it's about. And be honest about which of my verdicts the principle can't accommodate, because I suspect at least one of (1), (2), and (5) has to go. Please give me the answer as a short prose statement of the principle (a paragraph or two), then a table with these columns: Case | My verdict | Your principle's verdict | Agree? | One-line reason -- one row per case, mine and the new ones. End with a final row or short note listing which of my original verdicts you'd overturn and why that price is worth paying.
