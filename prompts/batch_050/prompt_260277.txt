A colleague in my logic reading group has an argument that purports to show constructive mathematics is not a coherent position at all — not merely weaker than classical mathematics, but outright self-contradictory. I cannot accept the conclusion, yet each step looks defensible and I cannot locate the break. I would especially like to keep steps (1) and (4), since they seem to be just descriptions of the constructivist stance and elementary logic, respectively. Here is the argument:

(1) Constructive mathematicians decline to accept the law of excluded middle — the schema P ∨ ¬P — as valid for arbitrary propositions P.

(2) To decline to accept a universal schema as valid just is to hold that it has counterinstances: a principle either holds for every proposition or fails for some, and refusing the first disjunct amounts to affirming the second.

(3) So the constructivist is committed to: there is some proposition P such that ¬(P ∨ ¬P).

(4) But even by the constructivist's own lights, ¬(P ∨ ¬P) yields a contradiction: from ¬(P ∨ ¬P) one can derive ¬P (since P would give P ∨ ¬P), and one can equally derive ¬¬P (since ¬P would give P ∨ ¬P) — and this derivation uses only reasoning the intuitionist accepts.

(5) Hence the constructivist's position, as characterized in (3), is inconsistent, and an inconsistent framework proves everything. Constructive mathematics therefore collapses: either it is contradictory, or its much-advertised rejection of excluded middle was never really a rejection of anything.
