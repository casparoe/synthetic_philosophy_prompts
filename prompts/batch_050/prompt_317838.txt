So I run a little decision-theory reading group at my department and last week we did Good's theorem, the result that for a Bayesian expected utility maximizer, free information before a decision never lowers expected utility and raises it whenever some decision's best choice actually depends on the info. Everyone nodded along, it's a clean proof. Then one of the grad students, Anna, came up with this extension that she claims shows the theorem is either trivial or that ordinary people are wildly irrational, and none of us could pin down where it slips. Here it is as best I remember:

(1) Good's theorem says that if information is free, a rational agent should never refuse it before making a decision.
(2) "Free" just means you don't pay anything for it -- no fee, no effort worth mentioning.
(3) So whenever someone offers you free information that bears on any decision you might face, rationality requires you to take it.
(4) Lots of decisions we might face hang on stuff we'd rather not know: whether you carry the gene for some incurable late-onset disease, whether your partner has ever cheated, your exact life expectancy, what your coworkers really think of you. Free tests and free ways of finding out exist for all of these.
(5) So rationality requires learning all of it, whenever the learning is free.
(6) But plainly it can be rational to refuse: declining the genetic test because the knowledge would poison your remaining healthy years seems perfectly sane, and whole medical ethics boards treat the right not to know as legitimate.
(7) So either Good's theorem is false, or expected utility theory is false, or "the right not to know" is just a polite name for irrationality.

Anna bites the bullet on irrationality, which feels like proving too much -- the argument would also show you should never watch a mystery film unspoiled if spoilers are free, never throw a surprise party, etc. I suspect something fishy happens around (2), or between (3) and (4), but when I pressed her she had a comeback for everything. Which step do you actually reject? For each candidate, tell me why it looked innocent and what it costs to reject it -- e.g., if the fix is to say information can carry costs that aren't fees, does that hollow out the theorem, or is that what it quietly assumed all along? I'd most like to keep Good's theorem itself and the sanity of refusing the test, so if there's a reading where both survive I'd take it.
