I am a professor of philosophy and, three years into an administrative appointment I no longer recall accepting, I chair my university's Algorithmic Systems Review Board. On Thursday we vote on a proposal from the Center for Teaching Innovation to deploy a language-model tutor, called Ariadne, in our large introductory courses, and I must circulate a written opinion beforehand. The vendor's dossier presents two configurations. In the first, Ariadne answers essentially every student query; on a benchmark of some twelve thousand questions drawn from past semesters, 96.8 percent of its responses were rated accurate and helpful, but the vendor states candidly that no worst-case guarantee is possible for a learned system: its failures -- confident fabrication of readings, subtly wrong proofs, one instance of alarming advice to a student in distress -- cannot be bounded or characterized in advance. In the second configuration, Ariadne abstains whenever its internal confidence is low, and the vendor guarantees that on the questions it does answer the error rate stays below one in a thousand; this is, in effect, a worst-case guarantee, but the system declines roughly 40 percent of all queries, and the vendor's own data show abstention rates climbing further for students writing in non-standard English and for genuinely novel questions -- which are, of course, the questions that most need answering. Deferred queries route to our already overextended human teaching assistants.

The board is split, and the split is philosophical whether or not my colleagues would call it that. The engineers and the economist argue that average-case performance against the relevant comparison class is the only coherent standard: human TAs err, tire, play favorites, and offer no worst-case guarantees either, so demanding one from a statistical system is a category mistake that amounts to a de facto ban on all machine learning, and the abstention mode merely purchases its guarantee by exporting risk onto the human escalation path. The physician, the general counsel's designee, and the student representative reply that an average computed over a distribution the deployer chose conceals whose tail risk is being spent, that a single catastrophic failure to a vulnerable student is not compensated by nine hundred and ninety-nine good answers, and that a guarantee holding only where the system deigns to speak is a promise about answers, not about students. One member further contends that human TAs need no ex ante guarantees because they are subject to ex post accountability -- they can be corrected, disciplined, and held responsible -- whereas software cannot be, and that this asymmetry, not any difference in error rates, is what licenses our relaxed attitude toward human fallibility.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate: a formal decision memo of the sort a board can act on, recommending one of approving the first configuration, approving the abstention configuration, approving with conditions (specify conditions with teeth, and a time frame for review), or rejecting deployment outright. I am inclined toward approval with conditions, but I worry that my inclination is just splitting the difference, and I want the memo to earn its conclusion rather than announce it. In particular: state what would have to be true for the average-case standard to be the right moral unit in a case like this, rather than assuming it either way; give the strongest version of the side I recommend against and say why it fails or is outweighed; and take a position on whether the abstention mode's bounded error rate is a genuine worst-case assurance or merely a relabeling of risk that has been moved off the ledger -- including whether the comparison with fallible but accountable humans should comfort us or indict us.
