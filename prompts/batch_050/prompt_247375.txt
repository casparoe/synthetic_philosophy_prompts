I've been reading about Jain philosophy and I've basically converted to anekāntavāda -- the many-sidedness doctrine. Not the watered-down version where it's just "be humble, everyone has their perspective," but the strong metaphysical version: reality has infinitely many aspects, every object bears infinitely many properties, so any flat one-sided predication is strictly false, and the right way to assert anything is conditionally (syāt, "in some respect"), with the sevenfold scheme of predications (it is; it is not; it is and is not; it is inexpressible; and the combos) doing the real work. The blind men and the elephant, except the elephant really does have all those shapes and no zookeeper's-eye view exists.

My question: what would actually count against this view? I don't want the standard "but the law of non-contradiction!!" gotcha -- the Jains have obviously heard that one, and the standpoint qualifier is their answer. I want to know which objections have teeth. Some things I keep circling:

- The self-application problem. Is anekāntavāda itself asserted conditionally or unconditionally? If conditionally, doesn't it undercut itself ("in some respects, truth is many-sided")? If unconditionally, doesn't it refute itself by its own lights? Does the usual reply -- that the doctrine is itself many-sided and one-sidedly true -- work, or is it wordplay?
- The collapse worry. Once you relativize every predicate to a standpoint (the pot exists relative to its own substance, not relative to other substances, etc.), haven't you just redescribed everything with more careful one-sided predicates? Then absolute truth sneaks back in as truths about standpoint-relative facts, and anekāntavāda turns out to be just... precise predication with extra ceremony. Is there a real difference here?
- The avaktavya predicate, the simultaneous "is and is not" from the *same* standpoint. Genuine third option, or the point where the doctrine pays the bill in incoherence? What would a defender have to say about what assertion even is for this to work?
- What would the world have to look like for many-sidedness to be false? Is there any possible observation or argument the doctrine rules out, or is it so flexible that nothing could ever count against it?

For each objection: say whether a committed Jain philosopher would concede it as a real defeater or dismiss it as confusing a naya (a partial standpoint) with the whole picture. Rank them fatal to merely awkward. And be honest about the insulation question -- if the answer is that nothing could count against the view because it makes no risky claims, tell me that straight, and tell me whether that's a vice for something that presents itself as a metaphysics rather than a piece of advice about intellectual humility. I guess I'd personally be most moved by the collapse worry, so weight that one accordingly.
