I keep running into the following configuration and I'd like to know whether it has an established name, and how it sits relative to some nearby claims I've only half-absorbed.

Two instances to fix the phenomenon. First: a hiring committee of two, both experienced and in good faith. They share every note, every interview impression, every reference call. Each then explains to the other, in full, how they weighted it all. One puts the candidate's chance of success at 80%, the other at 55%. After the full exchange, neither moves. Each says: given the interviews you happened to conduct and the hires you've watched succeed and fail over the years, your number is a reasonable response; and each freely concedes that had they lived the other's professional life, they'd be giving the other's number. Second: two friends who forecast elections as a hobby. They disclose not just their conclusions but their entire information diet — which analysts they read, which polls they trust and why. Same result: stable, fully transparent disagreement, with each granting the other's estimate is sensible given the evidence the other consumed.

Some stipulations to mark the edges. This is not mere stubbornness: both parties genuinely did absorb what the other said, and both would update on any new shared evidence. It is not a difference of values or tastes; the disagreement is over a factual probability. And it is not a case where one party has made a detectable mistake; by stipulation, neither can locate an error in the other's reasoning, only a difference in the evidential histories that fed it.

What puzzles me is how this relates to two things I've heard asserted. One is that rational people who start from the same view of the world cannot maintain a disagreement they both know about — that honest back-and-forth has to end in convergence. The other is the suggestion that for a given body of evidence there is exactly one rational credence, so any residual disagreement means someone is being irrational. My two cases seem to put pressure on both, but I can't tell where the pressure lands. Does the persistence of the disagreement show that the parties never really shared a starting point? Does it show that neither is fully treating the other's evidential history as evidence they themselves now possess? Or is the right moral that rationality simply underdetermines credence even for agents with identical starting positions?

So: does this configuration — stable, fully disclosed, mutually excused disagreement grounded entirely in divergent evidential biographies — have a name of its own? Please don't just hand me a label. Tell me what the nearest established concepts are, how the thing I described differs from each of them, which one fits best, and what my two cases would have to look like to fall cleanly inside or outside each candidate.
