I'm a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy of cognitive science course, and we've spent the last three weeks on the free energy principle and predictive processing. What strikes me is that the central dispute in this literature — call it the "is the FEP even a theory?" dispute — seems to have been going in circles for over a decade without either side conceding any ground or even clearly losing an argument.

Here's my understanding of the two camps. On one side, critics argue that the free energy principle is unfalsifiable in a damning way: any self-organizing system that persists over time can be redescribed as minimizing variational free energy, so the principle is compatible with every possible brain and every possible behavior, and therefore explains none of them. They sometimes compare it unfavorably to a just-so story — a mathematical redescription masquerading as a mechanism. On the other side, defenders (including Friston and those working in active inference) respond that the FEP is not meant to be a single falsifiable hypothesis but a framework or schema, on par with principles of least action in physics: vacuous in isolation, but generative of specific, testable "process theories" once you commit to particular generative models, particular priors, and particular neural implementations. They point to concrete predictions about precision-weighting, dopaminergic signaling, and conditions like autism and schizophrenia as evidence the framework earns its keep.

My working hypothesis — and I want this tested, not confirmed — is that the dispute has stalled because it is secretly three different disagreements sharing one name: a verbal disagreement about what deserves to be called a theory, a methodological disagreement about whether frameworks should be judged by their falsifiability or by their downstream yield, and a substantive empirical disagreement about whether the specific process theories the framework generates are actually doing better than their rivals. If I'm right, no single result could end the dispute, because each camp is keeping score differently.

What I want is a diagnosis of the impasse, not a verdict on predictive processing itself. Specifically: which of these three disagreements (or which others you identify) is actually doing the work when the two camps talk past each other? What does each side say that supports your attribution — where do defenders concede the principle's near-vacuity and pivot to fruitfulness, and where do critics concede the fruitfulness of particular models but deny it reflects credit on the principle? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction (say, between the principle, the framework, and a process theory), a formal result (a theorem showing free energy minimization constrains neural dynamics in some non-trivial way), or an experiment? If you think the dispute is genuinely ill-posed rather than merely hard, argue that directly — but show why the participants themselves are not irrational for continuing it.
