At a recent meetup, a friend made the case for futarchy with an argument I've encountered in various forms since I first read Robin Hanson: financial markets are astonishingly good at aggregating dispersed information -- a stock price distills the research of thousands of traders, and prediction markets routinely outpoll pundits on elections -- so instead of having legislators vote on policies, we should let markets price each policy's expected effect on some agreed national welfare metric and simply enact whichever policy the market prices highest. Policy would then absorb society's distributed knowledge the way prices do. The analogy was plainly doing the argumentative work: a policy market is just a stock market where the security pays out according to a national outcome, conditional on a particular policy being enacted. Something about it nags at me, though. A share of a company is a claim on cash flows that eventually arrive and can be arbitraged against; a conditional policy contract seems to live and die inside a counterfactual that may never be observed. I'm drafting a governance-reform essay and want to know whether this analogy can carry the weight before I lean on it.

So: audit the analogy as an argument rather than as rhetoric. Start with the explicit correspondence table -- what in a policy market plays the role of the firm's earnings, of the arbitrageur, of the short seller who disciplines hype, of liquidity, of the moment when a bet finally settles and forecasts get scored. Then walk the disanalogies: the thinness of participation relative to deep capital markets; the fact that the market only ever prices policies it expects to be adopted; the reflexivity problem that, once the decision rule is followed, the prices of rejected policies are never tested against reality, so the market's track record may be unscoreable in the cases that matter; and manipulation by actors who can move the outcome itself rather than merely forecast it. I care less about the length of the list than about the sorting: which of these breaks are fatal to the inference "markets work for stocks, therefore they can steer policy," and which are real but repairable with cleverer market design -- and what would the repairs have to look like?

Finally, the verdict: does the futarchy conclusion survive on the strength of the market-efficiency analogy alone, or does it require independent support that the analogy was quietly smuggling in -- for instance, assumptions about causation versus correlation in conditional prices? And if the stock-market picture was carrying more than it should, what is the better analogy to reach for when explaining the proposal to a skeptical audience?
