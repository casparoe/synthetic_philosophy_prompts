I defend the view that bare plural generics — sentences like "ducks lay eggs" — are true when the predicated property is in some sense characteristic of, or normal for, the kind, rather than when it holds of some threshold proportion of the kind's members. My motivation is the familiar spread of cases: "ducks lay eggs" is true although only mature females ever lay eggs, "mosquitoes carry malaria" is true although only a minute fraction of mosquitoes do, and "books are paperbacks" strikes me as false although the great majority of books in circulation are paperbacks. What I want is a map of this position's exposure. What would count against it? Please give me the observations, argument types, or formal results that should move me, ordered by how decisive they would be, including: (i) the specific problem cases for normalcy-based accounts — for instance generics that seem true but predicate nothing normal for the kind ("sharks attack bathers"), generics tied to rules or design rather than nature ("bishops move diagonally"), and generics that sound false even under the right statistical profile ("mosquitoes are male"); (ii) whether the notions of "characteristic" or "normal" can be spelled out without covertly appealing to generic truth itself, and what happens to the view if they cannot; (iii) what the view predicts that quantificational, probabilistic-threshold, and purely pragmatic rivals do not, if anything; and (iv) what evidence from how children acquire and understand generics, or from how adults actually judge borderline cases, would tell against it. Please distinguish what a defender of the view would herself concede as a genuine defeater from what only its critics regard as one, give an honest assessment of whether elasticity in "normal" lets the view absorb any counterexample and so renders it unfalsifiable, and say whether, if it is unfalsifiable in that way, that is fatal to it or merely a tax on it.
