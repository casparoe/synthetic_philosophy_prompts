I direct the patient advocacy office at a mid-sized regional hospital, and one of my standing responsibilities is reviewing complaints that a clinician ignored, minimized, or disbelieved a patient's account of their own condition — the kind of thing that gets called medical gaslighting in the press, though the complaints themselves range much wider. My office sees roughly thirty of these a month. I get about ninety minutes per case, my findings go to a peer review committee, and a substantiated finding can trigger mandatory coaching for the clinician, while an unsubstantiated one closes the matter against the complainant. The reviewers who work under me are nurses and administrators, not philosophers. I've been reading the literature on epistemic injustice — testimonial injustice, where a speaker's word is discounted because of a prejudice about the kind of person they are, and hermeneutical injustice, where someone cannot even render their experience intelligible because the shared concepts are missing — and I'm convinced this is exactly the framework my office needs. But it reaches me as theory, and what I need is a workflow.

Write me a decision procedure that translates these ideas into an ordered sequence of steps a reviewer without philosophy training can run on a case file: what questions to put to the record, what comparisons to make (for instance, how to operationalize the counterfactual of whether the same report from a different sort of patient would have been received differently), and how to tell a prejudicial credibility deficit apart from a clinically warranted one, since sometimes a patient's report really is inconsistent with the evidence and discounting it is good medicine rather than injustice. The procedure has to handle two complications my cases keep presenting. First, contaminated records: an early note describing a patient as dramatic or drug-seeking shapes everything downstream, so the file I read is not neutral evidence but partly the injustice's own output. Second, the reverse direction — credibility excess — since some complaints here involve an articulate, confident, well-presented patient whose self-diagnosis was accepted too readily and steered the workup off course. It should also say how to treat referrals from junior nurses whose reports about a patient were waved off by a senior physician, which I suspect is the same phenomenon wearing a different uniform. Please be explicit about which distinctions from the theory must survive the simplification and which can be dropped without loss, and mark every step that is a judgment call dressed up as a procedure rather than pretending the judgment away.

Then apply the procedure to three cases from my docket, described here in summary: (1) a woman with years of chronic abdominal pain and an anxiety diagnosis in her chart, repeatedly told her symptoms were stress-related, who was eventually found to have a serious gynecological condition; (2) a man whose chest pain complaint was, he says, brushed off, but whose file shows an appropriate workup and a genuinely shifting account of his symptoms — a case where I expect the procedure to vindicate the clinician against a complainant who will not accept the outcome; and (3) a refugee patient with limited English whose symptom reports reached the team filtered through a relative's interpreting and were charted as vague and noncontributory, which looks to me like the testimonial and hermeneutical problems stacked on top of each other. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: where my judgment remains irreducible, how it is likely to misfire (including the risk that it merely relocates the credibility judgment from the clinician to the reviewer, and the injustice that never makes it into any record at all), and what I should tell the committee the procedure's findings do and don't establish.
