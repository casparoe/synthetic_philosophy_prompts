I'm a PhD student in computational semantics, and for my dissertation I'm building a small annotated corpus of generic sentences in English -- things like "ducks lay eggs," "mosquitoes carry malaria," "lions have manes," "a triangle has three sides," "the German car industry exports worldwide." The annotations will be used to evaluate how well language models track the logic of generics: what they're actually committed to, how much exception they tolerate, and when they license inferences about individuals. My problem is that I need to write annotation guidelines for a team of annotators who are not semanticists, and the literature on generics gives me a thicket of partially competing analyses rather than anything operational. I'd like you to help me turn what semantics actually knows about generics into a decision procedure my annotators can run on each sentence: an ordered sequence of tests that classifies a generic by its source of tolerance for exceptions -- e.g., distinguishing cases where the claim holds of only a "relevant" subset (only female ducks lay eggs), cases driven by a salient dangerous or striking property (very few mosquitoes carry malaria, yet the generic feels true), cases that are near-definitional, and cases that seem to predicate something of the kind itself rather than its members.

The procedure should be concrete: for each test, say what the annotator should ask about the sentence, what counts as a pass or fail, what to do when the test is inapplicable or the answer is unclear, and where the procedure should stop and route the sentence to me as a hard case rather than forcing a label. Please also address how the procedure should treat adjacent phenomena that will otherwise contaminate the corpus: habitual sentences about individuals ("John smokes"), explicit quantificational paraphrases ("most ducks lay eggs"), and definite-generic uses ("the dodo is extinct"). I'd like three or four worked examples run through the whole procedure end to end, including at least one where the procedure delivers a classification a competent speaker would find counterintuitive, with an explanation of why the procedure lands there anyway and whether that's a defect or a feature.

One more request about calibration: the theoretical landscape here is genuinely unsettled -- whether generics are covert quantificational claims with pragmatically restricted domains, sui generis default generalizations tied to normalcy or kind-structure, or something else entirely -- and my annotators shouldn't inherit one theory's verdicts under the guise of neutral labeling. So where a step of the procedure quietly presupposes one analysis over its rivals, please flag that explicitly, say which step is theory-neutral and which is not, and where the field is genuinely in disagreement rather than just working out details, please say so rather than presenting one side as settled.
