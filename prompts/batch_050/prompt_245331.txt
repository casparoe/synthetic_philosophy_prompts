I help run a small reading group of software engineers -- longtime readers of rationalist blogs, the sort of people who have absorbed the slogan that information is physical and can gesture at Landauer's principle without being able to say where in any actual cycle the thermodynamic bill gets paid. Next month I'm leading our session on Maxwell's demon and its resolution, and I have ninety minutes and a whiteboard. No one in the room has thermodynamics beyond half-remembered high-school chemistry plus pop treatments, though they are all fluent with Shannon entropy from an information-theory-for-programmers angle.

Please take on the role of a statistical physicist with a decade of experience explaining this material to technical-but-untrained audiences. Think carefully about the dependency structure before committing to anything -- I'd encourage you to brainstorm two or three candidate orderings and candidate carrying examples first, weigh them, and then present the plan you actually endorse.

To be clear about the deliverable: do not explain the resolution to me. I know the standard Szilard--Landauer--Bennett story. Plan how I should explain it. Specifically:

First, the order of ideas. What has to be in place before the paradox can even be felt as a paradox rather than a curiosity? I'm fairly sure the entropy-of-a-probability-distribution view has to precede everything, and that the group needs to see thermodynamic and Shannon entropy as the same quantity in different units rather than as an analogy -- but tell me where each piece goes, what depends on what, and what I'd break by getting the sequence wrong.

Second, the carrying example. My instinct is to build the whole session on the one-particle Szilard engine with an explicit memory register, rather than on the original two-chamber demon sorting fast and slow molecules. Say whether you agree, and specify what the example must contain (an explicit reset step, a visible record) and what it must exclude at this level (quantum anything, multi-particle statistics, realistic door mechanisms) and why.

Third, the misconception map. List the wrong turns this particular audience will predictably take, in the order they'll take them, and mark exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact. I'd guess at least: the door-must-cost-energy red herring; the conviction that information is abstract and therefore thermodynamically free; the belief that the cost is paid at measurement rather than erasure; and, specific to this crowd, a premature confidence that they already understand it because they've read the slogan version.

Fourth, the check for understanding. Give me one question I can pose at the end whose answer would show the idea has actually landed -- my candidate is asking them to walk the full cycle and point to the precise step where the kT ln 2 per bit is paid, and then to explain why a demon with an unlimited supply of blank memory really would violate the second law. Improve on it if you can.

Finally, tell me what to openly refuse to cover at this level -- quantum generalizations, fluctuation theorems, experimental verifications, the philosophy-of-physics literature on whether erasure is truly the locus of the cost -- and how to say so without making the session feel truncated.
