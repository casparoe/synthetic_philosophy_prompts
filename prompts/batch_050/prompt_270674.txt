I teach an undergraduate seminar on statistical reasoning, and I want to run a 90-minute session on the phenomenon in which a trend that holds within every subgroup of a population reverses when the subgroups are pooled. I don't want to lecture on it, and I don't want the students to hear the standard name for it until the very end of the session, because the students who already know the name tend to pattern-match rather than think. There will be about 18 students, and I can split them into small groups. I can offer modest real stakes (say, points that convert into a small amount of extra credit, or a candy budget of a few dollars per group).

Design me an activity with the following properties.

First, the paradox should arise from something the students themselves do, not from a dataset I hand them. I don't want a worksheet where they compute success rates for two treatments and notice the reversal; I want the rules and incentives of a game or role-play to be arranged so that the students' own reasonable-seeming choices generate the confounding, and so that they then get burned by it -- ideally by making a consequential decision on the basis of aggregate numbers that the finer-grained record, which is sitting right there, contradicts. If a natural way to do this is to have them allocate scarce resources across cases of differing difficulty, or to compete under rules that make one option look worse in total despite being better in every stratum, something along those lines is welcome, but I'm open to a completely different mechanism if it works better.

Second, give me the full run-sheet: setup, the exact rules and payoffs as you'd state them to the room, how many rounds or phases, what information is visible and when, what the facilitator does and says at each point (including what to do while students are mid-mistake), and the moment of the reveal.

Third, a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the activity onto the structure of the phenomenon: which rule or incentive played the role of the lurking variable, which decision was the one the aggregate data licensed and the stratified data forbids, and so on. Also: what will predictably go wrong when this is actually run (e.g., small-sample noise washing out the reversal, a group that doesn't fall for the trap and therefore ruins the setup, a student who recognizes the paradox early and announces it), and how to handle each. And be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: presumably it can show that such reversals are possible and can be self-inflicted, but it cannot by itself teach how to detect a lurking variable in observational data in the wild, where nobody hands you the correct stratification -- say how the debrief should bridge that gap.

Finally, in the facilitator notes, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested. The bare arithmetic fact that such reversals can occur is not in dispute, but my understanding is that there is more disagreement -- historically and to some extent still -- about whether resolving a given reversal is a purely statistical matter or whether it essentially requires causal assumptions that the data alone cannot supply. Where the answer you'd be giving students is contested among people who actually work on this, say so explicitly rather than presenting one view as the consensus, and tell me how to phrase it to undergraduates without either overstating the controversy or papering over it.
