Below is a set of cases about how welfare should be aggregated across beings with very different capacities for converting resources into well-being. For each case I have reported my own verdict. I want to know whether some principle, or small set of principles, captures all of my verdicts; if not, I want to know precisely which verdicts I have to surrender.

The cases:

1. **The classic monster.** A being derives vastly more well-being per unit of resource than any human does, at every level of provision. Straight summation says we should give it nearly everything and leave humanity in misery. My verdict: that conclusion is wrong, and any theory that endorses it is thereby damaged.

2. **The diminishing monster.** A being whose marginal conversion rate is somewhat higher than any human's at low levels of provision but falls steeply after a modest threshold. Straight summation says we should feed it first, then turn to ourselves. My verdict: that conclusion is perfectly fine; prioritizing it up to the threshold is just what decency requires.

3. **The dud.** A being whose conversion rate is abysmal: enormous inputs yield only faint contentment. Straight summation says we may neglect it entirely, even at the cost of its suffering. My verdict: neglecting it is wrong; its low conversion efficiency does not erase its claim on us.

4. **The sadist.** A being of ordinary conversion efficiency whose greatest pleasures consist in contemplating the suffering of others, which it actively arranges when resourced. Straight summation counts those pleasures like any others. My verdict: its pleasures should count for nothing, or less than nothing.

5. **The swarm.** A billion tiny organisms, each capable of a whisper of pleasure, versus one human life of deep and varied flourishing. Summation, given enough organisms, sides with the swarm. My verdict: the human life wins no matter how large the swarm grows.

6. **The apprentice.** A being whose conversion rate is currently low but would, with investment, surpass every human's within a decade. Summation ranks it by its present rate. My verdict: the investment is at least permissible and arguably required, which means future conversion capacity must matter to the ranking now.

I am aware that my verdicts are in tension. Verdicts 1 and 3 want the distribution to be insensitive to conversion rates, while verdict 2 wants it to track them. Verdict 4 wants the source or quality of a pleasure to affect its weight, which seems to reopen the door that verdict 5 tries to close by making depth of experience lexically dominant over quantity. Verdict 6 seems to require modal facts about a being's possible future welfare to do work that the other cases never mention.

Your task: state a principle, or a set of candidate principles, precisely enough that each clause can be tied to the case that motivates it. For each candidate, tell me which of my six verdicts it preserves and which it overturns, and give its verdict on one case I have not considered: a being whose conversion rate is unknown and unknowable, about whom we can only form a probability distribution. Finally, say honestly whether any single coherent view saves all six verdicts, and if the price of saving them all is ad hoc complexity, say so plainly.

Please write your analysis in American English. After the prose analysis, include a summary table with exactly these columns: Case (a short label); My Verdict (one line); Principle's Verdict (one line, for your preferred principle); Preserved or Overturned; and Rationale (one sentence). If you offer more than one candidate principle, provide one table per principle.
