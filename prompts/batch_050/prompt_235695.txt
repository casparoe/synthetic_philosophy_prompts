I'm putting together next term's unit on advance directives for my personal identity & bioethics course (upper-level undergrads plus a couple of grad students auditing, about 16 people, one 100-minute session) and I want to replace my usual Dworkin-vs-Dresser discussion section with something they'll actually remember. Design me a game or simulation they play — not a discussion with extra steps.

The core requirement: I want students to feel, over the course of the game and in their own person, why the authority of the earlier self is both compelling and creepy. So the central mechanic has to be temporal rotation. Players take turns being the same fictional person at different life stages, such that everyone eventually gets to be (a) the competent author of an advance directive, (b) a "middle self" who is still competent, dimly aware of the directive, and in a position to revoke or amend it but has reasons not to bother, and (c) the occupant of the demented stage, who is bound by whatever the author wrote. The directive must be a real written text, fixed once written, and the author cannot communicate with the later-stage players beyond it — no passing notes, no clarifying what they "really meant," because that's how actual directives work.

Some more constraints:

- Two incommensurable scoring tracks: one for moment-to-moment experiential welfare (comfort, pleasure, freedom from distress) and one for life-narrative goods (dignity, reputation, coherence with one's projects, how the story ends). The rules must generate at least one situation where the tracks point in opposite directions and someone has to pick. This is how I want the experiential vs. critical interests distinction to show up — in the scoring, not in a lecture from me afterwards.
- The author writes the directive under time pressure and with incomplete information about what the later scenarios will actually be (I'd suggest the facilitator holds back the stage cards until each stage begins). Bounded foresight is half the problem in real life and I want it in the game.
- The middle self slot matters to me philosophically. Build in a genuine opportunity to revoke or amend that carries some small cost, so that when they let it slide we can ask in debrief whether the directive's authority at the end came from the original writing or from the non-revoking. I don't know of this ratification-by-omission angle being worked out carefully anywhere and I'm curious where student intuitions land on it.
- Small real stakes, please — points converting into the seminar snack budget or a homework-extension token, enough that experiential-track losses sting a little.
- The occupant-stage player needs real freedom of action within the directive's constraints. If they're just a puppet executing instructions, the game proves nothing.

Then the debrief plan: map specific moments of play onto the actual positions — Dworkin's precedent autonomy, the Dresser-style challenge (why should this person be bound by decisions made by what is, psychologically speaking, someone else), Parfit on connectedness doing quiet work under the hood, and if there's time Jaworska's line that even the demented self retains values deserving respect. Tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run this — students treating it as a pure optimization puzzle, the occupant player hamming it up and wrecking the stakes, the author writing a novel instead of a directive — and how to handle each. End with an honest paragraph on what the game structurally cannot reproduce (my own list includes the actual phenomenology of dementia, stakes spanning decades instead of minutes, and the fact that a real occupant can't just quit — but give me yours).

One warning: don't build it so one side obviously wins. If the directive the author writes is cartoonishly cruel, everyones a Dresserite by round three and the interesting version of the problem never shows up. The directives should be the kind a resonable, reflective person would actually write for themselves.
