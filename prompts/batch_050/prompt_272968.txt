I'm an economist working in organizational economics, and my working vocabulary is principal-agent models, mechanism design, incomplete contracts, and the usual apparatus of hidden action, hidden information, commitment, and enforcement. A colleague in Chinese history has been telling me that Han Feizi and the Legalist tradition anticipated much of what my field formalized in the twentieth century, and I'm skeptical enough of that kind of claim to want it tested properly rather than asserted. So here is what I'd like: take the core of Han Feizi's position on governing -- that a state should run on publicly promulgated, impersonal standards (fa) with rewards and punishments attached mechanically to performance relative to those standards, that the ruler should rely on technique (shu) and positional power (shi) rather than on personal moral excellence, that officials chosen for their reputation for virtue are a liability because reputations can be cultivated and performances of virtue can be faked, and that the ruler himself should remain inscrutable and unswayed by personal feeling so that subordinates cannot play to his preferences -- and restate this whole package in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Be specific: which pieces map onto incentive compatibility, which onto the case for rules over discretion, which onto the problems of screening and of preference falsification, which onto commitment devices, and so on.

The part I care about most is the residue report. Go through the translation honestly and tell me: which claims survive essentially intact, which survive only by being distorted (and say exactly what gets bent -- for instance, whether the Legalist ruler's studied inscrutability has a real counterpart in mechanism design or whether it gets flattened into something like not revealing your type, losing whatever it originally was), and which claims simply have no expression in the vocabulary of contracts and incentives at all. Candidates I have in mind for the last category include Han Feizi's treatment of the ruler's own conduct as exempt from the standards he imposes on everyone else, and his hostility to moral cultivation as such rather than merely to unverifiable claims of it -- but correct me if I'm wrong that these resist translation.

Then take a stand. Don't give me a balanced menu of interpretations: tell me, with reasons, whether the untranslatable residue shows that my economic vocabulary is missing something a theory of governance needs (and if so, what), or whether it shows that the Legalist position is less coherent or less substantive than its admirers claim once the parts with a clear functional interpretation are stripped out. If the truth, in your judgment, is some of each, say which specific elements fall on which side rather than splitting the difference. Please write your answer in American English.
