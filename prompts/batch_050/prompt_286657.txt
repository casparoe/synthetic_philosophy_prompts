As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I am preparing an essay on the Hart–Devlin debate and need help auditing a colleague’s summary. She writes:

Following the Wolfenden controversy, Patrick Devlin argued that a society is constituted partly by a shared moral order. Criminal law may therefore prohibit conduct condemned by the ordinary reasonable person, even when each particular act occurs privately and causes no measurable injury, because unchecked vice can weaken the bonds holding society together. On Devlin’s account, sufficiently deep majority revulsion is itself a legitimate ground for coercion, and any distinction between public and private immorality is ultimately artificial. H. L. A. Hart replied that punishment is justified only when conduct causes concrete harm to another person. Mere offense, disgust, or departure from conventional morality can never provide a reason for criminalization. Hart accordingly denied that shared morality has any legitimate role in law and treated Devlin’s claim about social disintegration as empirically refuted. The debate is therefore a straightforward contest between a majoritarian moralist willing to police every private vice and a liberal defender of a bright-line harm principle.

Which elements of this summary capture the central disagreement accurately, and which involve exaggeration or conflation? In particular, please consider whether Devlin equated social morality with simple majority opinion, whether he thought every breach of that morality should be punished, and whether his social-disintegration argument was purely empirical. On Hart’s side, consider whether harm should be understood as narrowly concrete injury, whether he denied law any connection with shared morality, and whether his position rules out every offense- or paternalism-based restriction in exactly the same way. Distinguish conventional from critical morality, public from private conduct, and the claim that immorality can sometimes justify prohibition from the claim that it always does. Conclude by judging whether the colleague’s final characterization remains defensible after the necessary corrections, and provide a fair two-sentence replacement for it.
