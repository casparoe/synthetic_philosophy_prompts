I teach metaphysics at a small liberal-arts college, and this semester's graduate seminar has left me in an honest muddle about grounding. My students pressed me last week on whether my assorted intuitions about what grounds what actually cohere, and I had to admit I didn't know. I'd like your help, as someone expert in contemporary analytic metaphysics, in working out what theory of grounding my verdicts add up to -- or which verdict I have to surrender. Please think carefully, and before settling on your answer, briefly entertain at least one rival principle and say in a sentence why it loses.

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can report them. (1) Socrates grounds the existence of his singleton set, and not the other way around; the direction matters to me. (2) The truth that grass is green or snow is chartreuse is fully grounded in grass's being green alone -- the false disjunct contributes nothing -- whereas the truth that grass is green and snow is white is grounded in the two conjuncts only jointly, never in one of them. (3) The rose's being scarlet grounds its being red, and its being red grounds its being colored, and I have no discomfort saying the scarlet thereby grounds the colored; transitivity seems innocent here. (4) Socrates himself is grounded in the aggregate of the simples composing him. (5) Yet I balk at saying those simples ground the singleton of Socrates: the set, I want to say, depends on Socrates, not on which particular atoms happen to make him up this Tuesday. I realize (1), (3), (4), and (5) may be jointly inconsistent, and that's precisely my problem. (6) That all bachelors are unmarried strikes me as grounded in no facts about actual bachelors whatsoever; I'm tempted to call it ungrounded tout court. (7) That there are no unicorns genuinely puzzles me -- half of me says it's grounded in the complete positive inventory of the animal kingdom, the other half says a negative truth like that wants no ground at all, and I cannot settle it.

What I'd like from you: first, a single displayed sentence stating the most precise principle (or compact set of clauses) that captures as many of these verdicts as possible -- specifying which of the standard formal features of grounding you are taking on board and which you are rejecting -- followed by a clause-by-clause defense tied to the cases that motivate each part. Then tell me frankly which of my seven verdicts your principle cannot save, which one you advise me to abandon and why that one rather than another, and how it adjudicates my wavering in (7). Finally, state what your account predicts for one grounding case I have not listed. Keep the entire reply under 500 words; if you must choose between a baroque principle that saves everything and a clean one that sacrifices a verdict, show me both but commit to one.
