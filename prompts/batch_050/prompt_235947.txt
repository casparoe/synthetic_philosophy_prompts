Exam question: "State the conceivability argument (the zombie argument) against physicalism. Identify which premise you take to be most contentious, and state and briefly evaluate one standard physicalist response to the argument."

Student's answer:

The conceivability argument runs as follows. A philosophical zombie is defined as a being that is physically identical to a conscious human, molecule for molecule, but that lacks phenomenal consciousness entirely — there is nothing it is like to be it. The argument then proceeds: (1) zombies are conceivable; (2) if zombies are conceivable, they are metaphysically possible; (3) if zombies are metaphysically possible, then consciousness does not supervene on the physical, so physicalism is false. The conceivability claim is supported by the fact that we can imagine the zombie scenario without any apparent contradiction, unlike, say, a married bachelor.

The most contentious premise is (2). We know from Kripke's discussion in Naming and Necessity that conceivability is not a reliable guide to possibility: we can seemingly conceive of water failing to be H2O, yet if water is H2O, this is impossible. Kripke's own view was that identity statements like "pain is C-fiber firing," if true at all, are contingent, and this is part of why he found the mind–brain identity theory attractive. Chalmers responds on behalf of the argument by distinguishing prima facie from ideal conceivability: water-without-H2O is only prima facie conceivable, whereas the zombie hypothesis survives ideal rational reflection, and ideal conceivability does entail possibility. Chalmers also invokes his two-dimensional framework, according to which the zombie scenario is verified at a world considered as actual, even if "consciousness" has a secondary intension that picks out a physical process at our world.

A standard physicalist response is the phenomenal concept strategy. Chalmers himself concedes that phenomenal concepts are special — we refer to our experiences directly, rather than via a description — and this explains the explanatory gap: the apparent conceivability of zombies reflects the distinctive structure of our phenomenal concepts rather than any gap in reality.

Overall, the argument succeeds in showing that consciousness cannot be physical. It therefore establishes property dualism, and more specifically epiphenomenalism, since the zombie's physical processes produce all its behavior without any contribution from consciousness.
