My father retires next month after forty-two years as a clockmaker and restorer of mechanical clocks, and I am giving the toast at his retirement dinner. The room will be split roughly in two. One half is colleagues: horologists, conservators from the museum he has worked with, and a few physicists he befriended through years of arguing about precision timekeeping -- people who know what a chronometer escapement is and will notice any technical carelessness. The other half is family and neighbors, who know him as the quiet man whose workshop always hummed and whose pockets always had small screws in them.

I want a toast of about five minutes, ready to read aloud, built around Augustine's discussion of time in Book XI of the Confessions -- specifically the puzzle that we claim to measure time, yet we can measure neither the past, which no longer exists, nor the future, which does not yet exist, nor the present, which has no extent at all and passes while we try to grasp it; and Augustine's resolution, as I understand it, that what we actually measure is something within the soul: a stretching of attention across memory of what has passed, direct regard of what is before us, and expectation of what is to come.

The philosophy must do real structural work in the speech, not sit on top of it as an ornament. On the surface, a toast to a man who spent his life measuring time could go one of two lazy ways: romanticize him as a keeper of time itself, or make a cheap joke about finally having time on his hands. Augustine's puzzle forbids both, and I want you to let it forbid them. Take seriously that, on Augustine's account, my father's forty-two years were spent doing something stranger and more human than the job description says: not measuring a stuff that flows past us, but cultivating in himself an extraordinarily disciplined memory, attention, and anticipation, and building instruments whose real function is to give strangers a shared present and a mutually trusted expectation of the future. The speech should honor him through that reframing -- through what his craftsmanship actually consisted in, and what his clocks actually did for people -- without ever claiming anything Augustine would have found confused, such as that he stored time up, gave people extra time, or held time still.

Practical constraints: the physicists should feel the argument was made correctly, without a single equation; the family should follow it without a single Latin word, or at most one, glossed in passing. Include exactly one moment of gentle humor, and make it depend on the philosophy rather than stand apart from it. End on something true about what a retirement means for a man whose soul has been stretched across other people's mornings for four decades.
