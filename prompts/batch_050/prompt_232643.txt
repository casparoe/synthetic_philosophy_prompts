I'm TAing a philosophy of social science seminar this semester, about 20 undergrads, and I get a 90-minute slot next week on Popper's Poverty of Historicism. Last year I tried to teach it straight and the students nodded along in the worst way -- they all came away thinking Popper's point is just that history is "too complicated to predict," like weather forecasting but worse, and then they spent the whole discussion on chaos theory. That completely misses what I want them to feel. The interesting claim is structural, not about complexity. Two structural things specifically: first, the Oedipus effect, where a prediction about people becomes part of what those people respond to, so the forecast can fulfill or wreck itself in a way that never happens with electrons or comets; and second, Popper's logical argument about the growth of knowledge -- if the future depends on inventions and ideas nobody has had yet, then predicting the future would require already possessing those ideas, which is the contradiction sitting at the core of the book. My students can recite both of these. They cannot feel either of them.

So design me a game or simulation that makes them run into these problems before anyone names them. Give me actual runnable rules: setup, materials, roles, rounds, and some small real stakes (I'm fine with candy, points that count for participation credit, whatever works). Here's the shape I'm imagining, though you can improve on it: an early phase where players make forecasts about the group's collective behavior while their forecasts are secret, and the task turns out to be pretty tractable; then a phase where forecasts must be posted publicly before anyone acts, so each forecaster now has to model everyone else modeling them, and the whole thing should visibly collapse into regress or self-defeat. Then a later phase where I (or the game) introduce a genuinely new option or rule mid-stream that no player could have anticipated in advance, as a stand-in for unpredictable knowledge growth -- it should be obvious in the moment that no amount of cleverness in the earlier rounds could have priced this in.

Then I need a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the game onto Popper's moves, including his distinction between unconditional prophecy and the conditional if-then predictions that legitimate social science is allowed to make, so students don't walk out thinking Popper rejected social science tout court. Tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run this -- e.g., the student who says "just average everyone's predictions," the one who concludes "so forecasting is just unreliable" instead of "impossible in principle," the chaos-theory kid from last year -- and give me a response for each. And be honest with me about which feature of the argument the game structurally cannot reproduce (I suspect the strictly logical character of the knowledge-growth argument survives only as an analogy in the game) and how to supply that piece in the discussion afterward. Write the whole thing in American English please.
