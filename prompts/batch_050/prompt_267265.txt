My roommate (a grad student) put this argument together and neither of us can find the hole, but the conclusion can't be right. (1) For a perceptual experience to justify a belief, it has to be available to the subject as a reason, not merely be what caused the belief. (2) Something can function as a reason for a subject only if the subject grasps it conceptually -- a non-conceptual "given" would just be a bare presence, and bare presences can't stand in rational relations to anything (this is basically Sellars's whole point against the myth of the given). (3) But conceptually articulated states are just beliefs, or at least belief-like states. (4) So only beliefs can justify beliefs, and experience as such never enters the epistemic picture. (5) If justification is only ever a relation among beliefs, then any sufficiently coherent system of beliefs is equally justified, including the contents of a consistent fantasy novel. (6) Empirical knowledge is possible and nobody knows anything by writing coherent fantasy. (7) So one of the premises has to go, and apparantly it's (2), i.e. Sellars was wrong. Where does this actually go wrong? If I have to give something up I'd much rather drop (4) or (5) than (2), because (2) feels like the entire lesson of classical foundationalism's failure. Also: price the alternatives for me -- what do minimal/non-conceptual content views, McDowell-style conceptualism, and straight reliabilism each pay to block the argument, and do they block it at the same step?
