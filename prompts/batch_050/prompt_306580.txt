Eighteen months ago I faced the biggest decision of my life: stay in a stable, well-paid position at a large firm, or leave to co-found a startup with two friends. The catch was that I had no probabilities I could honestly stand behind — not even rough ones. Every attempt to put numbers on the startup's success felt like fiction dressed up as rigor. In the end I decided by something close to minimax regret: I compared the worst-case outcomes of each path and asked which failure I could live with. The decision worked out, but what stuck with me was the reasoning itself, and I've since written a paper defending a restricted thesis: that under genuine ignorance — where the agent lacks defensible probabilities rather than merely having imprecise ones — maximin- and minimax-regret-style rules are rationally permissible, not just psychologically excusable.

Next month I'm presenting this paper at a decision theory workshop. The audience will be about fifteen people: roughly half economists and formal epistemologists trained in the subjective expected utility tradition, several of whom treat probabilism as close to constitutive of rationality; two or three people working on ambiguity aversion who will want to fold my position into existing maxmin expected utility models and will resist my claim that genuine ignorance is a separate category; and one political philosopher who keeps invoking Rawls against Harsanyi in ways I consider sloppy and who will try to recruit me. There are no friendly fellow-travelers in the room.

Act as an experienced decision theorist who has refereed for journals in both economics and philosophy and who has sat through many such workshops. Think carefully and work in stages: first brainstorm a long, unfiltered list of every objection this particular audience might raise — including technical ones about the axioms these rules violate, the standard ultra-pessimism counterexamples to maximin, the dependence of regret rules on how the choice set is described, and the pressure to reinterpret ignorance as extreme ambiguity — and then curate that list down to the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room.

Deliver the result as a prep sheet: each objection phrased the way that audience member would actually phrase it, ordered by how likely it is to come up and how much damage it does if I handle it badly; for each, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, an explicit note on what I should concede up front versus what I should hold, and a flag on where the reply is softest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and tell me why I should fear it.

Throughout, distinguish carefully between what is settled — the formal properties of these rules, the known axiom violations, the standard textbook counterexamples — and what is genuinely contested among specialists, such as whether those axioms are rationally mandatory under ignorance and how to interpret the Rawls–Harsanyi dispute. Do not present any contested position as the consensus view.
