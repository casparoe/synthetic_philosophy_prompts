So a friend and I were trying to reconstruct why exactly Rawls thought he could beat Harsanyi, and we ended up building this argument that seems to prove something neither of us can accept. Numbered so you can point at the step that breaks:

(1) A just social arrangement is whichever one a rational person would choose if they didn't know which person's position they'd end up occupying in it.

(2) If you genuinely have no information about which of n positions you'll occupy, rationality requires treating each as equally likely -- assign probability 1/n to each. That's just what "no information" means here.

(3) Facing equal probabilities over the positions, a rational chooser maximizes expected utility, which in this setup is just the average utility across positions.

(4) So justice requires maximizing average utility. Harsanyi wins, maximin loses.

(5) Application: Policy A gives everyone in a city a decent, modest life for sure. Policy B gives everyone a 99.9% chance of a wonderful life and a 0.1% chance of early death, and the average utility comes out higher than A. By (1)-(4), justice requires B.

(6) And this generalizes: at every margin, justice requires trading certain losses concentrated on a few for tiny average gains spread over everyone, whenever the arithmetic works out. So a just society should keep loosening safety regulations, pollution limits, etc., every time the average ticks up -- even when regulators know, before choosing, that the looser standard will produce specific excess deaths that the stricter one wouldn't.

(7) But a policy chosen knowing it will produce deaths that an affordable alternative wouldn't is murder-by-spreadsheet. Justice can't require that. So something above has to go.

The thing is, if I trace it backwards I only see two exits, and both are horrible: either deny (1) and throw out the whole impartial-contract way of thinking about justice, or deny that expected-utility maximization is rationally required even for an individual facing a genuine gamble -- which seems to just deny decision theory. If forced I'd rather keep (1) and take the second bullet, but I don't want to.

So: which step actually breaks? If it's (2), I need to hear why (2) isn't just a truism about ignorance -- what else could "no basis for probabilities" rationally produce? And two follow-ups: does your diagnosis change if the veil is a *real* lottery (you will literally be randomly reassigned to a position after the choice) rather than the usual thought experiment? And I keep suspecting something slides between (4) and (6) -- a move from one-shot choice to iterated policy -- but everytime I try to state the slide precisely it evaporates on me. If there's really no slide there, say so and tell me why (6) feels like a reductio anyway.
