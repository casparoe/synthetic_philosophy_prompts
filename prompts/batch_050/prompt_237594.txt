Some background so you know where I'm coming from: I spent fourty years as an experimental condensed matter physicist, and since retiring I've fallen hard for philosophy. There's a little reading group of other retired scientists that meets at my kitchen table on Thursdays, and last month we worked through Fitch's knowability paradox. It's been bugging me ever since, in the way a good physics problem used to. As I understand it: start from the innocent sounding claim that every truth is knowable, plug in for p the sentence "p and nobody knows that p", and after a few modal and epistemic steps you're forced to conclude that every truth is actually known. The derivation itself seems watertight to us, so the fight is over which premise has to give. My physicist's gut says the trick is in the substitution step, like when a proof quietly divides by zero, but I can't cash that intuition out.

Here's what we've already collected as responses, so please don't repeat any of these: (1) Tennant-style restriction -- limit the knowability principle to truths whose being known isn't itself inconsistent; (2) the Dummettian bullet-bite -- embrace a verificationist picture of truth where the conclusion stops looking absurd because truth just is warranted assertibility in principle; (3) Edgington's situational reading -- distinguish knowing p in some counterfactual situation from p being knowable in the actual one, and say the proof equivocates between them; (4) the relevant-logic move -- deny that knowledge distributes over conjunction in the way the derivation needs; and (5) the response that just concedes the conclusion to the realist and takes the argument as a reductio of anti-realism, full stop.

What I want from you: take on the role of a philosophical logician who's spent years thinking about epistemic logic, and think carefully before settling on anything. Start by brainstorming, say, five candidate responses that are genuinely different from the five above -- not rewordings, special cases, or combinations of them (so no tweaking the restriction principle and calling it new). Then pick the strongest candidate and develop it properly: what premise or step does it reject or reframe, what does it commit you to, and -- be honest with me here -- is it actually any good, or is it clever-looking but rotten underneath? If your best candidate turns out weak, I'd rather hear that than a sales job. Finish with a sentence or two I can bring back to the group about what your response would mean for our shared intuition that there are things nobody will ever know, like the exact number of blades of grass in my lawn at noon today.
