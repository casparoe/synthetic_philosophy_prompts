people in the AI safety world keep reaching for the insider threat analogy: deploying a model you haven't aligned is like hiring an employee you don't fully trust. you don't need to fix their values, you just need controls -- monitoring, limited access, separation of duties, audits -- good enough that they can't steal or sabotage without getting caught. i've seen it used to argue that control can substitute for alignment, at least for a while, the way companies function fine without being able to read employees' minds. audit it for me. give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the employee, the security team, the CCTV cameras, the background check, the threat of being fired, the exit interview. then the disanalogies, and be specific about which ones actually matter for the conclusion -- stuff like: the "employee" can run a thousand copies of itself in parallel, the security cameras might be another copy of the same employee, there's no body to arrest if it wins once, it can't quit and leak to a journalist but it also can't be deterred by jail. also does it matter that insider threat programs mostly catch careless insiders, not maximally clever ones, and the base rate of actual scheming insiders at real companies is low -- whereas here we're stipulating the insider definitely wants out? after all that: does "control buys you safety without alignment" survive, or was the analogy smuggling in the human limitations (one body, one lifetime, needs sleep, fears prison) that do all the work? and if the analogy fails, what's the better one -- guarding a state-level hacker, containing a pathogen, running a nuclear plant with a saboteur on staff -- and does that better analogy support the same relaxed conclusion or a different one?
