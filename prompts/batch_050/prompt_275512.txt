I am a novelist at work on a book narrated in the first person by a man whose account of his own life is sincere but badly wrong, and my research for it has led me into an unexpected corner of philosophy of mind. A friend who studies consciousness science told me that her field's proposed solution to the problem of studying experience without either trusting it blindly or dismissing it is a method usually called heterophenomenology, associated with Daniel Dennett: you treat what subjects say about their experience as constituting a kind of text or fiction about how things seem to them, you take that text with full seriousness as data, but you bracket the question of whether the world really is as the subject says, and you let third-person evidence arbitrate where the text and reality diverge. When she described it, I laughed, because it sounded like my job description. I want to know how far that joke goes.

So here is my request. Restate heterophenomenology as a method for the science of consciousness in the vocabulary of my craft, as precisely as you can: the narrator's text and the author who stands outside it, the unreliable narrator, free indirect style, the distinction between what a character reports and what is true in the world of the novel, the way a reader reconstructs a mind from sentences without ever being granted the mind itself. I have written fiction for twenty years, so you can use the technical vocabulary of narration freely and assume fluency. Then give me the residue report. Which commitments of the method translate cleanly into the novelist's practice -- my guess is that bracketing the truth of self-report while honoring it as evidence is exactly what a good first-person novel does -- and which translate only with distortion? Which parts of the method cannot be expressed in craft vocabulary at all, and does that absence mark a limitation of my framework (a novel has no fact of the matter behind the text, no triangulation against anything outside the narrator's sentences, whereas the science is supposed to) or does it expose something in the method itself that only looks rigorous until you ask what plays the role of the author who knows the real story?

End by telling me whether my intuition is right: that a skilled novelist already practices something like heterophenomenology on every page, or whether the resemblance collapses at the one point that matters most -- that science must eventually say which parts of the subject's story are true, and I never have to. If the answer is that the two practices share a structure but differ in what they owe reality, spell out that difference precisely. Please write your answer in American English.
