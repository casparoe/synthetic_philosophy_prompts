I'm putting together a philosophy of biology reading group and we're doing the Gould and Lewontin spandrels critique next month. Could you write the desiderata that an evolutionary explanation of a trait has to meet before it counts as genuinely showing the trait is an adaptation -- i.e. before it survives the spandrels-style objection?

For each requirement, give the consideration or case behind it, and be concrete about the motivating examples: things like the human chin (no-one thinks the chin itself was selected for, but it's there), male nipples, the redness of blood, that sort of thing. The kinds of desiderata I have in mind are stuff like: the explanation must say something testable that goes beyond just noting the trait is currently useful; it must rule out the byproduct/developmental-constraint alternatives rather than just not mentioning them; it must be clear about what the null hypothesis is (drift? constructional necessity?) and what evidence would shift it; it must specify the level at which selection is claimed to act; it has to cope with the distinction between a trait's origin and its current function so it doesn't fall over the first time something gets co-opted for a new use.

Then tell me which of these are genuinely non-negotiable -- i.e. any explanation that fails them simply isn't an adaptationist explanation at all -- versus which are really just the preferences of a particular camp (the strict adaptationists want X, the pluralists think X is asking too much, etc). And the bit I most care about: is there any subset of the desiderata that's jointly unsatisfiable or in serious tension, e.g. does demanding rigorous elimination of all byproduct hypotheses at some point make adaptationist explanation in principle unreachable for most traits, and if so is that a problem with the desiderata or with adaptationism? Also say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate explanations, because my guess is people will disagree there.
