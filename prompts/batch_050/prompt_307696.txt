I've landed on a pretty deflationary take about algorithmic fairness and I want to know what would count against it. The take: the standard impossibility results -- the ones showing that when base rates differ between groups and prediction is imperfect, you can't simultaneously have calibration within groups and equal false positive/false negative rates -- don't just create a practical headache for designers. They show there's no fact of the matter about whether an algorithm is fair, period. On my view "this system is unfair" is always shorthand for "it fails this particular metric," picking the metric is a values/politics choice all the way down, and there's nothing for theory to add beyond tallying who bears which costs. Anyone who says their model is fair full stop is confused or selling something.

I'm fairly convinced of this but I keep going back an forth on whether it's too easy. So: what would count against it? Some specific things I'm unsure about:

- Which parts of the position actually lean on the theorems as proved, and which lean on a vibe? Would it matter if the incompatibility turned out to be non-generic -- e.g. the conflict only bites in some special corner of cases, or approximate versions of the metrics are jointly achievable and the exact impossibility is doing all the argumentative work? My gut says "close enough" fairness is still fairness, so if approximate satisfaction is possible my conclusion looks way too strong.
- One thing that would genuinely move me: an argument that one of the desiderata in these theorems isn't really a fairness requirement at all -- say, that calibration is a fact about predictor quality rather than a demand of justice on decisions -- because then the theorem isn't even about fairness and my deflationism rests on a mislabeled result. Is that a live move or a dead one?
- The Arrow parallel worries me. Nobody sensible read Arrow's theorem as showing all voting rules are equally arbitrary and there's nothing more to say -- it reorganized the debate around which condition to relax and why. If the fairness results are like that, then "values all the way down" is a non sequitur. Are they like that? What would the analogue of structure even be here -- a principled ordering of the metrics, or a showing that in some decision contexts one metric is mandatory regardless of your values?
- What would a thoughtful defender of my own position concede as counting against it? I want that list, not just what critics would say.
- And the usual insulation worry: "it's just values" views have a way of absorbing everything -- show me a principled metric choice and I reply that the principle is itself a value choice. Does the position rule anything out? If not, is that a defect for a view at this level of abstraction, or just what being right feels like?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which one you'd actually push on if you were trying to talk me out of this.
