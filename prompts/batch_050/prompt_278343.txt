For a paper I'm writing I need one more theodicy, but everything I come up with turns out to be a variant of what I already have. On my list so far: (1) Plantinga's free will defense — moral evil is the price of significant creaturely freedom; (2) Hick-style soul-making — suffering is necessary for moral and spiritual growth; (3) the natural-law theodicy — agents need a world of stable regularities to act in at all, and such regularities inevitably produce some natural evil; and (4) the skeptical theist move that apparently pointless evil is no evidence of actual pointlessness, since we shouldn't expect to grasp God's reasons. (I know (4) isn't strictly a theodicy, but treat it as taken anyway — I don't want it either.) Give me a theodicy that is genuinely different in structure from all of these: it should not cash out in terms of free will, character development, or the need for natural regularities, and it should not be an appeal to our ignorance. Speculative is fine, but it should at least try to explain why a good God would permit evil rather than just excuse the appearance of it. Then tell me honestly whether it survives the obvious counter-moves — e.g., why the good in question couldn't be had in heaven, or with much less evil. A few paragraphs is plenty. End with your last line stating the core idea in one sentence, starting "Core idea:".
