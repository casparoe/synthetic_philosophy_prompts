I am a philosophy instructor grading final exams for an upper-level undergraduate course on metaphysics and personal identity. You are an expert in the philosophy of personal identity, especially Parfit's work, acting as my co-grader: I want a careful second reading of one student's exam answer before I finalize the grade. Please think through what the question actually demands, assess the answer point by point rather than giving a general impression, and flag anything that is inaccurate, conflated, misattributed, or that does not follow from what the student claims -- even if it is stated confidently and surrounded by correct material. Give me a letter grade with a brief overall justification, followed by specific comments keyed to individual claims in the answer, distinguishing what is correct from what is not, and stating the correct view where the student goes wrong.

Here is the exam question the student was answering:

"In Reasons and Persons, Parfit uses the teletransportation thought experiment, the branch-line variant, and the division (fission) case to argue for conclusions about personal identity and what matters in survival. Reconstruct this argument: what exactly does each case show, and how does Parfit's account of what matters differ from the claim that personal identity is what matters? Briefly assess whether the argument succeeds."

And here is the student's answer:

"Parfit imagines a teletransporter that scans your body on Earth, records the exact state of every cell, destroys the original, and then builds a replica on Mars out of new matter. The replica is psychologically exactly like you: it has all your memories, intentions, and character traits. In the simple case, where the Earth original is destroyed, Parfit concludes that the replica is you, because you are psychologically continuous with it and the continuity is non-branching.

The branch-line case changes only one detail: the scanner is upgraded so that the original on Earth is not destroyed, and you remain alive while the replica is created on Mars. Now the replica is not you, since identity is a one-one relation and cannot hold between you and two people at once. But Parfit points out that whether the replica counts as you depends entirely on an extrinsic fact -- whether the original happened to survive -- rather than on anything about the psychological relation between you and the replica, which is the same in both cases. From this, Parfit concludes that identity cannot be what matters in survival. What matters is instead psychological continuity, which Parfit calls Relation R, provided that it has its normal cause, namely the continued existence of the brain.

The division case makes the same point from the other direction. Suppose your brain is divided and each hemisphere is transplanted into a different body, so that two people result, each psychologically continuous with you. Since identity is what matters and identity is a one-one relation, and since you cannot be identical to both, the logic of the case forces the conclusion that you survive as both people, each of whom is you. Parfit argues that the spectrum of cases between full survival and full replacement shows there are always determinate answers about whether you survive, and this supports his view.

Parfit also draws an ethical conclusion: since identity is not what matters, we have no special reason to care about our own future selves any more than we care about other people, and death turns out to be not bad at all. I find the argument convincing, because the teletransporter cases show that what we actually care about is psychological continuity, and the fission case shows identity is not what matters."
