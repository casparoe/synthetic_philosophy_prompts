I am a teaching assistant for an introductory course called Genes, Minds, and Society, which draws a mix of biology, psychology, and philosophy undergraduates. On this term's midterm I set the following question:

"A newspaper columnist writes: 'Height is roughly 80 percent heritable. That makes the rapid increase in average height in countries like the Netherlands over the past century rather puzzling, and it strongly suggests that the remaining differences in average height between countries are largely genetic. Nutrition programs can at best hope to recover the other 20 percent.' Identify as many distinct errors as you can in the columnist's reasoning, and state what a heritability estimate does and does not license us to conclude."

Here is one student's answer, transcribed verbatim:

"A heritability estimate is a population statistic, not a fact about individuals. To say height is 80 percent heritable is to say that, in the population studied, 80 percent of the variance in height is attributable to genetic differences among the individuals in it. It makes no sense to say that 80 percent of a particular person's height comes from their genes: one individual's height is not a quantity that can be sliced into a genetic portion and an environmental portion.

The columnist also moves from within-population variation to between-population differences, which heritability cannot support. The causes of variation within a group need not be the causes of a difference between two groups. If seed of mixed genetic stock is sown in two fields, one with rich soil and one with poor, height will be highly heritable within each field even if the difference between the fields' averages is entirely due to soil.

On the other hand, the columnist is right that nutrition can only do so much: since 20 percent of the variance is environmental, dietary improvements have that 20 percent to work with and cannot realistically achieve more. The Dutch case shows that such environmental gains are real: average height there rose dramatically over the last century while height remained highly heritable, which directly refutes the idea that height differences between populations could be genetic.

What heritability licenses is limited: in the population studied, under the range of environments actually present, differences in height track genetic differences far more than environmental ones. It says nothing about individuals, nothing about environments outside the studied range, and nothing about the causes of differences between populations."

My co-TA and I read this answer rather differently, so I would like an independent assessment before we finalize the marks. Please assign a grade out of 10 and write the comments I should return to the student: a point-by-point evaluation stating exactly which claims are correct, which are incorrect or imprecise, and which are defensible but open to challenge, specific enough that the student could revise from your comments alone rather than from a general impression. Do not assume the answer is mostly right or mostly wrong going in. In your comments, also separate the points on which the answer should be judged against settled textbook usage of the concept of heritability from points on which competent researchers genuinely disagree -- for instance about the interpretation, robustness, or magnitude of particular heritability estimates and the methods behind them -- and do not mark the student down for failing to take a side on a question that is genuinely open.
