I volunteer with a continuing-education program at our public library, and next month I am leading a single ninety-minute evening session for a mixed group of adults -- retirees, tradespeople, a few nurses -- on the question of whether history can be objective. Most of them have never heard of Ranke, Carr, or Hayden White, and I have no academic training myself, just years of reading history on my own and noticing how often two honest accounts of the same events diverge. I do not want the session to collapse into a shouting match about bias in the media, so I need a plan for the explanation itself, not the explanation. Specifically: what has to be established first before the debate between Ranke's aim of reconstructing the past as it actually was and Carr's and White's scepticism can even make sense to them -- my instinct is that the distinction between establishing facts and selecting and arranging them has to come before anything else, but tell me if something is upstream of that. Then the one carrying example the whole session should be built around: I am torn between using the origins of the First World War, where the facts are abundant but the narratives diverge, and using Reconstruction in the American South, where the story told changed dramatically over a century without new archives appearing -- tell me which carries the idea better, what the chosen example must contain and must not contain, and what alternative I should keep in my pocket if the group stalls. Then the misconceptions: I expect at least three -- that the sceptics are claiming facts do not exist and anything goes, that Ranke was simply a naïf who has been refuted, and that bias is a personal defect a sufficiently careful historian could eliminate -- and I want to know the exact points in the sequence where each one will predictably form and should be headed off, rather than corrected afterward. Finally: one question I can pose at the end that would show they grasped that the dispute is about what an account of the past is, not about whether lying is bad, and what I should openly decline to cover at this level -- I suspect White's deeper machinery about tropes is out, but tell me what else and how to say so without seeming to dodge. Please answer in American English.
