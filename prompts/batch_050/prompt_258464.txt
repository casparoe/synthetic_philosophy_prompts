Here's a construction challenge on the nonidentity problem. Normally the problem shows up when a choice determines *which* child gets conceived (different timing, different gametes, different kid), so the kid who ends up existing can't say they were made worse off -- they owe their existence to the very act in question. I want a case that gets the same result without touching conception at all.

Construct a thought experiment where a decision is made on behalf of a child who already exists -- say a medical decision, made in infancy or early childhood -- such that:

- On an origins-based view of personal identity (you are whoever developed from this particular sperm-and-egg, full stop), the case is boring: the same child exists in every outcome, so you can just compare how well off they'd be under each option and the child's interests settle it.
- On a sufficiently demanding psychological-continuity view, though, the decision transforms the child's psychology so radically that the person who wakes up afterwards arguably isn't identical to the person who went in -- and now the nonidentity problem comes roaring back, because the "new" person owes their existence to the treatment and can't complain about it, even if we think something was lost.

Constraints: realistic present-day setting (a hospital or clinic, no mind-uploading, no teleporters), one paragraph for the scenario itself, and the decision has to be genuinely hard for the parents -- not a case where one option is obviously best on medical grounds, because then the identity wrinkle is doing no work.

After the scenario: walk through the verdict on each view of identity and explain exactly which feature of the case flips it from "ordinary welfare comparison" into "nonidentity problem." Then tell me what happens if you soften that feature (e.g., make the psychological change big but not total). Finally, how would someone who holds the No-Difference view -- who thinks it doesn't matter morally whether the same people or different people exist -- respond? Do they dissolve my case, or just refuse to care about the distinction it's built on?
