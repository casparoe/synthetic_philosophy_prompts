I'm putting together the final exam for my upper-level seminar on rational choice and value theory. I need one essay question on Quinn's self-torturer puzzle that students can't answer just by retelling the setup and muttering 'intransitive preferences, money pump, done'. Here's the twist I want to force them to deal with: the standard version leans heavily on the pain difference between adjacent settings being imperceptible, so a lot of students think the whole puzzle dissolves once you say 'well, you'd notice eventually'. On my exam I've added a variant (I'm calling it the Honest Torturer) where nothing is hidden: each notch up gets you another $1,000 and a small but clearly felt increase in pain, and by the agent's own lights every single trade is worth it -- she'd accept any one of them in isolation, eyes open, every time. Setting 1000 is still agony she'd pay a fortune to avoid. So if there's still a paradox here, it can't be blamed on vagueness, unnoticed creep, or failures of discrimination.

I want the question to push them on wether the paradox survives this variant, and if so what it actually shows: is it a problem about the seperability of reasons (does 'each step is an improvement' entail 'the endpoint is better'), a problem about evaluating individual acts versus evaluating whole plans or policies of action, or something about what rationality can demand of someone who knows in advance where the sequence ends? The best answers should probably also say something about what the honest variant changes relative to Quinn's original -- is imperceptibility doing real work in the original or is it a distraction?

Write it up as: the question exactly as it would appear on the paper (students get 75 minutes), a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit moves, with one line for each on how weaker candidates typically fumble it.
