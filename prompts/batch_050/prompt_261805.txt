A few months ago I served on a criminal jury, and the experience left me with a puzzle I keep turning over. There were twelve of us, and we were told our verdict had to be unanimous — if even one person held out, there would be no verdict at all and the whole trial might have to be redone. We did deliberate for three days, and I watched two jurors who were initially alone in their doubts gradually bring the rest of us around on one point, which made me grateful for the rule. But afterwards I started wondering what the rule is actually for, because the obvious justification — that twelve people together are less likely to convict an innocent person than one judge alone — doesn't seem to require unanimity at all. If the point of having twelve people is that individual mistakes cancel out in the aggregate, then a 10–2 or even 7–5 majority should capture most of that benefit, and insisting on unanimity would just hand veto power to the one unreasonable person in the room.

What makes this sharper for me is that other institutions that face the same basic problem — getting a group to reach a correct yes/no answer — are designed completely differently. Elections and legislatures use simple majorities of large numbers of people; appellate courts sit in panels of three and go with two out of three; my condo board decides things by majority of five. If large groups voting by majority are trustworthy, why entrust the most consequential decision of all, someone's liberty, to a small group that must be unanimous? And if unanimity among twelve is the gold standard for reliability, why don't we require it anywhere else? The two designs seem to presuppose opposite things: majority rule seems to assume that each voter is somewhat more likely than not to be right and that the numbers do the work, while unanimity seems to assume something else entirely — perhaps that a single dissenter's doubt is itself strong evidence the majority has missed something, or that the point isn't accuracy at all but forcing genuine deliberation, or generating public confidence in the outcome.

So here is my question: what does each of these institutional designs actually presuppose about the people composing them — about how reliable individual judgment is, about whether one person's error is independent of another's, about what happens to those assumptions when people deliberate together rather than voting privately? Can both designs rest on a single coherent view of group decision-making, or does one of them quietly assume something false? I'd also like to know what would have to change about jurors or voters for each design to stop making sense — for instance, if jurors' errors turned out to be highly correlated because they all watch the same news and share the same biases, does the case for twelve-person unanimity get stronger or weaker, and does the case for majority rule collapse entirely? And finally: which parts of the jury system are actually underdetermined by any of this — that is, features like the number twelve or the hung-jury rule that several rival theories could equally well license, where I shouldn't read too much philosophy into what may just be historical accident?
