I am a professor of philosophy who chairs the research ethics board of a large social science faculty. A growing share of the studies we review rest their central claims on generational categories — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z — and at present we have no consistent way of deciding whether a given use of such a category is methodologically defensible or simply a repackaged stereotype. I would like you to design a review procedure my board can actually run, grounded in what philosophy of science and demography genuinely know about this, rather than in whatever the committee's loudest member happens to believe.

The procedure should take the form of a short sequence of questions a reviewer works through in order, with a note on what counts as a satisfactory answer at each step and where in the sequence the procedure stops with a verdict. It will need to engage, at minimum, with the following issues, translated into terms a reviewer without philosophical training can apply: whether a generation is being treated as a social kind with causal structure of its own or merely as a label over an arbitrary band of birth years; the demographers' age–period–cohort identification problem, i.e. that any observed difference between birth-year bands is logically consistent with age effects, period effects, cohort effects, or any mixture of them, and what a study would have to do to make a cohort reading even eligible; the looping-effects worry that generational labels, once publicised, feed back into the self-conception of the people labelled, so that a category can become partially real through its own circulation; and the arbitrariness of boundary years, including whether a study's results survive redrawing the cut-offs a few years in either direction.

Please mark explicitly the points at which the procedure hands the question back to a human reviewer because the matter is one of judgement or values rather than something a checklist can settle, and say plainly which simplifications the procedure makes and what they cost. Then apply the procedure to three worked cases: first, a correlational study claiming that Millennials are less trusting of institutions than Boomers, based on a single cross-sectional survey; second, a longitudinal study of workplace behaviour that tracks the same individuals across twenty years and finds divergence between cohorts; third, a qualitative study arguing that Gen Z constitutes a generation in something like Mannheim's sense, organised around a shared formative experience. At least one of these should come out in a way a typical researcher will dislike, and you should not soften the procedure's verdict to avoid that.

Throughout, please distinguish carefully between what is genuinely settled among philosophers of science and demographers working on cohorts and what remains contested, and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus. Where the literature contains live disagreements — for instance, over whether looping effects strengthen or undermine a kind's scientific standing — present the disagreement as such rather than quietly choosing a side.
