I teach an upper-level undergrad course in normative ethics and I'm writing this year's final exam. I want one essay question on the doing/allowing distinction -- killing vs letting die -- worth 40 marks and answerable in about 50 minutes, and I want it to be impossible to answer by reciting Rachels. Every year I get the same rehearsed parade: Smith and Jones in the bathtub, "no moral difference per se," Foot on negative and positive rights, done. Students clearly memorize the moves and then pattern-match, and I can't tell who actually understands anything.

So here's the scenario I want the question built around. A colleague who does ICU ethics described a policy problem to me and I fictionalized it into a pair of cases. A hospital has one ventilator. In Case 1, Dr. A connects it to patient Rao, who is stable but has a poor prognosis; a week later patient Okafor arrives with a much better prognosis, and following the hospital's triage protocol Dr. A disconnects Rao (who dies) and connects Okafor. In Case 2, both patients arrive at the same time and Dr. B assigns the ventilator to Okafor from the start, so Rao is never connected. Every clinician I've asked says Case 2 is defensible triage and Case 1 is monstrous, but when pressed they can only mumble that withdrawal "feels different" from withholding. I want students to earn that intuition or destroy it.

The question should force them to: (i) work out what the standard views predict about the pair -- Rachels' bare-difference method, and the rights-based version of the distinction from Foot and Thomson -- without the question naming those authors, since recognizing which argument applies is itself part of the test; (ii) confront that the only apparent difference between the cases is a temporal baseline, i.e. what Rao already had, and ask whether a bare baseline like that can carry moral weight; and (iii) take an actual stand on whether the withdrawal/withholding asymmetry in medical ethics is defensible or just status quo bias in a lab coat.

Deliverables: the question exactly as it would appear on the exam paper (with the parts and mark allocations), a model answer of 600-700 words, and a marking scheme. The marking scheme has to name the specific moves that separate an A from a B -- for instance, an A-level answer should notice that Rachels' bare-difference strategy is hard to run cleanly here because the two cases aren't otherwise equal, or that the baseline move threatens to reclassify half of all omissions as doings -- and the specific mistakes that cost marks, like treating "active vs passive" as a physical description rather than a normative claim, assuming the triage protocol settles the moral question, or confusing doing/allowing with the doctrine of double effect.

At the very end, give a last line beginning "Verdict:" that states, in one sentence, the stand the model answer actually takes on whether Dr. A's withdrawal is morally worse than Dr. B's withholding.
