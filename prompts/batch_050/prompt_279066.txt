I'm trying to figure out whether I actually believe in genuine moral dilemmas — situations where you do something wrong no matter what you pick — so let me just report my gut verdicts on some cases and you tell me what they add up to.

- I skip my best friend's wedding to drive a bleeding stranger to the ER: clearly right, zero guilt, I owe my friend an explanation but not really an apology.
- Soldiers at the door ask if anyone is hiding in my cellar and someone is, so I say no: clearly right, I'd do it again, but the lying itself still sits badly with me somehow.
- Two hikers pinned by a rockslide, rising water, I can only free one before the flood, I free the younger one: I'd be wrecked about it for years and visit the other guy's grave, but I don't think I did anything wrong.
- A judge convicts an innocent man because a mob will otherwise lynch three people: this one feels wrong whichever way she goes — convict and she's done an injustice, refuse and she's let three die.
- I promised my dying mother I'd keep the family farm; years later selling it is the only way to pay for my kid's surgery: I'd sell, no question, but I'd feel I owed *her* something even though she's dead.

Is there a principle that sorts these — in particular, why does the rockslide come out "not wrong, just horrible" while the judge comes out "wrong either way"? They look structurally alike (forced choice, someone dies either way, lifelong regret), so if your best principle can't capture both, tell me which verdict should go. And while you're at it: is my bad feeling in the lying case just emotional residue, or is it tracking something real? Say what your answer implies about whether I owe anyone an apology there, given the soldiers are the only ones I lied to.
