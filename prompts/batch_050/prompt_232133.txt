A few weeks ago I watched a family argument that I haven't been able to stop thinking about, and it's led me to a concept I'm fairly sure philosophers have a name for, though I don't know what it is. My uncle spent twenty minutes at dinner sternly lecturing my cousin about her habit of lying to get out of commitments, and everyone at the table was visibly uncomfortable — not because anyone thought the criticism was false (it wasn't; she really does this), but because my uncle has done exactly the same thing his whole life, sometimes to the very people in that room. Nobody could quite articulate the objection. One person muttered "look who's talking," which got dismissed as childish, and yet the discomfort didn't go away. I've since noticed the same shape at work: a manager who writes up an employee for chronic lateness while himself arriving late most mornings, and a colleague who publicly denounced a coworker for taking credit for others' ideas — a few months after quietly letting a junior analyst's contribution go uncredited in a report they co-wrote.

What interests me is that in each case the complaint seems to split into two parts that our everyday reactions keep conflating. The judgment itself may be entirely accurate — the person really did the thing, and it really was wrong. And yet something about *this particular person* delivering it seems defective, as if they've spent a currency they don't hold. I've also noticed the phenomenon has edges I'm unsure about, and I'd like the concept's boundaries drawn: does it apply when the accuser committed the same wrong years ago but has since stopped and quietly regrets it; when they never did it themselves but knowingly benefited from others doing it; when they condemn the behavior in general but conspicuously exempt their friends; or when they themselves openly admitted their own identical failing before accusing the other person? I'm also curious how this relates to the familiar conversational dismissal — the one where pointing out the accuser's own identical conduct is waved away as a mere debating trick that changes the subject rather than addressing the argument — because it seems like sometimes that dismissal is right and sometimes it badly misses what the objection is actually about.

Please give me your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: the established name or names nearest to this phenomenon and which fits best; what precisely is defective when the judgment itself is true — the claim, the claimant, or something else; which of the edge cases above fall inside the concept and which outside, and why; how it differs from the mere rhetorical move of deflecting criticism by counter-accusation; and what, if anything, repairs the defect (confession, reform, distance in time, or nothing).
