My sister and I have been disagreeing about a family decision partly on the grounds of how much a risk of serious suffering should weigh against expected happiness, and she sent me a chapter from a popular book on suffering-focused ethics to settle it. The chapter glosses the lexical-threshold variant of negative utilitarianism as follows: 'Negative utilitarians hold that only the reduction of suffering matters and that pleasure has no moral value in itself. The lexical-threshold version softens this by allowing that pleasure can compensate for pain, but only for pains below a certain threshold of intensity; suffering above that threshold cannot be outweighed by any amount of pleasure. Since ordinary pains fall below the threshold, the view coincides with common sense in everyday life. Its commitment that pleasure and pain are incommensurable above the threshold means it would endorse painlessly ending all sentient life if that prevented one instance of threshold-level agony, which shows the position is untenable.' I have the sense this mixes up several distinct claims, but I can't say precisely where. Please respond with exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: identify what the chapter gets right about the view; where it conflates the lexical-threshold variant with stronger forms of negative utilitarianism; whether the incommensurability claim is accurately stated (does the view deny that pleasure and pain can be compared at all, or something narrower?); whether the everyday-common-sense claim holds up; and whether the 'ending all sentient life' argument refutes the view or merely restates a consequence its defenders accept.
