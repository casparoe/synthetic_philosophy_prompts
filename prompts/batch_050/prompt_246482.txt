I am a PhD student in philosophy of language, and my dissertation defends a strong version of Davidson's programme of radical interpretation. The claim is twofold: first, that the facts about what a speaker means are exhausted by the interpretations an idealized radical interpreter could converge on, working only from evidence about which sentences speakers hold true under which observable circumstances; second, that the principle of charity is not a heuristic shortcut but a constitutive constraint on interpretation, so that a pattern of utterances for which no charitable interpretation exists simply does not constitute a language at all. I have written the positive chapters, and my supervisor has now told me I cannot submit until I can state precisely what would count against the view. I am embarrassed to admit I cannot. When I try, I keep producing objections that my Davidsonian self absorbs effortlessly, and I have started to worry that this absorptive capacity is itself the objection.

Here is what I have considered so far, so you do not simply repeat it back to me. I know about Quinean indeterminacy of reference and the standard response that Davidson welcomes indeterminacy at the level of reference so long as sentence-level meaning is fixed. I know about the tension between charity and the possibility of massive error, and about Davidson's own treatment of malapropisms, which seems to loosen the link between convention and meaning in a way that either enriches the view or hollows it out. I know that non-assertoric utterances (imperatives, questions) and apparently non-truth-conditional phenomena (slurs, expressives, discourse particles) sit awkwardly with a truth-conditional base. What I want you to add to this familiar list, and to organize the whole thing, is a genuine map of the position's exposure. Which of these considerations would actually count against radical interpretation as a theory of what meaning is, as opposed to merely complicating its application? What would a committed Davidsonian themselves concede as a defeater, and what would they dismiss as changing the subject? To sharpen things, consider a case I constructed for a seminar: a community, call them the Vels, whose every utterance is accompanied by a context-setting gesture, such that two incompatible interpretations of their language, each rendering the speakers overwhelmingly rational and mostly truthful, fit the totality of the evidence equally well, and differ in what they attribute to the Vels as beliefs about central matters. Does this case refute constitutive charity, extend it, or merely restate the indeterminacy point with extra steps?

Please also address the insulation question directly. Does the position make any prediction that rivals (Gricean intention-based semantics, teleosemantics, inferentialism) do not also make, such that an observation or argument could separate them? Or has the view been so thoroughly qualified, through the distinction between prior and passing theories, the broadening of the evidential base, and the constitutive rather than methodological reading of charity, that it no longer risks anything at all? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say what you would find most persuasive if you were my examiner: mine is a soft spot for cases where interpretation appears to succeed precisely by attributing irrationality, so if there is a principled reason those cases cannot be made to stick, I especially want to hear it.
