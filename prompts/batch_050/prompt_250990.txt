I have no formal philosophy background, but I keep circling a few sentences from chapter 4 of Aristotle's On Interpretation (in E. M. Edghill's translation):

"Every sentence has meaning, not as being the natural means by which a physical faculty is realized, but, as we have said, by convention. Yet every sentence is not a proposition; only such are propositions as have in them either truth or falsity. Thus a prayer is a sentence, but is neither true nor false."

It struck me that a sentence like "There will be a sea battle in the straits tomorrow" seems, if the future is genuinely open, to be in the same boat as Aristotle's prayer: perfectly meaningful, grammatically fine, but not yet true and not yet false. And yet it feels unlike a prayer, because by tomorrow evening it will definitely have settled into one or the other, and because we can already argue about it rationally in a way we can't argue about a prayer. I want to figure out where I actually stand on this. Please build me a decision tree that starts from this passage and leads to a named position on the truth-values of statements about the open future. The first node should ask directly whether the prayer comparison is apt; every later node should be a concrete question I can answer without knowing any terminology, framed around specific cases (a coin I have not flipped, a prediction made yesterday that came true today, the disjunction "either there will be a battle or there won't" asserted this morning). The leaves should include at least: the view that the sentence is already straightforwardly true or false, the view that it is neither, a three-valued option, a supervaluationist option, a branching-time option, and the view that its truth is relative to the time at which it is assessed; add further leaves if the logical space calls for them. Note any combination of answers that nobody seems to occupy and briefly say why, and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate.
