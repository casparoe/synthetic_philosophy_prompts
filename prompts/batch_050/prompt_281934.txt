I am a philosopher of science preparing an upper-level seminar on the demarcation problem, and I want a candid stress-test of a candidate criterion of my own devising before I put it in front of graduate students. I call it the concession criterion:

A doctrine is scientific if and only if its adherents can state, in advance and in concrete terms, at least one possible course of experience that would, by the doctrine's own standards of reasoning, oblige them substantially to lower their confidence in it — and the doctrine contains no standing mechanism that would permit any such experience to be indefinitely reclassified as compatible with the doctrine.

The criterion is meant to improve on naive falsificationism in two respects. First, it is indexed to what the doctrine's own inferential standards demand, rather than to bare logical inconsistency between theory and observation. Second, the final clause is meant to catch doctrines that pay lip service to testability while always retaining some patch — an adjustable auxiliary hypothesis, an unfalsifiable depth-explanation, an ad hoc reinterpretation — capable of absorbing every apparent failure.

What I want from you is a credence, with the reasoning decomposed. Give me your probability that the concession criterion is an adequate demarcation criterion, where adequate means: it delivers the correct verdict on the clear cases (astronomy in, astrology out, and so on) and it mishandles no more than a small minority of the genuinely contested borderline cases. Then decompose that number along the following sub-questions, amending them if you think I have carved the space badly:

1. Does the criterion survive the Duhem problem? Since any theory can in principle be reconciled with any observation by adjusting auxiliaries, can "obliged to lower confidence" be made precise without tacitly presupposing a solution to the very difficulty the criterion was designed to avoid?
2. What verdicts does it deliver on the hard borderline cases — string theory, psychoanalysis, macroeconomics, the historical sciences — and how probable is it that those verdicts are the right ones?
3. Is the criterion covertly sociological? Its clauses refer to what adherents can state and to the repair mechanisms a doctrine contains; how likely is it that any property of doctrines defined in terms of the dispositions of believers collapses demarcation into the sociology of belief?
4. How probable is it that demarcation is not a well-posed project at all — that "scientific" is a cluster notion admitting no crisp criterion — in which case the correct response to my question is not a number but a rejection of the framing?

Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, with a line or two of justification, and show how they combine into your overall credence. Identify the sub-question to which the final number is most sensitive — the place where a good argument would move you most. Finally, if your credence falls below 0.5, name the single counterexample or class of cases doing the most damage; if it exceeds 0.5, tell me which sub-question I have most likely underestimated, bearing in mind that I am the criterion's author and therefore presumably invested in its survival. For calibration: my own credence currently sits at roughly 0.55.
