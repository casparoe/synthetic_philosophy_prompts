I'm drafting the final paper for my undergraduate metaethics seminar, and I've hit a snag I'd like help diagnosing. My thesis concerns error theory -- the view, associated with J. L. Mackie, that moral judgments are genuine truth-apt assertions but that all positive moral claims are false, since nothing in the world answers to the objective, categorically prescriptive values they purport to describe. My paper argues that error theory is not merely false but self-defeating in a way its proponents have missed. The argument runs as follows: if error theory is true, then anyone who understands it and nonetheless continues to make moral assertions is knowingly asserting falsehoods; knowingly asserting falsehoods is lying; lying is wrong; therefore error theory entails that its own informed adherents act wrongly whenever they engage in moral discourse, and so the view issues in a normative verdict that, by its own lights, cannot be true. In short, I claim the error theorist is committed to saying that moral talk is immoral, which collapses the position into incoherence.

My professor returned the draft with a single comment in the margin: the argument equivocates somewhere, and the incoherence I detect is an artifact of my own formulation rather than a feature of the view. She declined to say more before the revision deadline. I've tried to find the slip myself. One candidate I've considered is that 'lying is wrong' is itself a moral claim and therefore, on the error theorist's view, false -- but I'm not sure that rescues the position, since it seems to leave the error theorist unable to condemn anything at all, including dishonesty, which my instinct says is its own kind of problem. Another thought is that there might be a difference between a claim's being false and its being improper to assert, but I can't get this intuition into sharp enough focus to write it up rigorously.

Could you reconstruct where the equivocation actually occurs, and explain why it vitiates the argument? Ideally I'd like more than a verdict: I want to understand which premise fails, what the strongest repair of my argument would look like, and why even the repaired version presumably misses what the error theorist is actually committed to. If the error theorist really can consistently deplore dishonesty, cruelty, and the rest in some register without contradicting the view, I'd like that register described precisely, since it bears on whether my fallback thesis -- that error theory is extensionally inadequate to ordinary moral criticism even if coherent -- fares any better than the original.
