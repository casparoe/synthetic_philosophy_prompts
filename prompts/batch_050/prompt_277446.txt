I sit on my city's business-license appeals panel, and we've got a case coming up that's turned into a real headache. A members-only club -- adults only, signed consent forms, no physical injuries on record, nothing the police care about -- stages elaborate humiliation rituals for paying members to watch in person. The new wrinkle: it also livestreams the sessions, faces blurred, to subscribers around the world. Nobody disputes that all of this is legal under criminal law. But a neighborhood group found out about the streams, got alot of residents worked up, and the city council denied the club's license renewal on the ground that a business like this corrodes the moral fabric of the community and affronts ordinary people's sense of decency. The club appealed, and now it's on us.

The panel is split. Half of them basically talk like Devlin: a society is glued together by shared moral commitments, it's entitled to defend those, and the deep disgust of reasonable ordinary people is itself evidence that something threatens the common life -- and anyway this stopped being "private" conduct the moment it went online. The other half talk like Hart: nobody has shown harm to anyone beyond the consenting participants, bare moral revulsion can't justify state coercion, and if "the community will disintegrate" is doing the argumentative work you need actual evidence of disintegration, not just a packed public comment session and angry emails.

Please draft the opinion I'll circulate before the hearing: recommend a concrete disposition (renew, deny, or renew with conditions) with the reasoning behind it, and give a genuinely serious treatment of the strongest argument on the side I end up rejecting -- don't hand me a straw Devlin, the man's position deserves better. One thing I want addressed head-on: does the livestream actually change the Hart/Devlin calculus, or is it a red herring? A couple of us suspect the online element does work neither of them anticipated, since their whole debate presupposed a fairly clean line between private vice and public morality, but I'm honestly not sure and definately want your take.

Format it like this, roughly:

- Bottom line (3-4 sentences max)
- The facts I'm relying on (short; bullets fine)
- The strongest case for denying the license (one solid paragraph)
- The strongest case for renewing (one or two paragraphs -- put the real work here, or swap the two sections if I recommend denying)
- Why the livestream does or doesn't matter (one paragraph)
- Recommended conditions, if any (bullets)

Keep the whole thing under 900 words; it has to be readable in one sitting.
