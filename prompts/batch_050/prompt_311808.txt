I'm part of a small reading group on AI alignment, and at our last meeting we got stuck on an analogy one member kept leaning on. He'd recently argued, in a memo he circulated, that overseeing an AI system's training is fundamentally like raising a child: you don't judge a child solely by how her choices turn out, because even a child who reliably produces good outcomes might be getting there through luck, manipulation, or values that will fail her later; instead you attend to the process -- her reasons, her habits, the influences shaping her -- and you intervene on that. From this he drew a fairly strong conclusion: that process-based supervision of AI systems (rewarding legible, sound reasoning and penalizing bad reasoning, even when it happens to work) is strictly more principled than outcome-based supervision (rewarding results, however they're produced), and that anyone who defends outcome-based approaches is making the same mistake as a parent who only checks whether the homework got done.

Several of us felt the analogy was doing real work here but couldn't say exactly where it stopped holding. Some mismatches seemed damning: a child persists through time and carries her upbringing into novel situations for decades, whereas a trained model's "upbringing" is more like a set of frozen dispositions; a parent can't read a child's neurons but has rich causal access to a child's environment, while with a model it's arguably reversed -- we can in principle inspect internals and the full training distribution, yet the "reasoning" we're supervising may just be another output optimized to look good. Others felt the analogy's core survived anyway, since both cases involve a supervisor who can't fully verify competence and must decide what signals to trust.

Can you audit the analogy properly? Give an explicit mapping (a table of correspondences is fine -- parent, child, upbringing, homework, adult life, and so on, onto overseer, model, training process, outputs, deployment), then identify which features of child-raising the analogy quietly imports that AI training lacks, and which disanalogies actually matter for my colleague's specific conclusion versus which are mere cosmetic differences. Does his "process over outcome, full stop" verdict survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does the audit instead support a mixed or conditional position? Finally, if there's a better everyday analogy for the process-versus-outcome supervision choice -- hiring, auditing, coaching, certifying a pilot, whatever fits -- say what it is and what it gets right that child-raising doesn't.
