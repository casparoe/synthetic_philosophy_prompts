For my graduate seminar on modal metaphysics I owe a student a better answer than the one I gave in class. She asked: given that the deductive core of Gödel's modal ontological argument has been checked to the point of machine verification, is there anything left to dispute besides the premise that a Godlike being is possible? Two of my colleagues assign sharply different overall credences to the soundness of the argument — one below 0.05, the other above 0.6 — and I want to locate their disagreement precisely rather than gesture at it.

Take the proposition to be evaluated as follows: in Gödel's argument as transmitted in Dana Scott's notes, the axioms governing positive properties are true, the definitions are not question-begging, the modal logic employed is the correct logic of metaphysical modality, and the conclusion suffices for the existence of God rather than merely of something Godlike. Give me your own credence in that conjunction, stated as a number. But what I principally want is the decomposition. Break the question into the sub-claims on which it actually depends — at minimum: that the derivation is valid in the relevant logic; that the strong modal principles the argument requires (something in the neighborhood of S5) correctly govern metaphysical modality; that the primitive notion of a positive property is coherent rather than a merely formal placeholder; that each of the positivity axioms is true; that the possibility premise is true; and that a necessarily existent being satisfying the definition of Godlikeness would be God rather than an unintended artifact of the axiomatization. Assign each sub-claim a rough probability, show how these combine into your final number, and identify the single sub-claim to which the final number is most sensitive. On the basis of your decomposition, say where my two colleagues' disagreement most plausibly lives.

Two further instructions. First, treat the modal-collapse objection — the charge that the axioms, once granted, leave no room for contingent truth — as one of the sub-claims to be priced, and say whether you take it to bear on the truth of the axioms, on the adequacy of the logic, or on the inference from Godlikeness to Godhood; my colleagues disagree precisely about where it bites, and I would like a verdict with reasons. Second, I want a committed answer: actual numbers with their justification, including for the premises you regard as nearly certain, and a statement of what specific result or argument would move your final credence the most. Do not conclude with a menu of positions for me to choose among.
