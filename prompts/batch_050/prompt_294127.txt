A friend of mine has firm views about when apparent consent is genuine, and I want to figure out what principle she is running. Her verdicts so far:

- A researcher tells participants he is taking a routine blood sample and quietly draws extra vials for an unrelated study: consent invalid.
- A man on a first date lies about his age and invents an impressive hobby; the evening ends in bed: consent valid, though the lying is contemptible.
- A landlord tells a tenant he will report her to the immigration authorities unless she sleeps with him: consent invalid.
- A wealthy stranger offers a destitute woman enough money to pay for her child's surgery in exchange for sex, and she accepts: consent valid — exploitative, perhaps, but genuinely consented to.

Now the case I want to put to her. A man conceals from a prospective partner that he is married. He knows she has a firm, frequently stated policy of never sleeping with married men, and she says afterward, credibly, that she would never have consented had she known. He told no lies about the encounter itself; he simply omitted the one fact he knew would settle the matter for her.

Predict her verdict, and justify the prediction. As part of doing so: lay out at least two general principles that each fit all four of her existing verdicts, show that they diverge on this case, and tell me which further case — real or invented — would most cleanly tell the two principles apart. If you think the four verdicts actually underdetermine the new case to the point where no prediction is better than a coin flip, or that one of her verdicts doesn't cohere with the others in the first place, say that plainly instead of forcing an answer.
