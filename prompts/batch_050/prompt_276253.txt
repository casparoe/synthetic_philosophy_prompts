I work in rational choice theory and welfare economics, and I'm comfortable with the standard machinery: utility functions and their representations, completeness and transitivity axioms, interpersonal comparisons, social welfare functionals, preference aggregation, Pareto conditions, and the impossibility results that arise when you try to combine orderings under various constraints. I am not fluent in the nineteenth-century intuitionist vocabulary in which Henry Sidgwick poses what he called the dualism of practical reason: his view that two principles each present themselves as self-evidently rational -- that one ought to pursue one's own good on the whole, and that one ought to pursue the good of all impartially -- and that practical reason delivers conflicting verdicts with no further principle to adjudicate between them, absent some assumption that duty and interest in fact coincide.

Restate this dualism as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use. I want the translation to be systematic rather than impressionistic: say exactly what formal object each side of Sidgwick's conflict becomes (for instance, whether rational egoism and impartial benevolence are best rendered as two utility functions over the same outcome set, as two orderings the agent is required to satisfy, or as an agent-level ordering versus a social welfare functional), and reconstruct his claim that the conflict is irreducible -- i.e., that there is no natural way to combine the two rankings into a single ordering the agent can act on, so that any resolution (lexicographic priority, a weighted sum, a rate of time-and-person discounting) amounts to abandoning one of the principles rather than reconciling them. Where he appeals to the coincidence of duty and interest secured by a divine ordering of the world, say what formal assumption would have to hold for the two orderings to coincide in practice, and why he thought that assumption was an empirical posit rather than something reason could deliver.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually care about. Which of Sidgwick's claims translate cleanly, which survive only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my framework at all? Three candidates I'm particularly unsure about: first, his insistence that the dualism is a contradiction within practical reason rather than a mere clash of preferences -- does my framework have the resources to distinguish those, or does every conflict come out as incomplete preferences? Second, his appeal to the point of view of the universe as a standpoint from which no one's good matters more than anyone else's -- is that a claim about aggregation, about the irrelevance of agent-relative weightings, or something my vocabulary simply cannot state? Third, his intuitionist epistemology, the claim that both principles are self-evident rather than derived -- is there any way to carry that into a framework where axioms are just posits, and does anything important depend on carrying it?

Finally, give me your verdict on the residue: for each item that fails to translate, is the failure a defect of my framework -- something decision theory is missing and should want -- or evidence that Sidgwick's formulation was doing less work than it appeared to be? I'd like you to take a position rather than split the difference, and to flag the single point where you think the translation is most likely to smuggle in my framework's assumptions while presenting itself as neutral restatement.
