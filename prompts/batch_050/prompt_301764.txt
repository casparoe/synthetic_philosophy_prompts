I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm preparing a long feature about an epistemological dispute I stumbled into while reporting a piece on misinformation. The question that interests me is why almost everything you and I know — that water is H2O, that my own birthday falls when it does, that the reporter's source was really in the room — rests on what other people have told us, and what makes that reasonable. The philosophy of testimony literature has, as far as I can tell, been stuck for roughly three decades between two positions. Reductionists, in a tradition they trace to Hume, hold that believing someone is justified only insofar as the hearer possesses independent, non-testimonial grounds for taking the speaker to be reliable — evidence about this speaker's track record, or inductive evidence about human sincerity and competence generally. Anti-reductionists, claiming Reid as their ancestor, hold that testimony is a basic source of justification in its own right: absent specific reasons for doubt, a hearer is entitled to accept what she's told, without needing to have assembled any positive case for the speaker's reliability.

Here's what strikes me as strange, and what I want your diagnosis of. Both sides seem to deliver nearly identical verdicts about nearly every actual case. The anti-reductionist's no-defeaters condition quietly builds in the sensitivity to signs of unreliability that the reductionist demands more explicitly; the sophisticated reductionist, meanwhile, allows that the required background evidence can be tacit, defeasible, and acquired without anything like deliberate vetting. Meanwhile both sides wield the same exhibits: the reductionist points to gullibility, to children and our helpless epistemic dependence in early life; the anti-reductionist replies that small children couldn't possibly possess the reductionist's inductive base and yet plainly acquire knowledge by being told, and asks where the reductionist's own inductive premise — that testimony is generally reliable — could come from without circularity. Round and round it goes, with impressive technical refinement and, as far as an outsider can tell, no movement.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis from a non-philosopher: the dispute survives because it's not really about which beliefs are justified but about bookkeeping — about where in the account the justificatory work is said to happen — and underneath that, about something closer to temperament: whether the default epistemic posture of a social animal should be modeled as trust with monitoring, or as self-reliance with reluctant extensions of credit. In other words, I suspect the two camps are offering rival pictures of what a knower is, dressed up as rival analyses of justification, and that this is why no case or counterexample has ever settled it.

So my questions: What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a substantive dispute about the structure of epistemic justification, a largely verbal dispute about where to place the default, a covert disagreement about which cases count as the data (ordinary adult testimony versus children's versus chain-of-sources cases like the ones journalists traffic in), or an irresolvable clash of starting intuitions about epistemic autonomy? If your diagnosis differs from mine, I want that argued, with attention to what each side actually says rather than to textbook caricatures. And given the diagnosis: what would progress even look like here — a formal result, a distinction nobody has drawn yet, findings from developmental psychology about how children calibrate trust, or a reframing that dissolves the question? Or is the honest answer that nothing could settle it, and if so, what does that imply about the questions a feature like mine should be asking instead?
