In a seminar on moral sociology we recently discussed the well-known trichotomy of moral cultures -- honour cultures, in which reputation must be defended personally and insult demands a riposte; dignity cultures, in which each person is presumed to possess an inherent worth that does not rise or fall with others' opinions, and serious grievances are routed to third parties; and victimhood cultures, in which appeals to injured status are made publicly and to authorities, so that sympathy becomes a contested resource. What struck me is that the standard debate about these cultures asks which one is best, full stop, as if a society could simply select one off the shelf. I want an essay that takes a different cut at the problem.

Write an essay on whether any of these three cultures is morally superior, but organise it around the following hypothesis: each culture has a characteristic pathology -- feuding and cycles of vengeance for honour culture, litigiousness and the outsourcing of conflict to impersonal institutions for dignity culture, competitive claims of suffering and the incentivisation of fragility for victimhood culture -- and the right question is not which culture is best in the abstract but which culture's pathology is most tractable, that is, most capable of being contained or corrected from within the culture's own resources. Consider, for instance, whether honour cultures carry internal mechanisms for ending feuds (mediation, compensation, hospitality codes), whether dignity cultures can restrain their own tendency toward bureaucratic overreach, and whether victimhood cultures have any native check on the inflation of grievance -- or whether that check can only come from outside.

Weigh the serious considerations on each side, including the possibility that the question is ill-posed because the three cultures are adaptations to different material and institutional conditions (weak versus strong third-party enforcement, for example) rather than freely choosable options. But do not end on a non-committal note: defend a verdict. A conditional verdict -- of the form that one culture is superior given such-and-such conditions, or superior along one dimension but not another -- is acceptable, provided it is genuinely argued rather than merely gestured at.
