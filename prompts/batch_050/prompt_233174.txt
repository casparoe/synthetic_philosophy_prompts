I teach an intro philosophy course, about 18 first-years, 75-minute session, and I want to do the "are virtual objects/harms real" debate without lecturing at them first. Design me an in-class activity where the students end up genuinely upset about the loss or theft of something that obviously doesn't exist -- e.g. they spend part of the session earning or building some pointless artifact (a drawn card, a title, a spot on a leaderboard, whatever works with paper and a whiteboard), and then the rules allow it to be taken, copied, or deleted, and they have to watch it happen. The key thing is the moment should come from the rules, not from me provoking them, and afterwards they should be unable to honestly say "it's nothing, it was never real" because they just watched themselves act like it was. Then a debrief that splits the question into two: was the *object* real, and was the *harm* real, since the interesting position is that you can say no to the first and yes to the second. Don't name any positions in the debate until the very end.

Format-wise: give me your answer as exactly five numbered items, no intro or outro -- (1) setup and materials, (2) the full rules including how the theft/destruction mechanic works, (3) how to stage the session so someone actually gets invested, with rough timing, (4) the debrief mapping specific moments onto the object-real vs harm-real distinction, (5) what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. Also, somewhere in item 4 or 5, tell me which feature of the real debate the game can't reproduce and I have to supply by talking, and what you'd have to see when running it (or what objection) that would make you redesign the central mechanic.
