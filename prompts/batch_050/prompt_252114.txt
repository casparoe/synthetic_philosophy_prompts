Okay so I just spent like three hours arguing with a friend about Mencius vs. Xunzi and I'm still annoyed about it. He kept saying their whole debate is basically a semantic trick -- they just define "human nature" differently so of course one says it's good and the other says it's bad, and underneath they basically agree. I think that's way too cheap. Anyway, this actually gave me an idea for my final exam, since I TA for an undergrad Chinese philosophy course.

Write me one essay question on Mencius vs. Xunzi on human nature that a student can't answer just by reciting "Mencius says human nature is good, Xunzi says it's bad." The question should make them grapple with my friend's move: does the disagreement survive once you notice they may be using "nature" (xing) differently -- Mencius pointing at our incipient moral reactions (the sprouts), Xunzi at our raw desires? A good answer should have to take a stand on whether this is a real substantive disagreement or a verbal one, and actually argue for it, not just describe both sides.

Give me the exam question itself, a model answer of about 500 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that spells out what seperates a top answer from a middling one -- like, what specific moves a full-marks answer must make (e.g. engaging with the sagehood point, since both thinkers think anyone can become good, which cuts weirdly across the disagreement) and which common mistakes (like treating "nature is bad" as meaning Xunzi thought people are evil) should cost marks.
