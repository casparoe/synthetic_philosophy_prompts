I'm a novelist working on an alternate history book, and the whole thing hinges on a what-if: what if the driver of the archduke's car in Sarajevo in 1914 doesn't take that famous wrong turn, the assassin never gets his second chance, and Franz Ferdinand lives. My draft has WWI never happening, at least not on schedule, and the twentieth century going wildly off script. My critique partner read the first fifty pages and told me the premise is cheaty. Her line was: Europe in 1914 was a powder keg, Sarajevo was just the spark, and if it hadn't been that spark it would've been another one within a few years -- the alliances, the arms race, the mobilization timetables were all loaded in. So my novel, she says, is only allowed to delay the war, not prevent it. Meanwhile a friend of mine who's a history buff takes the opposite view, the for-want-of-a-nail picture: tiny contingencies cascade, and if you remove this one the whole chain unravels and nothing like the same war ever happens.

Here's what I actually want help with. Take the powder keg / spark analogy seriously and audit it for me. What maps onto what -- the powder, the keg walls, the spark, the explosion? Where does it break down? For instance, a real powder keg doesn't care which spark hits it, but does "Europe" in 1914? It seems like the analogy quietly builds in the conclusion (the explosion is guaranteed, only the timing is contingent) precisely at the point where historians actually disagree. Also, one thing that bugs me: the analogy treats the keg as something that's just sitting there fully assembled, but maybe the assassination itself helped pack the keg -- like, the response to it created some of the conditions that made war likely, in which case spark and powder aren't so seperable. And then on the other side, does the for-want-of-a-nail picture have the opposite problem, treating history like a line of dominoes where every event is load-bearing, when really some events are more like sparks (easily replaced) and some are more like kegs (hard to rebuild)?

Give me a way to think about which parts of 1914 were spark-like and which were keg-like, and whether "inevitable eventually" is even a coherent verdict or just the analogy doing the arguing for us. Bonus: if the powder keg image can't carry the weight my critique partner wants it to, what's a better analogy I could give her character in the book -- because yes, I'm putting this argument in the novel, and I'd like her to lose it honestly rather than win it badly.
