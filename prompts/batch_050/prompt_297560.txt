My cousins inherited a small charitable foundation from their grandfather and every year they have to agree on where the money goes. Maya is convinced the only defensible use of it is stuff like malaria prevention, since that's where a dollar does the most measurable good. Dev thinks the foundation owes something to their actual town first — the food bank, the youth center — special obligations to the people around you, not just whoever on the planet is cheapest to help. Last year they deadlocked so badly they almost dissolved the whole thing, so they settled on a rule: split the money 50/50 every year, each directs there half, no re-litigating.

Now they're fighting about whether that rule even makes sense, and they roped me in as referee, which was a mistake because I genuinely can't tell. The argument went:

(1) Maya said the split is irrational by both their own lights. By her lights, half the money is wasted every single year with certainty. She proposed an alternative: flip a coin each year, winner directs the whole pot. Ex ante that's the same expected good for each of them as the split, so the split can't be justified on efficiency grounds — it just *feels* safer.

(2) Dev said that's a category error. His position isn't a bet on outcomes, it's a duty. A 50% chance of fully doing your duty is not equivalent to definitely half-doing it. Duties don't average out like lottery tickets.

(3) Maya said that cuts against him: if it's really a duty, the split guarantees a definite shortfall every year forever, whereas the coin flip at least produces years where the duty is fully met. Guaranteed partial failure seems strictly worse than a toss-up between full success and full failure.

(4) Dev said she's treating "the duty" as one lump, but it's a duty to actual people who keep existing. The food bank's clients are hungry in 2026 regardless of coin flips; feeding half of them every year is genuinely better than feeding all of them half the years, because the unfed years don't get cancelled out by the fed ones. You can't tell a hungry kid "we won the toss last year."

(5) Maya's last move: even granting all that, 50/50 is arbitrary. The number just tracks the fact that there happen to be two equally stubborn cousins, nothing moral. If grandpa had had three grandkids the "right" answer would suddenly be 33/33/33. Morality shouldn't be a function of how many people are at the table.

(6) Dev's reply: every workable arrangement in history is partly a function of who's at the table. The split's legitimacy doesn't come from getting the ratio "right," it comes from both of them having genuine standing over money neither of them earned. Demanding a morally exact split assumes one of them is simply correct — which is exactly the thing they can't agree on.

So who's right about which specific points? I want a verdict move by move, then who you think is more right overall, then anything both of them are missing (I suspect there's something). Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then go through it — no preamble, and don't summarize the scenario back at me.
