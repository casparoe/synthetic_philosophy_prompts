I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm preparing a column on a forthcoming experiment. A behavioral-game-theory group plans to run a one-shot, fully anonymous online contest with 10,000 verified participants, all strangers to one another. The rules: each person may submit at most one entry; if exactly one entry is submitted, its author wins $1,000,000; if zero or more than one entry arrives, the prize lapses. Before deciding, every participant watches the same short video laying out the symmetric-reasoning argument associated with Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma and Luring Lottery — that similar agents who cannot communicate should each enter with probability 1/10,000, so that in expectation exactly one entry arrives and the winner can be expected to share the prize — and is told that every other participant watches it too.

Please give me your credence that the contest ends with someone actually winning the money, decomposed into the sub-questions your answer depends on: the share of participants who will follow the symmetric mixed strategy, whether common viewing of the video generates the required common knowledge, how defection and sheer noise scale with 10,000 players, and whatever other terms you think belong — with a rough number and one line of justification for each, then how they combine. Be sure your reasoning states what the win probability would be even under flawless universal compliance with the recommended strategy. Finally, tell me which term you are least confident in, and what prior experimental result or design modification would shift it most.
