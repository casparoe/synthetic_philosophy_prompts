I'm a PhD student in decision sciences (choice theory, basically — I live in vNM axioms, multi-attribute utility theory, a bit of Savage and regret minimisation on the side), and my flatmate is a classicist who insists the Greeks got to everything first. Over pints last night he claimed that Plato had already committed to full value commensurability two millennia before MAUT, and he waved this bit of the Protagoras at me — Socrates is mid-argument trying to show that what we call being overcome by pleasure is really just a kind of measurement error. This is from Jowett's translation, around 356d–357b:

"Now if our welfare consisted in doing and choosing things of large dimensions, and avoiding and not doing those of small, what would be our salvation in life? Would it be the art of measurement, or the power of appearance? Is it not the latter that leads us astray, as we saw, and many a time causes us to take things topsy-turvy and to have to change our minds both in our conduct and in our choice of great or small? Whereas the art of measurement would have made this appearance ineffective, and by showing us the truth would have brought our soul into the repose of abiding by the truth, and so would have saved our life. [...] Well then, my friends, since we have found that the salvation of our life depends on making a right choice of pleasure and pain—of the more and the fewer, the greater and the smaller, and the nearer and the remoter—is it not evident, in the first place, that measurement is a study of their excess and defect and equality in relation to each other? And being measurement, I presume it must be an art or science?"

Translate as faithfully as you can, and flag where Socrates is quietly assuming things we now treat as optional axioms (completeness, transitivity, a single cardinal scale, the commensurability of pleasure and pain themselves). Then, the part I actually care about: a residue report. My corner of the literature is having a low-grade crisis over incommensurability and hard choices — the small-improvement argument, 'parity' as a possible fourth comparative relation alongside better/worse/equal, constitutive goods that allegedly can't be traded without loss — and I want to know which bits of that modern debate have no counterpart in the measuring-art picture.
