For twenty years I have earned my living as a novelist, and I am fluent in the working vocabulary of my craft: plot and causal structure, characterization, point of view and free indirect discourse, drafts and revision, and the gap between what an author intends and what a finished text actually does. My knowledge of physics and theology, by contrast, is a lay reader's. Lately I have been preoccupied by the suspicion that the way novelists talk about their characters — she came alive on the page; she refused to do what I had planned for her — rehearses, in a debased currency, real questions about freedom and necessity. So take Einstein's views on religion and free will as they are commonly reported: his conviction that events are fully determined by prior causes, his denial that human beings possess free will in any philosophically serious sense, his rejection of a personal God who concerns himself with individual conduct, his professed affinity with Spinoza's identification of God with the lawful order of nature itself, and his appeals to a religious awe at the intelligibility of the universe as the true motive of science. Restate each of these commitments in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then give me a residue report. I want to know which commitments translate cleanly — the determined character in a finished manuscript seems promising, but tell me what is wrong with it — which survive only with distortion, and which find no expression at all in the language of plot, character, and narration. Where the translation seems to flatter the comparison — the novelist standing to her characters as the very personal God Einstein rejected — say plainly whether that exposes a defect in my framework or reveals that his position leaned on metaphor in the first place. Keep the translation honest: do not import a novelist's assumptions and pass them off as his. Give me your answer as exactly five numbered items, with no introduction or conclusion: the first three translating the central commitments (determinism; the denial of free will and its implication for moral responsibility; the impersonal God and the religious feeling), the fourth stating the residue and why it would not carry, and the fifth your verdict on where the failure lies — each item confined to two or three sentences.
