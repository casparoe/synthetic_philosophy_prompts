I teach moral philosophy and am putting together a departmental handbook entry on the doctrine of double effect. A colleague who works in a different field kindly drafted a summary for the entry, and before I send him revisions I would like an independent audit of it. Here is his draft:

"The doctrine of double effect, traceable to Aquinas's treatment of killing in self-defense, holds that an action foreseen to have both a good effect and a bad one may be permissible where an otherwise similar action aimed at the bad effect would not be. In its standard modern formulation, such an action is permissible when four conditions are met: the action itself is good or at least morally neutral; the agent intends the good effect and not the bad one; the bad effect is not the means by which the good effect is achieved; and the good achieved is proportionate to the harm done. The doctrine is often illustrated by the contrast between the bystander-at-the-switch case, where it licenses diverting a runaway trolley away from five workers and toward one, and the footbridge case, where it forbids pushing a large man into the trolley's path, since there the man's death is the very means of saving the five. More generally, the doctrine shows that an agent who knowingly brings about a death as a foreseen side effect is blameless, provided the stakes are sufficiently high, and it thereby captures the intuitive moral difference between killing and letting die."

I would also like your view on how the doctrine, as correctly stated, handles two cases I devised for the seminar, because I suspect they put pressure on his formulation. In both, a runaway trolley will kill five workers unless it is diverted onto a siding. Case A: a heavy repair vehicle is parked on the siding and will absorb the trolley; its driver is trapped inside and will die on impact, but the vehicle's mass alone would have stopped the trolley whether or not anyone were inside. Case B: the vehicle is empty and too light to stop the trolley on its own; the trolley will halt only if the trapped driver remains aboard, so that his body being crushed is what brings the trolley to rest, with his death following as an unavoidable consequence of the crushing.
