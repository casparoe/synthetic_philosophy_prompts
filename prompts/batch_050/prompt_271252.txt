Construct a pair of thought experiments about everyday conventions -- tipping, queueing, jaywalking, that whole genre -- and what visitors owe to local custom. The two cases should come out on opposite sides:

Case A: a visitor is morally required to follow a local convention even though the same behavior would be pointless or even slightly rude back home, and even though they get nothing out of it personally and will never see these people again.

Case B: a visitor is morally required NOT to follow a local convention, even though the locals all do it, genuinely expect it, and will be mildly annoyed or inconvienced if the visitor refuses.

Constraints: both scenarios should be realistic (actual travel situations, no trolleys, no dictatorships demanding atrocities -- keep the stakes at the everyday level of money, time, and rudeness), each scenario should be one short paragraph, and the two cases should differ in one isolatable feature, not in a whole pile of stuff. The pair should function as a diagnosis: whatever you vary between them is supposed to be the thing that flips the verdict, so build them to be as alike as possible except for that.

After the scenarios: state the verdict in each case and why, name the feature that does the work, and show that if you remove or reverse that feature, each case flips to the other side. Then give me two pushbacks: (1) how someone who thinks conventions create expectations but never real obligations would attack Case A, and (2) how a "when in Rome" hardliner would attack Case B. End with an honest assessment of whether those attacks land -- and in particular whether your asymmetry secretly rests on actual harm vs. mere annoyance rather than on anything interesting about convention as such. If it does, fess up.
