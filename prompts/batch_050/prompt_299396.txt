I am a PhD student in philosophy, and my dissertation includes a chapter on conceivability arguments for mind-dependence. I keep returning to Berkeley's so-called master argument in Part I of A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge (around sections 22–23), where, in effect, he wagers everything on a single challenge: try to conceive of a body existing unperceived, and you will find that you have perceived it in the very act of conceiving it, since you framed an idea of it in your mind. He even concedes, in my paraphrase, that the bare conceivability of unperceived existence would count as an argument for it -- so the bar he sets is remarkably low, and yet he thinks it cannot be cleared.

I have spent a term clearing my diary each morning to attempt the challenge in different ways and writing down my verdicts. They do not cohere, and I want to know what principle, if any, lies beneath them. Here they are.

Case 1, the closet (Berkeley's own): I picture books in a closet with nobody perceiving them. Verdict: the attempt fails. Berkeley is right that I framed ideas of the books and merely omitted to frame an idea of a perceiver; I was perceiving them in imagination all along.

Case 2, the unobserved galaxies: I entertain and assent to the proposition that there are galaxies beyond the observable universe that no mind will ever perceive, with no imagery involved at all -- the way I believe things in set theory. Verdict: the attempt succeeds. Conceiving is not imagining, and the challenge is met discursively.

Case 3, the unfelt pain: I try to conceive of a pain that no one feels. Verdict: the attempt fails, and fails harder than Case 1. A pain no one feels strikes me as a contradiction outright, not merely something I happen not to have conceived. So the master argument works for sensations even if it fails for trees.

Case 4, the far side of the moon: I picture the far side of the moon as it was before any observer existed -- grey rock, rendered like a survey map in three dimensions, from no one's vantage point. Verdict: the attempt succeeds even though it is imagistic, because a point of view in a picture need not be anyone's point of view; the imagery implies no perceiver.

Case 5, the overnight lamp: I conceive of my desk lamp existing while I slept and no one perceived it. Verdict: it succeeds, but only in the discursive way of Case 2, and on reflection I seem to infer the lamp's persistence from evidence rather than conceive it directly. I am unsure whether inference counts as conception for the purposes of the challenge.

Case 6, the non-idea-like: I try to conceive of something that is neither an idea nor anything like an idea. Verdict: I cannot even begin; I do not know what would count as trying. Yet this verdict sits badly with Case 2, since the unobserved galaxies were supposed to be my example of exactly such a thing -- extended, coloured, shaped, hence apparently idea-like after all.

The tensions I cannot resolve: Case 1 against Case 4 (both imagistic, opposite verdicts), and Case 2 against Case 6 (my discursive success seems to depend on covertly conceiving the galaxies as idea-like, which would mean I never conceived the non-idea-like at all).

What I want from you: first, a principle -- or at most two candidate principles -- stated precisely enough that I could apply it mechanically, capturing the largest coherent subset of these six verdicts, with each clause of the principle tied to the case that motivates it. Second, if no principle saves all six, tell me which single verdict you would overturn and why it rather than the others. Third, apply your principle to two cases I have not tried: a tree registered only by an automated camera whose footage no one ever views (is it perceived?), and the number 7 during a stretch of time when no one is thinking of it (does your principle treat abstracta differently, and does the challenge even generalize to them?). Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
