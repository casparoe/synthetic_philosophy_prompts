I am a high school student serving on a student council constitutional committee. I understand school institutions much better than theories of democracy: enrollment rolls, class rosters, club membership, eligibility rules, visiting students, parents, teachers, alumni, applicants, transfer students, and students at neighboring schools. Please use that vocabulary to translate the boundary problem in democratic theory: before democratic procedures can decide anything, who is entitled to participate in deciding who belongs to the demos?

Use the following fictional case. Five schools are considering whether to form a Valley School Union governed by an elected council. The Union would control graduation requirements, disciplinary procedures, school-bus routes, surveillance cameras, lunch contracts, club funding, and admissions to specialized programs. Its founding charter must first be ratified by a vote, but the organizers must decide who gets ballots. The possible voters include currently enrolled students, students at nearby schools whose neighborhoods would be crossed by Union buses, rejected applicants to specialized programs, future students who have not yet enrolled, recent graduates, parents, teachers, students enrolled only in an online program, and students who expect to transfer into the Union next year.

First, restate the main proposed solutions to the boundary problem in this school-governance vocabulary: the principle that everyone whose interests are affected should have a say; the principle that everyone subject to coercion should have a say; the principle that everyone subject to the institution’s rules should have a say; and a social-membership view under which ongoing relationships, residence, dependence, or shared practices matter more than being affected by one isolated decision. Explain where each principle draws the ballot boundary in this case, and distinguish coercion from being merely affected and being formally under a rule.

Second, break participation into four separate statuses rather than treating membership as all-or-nothing: the right to vote on the founding charter, the right to place an item on the agenda, the right to present testimony without voting, and the right to have one’s interests officially considered even without any direct voice. For each proposed boundary principle, say which groups receive which statuses and whether the principle can justify separating these statuses.

Finally, give a residue report. Which parts of the boundary problem translate cleanly into enrollment, eligibility, jurisdiction, and school authority? Which parts must be distorted because schools treat students as minors, assign membership administratively, or separate political voice from parental and employment relationships? Which parts seem impossible to express in my school vocabulary at all? For each residue item, say whether it reveals a limitation of the school framework or an ambiguity in the original democratic principle. In particular, address whether the founding vote is itself undemocratic if its electorate was chosen before the Union existed, and whether any of the proposed principles can avoid that circularity without quietly assuming an answer.
