My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about how much individual people actually matter in history, and we ended up stuck, so I want help sorting out what I actually think. Below are my gut verdicts on a series of counterfactuals, roughly in the order we argued about them. I'd like to know whether any coherent principle of historical explanation generates them -- and, if not, which verdicts I should abandon.

First, the verdicts:

1. Had Princip's bullet missed Franz Ferdinand in 1914, a general European war still breaks out within a few years. The alliance system, the arms race, and the Balkan flashpoints made some such war overwhelmingly likely; Princip was a trigger, not a cause. The individual barely mattered.

2. Had Einstein died in childhood, special relativity arrives within a decade anyway -- Poincaré, Lorentz, and Minkowski were already circling it. But general relativity might have been delayed by twenty years or more, since almost nobody else was even asking the right questions. So science is structural, but only mostly.

3. Had Hitler been killed in the trenches in 1918, Germany still gets some kind of authoritarian, revanchist, antisemitic regime in the 1930s -- the Treaty of Versailles, the Depression, and the collapse of Weimar legitimacy made that very likely. But the Holocaust, specifically, does not happen; industrialized genocide of that scale and character required Hitler in particular. The regime was structural; the genocide was personal. My friend thought this combination was obviously inconsistent, and I admit I'm not sure how to defend it.

4. Had Zuckerberg never been born, some social network still ends up dominating -- the demand and the technology were both there, as the near-simultaneous rise of Friendster, MySpace, and others suggests. The winner might look different, but the winner would exist.

5. Had Newton died young, calculus still gets invented -- Leibniz did it independently. But had Bach died young, nothing like the Mass in B minor ever gets written by anyone. So discovery is fungible and creation is not, and this feels like the same distinction at work in the Einstein case.

The trouble is that verdicts 1 and 3 seem to pull against each other. If Princip was interchangeable because the system was primed for war, why wasn't Hitler interchangeable given that the system was primed for an antisemitic dictatorship? My friend's position was that my verdicts simply track whatever I already believe about background conditions, so that "the individual mattered" is just another way of saying "I haven't thought about the structure enough." I want to resist that, because it seems to dissolve verdict 2 and 5 too -- you could always say that about Einstein or Bach.

So: is there a principled distinction here? I'm imagining something along the lines of -- an individual matters historically to the extent that no substitute existed who would have done relevantly similar things, where the existence of substitutes depends on both demand-side pressures (whether the era was pulling toward the outcome) and supply-side facts (how many people were positioned and able to do it). But I suspect that principle, stated precisely, either forces me to give up the Hitler/Princip asymmetry or collapses into my friend's deflationary view, and I'd like to know which.

Please state the best candidate principle (or two rivals, if they genuinely diverge), identify which clause of it each of my five verdicts is doing the work for, and say honestly which verdicts it cannot accommodate. Then test it on two cases I went back and forth on without reaching a verdict: had Napoleon never existed, does revolutionary France still end up in decades of continent-wide war under some military dictator? And had Genghis Khan died in infancy, does the steppe still produce a world-spanning conquest in the thirteenth century?
