So I'm in a small reading group on animal minds (mix of philosphy students and one behavioral neuroscientist, Tomás, who is EXTREMELY into Morgan's canon) and we got stuck on something last week. Tomás keeps wielding the canon against any remotely anthropomorphic reading of animal behavior, always in the wording of the second edition of C. Lloyd Morgan's An Introduction to Comparative Psychology (1903): "In no case is an animal activity to be interpreted in terms of higher psychological processes if it can be fairly interpreted in terms of processes which stand lower in the scale of psychological evolution and development." (The 1894 first edition apparently framed it more in terms of higher versus lower psychical faculties, but the 1903 version is the one he quotes.)

I tried to push back by arguing that the canon proves too much, and I want to know where my argument goes wrong, because I find the conclusion absurd:

(1) Take the canon seriously as a general rule of interpretation: never explain behavior by appeal to a higher psychological process if a lower one can fairly account for it.
(2) For basically anything another human does -- ordering coffee, answering an exam question, apologizing -- some lower-process redescription is available: habit, conditioning, mimicry, stimulus-response chains, overlearned verbal routines. The behaviorists spent half a century showing you can redescribe human action this way; that was their whole program. And nothing in the canon's wording restricts it to non-human animals.
(3) So, by the canon, we should never interpret other people's behavior in terms of reasoning, deliberation, or understanding -- only in terms of habit, conditioning, and the like.
(4) But that's absurd. A methodological rule that forbids me from saying the barista got my order right because she understood my sentence proves too much; it makes radical behaviorism about other people mandatory.
(5) So either the canon is just wrong, or it carries a silent "animals only" clause -- and then the interesting question is what justifies the asymmetry, since blocking the "they're like us" inference was the canon's whole job in the first place.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? If forced to choose, I'd rather keep the canon (it's the only thing standing between us and pure anecdote) than accept (3), but I want to know what I'm paying. Two more things. First, apparently Morgan himself later added a rider to the effect that more complex interpretations are fine once a species has given independent signs of high intelligence -- if that rider already answers my argument, say so, but then tell me what counts as an "independent sign" and why human language use wouldn't trivially be one. Second, please engage the actual wording of the quote, especially "fairly interpreted," because I suspect the whole thing turns on it one way or another. Give me a committed answer: name the step you'd actually reject and state what form of the canon (if any) survives, rather than handing me a menu of options to pick from.

Structure the answer in these sections, in this order: "The weakest step" (about 120 words -- name it and explain why it looked fine), "Why the other exits cost more" (about 180 words -- price the alternatives, including the animals-only reading and the appeal to Morgan's rider), "Does the wording save Morgan?" (about 150 words -- what "fairly interpreted" and the higher/lower scale do and don't rule out), and "My verdict" (about 80 words -- the bottom line on the canon's fate).
