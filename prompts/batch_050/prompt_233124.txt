In my political philosophy seminar we spent two weeks on the human-rights tradition, and afterward a few of us kept arguing at the pub. I had said that some things -- being tortured for a confession, say -- are wrongs against a person even in a jurisdiction whose law permits them, and that this is what people mean by a right that exists independently of the law. One of my classmates, who has been reading Bentham, ended the discussion by declaring the whole notion of pre-legal rights to be "nonsense upon stilts" and refused to say anything further, on the grounds that Bentham had settled it. I suspect the phrase is doing several different jobs at once and that he was trading on the ambiguity, but I can't yet separate the jobs out.

Here is what I'd like. Lay out, as a menu I can annotate before our next session, the distinct precise theses that this slogan could be expressing. Candidates I half-see include: a thesis about meaning (talk of natural rights is literally senseless, like squaring a circle, because rights are creatures of law and "right without law" names nothing); a thesis about ontology (the phrase is meaningful but there simply are no such entities -- everything called a right is conferred by some legal or social practice); an epistemic thesis (even if such rights existed, no one could ever establish which ones there are, so appeals to them are unverifiable assertion); a psychological or rhetorical thesis (declaring a natural right is just dressing up one's own preferences or interests in impressive language -- which may be what the "stilts" are); a thesis about argumentative priority (rights-talk does no independent work, since any defensible rights claim collapses into a calculation of consequences); and a political thesis (natural-rights talk is not merely confused but dangerous, because a supposed right that trumps all law licenses resistance to any law whatever). For each reading, tell me: is it a platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or actually false or self-undermining -- I have in mind here the worry that Bentham's own principle of utility is itself a standard standing outside the law, and I'd like to know whether that objection lands against every reading or only some. Then, most importantly, tell me which of these readings, if any, would actually support my classmate's conversational move of dismissing my torture claim without further argument, and which would leave the claim untouched even if true.

Two requests about how to answer. First, present this as a disambiguation with verdicts rather than an essay championing one interpretation -- I want to see the whole space before committing. Second, push back where warranted: if my framing rests on a confused premise -- for instance, if I am wrong to treat the slogan as an argument rather than as the punchy conclusion of a background theory of law that does the real work, or if some of my candidate readings collapse into each other on inspection -- say so plainly rather than politely organizing the answer around my mistakes.
