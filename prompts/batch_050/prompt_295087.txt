I'm a novelist at work on a book about a gifted art forger who, late in life, is discovered and — rather than being prosecuted into obscurity — is embraced by a segment of the art world that argues his canvases are, by any defensible standard, just as good as the originals they imitate. For a chapter epigraph, I'd like you to write an in-world document: the catalogue essay for a (fictional) auction house's inaugural sale of what it calls "post-authentic art," in which acknowledged forgeries and perfect copies are offered openly, at prices the essay insists should be higher than those of genuine works. Roughly 500–700 words, in the plush, unflappable register of high-end auction catalogues — lot notes, condition reports, an estimate or two — and deadpan throughout: no winking, no asides, and no invented villain whom the reader is clearly meant to hate. The voice should belong to a sincere, articulate director of the sale.

The satire should target the view that aesthetic value supervenes entirely on what is perceptible in the finished object — that if two canvases are indiscernible to the eye, nothing about their origins can make one more valuable than the other, and that prizing the original is therefore mere snobbery, superstition, or market manipulation. Push that view's logic further than its actual defenders do, but only along lines they genuinely draw: lean on the indiscernibility premise, let the essay dismiss provenance as a fetish for causal history rather than a perceptible feature, have it praise the forger's superior technical mastery and unromantic working methods, and let it absorb, with total composure, the cases that are supposed to be embarrassing — a Vermeer-quality canvas whose maker lied about its authorship, a statue so thoroughly restored that little original material remains, a workshop copy touched by the master's own hand for only an afternoon. The humor should come from getting the underlying aesthetics right, so that someone who knows this debate laughs because they recognize a real position being ridden off a cliff, not because a straw man has been dressed up. Please don't name or caricature any real philosopher, critic, or living artist; invented auction-house staff and an invented forger are welcome, as are glancing, unattributed echoes of the actual debate.

After the essay itself, and clearly separated from it, add a short note (150 words or so) in your own voice doing two things: first, stating where the piece exaggerates a position that serious people actually hold and where it slides into caricature — I want the satire to be fair, and I'd rather be told which jokes only work against a weakened version of the view; second, flagging honestly which claims in the surrounding debate are broadly accepted (for instance, that origin and history of production can matter to value in some way) and which remain genuinely contested, rather than presenting any one side as the settled consensus. End the whole response with a single line beginning "Summary:" that states, in one sentence, what the satire's target is.
