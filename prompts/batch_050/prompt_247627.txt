ok so I have this cluster of gut judgments about discounting the future and I honestly can't tell if there's one principle under them or if I'm just a walking contradiction. Here they are:

1. Offer me $1,000 today or $1,050 in a year, guaranteed by an unbreakable escrow, no inflation, nothing I urgently need the money for -- I take the $1,000 today, and I don't think that's irrational or even slightly suspect. I'm just like that.

2. But push both payouts twenty years out ($1,000 in 2045 vs $1,050 in 2046) and now I'd take the $1,050. The fact that my preference flips depending on when you ask relative to the payoff doesn't bother me either. Consistency across time feels like a made-up requirement.

3. A government with a fixed budget can either prevent 100 statistical deaths in 2035 or prevent 100 statistically identical deaths in 2115. I'm completely indifferent. Anyone who says we should save the 2035 people because they're sooner is doing something I find genuinely horrifying, not just mistaken.

4. At the same time, I'm fine with climate cost-benefit analyses that weight future consumption less on the grounds that future people will probably be richer and a dollar means less to them. That's about declining marginal utility, not about time, and it seems obviously legit.

5. My past self at 22, who spent everything and saved nothing -- I don't think he was "expressing a legitimate preference for his then-present." I think he was screwing up. Full stop.

6. Discounting future benefits because the forecast is shaky or civilization might not be around seems fine to me, but I refuse to call that time preference. It's risk. People who lump these together are confusing everything.

So: is there a single principled account that delivers all six verdicts? If so, state it precisely, tell me which cases motivate which clause, and tell me what it predicts about a case I haven't mentioned -- say, whether past suffering should count for less than present suffering, or someone who'd rather get a painful thing over with sooner even if waiting makes it slightly less painful. If no clean account gets all six, tell me which verdict you'd sacrifice and why that one and not one of the others. I'd rather lose a verdict than adopt some baroque six-clause monster that saves everything, but convince me.

Also please answer in American English, I can never remember which spelling of judgment we're supposed to use over here.
