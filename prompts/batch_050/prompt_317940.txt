I want to defend a position in the philosophy of comparative cognition, and I'd like you to help me stress-test it rather than corroborate it. The position is this. Morgan's canon -- the injunction that an animal's behavior should not be interpreted in terms of a higher psychological faculty if it can be adequately interpreted in terms of one lower on the scale of psychological evolution -- is usually read as a methodological counsel of parsimony, and on that reading it has taken heavy criticism: that it smuggles in a scala naturae, that it is asymmetrically skeptical (never cautioning against attributing too little cognition), and that "lower" processes like associative learning are not in fact simpler to posit than "higher" ones like reasoning or metacognition, so the canon fails even on its own parsimonious terms. My position is that these criticisms mostly miss, because the canon is best read not as a parsimony heuristic at all but as a claim about evidential structure: roughly, that behavioral evidence underdetermines cognitive mechanism in a systematic way, such that the same observable repertoire can typically be generated by mechanisms of quite different complexity, and that in conditions of such underdetermination the burden of proof falls on the richer attribution. On this reading the canon is not a bias against animal minds but a general point about inference to psychological mechanism from behavior alone -- one that would apply equally to the interpretation of human infants, of artificial systems, and of any case where we have only input-output patterns to go on.

What would count against this position, taken seriously? I would like several things. First, an identification of what the position actually predicts or excludes: does it commit me to anything about the distribution of cognitive mechanisms across taxa, or is it compatible with the empirical discovery that rich cognition is widespread? If the latter, say whether that compatibility is a virtue or a sign that the position does no real work. Second, the strongest candidate defeaters, ordered from most to least damaging: consider, for instance, the objection that the lower/higher ordering presupposed by the canon cannot be made out in any principled way once behaviorism's preferred ordering is abandoned; the objection that the underdetermination claim proves too much, since behavior underdetermines mechanism in the human case too and we do not withhold rich attributions there; and the objection that convergent evidence from neuroscience and homology already dissolves the underdetermination the canon rests on, making it an artifact of behavior-only experimentation. Third, for each defeater, say whether a defender of my reading would concede it as genuinely damaging or absorb it as a clarification -- and where absorption would amount to retreating into a claim so weak that nothing in comparative cognition practice turns on it. Finally, I'd like an honest verdict on whether the position, charitably interpreted, risks anything empirical at all, or whether it has been rendered unfalsifiable precisely by the move from methodological maxim to claim about evidential structure. If the latter, tell me whether that is a defect for a position of this kind or simply what it is for a claim to be epistemological rather than zoological.
