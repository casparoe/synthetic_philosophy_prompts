The exam question was:

"State the demandingness objection to consequentialism. Explain how satisficing consequentialism and scalar consequentialism each attempt to respond to it, and say which response you find more successful and why."

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

The demandingness objection says that consequentialism, because it tells us to maximize the good impartially, requires ordinary people to make enormous sacrifices — e.g. giving away most of your income to effective charities rather than spending it on your own life. Singer's drowning child argument generalizes: there is always a child somewhere, so morality is basically a full time job. In other words, consequentialism demands that agents abandon their ground projects and personal commitments, which most people find absurd.

Satisficing consequentialism (Slote) responds by dropping maximization: an action is morally right as long as its outcome is good enough, even if a better option was available. So you don't have to donate everything you own; a decent contribution suffices, and anything beyond that is more than morality can ask of you.

Scalar consequentialism (Norcross) responds differently. Instead of asking whether actions are right or wrong, we should only rank them as better or worse, which makes the right/wrong distinction a matter of degree. Since the demandingness objection only bites when there's a sharp line of duty, turning rightness into a scalar notion dissolves the objection entirely.

I find satisficing more convincing, because it keeps an intuitive notion of moral duty while scalar views just refuse to answer the question we actually care about, which is what we ought to do.
