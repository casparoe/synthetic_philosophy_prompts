I'm assembling the final exam for my upper-level undergraduate seminar on Naming and Necessity and its aftermath, and I need one essay question, answerable in 75 minutes and worth 20 marks, that tests something beyond recitation of Kripke. My recurring frustration is that students can parrot the slogan examples -- water and H2O, Hesperus and Phosphorus, the standard meter stick -- and can define "rigid designator," but fall apart when asked to apply the machinery to a case they haven't seen, especially when the case turns on the difference between using a description to fix a reference and using it to give a synonym. So I'd like the question to be built around the following scenario, which you should present in the question text itself: On a generation ship, two measurement traditions have developed in isolation. The spectrometry lab introduces the term "cryth" by stipulating that it names, if anything does, whatever actually produces the reading of type R on their instruments. The calorimetry lab independently introduces "tharn" by stipulating that it names, if anything does, whatever actually causes their thermometers to spike by amount S. Years later the two labs compare notes and conclude, empirically, that cryth is tharn. The question should require candidates to determine: whether "cryth is tharn" is necessary a posteriori and what that verdict depends on; whether each term is in fact rigid, given how it was introduced, and what would settle that question; whether each lab, prior to the discovery, knew a contingent truth a priori (that cryth produces R-readings, that tharn causes S-spikes) and how this relates to the meter-stick case; and what it could mean to say the identity "might have turned out false" without collapsing epistemic into metaphysical possibility.

The deliverable is threefold. First, the question exactly as it would appear on the exam paper, including the scenario, phrased so that a student cannot score well by summarizing the reading -- the marks should be earnable only by working through the shipboard case. Second, a model answer of roughly 600-800 words written at the level of your best student, which takes definite positions (e.g., on whether reference-fixing descriptions make the associated stipulative truths contingent, and if so in what sense of contingency) rather than hedging through a survey of options. Third, a marking scheme built around exactly five must-hit points, stating the marks each carries out of 20, and for each point a one-line note on the characteristic way candidates get it wrong -- for instance, treating "cryth" as rigid merely because the shipmates intend it to name a substance kind, or inferring from the apriority of the stipulation that the stipulation is necessary. Where the model answer makes a move that a marker might reasonably debate (such as leaning on the distinction between deep and superficial contingency), flag it and say how a marker should credit a candidate who takes the other side well.
