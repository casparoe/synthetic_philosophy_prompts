Exam question: Raz famously argues that conformity to the rule of law is, in his words, an essentially negative virtue of a legal system. Explain what Raz means by this and how his view relates to Fuller's claim that the desiderata of legality constitute an internal morality of law. Then consider whether Waldron's emphasis on the procedural and dignity-related aspects of legality offers a middle position between the two. Use the theorists' own arguments, not just their conclusions.

Student answer:

Raz's point is that the rule of law doesn't guarantee that a legal system is morally good. A wicked regime can conform to the rule of law just as well as a decent one, since the ideal only concerns the form laws take rather than their content. Raz compares the rule of law to a knife: a good knife is sharp, but sharpness can serve good or evil purposes, and the same goes for legality. So for Raz the rule of law has no moral value at all -- it's purely a matter of efficiency, helping the law guide behavior the way a sharp knife cuts. This is why he says it's a "negative" virtue: it can prevent certain harms of arbitrary power but contributes nothing positive.

Fuller disagrees. His eight desiderata (generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, consistency, practicability, stability, and congruence between official action and declared rules) are not mere efficiency conditions but an internal morality, because a system that fails them badly isn't just inefficient, it fails to be law at all. Fuller also thought there was an affinity between legality and substantive justice -- it's harder to pursue evil ends through general, public, prospective rules. So where Raz separates form from content completely, Fuller thinks legality and justice tend to travel together.

Waldron tries to split the difference. He thinks the Fuller/Raz picture is too thin because it focuses only on the formal features of rules and ignores the procedural side: courts, hearings, the opportunity to present arguments and be heard. For Waldron, the rule of law includes substantive rights like equality before the law, which Fuller and Raz leave out entirely. Treating people as agents who can understand and respond to reasons, rather than merely manipulating them with threats, is what gives legality its moral weight. This is a middle position because it keeps Raz's insight that form matters while adding Fuller's moral ambitions back in via procedure.

Since Raz thinks a wicked regime can satisfy the rule of law, he must deny that such a regime's directives count as genuine law, which is what Fuller meant by an internal morality. So in the end the disagreement between them is mostly verbal.
