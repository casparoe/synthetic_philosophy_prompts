So I'm a journalist on the ideas desk of a general-interest magazine, the kind that runs a "what the fall of Rome teaches us" piece occassionally whenever democracy has a bad news cycle. My editor has extremely firm views about Rome analogies and I've started keeping score of her verdicts on my pitches:

- Caesar crossing the Rubicon as a frame for a modern leader refusing to accept an election result: killed before I finished the sentence.
- The Republic's long norm war -- tribunes weaponizing the veto, senators inventing new obstruction tricks, each side treating the other's escalation as permission for its own -- mapped onto the procedural tit-for-tat in today's parliaments and congresses: printed, did great traffic, she wants more like it.
- Cato the Younger as the patron saint of principled obstruction, with a modern hardliner of your choice in the headline: killed.
- Augustus's settlement, every republican form lovingly preserved and every bit of the substance quietly removed, mapped onto elected governments that keep the courts and the constitution while hollowing both out: printed, she called it "the good kind of Rome piece."
- The bread-and-circuses column: the Roman crowd trading its political inheritance for handouts and spectacles, modern voters doing the same for giveaways and content: killed so fast it left a mark.

Now I owe her a pitch Friday and I want to build it around this passage from Polybius, Histories book 6, chapter 57 (Shuckburgh translation) -- the less-quoted bit near the end of the book, after all the famous cycle-of-constitutions stuff:

"When a commonwealth, after warding off many great dangers, has arrived at a high pitch of prosperity and undisputed power, it is evident that, by the lengthened continuance of great wealth within it, the manner of life of its citizens will become more extravagant; and that the rivalry for office, and in other spheres of activity, will become fiercer than it ought to be. And as this state of things goes on more and more, the desire of office and the shame of losing reputation, as well as the ostentation and extravagance of living, will prove the beginning of a deterioration. And of this change the people will be credited with being the authors, when they become convinced that they are being cheated by some from avarice, and are puffed up with flattery by others from love of office. For when that comes about, in their passionate resentment and acting under the dictates of anger, they will refuse to obey any longer, or to be content with having equal powers with their leaders, but will demand to have all or far the greatest themselves. And when that comes to pass the constitution will receive a new name, which sounds better than any other in the world, liberty or democracy; but, in fact, it will become that worst of all governments, mob-rule."

My column would basically operationalize him: prosperity makes status competition fiercer than it should be, the public becomes convinced some people are cheating it while others flatter it, it stops settling for equal footing with its leaders, and you wake up keeping the fairest name while living under the worst reality. Modern mapping: attention-economy office-and-status rivalry among elites, algorithmic flattery-and-grievance at mass scale. No Caesar figure anywhere in the piece, no single event, the engine is rivalry and flattery, not a villain.

But I honestly can't tell if she takes it, because her record underdetermines her rule. If the rule is "no protagonists -- institutions and processes only, no Caesar, no Cato, no mob-as-morality-play," my pitch is clean and she prints it. If the rule is "republics are broken by their elites, and I will not run anything that ends by blaming the crowd," then Polybius himself is my problem, because the passage flat-out credits the people as the authors of the change, and she killed my bread-and-circuses pitch for exactly that move.

So: predict her call and defend it. Tell me which of those two rules (or some third rule I've missed that also fits all five verdicts) is the better reconstruction of her record, what each rule says about this specific pitch, and where the passage itself tips it -- does it read to you as elite failure, mass failure, or genuinely both, and would that distinction even survive an edit meeting? And give me the test pitch that would settle which rule she's actually running, in case this one comes back marked "nice try."
