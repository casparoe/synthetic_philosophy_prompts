I've read rationalist blogs and forums for years, so I'm reasonably fluent in the standard paradoxes of collective choice: Condorcet cycles, Arrow's theorem, strategic voting, the usual zoo. But I've been sitting on a university committee lately, and I keep running into a configuration that doesn't seem to reduce to any of those. I want to know whether it has an established name and literature, or whether I'm redescribing something familiar from a strange angle.

Stripped to its skeleton: a group must settle a verdict that is logically fixed by the answers to several prior questions, and the method of aggregation determines the outcome even though every member is individually consistent and votes honestly. Two instances:

A fellowship committee whose bylaws say a candidate is funded if and only if the proposal is excellent and the budget line exists. Five members vote clause by clause. Three judge the proposal excellent (though two of those think the budget isn't there); three judge the budget adequate (though two of those think the proposal is mediocre). Only one member endorses both. If the chair aggregates clause by clause, the committee has "found" excellence and budget, and its own bylaws compel funding — while four of five members, asked directly, would vote against it. Same ballots, no dishonesty, opposite verdicts depending on procedure.

An appellate panel of three judges, where damages are owed if and only if a valid contract exists and was breached. Judge one: valid, no breach, no damages. Judge two: invalid, breached, no damages. Judge three: both, damages. Issue by issue, the court finds validity and breach, hence damages; judge by judge, damages loses two to one.

What I want is not a bare label. Is this one phenomenon or a family? How exactly does it differ from the Condorcet paradox — there, transitive individual rankings aggregate into cyclic majorities, but here there are no cycles anywhere, just binary judgments with logical connectives — and from Arrow's theorem, which concerns orderings over alternatives rather than interconnected propositions? Is it a corollary of Arrow, a neighbor, or independent? Where are its edges: does it require binary premises, survive under supermajority rules, depend on the agenda being fixed? Is there an impossibility result characterizing which aggregation rules avoid it, and what do premise-based and conclusion-based procedures each cost? Finally, the question that nags me: when the two procedures diverge, does either express what the group thinks, or is the real lesson that "what the group thinks" needs to be retired?
