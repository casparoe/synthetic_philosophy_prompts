I cover physics and the philosophy of science for a general-audience magazine, and I am drafting a long-form essay on a dispute that has run all year through conference corridors and letters pages. The occasion is this: a wealthy foundation has announced a standing prize for the first experiment that measures the gauge potential of electromagnetism itself — not the field strength, not a force on a charge, not an interference fringe read off as a proxy, but the potential. Three camps have formed around whether the prize can in principle be won. The first says it is winnable and arguably won already: the Aharonov-Bohm phase shift shows something acting on electrons in a region where the magnetic field is zero, and the natural reading is that the potential is physically real and measurable. The second says the prize is unwinnable in principle: every observable, the Aharonov-Bohm phase included, is gauge-invariant, and what that experiment actually measures is a loop quantity, not the potential; the potential is surplus mathematical structure, as absolute velocity was in Newtonian mechanics, and a prize for detecting it is like a prize for detecting the ether. The third camp splits the difference: only loop phases are real, but those phases are non-local in a way that should make us give up the electromagnetic field as fundamental too.

Write the essay. It should represent all three positions seriously and fairly, and it must confront the methodological crux head-on: what is the test for whether a piece of a theory's formalism corresponds to something real? Physics has sometimes discarded unobservable structure (absolute simultaneity, the ether) and sometimes retained it; does empirical inaccessibility settle the question, and if not, what does? The essay should reach and defend a verdict — both on whether the foundation's prize can ever be awarded and on what follows, more broadly, about what is real in a gauge theory. Do not end on a shrug.
