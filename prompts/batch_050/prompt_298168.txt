In the opening books of the Laws, Plato has the Athenian stranger defend a practice modern readers often find startling. Against the Spartan and Cretan policy of prohibiting wine, he argues that because drink loosens shame and weakens self-command, the well-ordered city should actually stage supervised drinking festivals under the direction of a sober master of the feast. These serve, he claims, two purposes: they train citizens in self-mastery precisely where temptation is strongest, and they function as a safe, inexpensive test of character, exposing who can and cannot be trusted before real responsibility is at stake.

Take this position as given, and suppose Plato is confronted with two features of the present day. First, a wellness culture that treats total abstinence — dry months, lifelong sobriety as a badge of discipline — as the pinnacle of self-control. Second, the medicalized understanding of alcoholism as a chronic, substantially involuntary condition, to be met with treatment and harm reduction rather than blame or moral discipline.

Reconstruct his likely verdict on each, reasoning strictly from his premises: that virtue is habituated mastery over appetite, that the city rightly regulates pleasure, and that the voluntary surrender of reason is blameworthy. In particular: does the committed abstainer display the virtue he prizes, or merely remain untested by it? And can he make sense of drunkenness as involuntary without collapsing his framework — and what would follow for blame, and for who should be allowed near wine, if he granted it? Where his premises genuinely underdetermine an answer, say so rather than forcing a verdict, and resist flattening him into a slogan about moderation in all things.
