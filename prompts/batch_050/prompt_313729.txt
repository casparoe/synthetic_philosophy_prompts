I'm a retired forester who has been reading philosophy of biology for pleasure, and I keep running into a pattern I don't have a word for. I'll describe it with the three cases that made me notice it, because the pattern only shows up when you put cases side by side.

In my working life I cruised timber, and I once inventoried a stand of quaking aspen that a geneticist colleague later told me was, by his count, a single plant: one root system, one genotype, thousands of stems. Neither of us was wrong about any fact. We disagreed only about how many things were standing there, and we could not find a further fact to appeal to. Second case: the Portuguese man o' war. My zoology handbook calls it a colony of many zooids; a physiology text describes the same animal as one body whose parts happen to have once been capable of independence. Again, total agreement on the anatomy, disagreement on the count. Third: a beekeeper friend insists his apiary contains twenty hives, meaning twenty units he manages, treats, and bills for; his entomologist daughter counts hundreds of thousands of bees and considers the hive-level talk a convenience.

What strikes me is not the disagreement itself but its structure. Each person reaches for a test — descended from a single fertilized cell, or incapable of surviving apart, or unable to reproduce independently of the whole, or treated as one unit by the surrounding practice — and each test gives a clean answer. The trouble is that the tests disagree with each other, and, so far as I can tell, which test someone finds obviously decisive lines up suspiciously well with which variable their own discipline manipulates for a living. Worse, I catch myself switching tests mid-conversation depending on whether I'm thinking about inheritance, about disease, or about who can be harmed, without noticing the switch until afterwards.

So my question: does this configuration have an established name? I don't mean a name for the general problem of saying what an organism is — I know there is a literature on that, and I know some writers defend several legitimate notions of biological individual side by side, and others distinguish counting by ancestry from counting by functional independence. What I want named, if anything has named it, is the specific diagnostic pattern: a classification dispute that stays perfectly stable because each party's criterion is built into the explanatory questions of their own field, so that conceding the count would feel like conceding the discipline. It seems close to, but not identical with, pluralism about individuality — pluralism is a proposed remedy, not the phenomenon — and the ancestry-versus-function distinction covers only one axis of the switching I described.

If there is no established name, please say so plainly and coin one, with a definition sharp enough to distinguish this from ordinary ambiguity, from merely verbal dispute, and from healthy pluralism. In particular: is the right carve to say that "individual" has several senses and the disputants talk past each other, or that there is one concept whose application is genuinely question-relative, or that one of the tests is metaphysically basic and the others are practical shorthand? And as a boundary check: I notice what looks like the same pattern in disputes over what counts as one species and over what counts as one gene. If it's the same phenomenon, your definition should cover those; if it isn't, tell me what the difference is. A bare label without the carving won't satisfy me — I want to know where the edges of the thing are.
