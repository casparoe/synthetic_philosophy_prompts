So I'm doing a PhD in philosophy of maths and my supervisor has asked me to write down where I stand on constructivism before we start arguing about the thesis properly. Here it is, roughly:

1. I think the meaning of the logical constants is given by their proof conditions (the Brouwer-Heyting-Kolmogorov reading, roughly), and that's why classical logic is unjustified in general: nobody has, or could have, a proof of A or not-A for arbitrary A.
2. I'm perfectly happy using excluded middle whenever the domain is finite or the predicate at issue is decidable. Classically-flavoured reasoning about, say, finite graphs or propositional tautologies bothers me not at all.
3. I think Brouwer's real analysis -- choice sequences, bar induction, the continuity principles, all of it -- is the reals done properly, and the standard classical treatment is a sort of formal game that happens to be useful.
4. My main practical reason for preferring constructive proofs is that they're more informative: you can read off witnesses and algorithms. Classical mathematics is fine as far as it goes, just less useful -- not false, exactly.
5. I take the double-negation translation results (Gödel-Gentzen and friends) to show that classical arithmetic is safe: anything the classical mathematician proves can be recovered in Heyting arithmetic, so they were never really doing anything wrong.

Question: does this lot actually hang together? My suspicion is that at least one of these is committing me to more than I realise, and possibly two of them can't both be true, but I can't see where. Tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only look it, and for the real ones what's the cheapest revision -- where 'cheap' means I lose the least I care about, not what's most fashionable. Rank them by how bad the damage is rather than treating them all as equally urgent. And if one of the five is doing quiet work propping up two others in a way that would collapse if I dropped it, flag that too.
