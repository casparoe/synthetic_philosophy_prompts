I am a novelist at work on a drawing-room comedy, and I have run into a problem that is, I realize, really a problem in the philosophy of humour. I have a scene — a charity dinner at which my protagonist, a fastidious museum curator, must give a speech — and I want a single incident within it that will let me test my own instincts about why audiences laugh. What I would like you to construct for me is a thought experiment built to the following specifications: one event at this dinner (not a string of jokes, and nothing involving physical harm or humiliation so extreme that it stops being comedy) such that the incongruity theory of humour, taken strictly on its own, predicts no amusement — the event must involve no violated expectation or absurd juxtaposition of the sort that theory trades in — while the benign violation theory predicts genuine amusement, because the event transgresses some norm in a way the onlookers simultaneously register as permissible. The scene must be realistic enough to appear in literary fiction, described in one paragraph, and the violation must not depend on the audience disliking the victim, since I want superiority theory to be silent here too, not merely outvoted.

Once you have the scenario, demonstrate that it meets the specifications: state the verdict each of the four classical accounts — superiority, incongruity, relief, and benign violation — delivers on the incident and why, and flag any theory whose verdict is genuinely indeterminate rather than simply negative. Then identify which single feature of the scene is doing the work of separating incongruity from benign violation, and tell me what happens to each theory's verdict if that feature is removed or inverted. I would also like a companion variant of the same dinner in which a second event, equally realistic, produces amusement that relief theory predicts and benign violation theory cannot easily explain — my intuition is that nervous tension discharged as laughter at a memorial-adjacent moment is the natural home of this, but I will leave the engineering to you.

Finally, an honest postscript: tell me how a defender of the theory that comes out worst in your construction would attack the case — whether by denying the predicted verdict, denying that the theory was fairly characterized, or denying that your specification isolated what it claims to isolate. I intend to use the winning incident in the novel, so I would rather know its philosophical weak points now than discover them in a review.
