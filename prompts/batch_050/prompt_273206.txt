Having retired a few years ago from a career in population ecology, I have lately been reading philosophy, and I keep circling back to population axiology — the question of how to rank outcomes that differ in how many people will exist and how well their lives go. My interest is not idle: I want a standard by which to judge the long-horizon policy arguments one now hears about climate, extinction risk, and pronatalism, all of which turn on comparing populations of different sizes. The field, however, seems to proceed by proposing an axiology, exhibiting a ghastly consequence (the repugnant conclusion being only the most famous), and discarding it. I would rather have the specification before the candidates. So: write me the desiderata that any adequate theory of the value of a population must meet — the cases it must rank correctly, the distinctions it must respect, the intuitions it must either honor or argue us out of — and for each requirement give the case or consideration that motivates it and what we lose if we abandon it. I am aware the literature contains impossibility results suggesting that no axiology satisfies all the natural adequacy conditions simultaneously; if that is so, the list itself is the result I want, and I want it drawn so that the tensions are visible rather than papered over.

A few cases from my own thinking that I would like the list to engage, since the standard examples seem not to cover them. First, what I think of as echo worlds: imagine two futures identical in every detail, except that in the second every person who exists in the first has an exact counterpart living a duplicate life on a second, causally isolated Earth. Does doubling everything double the value? My physicist's instinct says a theory should have something principled to say about whether duplicate lives add value, and I notice most informal discussions never touch it. Second, what I call staggered endings: two futures contain exactly the same total person-years and the same distribution of welfare, but in one humanity dwindles gradually over two centuries while in the other it ends abruptly at its peak. Should a population axiology be indifferent between them, or is the temporal shape of a population's arc a distinct dimension of value? Third, cases where the size of a population feeds back into individual welfare — overcrowding on one side, loneliness and loss of collective projects on the other — so that the value of a life is not separable from how many others share the world. I would like to know which desiderata such cases bear on and whether respecting them costs anything elsewhere.

Please organize the answer as a requirements list rather than an essay: each desideratum stated precisely, its motivating case, and a verdict on whether it is genuinely non-negotiable or merely the prejudice of one camp. Where two or more requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, say so explicitly and indicate which is cheapest to relax. Distinguish, where relevant, between desiderata governing same-people choices and those governing choices that determine which people will exist at all, since I gather the non-identity problem makes the two come apart. If it helps, you may close with a brief indication of how the usual suspects — total utilitarianism, average utilitarianism, and critical-level views — fare against the list, but keep that part short; the criteria, and the reasons for them, are what I will actually be taking to my reading group.
