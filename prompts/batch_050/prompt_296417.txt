Write a decision-tree taxonomy of philosophical positions on whether torture could ever be justified, designed for a graduate ethics seminar. The tree's real work: most positions converge on the same verdict in the classic ticking-bomb case (a captured terrorist, a known live bomb, one hour left), so a tree that simply asks "would you torture here?" sorts almost nobody. Instead, build the branches from the underlying commitments that the shared verdict hides. Each branch node must be a question a student can answer without knowing which position it leads to — candidate splitters include whether torture's wrongness is agent-relative or outcome-based, whether it admits of being outweighed by sufficiently many lives or is a categorical side-constraint, whether the idealized epistemic certainty stipulated by the case may legitimately be granted at all or whether objecting to the stipulation is itself a substantive position, whether a one-off justified act and a standing institutional practice (torture warrants, trained interrogators, legalized exceptions) must be judged together, and whether a justified act can still leave the agent morally stained in a way that calls for punishment or apology afterward. Use three fixed probe cases to anchor the nodes: the classic bomb; the variant in which only torturing the suspect's innocent child will work; and a peacetime proposal for a regulated torture-warrant statute. Leaves should carry standard labels where they exist (absolute deontology, threshold deontology, act-consequentialist permission, dirty-hands/tragic-choice views, rule-consequentialist prohibition, and the position that rejects the case as a sophistical thought experiment altogether) and "unnamed — occupied?" where the combination of answers is coherent but has no standard defender. Note explicitly at least two pairs of positions your tree cannot separate by their answers alone and say what further question would split them. Keep the tree to no more than seven branch nodes.
