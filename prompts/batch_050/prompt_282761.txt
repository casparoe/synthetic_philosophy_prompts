So I'm giving a talk in about ten days at a small interdisciplinary workshop on mass violence -- audience is mostly social psychologists in the Milgram/Zimbardo lineage, plus two Holocaust historians, plus (annoyingly for me) one person who does the situationist-vs-dispositionist critique stuff (the Doris/Harman-adjacent end of things). My talk defends a slightly heterodox take on the Browning-Goldhagen fight over Reserve Police Battalion 101: I argue the whole "situation vs. eliminationist antisemitism" framing was a false fork, because both sides underweight what I call off-ramps -- the fact that Major Trapp explicitly offered the men a chance to step out before Józefów, only a handful took it at first, but refusals got easier and less punished as the killings went on. My claim is that the causal action is in the *sequence*: the first killing happens under peer-pressure-plus-ambiguity conditions (Browning's territory), but what sustains participation is that refusing early feels like a costly public deviation while refusing late becomes normalized -- so the explanatory target should be the choice architecture of escalation, not a static verdict about the men's prior character or the situation's power. I think this dissolves most of the debate and annoys both camps equally.
