In the Pasadena paradox (a variant on the St. Petersburg problem), a game offers payoffs that alternate between gains and losses while growing without bound—roughly, round k pays a prize whose magnitude is on the order of 2^k/k dollars, positive on odd rounds and negative on even rounds, with round k reached with probability 2^-k. Unlike the St. Petersburg game, whose expected value is infinite, this game's expected-value series is only conditionally convergent: rearranging the order of the terms makes it sum to any value whatsoever, so the game has no well-defined expectation at all. Please describe the most important implications of this for how I should approach real decisions involving improbable but enormous gains and losses—evaluating speculative career gambles, insurance against catastrophic risks, or charitable interventions with tiny chances of transformative impact. In particular: when the expected-value machinery fails to deliver a determinate answer, what should guide choice instead, and does the paradox undermine expected-utility reasoning as such or only expose its limits at the extremes?
