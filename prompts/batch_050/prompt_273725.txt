So I'm a novelist, mid-draft on a book about a traffic engineer in the 1960s who slowly realizes his freeway designs are doing political work he never signed off on, and as research I've been rereading Le Guin's The Dispossessed alongside Langdon Winner's old essay about whether artifacts have politics. Winner's big example is Robert Moses allegedly building the parkway bridges to Jones Beach too low for buses, so poor people couldn't get to the beach -- but I gather that story is actually contested as history (the bridge heights were standard, buses could get there other ways, etc.). I want a careful reading of The Dispossessed through Winner's lens that I can steal from (with a clear conscience, in the way novelists steal). Specifically: Winner distinguishes technologies that are political because of how someone arranged them from technologies that seem inherently political, demanding a certain kind of social order just to function at all. Run the novel's artifacts and designs through that distinction: the wall in the famous opening that looks like it encloses everything outside it, the port walls that are the only legal point of contact between Anarres and Urras, the constructed language Pravic with no possessives, the labor rotation and posting system, the absence of cars, and the ansible Shevek builds, which is a piece of physics made deliberately so that no society can wall itself off again. Where does Le Guin genuinely dramatize artifacts carrying politics baked in, and where is she actually showing the opposite -- people and politics choosing what the artifacts mean, so the tech is more neutral than Winner allows? Be honest about where the novel resists the reading; I don't want book-report enthusiasm. Also: does it matter to the philosophical claim (and to my novel) if the Moses bridges story is a myth -- does Winner's point survive his best example being shaky? End with what the novel gives the determinism debate that a straight philosophical argument can't, and give me a one-line verdict I can act on: \answer{...} saying whether Le Guin ultimately comes out closer to technological determinism, to "society chooses its tools," or to some third thing, named in a few words.
