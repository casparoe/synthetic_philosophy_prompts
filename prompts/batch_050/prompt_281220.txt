I am a senior analyst at a foreign-policy think tank, and next month I am giving the opening talk at a one-day colloquium hosted by a university history department on the theme of usable pasts. I want a Q&A prep sheet.

My thesis is deliberately unwelcome for this room: historical analogy is an unavoidable instrument of practical reasoning in foreign policy — every policy choice is made under some framing of what the situation is a case of — so the scholarly injunction to simply stop reasoning analogically is unmeetable advice. The live question is whether analogical reasoning can be made corrigible, and I will propose a procedure I call an analogy audit: (1) a similarity ledger listing the respects of similarity and difference between the source episode and the present case, explicitly separating structural features (capabilities, incentives, constraints) from surface ones (imagery, personalities, rhetoric); (2) a provenance check asking why this analogy came to hand rather than rivals, and what institutional or biographical availability explains its selection; (3) a track-record check enumerating notable past invocations of the same analogy — Munich's long afterlife from 1956 onward, the domino framing running from Korea through Vietnam — and assessing, as far as the record allows, whether acting on it went well or badly; (4) a red-teamed statement of the strongest case that the disanalogies dominate; and (5) a standing rule for when the audit should counsel setting the analogy aside entirely.

The audience: roughly fifteen diplomatic historians whose disciplinary default is the particularity of events and who regard analogical policy reasoning as the way officials launder presentism; two international-relations political scientists, one of whom has published skeptically on the Thucydides's-trap framing of rising-power conflict; a classicist who works on Greek historiography; and a few historically inclined journalists. The historians know the political-science literature arguing that policymakers use analogies to diagnose situations, define stakes, and generate prescriptions — and largely read it as a chronicle of error.

Please prepare the following:

1. The eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience — not the ones I would get at a security-studies conference — ordered by expected damage, with likelihood noted. Attribute each to the segment of the audience most likely to raise it, and phrase it the way they would phrase it, in their idiom.
2. For each objection: a reply deliverable in about two minutes, plus a candid note on where that reply is soft or question-begging.
3. What I should concede in the talk itself rather than fight over in Q&A — my sense is that the analogy-selection step is the least disciplinable part of the audit and that the track-record check leans on counterfactuals no one can settle, but tell me if I am conceding too much or too little.
4. The question I am hoping nobody asks, and why.

Finally, be self-critical about the prep sheet itself. Tell me which of your predicted objections you are least confident this particular audience will raise, what additional information about them would most change your list or its ordering, and where your advice on how to reply is most likely to fail once I am actually in the room.
