I hold the view that there is no shared phenomenal nature common to all pleasures — that the pleasure of eating ripe mango, the pleasure of finally understanding a proof, the relief when a headache lifts, and the glow of hearing good news about a friend do not feel alike in any respect that makes them pleasures, and that what unifies them is instead something non-experiential: roughly, that each is a state its subject is disposed to welcome, want to continue, or take a positive attitude toward for its own sake. On my view, "pleasure" names a family held together by this attitudinal or motivational profile, not a natural kind marked by a single felt quality, and any introspective impression that all pleasures share a distinctive glow is a confabulation or an artifact of lumping.

What would count against this position? Please map its exposure. I would like, first, the strongest arguments and evidence that should move someone who holds it — introspective or phenomenological considerations, empirical findings (for instance, anything suggesting a common neural or computational currency for positive affect across domains), linguistic and developmental considerations, and formal or theoretical arguments — sorted by how decisive they would be, distinguishing what would sink the view outright from what would merely be awkward for it. Second, I want the cases the view handles worst: are there experiences that are plainly pleasant yet not welcomed, wanted, or attended to positively, or states that are welcomed without being pleasant, and how damaging is each? Third, please distinguish what opponents of the view take to count against it from what its own defenders would have to concede as genuine costs or retreat points. Fourth, consider whether the attitudinal unification I rely on quietly smuggles the phenomenal commonality back in, or conversely whether my view collapses into the claim that pleasure is simply whatever plays a certain functional role, and if so whether that is a refutation or merely a relabeling. Finally, give an honest verdict on whether the position as I have stated it risks anything at all — whether "no shared felt quality, only a shared attitude" has been so framed that no introspective or empirical result could trouble it — and if it is unfalsifiable in practice, whether that is a serious defect for a view of this kind or just what one should expect from a thesis about the structure of experience.
