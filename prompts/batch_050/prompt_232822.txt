I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm preparing a long feature on the debate over releasing the weights of frontier AI models. My editors want a piece that doesn't just tally familiar talking points but shows what the two serious camps actually agree on and where their disagreement really lives. Rather than interview summaries, I want to present my readers with the document the two sides would produce if they had to do an adversarial collaboration, and I'd like you to draft that document for me.

Here is the setup I'd like you to work with. The two camps are people I call the Proliferation Pessimists and the Decentralization Optimists. The Pessimists hold that once weights for a frontier-capability model are released, misuse by bad actors and gradual erosion of safety mitigations are effectively irreversible, and that no foreseeable defensive benefit justifies that risk at the frontier. The Optimists hold that concentrating frontier capability in a handful of firms and governments is itself a catastrophic-risk pathway -- through power concentration, security monocultures, and the inability of independent researchers to audit, red-team, and build defenses -- and that open release is the only credible counterweight. Both camps claim the mantle of catastrophic-risk reduction; neither is commercially motivated in the framing I want.

To keep the collaboration honest and away from stock arguments, I want the document organized around three specific mechanisms rather than vague appeals. First, the fine-tuning asymmetry: the claim that releasing weights, unlike offering API access, permits anyone to strip or retrain away safety behaviors, and that this difference -- not raw capability -- is the real policy variable. Second, what I'll call the threshold question: whether the two camps actually disagree about principle or only about where on a capability ladder the calculus flips, i.e., whether both would sign a statement that some models are safe to open and some are not, with the fight being about how to locate and verify the boundary. Third, the defense-offense balance: the empirical question of whether open weights in a given domain (say, bioinformatics or cyber) accelerate defensive work by legitimate actors more than they lower the cost of attack, and what evidence would settle it.

The deliverable should have three parts:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims -- statements both camps can sign without reservation. These should be substantive, not platitudes: claims about the irreversibility of weight release, the limits of current evaluations for dangerous capabilities, the real costs of concentration, the track record of independent security research, and whatever else genuinely survives scrutiny from both sides.

2. A disagreements table with three columns: the precise point of disagreement phrased neutrally; the Pessimists' position in wording they would accept; the Optimists' position in wording they would accept. Take care that each side's wording doesn't caricature the other -- no attributing indifference to misuse to the Optimists or indifference to authoritarian concentration to the Pessimists.

3. A cruxes section: for each of the three mechanisms above, state the sub-question such that resolving it would move at least one camp, the concrete evidence, observation, or track record both camps agree bears on it, and each camp's advance prediction about what that evidence shows. If you conclude that part of the dispute is verbal -- for instance, that the camps use 'open' or 'frontier' differently -- identify which part and argue the diagnosis.

Keep the tone of the document itself neutral and institutional, as if it will be published jointly. Aim for something I could excerpt directly in the feature.
