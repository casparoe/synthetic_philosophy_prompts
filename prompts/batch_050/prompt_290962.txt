Two friends of mine, Mara and Jonas, both volunteer for the economics department's paid experiment pool, and last week they participated in a session of the centipede game: a pot starting at two dollars doubled with every pass, up to six rounds, and whichever player chose to take ended the game, keeping a larger share for themselves while the other got the remainder. Over dozens of rounds in the session, almost nobody took at the first node, and most games went three or four passes deep. On the walk home, Jonas said this just showed how irrational the average participant is. Mara said he had it exactly backwards, and they argued the whole way. I stayed out of it, but I've been turning it over since, and I'd like a proper refereeing of the exchange, which went roughly as follows.

Jonas opened with the standard argument: the player who moves last strictly prefers taking to passing, the player who moves second-to-last knows this and so takes there, and so on back to the first node, so the only defensible play is to take immediately, and anyone who passed was leaving guaranteed money on the table. Mara replied that this reasoning is self-undermining: the backward induction only bites if you can go on treating your opponent as rational no matter what they do, but an opponent passing at the first node is precisely the move the theory says a rational opponent never makes, so once Jonas actually passed against her, she had direct evidence that he wasn't playing the equilibrium, and continuing to pass on her turn was rational given that evidence. Jonas answered that this cuts both ways and ultimately worse for her: if passing signals generosity or confusion, then a rational player has every reason to fake it by passing early to build trust and taking late, but if everyone knows that passing might be strategic mimicry, then passing conveys no information at all, and the argument unravels right back to taking at the first node. Mara's final move was that his instability argument proves too much: it implies that no observation of an opponent's play could ever rationally change your beliefs, which is absurd, and that refusing to update after watching someone pass three times in a row isn't sophistication but dogmatism; besides, she added, a theory that says certain nodes are never reached is in no position to condemn the decisions people make when they are reached.

Please go through the four moves in order and tell me who is right about which specific point — not just a verdict on the exchange as a whole, but an assessment of each step, including whether Mara's last two points are as strong as she thinks and whether Jonas's unraveling argument really closes the loop. If there is something both of them are missing about what backward induction does and doesn't assume, or about how beliefs should behave at off-equilibrium nodes, please say so. And if I've garbled the structure of the game or misstated what the standard analysis actually concludes, I'd rather you correct me plainly than grade a confused version of their dispute.
