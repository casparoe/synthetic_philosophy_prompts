I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-audience magazine. I recently reported a piece on prediction markets, and while fact-checking it I kept tripping over a remark from a philosopher of probability I interviewed: that the rational case for having probabilistically coherent degrees of belief ultimately rests on Dutch book considerations. The more I read around it, the less clear it became to me what that argument actually establishes — whether it shows incoherence is a financial liability, a logical defect, or an epistemic failing, and the sources I consulted seemed to slide between these. As part of an education series the magazine runs with a nearby university, I've agreed to contribute one essay question to the final exam of an upper-level undergraduate seminar on formal epistemology, and I'd like your help drafting it.

Please write me one exam question on Dutch book arguments, answerable in 75 minutes, that cannot be answered by reciting the slogan that incoherent credences expose you to a guaranteed loss. The question should force candidates to confront at least three things: first, the bridge principle connecting credences to betting behavior (the Ramsey–de Finetti operationalization of degree of belief as fair betting price) and what must be assumed for it to work — e.g., that willingness to bet is constitutive of credence rather than merely evidence of it, and what happens to the argument if agents can decline bets or assign value non-linearly. Second, the difference between the synchronic Dutch book for probabilism and the diachronic Dutch book for conditionalization, and why the latter is generally thought to need an extra premise about how the agent plans to update. Third, the Kyburg-style objection that avoiding sure loss is a pragmatic virtue rather than an epistemic one, and what a defender of the argument must say to get from "incoherent agents can be moneypumped" to "incoherence is a defect of rationality" without simply begging the question.

Alongside the question, give me: a model answer of no more than 500 words; and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, worth a total of 20 marks, where each point comes with its mark allocation and a one-line note on the characteristic way a weaker candidate fumbles that point (for instance, treating the diachronic argument as if it were just the synchronic one applied over time, or assuming the betting operationalization is uncontroversial). I'd also like a brief closing note — two or three sentences — stating what specifically separates an A answer from a B answer, since the course convener will moderate borderline scripts. Please keep the entire response under 900 words.
