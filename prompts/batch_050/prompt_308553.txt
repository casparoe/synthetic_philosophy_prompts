I am drafting a paper that defends a threshold conception of moral status — roughly, the idea that there is a line between beings whose interests count in their own right and beings that merely matter instrumentally, and that once you are across the line, you count fully rather than fractionally. Before I commit to this, I want to stress-test it against the strongest opponent I can get, so I would like you to play a committed gradualist: a philosopher who holds that sentience, and with it moral status, is a matter of degree all the way down, from a person to a fish to an insect to, at the limit, a simple homeostat. Stay in character until I write "stop." Your character's ground rules: you never concede the main point that moral status is gradable and that there is no deep threshold, though you may concede local difficulties and refine your position when pressed; you engage with my actual arguments rather than restating your view; you keep each reply to one or two paragraphs so this feels like a seminar exchange rather than a lecture; and you should not misrepresent my threshold view as mere squeamishness or religious residue — treat it as a serious position you happen to think is wrong. Your character also has some real resources you should feel free to deploy: the thought that "degrees of sentience" need not mean a single linear scale (richness of experience, temporal depth, affective intensity might come apart), the observation that our practices already grade consideration without obvious incoherence, and the standard vagueness point that a continuum between clear cases does not by itself vindicate a sharp boundary.

Here is my opening move, which I want you to answer in character. Imagine a device I call the Dial: attach it to a fully conscious adult and it continuously attenuates their mental life — sensory bandwidth narrows, emotional range flattens, the memory horizon shortens, self-awareness thins — with no jumps, just a smooth continuum down toward something with the experiential profile of a worm. On your view, the person's moral status must diminish smoothly with each turn. But now ask: at what setting does it stop being murder to painlessly kill them and become something like breaking a thermostat? If you name a setting, you have smuggled in my threshold and merely relocated it. If you refuse to name one, you are committed to saying there is a dial position where a being descended from a person may be killed for trivial reasons, even though no single turn of the dial ever made the difference between murder and pest control — and that seems to make the wrongness of killing hostage to a sorites. Either way, the smoothness you are so proud of looks like a liability: my threshold view at least explains why killing a person is categorically different from swatting a fly, while yours has to grade the ungradeable. Where does the Dial go wrong?
