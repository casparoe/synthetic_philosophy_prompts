I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm writing a column on the 2006 International Astronomical Union vote that reclassified Pluto as a dwarf planet — the "cleared the neighborhood" criterion, the immediate public backlash, and the continuing dissent of a faction of planetary scientists nearly two decades on. I don't want to rehash the astronomy, which my readers have seen. I want to use the episode as a test case for Wittgenstein's account of family-resemblance concepts and language games: the idea that terms like "planet" may hold together through overlapping similarities rather than a common essence, and that meaning lives in use within a practice rather than in any definition a body can vote into being.

Please analyze the episode through that lens, in both directions. On the theory-illuminates-case side: does the Wittgensteinian picture explain why no empirical discovery could have settled the dispute, why the vote felt to many like a category mistake (a committee legislating about a word that belongs to everyone), and why the public's usage simply refused to update — is that refusal evidence for meaning-as-use, or just sentimentality? On the case-tests-theory side: does the episode strain the picture? Astronomers would say they weren't legislating arbitrarily but precisifying a fuzzy term the way sciences routinely do (compare "species" or "element"), and that the dynamical criterion tracks something real about how solar systems form — is that an improvement within a practice, which the family-resemblance view can accommodate, or a revelation that the concept had a discoverable structure all along? Also consider the counterfactual: had the IAU instead adopted a geophysical definition that made round moons planets too, would the picture predict more or less resistance, and does your answer to that sharpen or blur the theory? Be disciplined about where the lens stops working rather than forcing it to speak; if the dispute is, on this view, partly verbal and partly not, say which parts.

Structure the piece as follows, roughly 1,300 words total:

**The Episode, Briefly** (~150 words): the facts a reader needs, no more.
**The Wittgensteinian Reading** (~300 words): what the lens says the IAU was doing and what the disputants were actually disagreeing about.
**Where It Fits Best** (~250 words): the features of the episode the reading explains better than a "science corrects language" story does.
**Where It Strains** (~250 words): the strongest objection — the precisification reply — taken seriously, plus the counterfactual above.
**What Pluto Does to the Theory** (~250 words): does this episode confirm family resemblance, expose a blind spot about institutional definition-making, or show the view was never about cases like this?
**Judgment for the Column** (~100 words): the bottom line I'd print.
