The tempo theory of virtù

Everyone reads chapter 25 of The Prince as being about luck. Machiavelli compares fortune to a flooding river: you can't stop the rain, but you can build dykes and ditches while the weather's calm, so the flood does less damage. The standard classroom reading is: half of what happens to us is fortune, half is down to us, so prepare in advance.

I think that misses the interesting bit. My thesis: for Machiavelli, fortune is less like weather and more like tempo. What ruins rulers isn't random bad luck, it's a mismatch between their personal tempo — cautious or impulsive, patient or aggressive — and the tempo of the times they're ruling in. Machiavelli points out that some men succeed by being impetuous and others by being cautious, and that nobody stays on top for long because nobody can change their own nature when the times change around them. So virtù, on my reading, isn't skill or boldness in general. It's the extremely rare ability to retune yourself — to genuinely become a different kind of person when circumstances shift. Almost nobody has it, which is why almost nobody stays fortunate.

My carrying example is Cesare Borgia. Machiavelli clearly admires him: he built his dykes, dealt with his enemies, thought several moves ahead — and he still got destroyed when his father the Pope died at exactly the wrong moment, while Cesare himself was seriously ill. I'm going to argue this proves my thesis: Cesare couldn't retune fast enough when the tempo changed.
