I have spent several years defending illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, and I want to stress-test it rather than collect more ammunition for it. The view, as I hold it, is the strong one: there are no phenomenal properties — no intrinsic, ineffable, private "what-it's-like" features of experience. Introspection represents our brain states as having such properties, but that representation is systematically misleading, produced by mechanisms that give us only partial, schematic access to our own processing. On this picture the so-called hard problem is not solved but dissolved: the real task is the meta-problem of explaining why creatures like us find it irresistible that they are phenomenally conscious, and why the intuition survives even in people who deny it is true. I am not asking for the standard rejoinders — the incredulous stare, the bare insistence that one cannot be wrong that anything seems any way at all. I want a genuine map of the view's exposure: what would actually count against it, and how much.

Please structure your answer in the following sections, in this order:

**The commitments that do the work** (about 200 words): State precisely which claims are load-bearing — for instance, that introspective seemings are representational states that can occur without the properties they represent, and that a complete functional account of our judgments about consciousness would leave nothing unexplained. Flag any place where my statement of the view quietly smuggles in the very thing it denies.

**Decisive defeaters** (about 300 words): Arguments, results, or developments that would sink the view outright if established. For each, say what the world would have to look like for it to materialize, and whether anything in current philosophy or science of consciousness is even arguably pointing that way. Rank them by how fatal they would be.

**Merely awkward pressure points** (about 250 words): Objections the view can absorb but at a cost — for example, the worry that the illusion itself must seem some way and so reintroduces phenomenal character at one remove, or that the view cannot say what the misrepresentation is a misrepresentation of. For each, say what the illusionist's cheapest honest response is and what it concedes.

**What defenders concede versus what they dismiss** (about 200 words): Which of the above would a committed illusionist accept as real costs, and which would they wave away — and is the waving-away principled or protective?

**The falsifiability audit** (about 200 words): Has the position, through its qualifications, been rendered incapable of losing? If so, say whether that is fatal for a view of this kind, given that its rivals are arguably in the same position.

**My bottom line** (about 100 words): The single consideration I should lose the most sleep over, and the one I can safely set aside.
