At my university there are two ethics committees for research. Anything with human subjects goes to the IRB, and the rules there are basically absolute: every participant has to give informed consent, there's a hard cap on acceptable risk, and you can never justify hurting one volunteer by pointing to how much the knowledge would benefit others. No amount of scientific value overrides a refusal. Animal research goes to a different commitee, and the logic there is basically cost-benefit: how much will the animals suffer, how important is the question, could you answer it with fewer animals or less pain. If the expected benefit is big enough, procedures causing real suffering get approved, and obviously nobody asks the animals to consent to anything.

So: same university, same stated goal of ethical research, two completely different logics. What moral theory does this two-track setup actually presuppose? The obvious reading is something like the combo Robert Nozick once floated, roughly utilitarian reasoning for animals and Kantian-style inviolability for people, where animals count in the calculus but hold no rights against being traded off. But I want to know whether the practice really needs that specific combination. Could you defend the animal track while holding that animals have genuine rights? Could you defend the human track while holding that animals matter just as much as humans, by leaning on something else, like the fact that adults can understand a protocol and rats can't? And if the two committees' logics do presuppose different theories of moral standing, is that a genuine inconsistency in the institution, or can one coherent theory generate both rulebooks? Finally, what would each track actually have to look like if we dropped the asymmetry in one direction, extending consent-style side constraints to animals, or in the other, letting the IRB trade off human welfare when the benefits get big enough?
