I was recently on a long train ride with an old friend who edits a popular science-and-ideas magazine, and we got onto the topic of truth after an argument about whether anything is really true in politics. He mentioned a piece his magazine had just run, and later sent me the passage. It summarizes a philosophical position called deflationism about truth as follows: "Deflationists hold that truth is not a real property at all. Saying that a claim is true adds nothing to simply making the claim: to say 'it is true that water boils at 100 degrees' is just to say, with extra ceremony, that water boils at 100 degrees. The word 'true' survives in our language mainly as a device of emphasis and generalization, letting us endorse things like 'everything the witness said was true' without repeating them. It follows that there is no deep fact of the matter about which beliefs are true; truth is whatever a community's conversational conventions let it get away with calling true, and the age-old philosophical quest for a theory of truth — correspondence to reality, coherence, pragmatic usefulness — was a wild goose chase that deflationism has now shown to be based on a grammatical confusion. This also explains why deflationists think debates about objective truth in science and ethics are empty: since 'true' means no more than 'I agree,' nothing objective hangs on it."

Something in this bothers me, though I can't locate the problem precisely. The core observation — that calling something true seems to add little beyond asserting it — strikes me as genuinely clever, and I gather it traces back to Frank Ramsey in the 1920s. But the passage then seems to sprint from "truth is a thin notion" to "there is no objective truth," and my instinct is that a careful deflationist would refuse that inference: if saying "snow is white is true" really is equivalent to saying snow is white, then snow's being white had better be an objective matter, and the thinness of the word would not make the world thin. I also dimly remember an older objection, usually credited to Frege, that tries to show truth cannot be defined at all because any proposed definition would already presuppose truth when applied — and I am unsure whether that argument, if sound, would rescue the very "deep" theories the passage dismisses, or whether deflationists have a standard reply to it that the passage should have mentioned.
