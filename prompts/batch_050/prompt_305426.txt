I am a doctoral student in economics, and my dissertation concerns the normative foundations of financial regulation -- in particular, the reasoning by which regulators, consumer advisers and the textbooks they draw on decide which transactions count as prudent. The case I keep returning to is ordinary insurance. Consumer guidance in most countries treats buying insurance at an actuarially unfair premium -- that is, paying more than the expected value of the payout -- as the mark of a sensible person, and regulators go further still, mandating certain covers outright and requiring insurers themselves to hold capital against tails. The canonical explanation I was taught invokes diminishing marginal utility of wealth: the certain loss of the premium hurts less than the small chance of a catastrophic loss, so both parties to the contract can gain. Yet the very same frameworks evaluate other products, from lottery tickets to leveraged investments, largely by their expected monetary value, and warn consumers away from anything whose expectation is negative. What puzzles me is that the justification for the insurance rule seems to live in the shape of a utility function, while the justification for the warnings seems to live in the arithmetic of expectations, and I cannot tell whether these are one theory or two.

The puzzle sharpened for me after a seminar in which a colleague presented the following line of argument. Consider a multiplicative gamble offered repeatedly: each round, your wealth either rises by fifty per cent or falls by forty per cent, with equal probability. The ensemble expectation across parallel copies of you is positive, yet the time-average growth rate along any single long trajectory is negative, since a gain of fifty per cent does not undo a loss of forty per cent. On this view, the individual insured and the insurance pool are not in the same epistemic position at all: the pool genuinely samples the ensemble, while the individual lives exactly one trajectory, and so both can rationally gain from an unfair premium without any appeal to the curvature of anyone's utility function. If that is right, then the standard explanation of why insurance is prudent may be not merely incomplete but unnecessary, and the expected-value warnings may rest on a conflation of two averages.

My question is about the practice rather than the mathematics. What does the existing pattern of insurance regulation and consumer advice -- prudent insurance bought at unfair premiums, expected-value tests applied elsewhere, mandatory cover and capital requirements -- presuppose about which average is normatively relevant to an individual's choices, and about whether the rationality of a choice must be explained through the chooser's preferences over outcomes at all? Does the practice strictly presuppose expected-utility reasoning, or does it merely sit comfortably with it while remaining available to a rival foundation? And if one rebuilt this regulatory apparatus on the time-average picture instead, which elements would survive unchanged, and which would have to be rethought -- for instance, whether the very wealthy should be advised to self-insure, or whether the standard warnings against leverage survive? Please write your answer in British English.
