Take the Snowden leaks in 2013 and run them through the ethics of loyalty. The basic facts I take as given: he was an NSA contractor, swore to protect classified info, concluded the bulk surveillance programs were unconstitutional, went to journalists instead of internal channels, and ended up in exile while former colleagues called him a traitor and others called him the most loyal public servant around. What I want is the loyalty analysis done carefully. Who are the objects of loyalty actually in play -- the agency, the oath, the Constitution, his coworkers whose safety he arguably put at risk, the public? Does the common move of saying he was loyal to the agency's *mission* rather than the agency do real work, or is it just redefining loyalty so the whistleblower always wins? Use something like Royce's idea of loyalty to loyalty if it helps, or whatever framework you think fits better. Then the other direction: does this episode expose a problem in loyalty-based ethics generally -- like that it has no principled way to rank competing loyalties, so verdicts about whistleblowers end up tracking which object you started with? And where does the analysis break: is there anything about the case (the fleeing, the scope of what he took) that loyalty talk just can't process?
