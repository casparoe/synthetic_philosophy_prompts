I am a novelist by trade, and I find myself in an unlikely public role. Nine years ago I published a historical novel about the 1931 riverfront clearances in my home city of Aldgate: the council of that era condemned a working-class district of some four hundred households, most of them immigrant families, under a slum-clearance ordinance, demolished it, and sold the land to a private developer at a price the minutes describe as favorable to the buyer. Compensation to residents ran well below assessed value, several aldermen held undisclosed interests in the development company, and the city's own health inspector had certified the district sound the year before. My novel was why the mayor appointed me to the Historical Truth and Commemoration Commission, a nine-member body of historians, two descendants of displaced families, the city attorney, a member of the clergy, and myself. Our remit is narrow: to advise the current city council on whether Aldgate should issue a formal apology for the clearances ahead of the centenary.

I have been asked to draft the commission's written opinion, and I want help producing a document of genuine argumentative quality rather than ceremonial prose. The opinion should recommend one of three courses — issue the apology, decline, or issue it conditionally — and give the reasoning a skeptical councilor could engage with. It must wrestle, at minimum, with the following: whether a council can meaningfully apologize for the acts of predecessors none of whose members survive, and what makes an institution the kind of thing that persists through such wrongdoing; who, if anyone, is positioned to receive or refuse an apology on behalf of the dead, given that the descendants' association is itself divided, its older leadership favoring an apology paired with a memorial endowment while a younger caucus argues that an apology without restitution would let the city close the matter cheaply and should be rejected; and whether an apology drafted, as the city attorney insists, to disclaim any legal liability still counts as an apology at all, or whether the disclaimer defeats the act it accompanies. The opinion should state what a genuine apology minimally requires, test each requirement against Aldgate's situation, and give an honest, unstrawmanned account of the strongest case for declining — I expect that case to come from the younger caucus rather than from council fiscal conservatives, though the fiscal worry that an apology will be entered as evidence in the descendants' pending title suits deserves fair treatment too. Whatever course is recommended, the opinion should specify the accompanying actions or wording constraints the recommendation depends on.

Roughly twelve hundred words, addressed to the commission, written so it could be forwarded to the council unchanged. After the opinion, append a JSON object with exactly these fields: "recommendation", one of the strings "apologize", "decline", or "conditional"; "conditions", an array of strings naming each condition the recommendation depends on, or an empty array if none; "strongest_objection", a string summarizing the most serious consideration against the recommendation; "response_to_objection", a string giving the opinion's answer to it; and "risks", an array of objects each with a "risk" field and a "mitigation" field. Please work only from the facts as I have stated them and from general reasoning; do not invoke specific real-world apologies, statutes, or scholarly sources.
