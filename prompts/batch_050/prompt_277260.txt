I've been reading a pop-physics book that tackles Maxwell's demon (the wee gatekeeper Maxwell imagined in Theory of Heat, sorting fast and slow molecules through a trapdoor). The book's way of dissolving the paradox is an analogy it leans on constantly: the demon is like an office clerk with a finite stack of notepaper. Watching the molecules and opening the door is free, just like reading and jotting things down is free; the catch is that the clerk eventually runs out of paper and has to shred his old notes, and it's the shredding that heats up the office. The book says this is exactly what Landauer's principle establishes -- erasing information is what costs you, not measuring -- and concludes that the second law was never in any real danger and that the whole 'physics of information' framing tidily closes the case.

My physicist friend thinks the clerk analogy is doing all the work and that the tidy conclusion doesn't survive contact with the details. Can you audit it for me? Give me the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the notepaper, the jotting, the shredding, the heating of the office -- then where the mapping breaks. In particular: is physical destruction of notes really the analogue of logically irreversible erasure, or does that conflate two different things? Does the analogy smuggle in the assumption that measurement is thermodynamically free, which I gather is itself contested? And does the argument that erasure must cost kT ln 2 quietly presuppose the very second law it's meant to be defending, as some critics claim? Finally: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the book's conclusion -- paradox fully dissolved, nothing interesting left -- actually follow, or is there a better analogy for whatever residual puzzle remains?
