So I'm a retired marine biologist and about six months ago I fell down the philosophy rabbit hole, and the thing currently bugging me is the lying versus misleading distinction. Everyone seems to think lying is worse, but when I actually test my gut on cases it comes out all over the place. Here are my verdicts, and I want to know what principle they add up to, if any.

1. An angry ex-boyfriend with a history of violence knocks on my door and asks where his ex-girlfriend is. I know exactly where she is. I say "I have no idea." Verdict: completely fine, I'd do it again.

2. Same guy, but instead I say, truthfully, "She was at her sister's place all last weekend," knowing he'll conclude she's still there. She isn't. Verdict: also completely fine. Honestly this one feels maybe even cleaner than #1, which is where the trouble starts.

3. A car buyer asks the seller "any engine trouble?" The seller says "I drove it three thousand miles last month without a single problem," which is true, but he knows the transmission is about to die and the buyer's question was obviously about the car's condition generally. Verdict: that's wrong, clearly wrong, refund-the-money wrong.

4. A job candidate corners the interviewer at the end and asks "Am I going to get the job?" The interviewer says "You were a genuinely strong candidate," which is true, but the decision's already made and it's a no. Verdict: totally fine, maybe even kind.

5. My wife asks where I was last night. I say "I was at the office until nine." True. Then I went to a bar until midnight with an old flame, nothing happened but I know my wife would want to know. Verdict: wrong, and here's the kicker -- if I had instead said "I worked late" flat out, no truth in it, my gut says that's about equally wrong, maybe a hair worse but not much.

6. But then when I introspect in the abstract, away from cases, I still have this stubborn feeling that lying is specially bad -- that if someone lied to my face I'd be more betrayed than if they misled me with true words, other things equal. Kant-style, the lie feels like it does something worse to the relationship.

So: 1 and 2 say lying and misleading are morally on a par when the cause is good. 3 and 4 say two structurally identical misleading-with-true-words moves differ wildly. 5 says the lying/misleading line does almost no moral work. And 6 says the line does real moral work. My own tentative principle was something like: what matters is whether you intend the listener to walk away with a false belief that's relevant to their decision, not whether the words were literally true. But I'm pretty sure that principle blesses case 3, which my gut condemns, so something's off.

Test my principle against these six cases and repair it, or scrap it and give me a better one. State the principle precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and tell me honestly which of my six verdicts it can't save -- I suspect 6 has to go but I'm attached to it. Then tell me what your principle predicts for two cases I didn't list: a doctor who answers a scared patient's "should I be worried?" with the true statement "this is a routine procedure," knowing this particular patient has a rare risk factor; and a kid who tells his mom "I finished my homework" because he defines finished as "wrote something on every question." Please write in American English, casual is fine.

At the very end, give me the repaired principle in one sentence, on its own last line in the form \answer{...}.
