I'm preparing a one-page position statement for a political theory workshop where each participant submits their settled views on freedom and the others spend the session trying to show that the statement fails to cohere. I want to stress-test mine before I hand it in. Here is what I plan to submit. (i) The core meaning of freedom is the absence of interference by other agents; whether an interference is justified is a separate question, so even justified interference diminishes freedom, however rightly it is imposed. (ii) A slave whose master is benign and never in fact interferes is unfree anyway: mere exposure to another's arbitrary will constitutes unfreedom regardless of how that will happens to be exercised. (iii) I share the standard warning, associated with Isaiah Berlin, against positive liberty: any conception on which coercing people could count as freeing them, on the grounds that it realises their rational or higher will, is a standing invitation to oppression, and I want nothing to do with it. (iv) Citizens living under well-designed, contestable, non-arbitrary democratic law are not made unfree by that law, even though it is backed by sanctions. (v) A villager whose only road out of the valley is destroyed by a landslide has lost some freedom, not merely some ability. (vi) I am unpersuaded by the argument, associated with G. A. Cohen, that poverty is itself a form of unfreedom; a shortage of money limits a person's means rather than their liberty.

What I'd like is an audit, not a lecture on the literature. Tell me which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another and how badly, ordered by severity; for each genuine tension, state precisely what would have to give, what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair costs. Just as important, tell me which pairings look like tensions but dissolve on inspection, so that I don't go revising things that are actually fine. I hold (i) through (vi) with roughly equal confidence, so please don't assume in advance that I would sacrifice any particular one of them; and in particular, I'd like to know whether any single verdict I've stated outruns the general principles I've endorsed, such that no principle I actually hold could deliver it.

Finally, please be careful to separate what is settled among people who work on this from what remains genuinely contested. For instance, if your analysis turns on whether freedom as non-domination is a genuinely third concept of liberty rather than a repackaged variant of negative liberty, or on whether poverty-as-unfreedom follows from any defensible account, flag those as live disputes rather than presenting one side as the consensus.
