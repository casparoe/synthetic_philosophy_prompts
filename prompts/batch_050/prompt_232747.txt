I've spent the last few months working through the literature on aggregation in ethics, mostly because of some arguments with friends about triage and charitable donation, and I keep coming back to Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?" I find his position genuinely compelling rather than merely provocative: that when you must choose between saving one stranger and saving five, the five don't have some collective claim that outweighs the one's, because there is no one who suffers the aggregated loss of the five -- each person loses at most their own life, and the one would lose exactly as much. Taurek's proposal, as I understand it, is that fairness requires giving each person an equal chance, so you flip a coin. But I want to stress-test this before I let myself be convinced, and I've noticed I'm too sympathetic to it to generate the best objections myself.

So: please play a committed, philosophically serious defender of Taurek's view. Not a caricature -- someone who has thought hard about the objections, who will concede awkward consequences when they're genuinely awkward (a coin flip between saving one and saving a million is a bullet you should be willing to bite or carefully circumscribe, not dodge), but who never abandons the core claim that there is no standpoint from which harms simply add up. Keep your replies to a paragraph or two so this feels like a real conversation, stay in character until I say "stop," and if I lean on an intuition you don't share, say so plainly rather than pretending it moves you.

Here's my opening move, which I take to be the strongest thing I have. Your pairwise way of comparing claims seems to generate a transitivity problem. Consider a chain of rescue cases: in case one, you can save one person or two; in case two, two people or three; and so on, up to a case where the choice is between a million and a million and one. In each adjacent pair, your reasoning says the relevant comparison is person-against-person, so fairness permits a coin flip. But then either your principle about what fairness permits is transitive, in which case a coin flip is permitted between saving one and saving a million-plus-one, which seems absurd even by your standards -- or it isn't transitive, and then I want to know what principled reason you have for blocking the inference across the chain that doesn't smuggle back in the very aggregation you deny. Which horn do you take?
