In An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, chapter xxvii (especially §§9–10), Locke ties personal identity to consciousness: a person extends as far back in time as their consciousness can reach, so that whatever past action their present consciousness can join to itself is their own, and whatever it cannot is not. Take this criterion literally and construct a thought experiment meeting these specifications:

1. The case must make Locke's criterion deliver a verdict about responsibility that Locke himself cannot accept, given his insistence (in the same chapter) that personhood is a forensic notion suited to divine judgment at the Great Day — that is, the verdict must be one no just judge could act on.
2. No swapping of souls or consciousnesses between bodies (the prince and the cobbler have that covered), and no Reid-style chain of a boy flogged, an officer who remembers the flogging, and a general who remembers the officer's deeds but not the flogging. Your case should isolate a different structural feature of the criterion: what it implies about the present, not about transitive memory chains.
3. Keep the scenario ordinary: a courtroom, a sickbed, or a family — no teleporters, no mad scientists.

After the scenario, do the verification work: state exactly what the consciousness criterion says about the case and why; state why Locke's own remarks about reward and punishment commit him to the opposite verdict; identify which clause of the quoted doctrine is doing the work; and say whether dropping that clause collapses your case into a mere forgetfulness objection Locke already anticipates. Finish with the strongest defense a Lockean could offer — ideally one that concedes the letter of the criterion while saving its forensic point — and your honest assessment of whether that defense succeeds.
