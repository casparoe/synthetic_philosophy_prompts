Exam question: "Bongard problems present a solver with two small sets of simple line drawings: every drawing on the left exemplifies some concept, and none on the right does. The solver must state the rule distinguishing the sets, typically after seeing only about a dozen drawings total. (a) Explain why these problems proved resistant to classical pattern-recognition programs despite involving only simple black-and-white figures. (b) What do Bongard problems reveal about the role of prior knowledge in concept induction? Your answer should engage with the worry that, since any finite set of examples is consistent with infinitely many rules, selecting one rule must be arbitrary."

Student's answer: "(a) Classical pattern-recognition programs worked by template matching or by extracting fixed statistical features from images, and this approach breaks down on Bongard problems because the distinguishing rule usually concerns abstract, relational properties of the drawings rather than anything about their pixel patterns. A rule might be, for example, that one shape contains another, or that there are exactly two objects of the same type. Since no template can capture 'containment' as such, the programs failed even though the drawings themselves are trivially simple. Bongard designed the problems so that each one admits exactly one correct rule, which is what makes them usable as a rigorous test of a recognizer's competence. (b) The worry about infinitely many compatible rules is essentially Goodman's point about 'grue,' and it shows that successful induction is impossible unless the learner's hypothesis space is fixed in advance by innate constraints, as Chomsky argued for language. Because Bongard problems each have a unique intended solution, however, the underdetermination worry does not arise for them in practice: the learner just has to find the rule, not choose among equally good ones. Modern convolutional neural networks handle these problems without difficulty, since they learn the relevant visual features from large datasets rather than relying on hand-coded templates. In this respect Bongard problems function much like items on the Raven's Progressive Matrices, where the solver likewise has to spot a pattern across visual figures."
