My book club does a movie night every few months and next up is Brief Encounter (the 1945 David Lean one, with Celia Johnson and Trevor Howard). I want to lead the discussion by reading it against Bertrand Russell's Marriage and Morals (1929). Roughly, Russell's line there is that once a marriage has produced children, its job is to hold steady for their sake, and that a passing infatuation with someone else, even a genuinely intense one, isn't by itself a good reason to blow the family up -- though he's also scathing about the idea that jealousy and sexual exclusivity are somehow sacred in themselves. Paraphrasing, not quoting, since I don't have the book handy.

So: read the film through that lens. On a Russellian view, is Laura's decision to go back to Fred and the kids not a tragic defeat but basically the *right* call, and does the film secretly agree with Russell even while it wrings maximum heartbreak out of the renunciation? Or is the film doing something Russell can't make sense of -- treating the affair as the one moment of real life in an otherwise deadened existence? Where does Russell's cool, utilitarian-ish framing flatten what the film shows (the shame, the narrator's guilt, the class thing, the sheer physical longing), and where does the film indulge exactly the romantic-love mysticism Russell thought was a recipe for misery? Which scenes would you point the club to?

Be honest if you think the pairing is forced or I've got Russell wrong -- I'd rather have you push back than watch me get corrected in front of everyone.
