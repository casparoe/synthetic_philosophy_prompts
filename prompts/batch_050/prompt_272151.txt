Let me lay out the material as I understand it, so we are working from the same picture, and correct me where I have it wrong. The Mīmāṃsā tradition holds the Veda to be apauruṣeya: authorless and beginningless, composed by no human or divine author. The motivation is epistemic rather than merely pious — dharma is held to be knowable only through Vedic injunction, and any authored text would inherit its author's fallibility, interests, and capacity for deception, and so could not ground dharma without a circular appeal to some further certification. The school's general epistemology is svataḥ prāmāṇya, intrinsic validity: a cognition is valid simply in arising and is presumed true until defeated, whereas invalidity is extrinsic and must be established by a later cognition. Nyāya holds the mirror-image view, parataḥ prāmāṇya, on which validity itself is conferred from outside — for testimony, by the speaker's competence and sincerity (the āpta). On the Mīmāṃsā picture, then, testimony is valid by default, and the Veda's lack of an author is a positive epistemic asset: there is no speaker whose error or insincerity could defeat it. The school divides internally. Prabhākara treats every cognition as valid and analyzes error as a failure of discrimination between cognitions (akhyātivāda), while Kumārila allows genuinely erroneous cognition (viparītakhyātivāda); and the two sides divide again over whether words convey meaning only within sentential connection (anvitābhidhāna) or carry independent meanings that are afterwards combined (abhihitānvaya).

My question is which open problems in this territory are most worth my three years, and why. To anchor you, here are the lines of work I have been considering, but do not treat the list as exhaustive:

1. A reconstruction of svataḥ prāmāṇya as a default-entitlement theory of justification: what the view precisely commits to, whether it collapses into familiar internalist or knowledge-first positions, and how it handles Gettier-style cases and skepticism.
2. The coherence and payoff of authorless testimony for contemporary testimony debates: does the dispute between reductionism and anti-reductionism — whether a hearer must positively establish a speaker's reliability or is prima facie entitled to accept testimony — change when there is no speaker at all? And does the modern proliferation of text with no asserting author (traditions, collaborative documents, machine-generated text) give this old doctrine new work to do?
3. The soundness of the core Mīmāṃsā inference from authorship to fallibility: does removing the author remove the defeaters, or merely relocate them to redactors, transmitters, and interpreters?
4. The Mīmāṃsā hermeneutics of injunction (vidhi) as a contribution to speech-act theory or deontic logic — though I suspect this drifts from epistemology into philosophy of language, and perhaps away from my strengths.
5. The Prabhākara–Kumārila disputes about error and sentence meaning, and whether either side maps onto live positions in contemporary philosophy of language and mind.
