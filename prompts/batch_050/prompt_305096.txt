So I just reread Kierkegaard's The Present Age (the "A Literary Review" essay) and I keep thinking it predicted Twitter pile-ons, and I want to pressure-test that mapping rather than just vibe about it. Take the Justine Sacco case, which is probably the canonical example: a PR director with like 170 followers tweets a dumb edgy joke about AIDS before boarding an 11-hour flight to Cape Town, lands to find herself the #1 worldwide trending topic, loses her job, etc. Kierkegaard's whole thing is that the press manufactures "the public" — a phantom that's everywhere and nowhere, lets everyone pass judgment while no one is ever responsible, and levels real distinctions into formless chatter.

Analyze the case through this, in both directions:

1. The mapping question: who plays the role of "the press" here? Twitter itself, the journalists and outlets who amplified the hashtag, or nobody — because there's no editor in the loop at all? K thought the press was the instrument of the abstraction. Is a recommendation algorithm a better fit for that role than an 1840s Copenhagen newspaper, or does it break the model because it has no intentionality?

2. Where the theory strains: he figured leveling flattens passion into apathetic "chat," but pile-ons seem wildly passionate — just in a crowd-shaped, impersonal way. Is that a genuine counterexample, or can you square it by saying the outrage is itself leveled (nobody cares about Sacco specifically, shes just filling the daily slot)?

3. What the episode shows about the theory, and about his remedy, which was basically inwardness, silence, becoming "the single individual" before God — i.e., roughly, log off and stop participating. Is that an adequate response when the machine can make you its object without your participation at all?

I already know the framework, so don't summarize The Present Age at me — I want the case analysis, and I'm fine with the answer being "the mapping half-works, here's where."
