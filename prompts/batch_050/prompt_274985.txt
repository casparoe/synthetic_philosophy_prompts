A friend and I are designing a university seminar on the nature–nurture question in cognitive development, and at our last planning session we realized we had both been invoking poverty-of-the-stimulus arguments freely without ever agreeing on when such an argument actually succeeds. We went back to what may be the original instance: in Plato's Meno (85c–d), Socrates questions Meno's slave boy, who has never been taught geometry, about how to double a square, and then draws the moral:

Socrates: "And at this moment those opinions have just been stirred up in him, like a dream; but if he were repeatedly asked these same questions in a variety of forms, you know he will have in the end as exact an understanding of them as anyone."
Meno: "So it seems."
Socrates: "Without anyone having taught him, and only through questions put to him, he will understand, recovering the knowledge out of himself?"
Meno: "Yes."
Socrates: "And is not this recovery of knowledge, in himself and by himself, recollection?"

Part of me finds this compelling, and part of me resists it, since Socrates is plainly doing much more than nothing: he steers the boy through the proof step by step, and shortly before this exchange he even supplies the standard term for the diagonal himself. So I sat down and wrote out my verdicts on the modern cases, hoping a principle would fall out. (1) Deaf children brought together for the first time at a Nicaraguan school in the 1980s, none with a full language, converged within a cohort on a sign language with grammatical structure their input lacked: genuine poverty of stimulus, strong evidence for an innate language-building capacity. (2) An ordinary toddler acquires English word order from parental speech riddled with false starts and is essentially never corrected for grammar: also a real poverty-of-stimulus argument — the input is poorer than it looks. (3) A chess master recognizes tens of thousands of board configurations at a glance, though nobody ever explicitly taught her the patterns and her input was simply games played: obviously learned, and it would be absurd to posit an innate chess faculty — even though the explicit instruction was every bit as poor as in (2), which is the pair I cannot reconcile. (4) Lab-reared monkeys show no fear of snakes until they watch another monkey react fearfully, after which a single observation suffices: the fear is not innate, and this shows that mere universality in the wild would have proved nothing. (5) Newborns orient toward face-like patterns over equally complex scrambled ones: I am inclined to discount this, because a general bias toward top-heavy, high-contrast configurations predicts the same result without any innate knowledge of faces. (6) Songbirds raised in acoustic isolation sing ramshackle songs that nonetheless preserve species-typical phrasing: a partially innate template. (7) Disgust at feces is universal, develops without instruction, and resists later reflection: it feels innate to me, though I notice my grounds are exactly the ones I rejected in (4) and (5).

What I want is the principle, if there is one. My own hunch is that everything turns on what information the input actually contains rather than on whether anyone was teaching, but I cannot make that hunch deliver verdict (2) without also delivering verdict (3). Please state a principle — or a small set of candidates — precisely, tie each clause to the case that motivates it, and give its verdicts on cases I have not listed: a child with only six months of exposure to a first language; a universal incest taboo; perfect pitch. Be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate; I assume at least one of (2) and (7) is in trouble, and I would rather lose a verdict than keep an incoherent principle. Finally, apply the principle to the passage itself: does Socrates' procedure, as he describes it, satisfy the conditions for concluding that something was already within the boy, or is Socrates conflating the absence of teaching with the absence of information — two things the principle, if it is any good, should keep apart?
