I keep finding myself of two minds about strategy-stealing arguments, and I'd like to know whether there's a single principle behind my verdicts or whether I'm just inconsistent. Here are the cases.

Case 1: In Hex, the classical argument that the second player cannot have a winning strategy — suppose they did; then the first player makes an arbitrary opening move and thereafter follows that strategy, treating the stray stone as irrelevant and playing anywhere if the strategy ever calls for the occupied cell. I accept this completely. Combined with the impossibility of a draw, it settles that the first player wins, and the fact that it exhibits no actual strategy doesn't bother me at all.

Case 2: In Chomp, the same form of argument — the first player takes the single poison-adjacent square and then steals whatever winning reply the second player supposedly has. Again I accept it: the first player has a winning strategy on any rectangular board, even though nobody can say what it is for boards of arbitrary size.

Case 3: In strong positional games generally, the claim that having an extra mark of your own on the board can never disadvantage you, because you can simply play as if it weren't there. I accept this for games where placing a mark is the only move type and your own marks never obstruct one another.

Case 4: Games where occupying extra cells can genuinely hurt — compulsory-move games with blocking, misère variants, anything with zugzwang. There I think the argument straightforwardly fails, which tells me its validity depends on some kind of monotonicity condition rather than on anything about games in general.

Case 5: The strategy-stealing assumption in AI alignment, roughly the claim that for any strategy by which a misaligned advanced agent could acquire power or achieve its ends, there exists an analogous strategy available to an aligned agent of comparable capability, so that nothing is forfeited by aligning our systems. Here my verdicts fragment: the assumption seems fine when the relevant actions are ones an aligned agent would also be willing to take; shaky when the misaligned agent's advantage derives precisely from options the aligned agent has forsworn — deception, coercion, manipulation — since stealing those would mean becoming the thing the alignment was meant to prevent; and shaky again if executing the stolen strategy requires knowing the opponent's strategy in advance, which the Hex argument conspicuously does not.

Here's my problem. In cases 1 and 2 I accept a purely existential argument with zero constructive content as settling the outcome; in case 5 I refuse to accept an argument of exactly that form as settling that alignment costs nothing. Either my confidence in the game cases outruns what they prove, or my skepticism about the AI case reduces to the substantive claim that it is really an instance of case 4 — that alignment constraints (or the misaligned agent's extra degrees of freedom) break the monotonicity the stealing move needs. I want a principle: a condition, as precise as you can make it, for when a strategy-stealing argument is sound, stated so that each clause is motivated by one of my cases. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts it saves and which it overturns. Then apply it to two cases I haven't decided: first, a game where the opening move is compulsory, irreversible, and can only ever restrict your own later options — can the first player still steal? Second, stealing across different goals — can an agent appropriate the structure of an opponent's strategy when the two players don't share a win condition at all, or does the very form of the argument presuppose that they do?
