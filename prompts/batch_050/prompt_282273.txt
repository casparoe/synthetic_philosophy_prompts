I share a house with two friends who've been arguing on and off for a fortnight, and it's become weirdly relevant to me because I'm currently dithering over whether to leave a stable job for a riskier one and keep catching myself using both of their logics. The argument started over a bet. Sam offered Hannah a coin flip: heads she loses £20,000, tails she gains £45,000. Hannah refused, and said she'd only take it if the upside were much bigger. Sam says this is straightforwardly irrational: the average payoff is +£12,500, she's passing up free money, and her squeamishness is just a bug. Hannah says she simply cares more about avoiding the really bad outcome than the average suggests she should — she weights the worst case extra heavily, and she thinks that's a perfectly respectable attitude, not a miscalculation.

Round two: Sam says her worry is already accounted for. Money has diminishing value — losing £20k when you're stretched hurts more than gaining £45k helps — and once you've adjusted the numbers for that, weighing the bad outcome *again* is double-counting, like applying a discount twice. Hannah replies that even if you redid the bet in units where each extra unit is worth exactly as much as the last — say extra years of financial security rather than pounds — she'd *still* weight the downside more. Her caution isn't really about the currency, it's about the shape of the whole outcome spread, so no conversion into the right units will capture it. Sam then wheeled out what he clearly thinks is his killer move: anyone who decides like Hannah can be stitched up. Because she doesn't evaluate choices by simply averaging, you can construct pairs of options where she prefers A to B, then swap out an identical chunk of both and watch her flip to preferring B — and worse, she could plan a sequence of choices today that she'd abandon halfway through tomorrow, paying to avoid a risk she'd already paid to avoid, so a clever bookie could in principle drain her. Hannah says she evaluates outcomes as wholes rather than piecemeal, a plan only counts if re-examining it as a whole still endorses it, and being hypothetically exploitable by an imaginary adversary isn't the same as being irrational about your actual one-shot life decisions.

They've both demanded I referee and I genuinely can't. So: who's right about which specific point — the double-counting charge, the exploitability argument, whether Hannah's pattern can be rational at all — and who's more right overall? And is there something both of them are missing? Please also say how confident you are in your verdict and flag the single point you're least sure about, because I'll definately bring that bit back to them.
