Write an essay on the debate over photographic transparency—the thesis that photographs, unlike paintings or drawings, allow us to literally see the objects they depict. Structure the essay around the following sequence of cases, designed to stress-test the standard positions. A security system works as follows: photons from a scene strike a sensor, and each detection event instantaneously triggers the emission of a corresponding photon from a display, with no storage, delay, or processing beyond a fixed point-to-point mapping. Now modify the system in stages: first insert a one-hour recording buffer between detection and display; then add a compression algorithm that smooths sensor noise using statistical priors about what such scenes typically look like; then add a generative component that fills in occluded or low-resolution regions with plausible detail. At which stage, if any, does the viewer stop seeing the scene itself and start merely seeing a representation of it? In answering, weigh the two main families of considerations in the literature: on one side, the idea that transparency turns on counterfactual dependence that holds independently of anyone's beliefs or intentions (which would place live feeds and photographs on a continuum with mirrors and periscopes); on the other, the idea that what matters is the kind of information channel or the involvement of intentional, design-driven selection in producing the image (which would sever photographs from transparent devices much earlier). Do not end on a non-committal survey: defend a definite verdict about which modifications break transparency, justify it, and state what follows from your verdict for the epistemic standing of ordinary photographs as compared with AI-restored or AI-generated imagery.
