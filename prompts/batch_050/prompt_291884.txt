Two friends of mine — Marcus and Dana — argued about taxation over dinner last week, and I stayed out of it. I'd like an outside referee now. The exchange went like this:

(1) Marcus opened by saying that taxation is theft: money is taken from you under threat of punishment, without your individual consent, and forcibly taking someone's property without their consent is theft regardless of whether the taker calls itself a government.

(2) Dana replied that this begs the question. "Theft" is defined relative to legitimate entitlements, and what counts as your property is itself fixed by a legal framework — one that includes tax law. You never had a right to your gross, pre-tax income in the first place, so nothing that belongs to you is taken. The concept of theft cannot be turned against the very rules that constitute ownership.

(3) Marcus answered that this proves far too much. On Dana's logic, if the state legislated the seizure of everything owned by some unpopular minority, the victims couldn't even complain of theft, since the law would have redefined their entitlements — which is absurd. Besides, some property rights don't wait for legislation: if he builds a chair out of wood he gathered with his own labor, the chair is his before any statute says so.

(4) Dana said the chair case doesn't generalize. In a modern economy, income isn't gathered from the state of nature; it exists only inside markets, contracts, courts, currencies, and infrastructure that a functioning state creates and maintains. The natural-rights story might cover acorns and chairs, but not a salary. And Marcus's minority-seizure example is disanalogous in the relevant respect: taxation funds the institutions that make holdings possible at all, which is why we don't call fines or license fees theft either — coercion alone doesn't settle the moral verdict.

(5) Marcus's last move: fines are penalties for wrongdoing, and he did nothing wrong, so the analogy fails. And the claim that the state "made your income possible, so it may take a share" has no principled limit — a benefactor's contribution doesn't generate an open-ended claim on the beneficiary. At most, the argument justifies itemized fees for services actually rendered, not open-ended progressive taxation redistributed at the legislature's discretion.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it is sound, partially sound, or mistaken, and identify the strongest objection it leaves unaddressed. Then give an overall verdict — who is more right, and about what — and note anything both parties are missing (for instance, whether the dispute is really about the definition of "theft," about the moral standing of pre-legal property rights, or about the justifiability of coercion, and whether those questions come apart).
