I am preparing a single eighty-minute session for a third-year undergraduate philosophy seminar on the question of whether love is a response to reasons. The students have studied value theory in general terms but have not encountered the appraisal-versus-bestowal debate; the readings are assigned for after the session, so the distinction has to be built live, from the front of the room. I want a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself.

Please set out the sequence in which the components have to be introduced, with the dependency structure made explicit: what must already be in place before the appraisal view -- love as a response to the beloved's valuable qualities -- can even be stated as a position rather than a platitude, and what then has to be said before bestowal -- love as conferring value rather than finding it -- can appear as something other than the claim that love is arbitrary or irrational. Indicate where, if anywhere, the relationship-based alternative associated with Kolodny should enter the sequence, and whether it should be presented as a third position or as a refinement of one of the first two.

Specify the one example that should carry the session. I am inclined to build it around a parent's love for a child, since that seems to be where bestowal looks most compelling, but I want the case for or against that choice made explicit: what the carrying example must contain (a beloved whose qualities seem neither necessary nor sufficient for the love, the possibility of loss and persistence, some asymmetry with mere liking) and what it must not smuggle in. If romantic love is the better carrier despite being more intuitive for the students, say why.

Then map the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they will arise, and mark the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than merely corrected afterward. I anticipate at least these: that appraisal means loving someone as a ranking of merit and so commits one to trading up whenever a better specimen appears; that bestowal means the beloved has no valuable qualities or that valuing something makes it valuable by sheer act of will; that the two views disagree about what love feels like rather than about the direction of fit between love and value; and that the debate is about whether love is voluntary. Correct me if any of these is unlikely or if I am missing a more predictable one.

Finally, give me one question I can put to the room at the end whose answers would show whether the distinction has actually landed -- ideally something that would catch students who have merely memorized the two labels -- and tell me what I should openly defer to the readings rather than attempt live, given the time.
