I'm looking for the established term, if there is one, for a configuration I keep meeting in my work, and for a verdict on whether it has been cleanly separated from two nearby puzzles I do know by name.

Here is the configuration, as neutrally as I can state it. A person holds a large set of beliefs, each on good grounds, and also has excellent aggregate-level grounds for thinking the set contains errors — not because any particular belief is shaky, but because of known track records and base rates for the kind of process that produced them. So far, so familiar. What makes my cases feel different is a third feature: the person faces a single decision that treats the whole set at once, so they cannot take the comfortable route of endorsing each belief separately while declining to join them together. Acting as if every member of the set were true is predictably ruinous; acting as if any particular member were false is unjustified; and picking some arbitrary subset to distrust seems worse than either.

Two instances. (1) A hospital pharmacist is, for each of the month's ten thousand prescriptions, amply justified in believing it was filled correctly, and — from years of audit data — amply justified in believing the month's output contains a handful of errors. She must decide whether to sign off on the entire month's dispensing for the insurer's review. (2) A structural engineer believes, of each weld in a bridge, that it will hold; she also believes that among the tens of thousands of welds, a few are defective; she must decide whether to certify the bridge, a decision that concerns all the welds jointly and cannot be decomposed weld by weld.

I know the literature contains the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, but both are usually told as stories about what it is rational to believe or assert, and in both the tension can seemingly be dissolved, or at least postponed, by refusing to conjoin. My cases add stakes and a forced joint decision, and I cannot tell whether that addition is a superficial change of costume or takes us to a genuinely different problem with its own name and its own proposed solutions. So: does this action-forcing version have an established name of its own? How exactly does it relate to the lottery and preface cases — special case, generalization, or distinct problem? If it has no name, coin one, define it precisely, and give one or two cases that sit just outside its boundary. And throughout, please separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this from what remains contested — in particular, I don't want one school of thought (say, on whether outright belief merely masks degrees of confidence, or on whether stakes change what rationality requires) presented as the consensus view.
