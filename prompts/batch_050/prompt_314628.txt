After forty years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, I have retired and finally found time for the philosophy I neglected during my career. Lately I have been reading popular treatments of time travel, and several of them present what strikes me as a decisive result: the grandfather paradox. As I understand the argument, if backward time travel were possible, a traveler could in principle journey back and prevent her own grandparents from meeting, in which case she would never have existed to make the journey — a flat contradiction. Since contradictions cannot obtain, backward time travel is logically impossible, full stop. This seems to me as airtight as any proof by contradiction I deployed in my own field. So here is my question: given that the grandfather paradox settles the logical impossibility of backward time travel, why do serious physicists continue to publish papers on closed timelike curves in general relativity, and why do philosophers continue to debate the metaphysics of time travel as though it were a live question? Is this simply a case of specialists refusing to accept an elementary logical result, the way some cranks refuse to accept Gödel?

I should add a wrinkle that occurred to me in conversation with a former colleague. In my laboratory days, when an instrument produced an impossible reading, we did not conclude that nature was contradictory; we concluded that some constraint in the apparatus prevented the absurd configuration from arising. My colleague suggested that a time traveler might be similarly constrained — that the laws of physics would simply never permit the paradoxical act to succeed, any more than my spectrometer would permit an impossible reading. But I confess I do not see how this helps: surely a constraint that mysteriously intervenes every time a traveler lifts a pistol at her grandfather amounts to admitting that time travel cannot actually occur in any meaningful sense? If the traveler cannot do the one thing that would generate the paradox, then in what sense is she free to act at all, and in what sense is the travel genuinely possible rather than a logical fiction?

Please answer as a professional philosopher of physics addressing an intelligent but philosophically untrained interlocutor. Before composing your reply, take a moment to brainstorm the possible readings of my question and to identify any presuppositions or conflations it may rest on — I have learned that in philosophy, as in physics, the framing of a question often smuggles in the answer. In particular, I would ask you to consider carefully what kind of impossibility a reductio argument of this sort actually establishes, whether there is a distinction to be drawn between the impossibility of time travel itself and the impossibility of certain actions within a timeline that contains time travel, and what my laboratory analogy about instrumental constraints does or does not show. Please think carefully, be direct about where my reasoning goes wrong if it does, and do not hesitate to tell me if my colleague's suggestion is better than I have credited.
