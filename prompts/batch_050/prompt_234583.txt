You are a historian of early modern philosophy who has taught the beast-machine controversy for years, and I need your help designing a teaching tool for my graduate seminar. At our last meeting, a colleague declared flatly that on Descartes' view a whipped dog feels nothing at all, and within ten minutes the students had produced four mutually incompatible readings, each claiming support from the same few texts. The trouble started with a sentence from Part I of the Discourse on the Method of Rightly Conducting One's Reason and of Seeking Truth in the Sciences (1637, here in John Veitch's translation), where Descartes, fresh from saying that good sense is the most equally distributed thing in the world, adds: "for as to the reason or sense, inasmuch as it is that alone which constitutes us men, and distinguishes us from the brutes, I am disposed to believe that it is to be found complete in each individual; and on this point to adopt the common opinion of philosophers, who say that the difference of greater and less holds only among the accidents, and not among the forms or natures of individuals of the same species." Some students took this to settle that animals are mere automata; others objected that a claim about reason — its completeness in each human, its difference in kind rather than degree — licenses no conclusion whatsoever about what, if anything, animals feel.

Before our next meeting, think carefully and build me a decision tree that sorts the live interpretations of Descartes on animal minds, using the quoted sentence as its hinge: the first node should be a question about what that sentence, fairly read, actually commits Descartes to regarding brutes. A few specifications. Limit the tree to six or seven leaves. Phrase every branch node as a question a student could answer from the texts without knowing which interpretation it leads to, and wherever possible anchor the question in a concrete case rather than a label: a parrot that imitates speech, a dog that yelps and cowers when struck, an animal that has been trained to perform on cue. Do not build the tree from a list of scholarly names; build it from the logical space first. Sketch that space before drafting: list the capacities that are actually in play (reason, genuine language, sensation, self-conscious awareness of one's own states, passions such as fear, pain, memory and learning) and the distinct stances an interpretation can take toward each (deny it outright, grant it but only as a purely corporeal process involving no thought, or decline to pronounce). Then locate the readings in that grid. The leaves must include at least the full-automaton reading on which animals have no feeling at all, a reading granting sensation but no thought, a reading granting sensation but no consciousness of it, a resolutely agnostic or noncommittal reading, and a developmental reading on which Descartes' mechanist physiology and his later correspondence pull apart. Mark any unoccupied cells of the grid and diagnose each briefly: is nobody there because the combination is incoherent on Descartes' own premises, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? Flag any pair of readings your tree cannot cleanly separate rather than papering over the ambiguity, and give each leaf one sentence stating the strongest piece of textual evidence against it.

You do not need to track down exact letters, editions, or page numbers; work from the texts as you know them, and reason about what the arguments establish rather than about who has endorsed what. Present the sketched capacity-and-stance grid first, then the tree, then a short closing note. In that note, tell me how confident you are overall that the tree covers the actual interpretive landscape rather than a tidied-up version of it, and single out the one leaf or attribution you are least sure of — whether because the texts genuinely underdetermine it, because you may be conflating Descartes with his followers, or for whatever reason — and explain why that point, specifically, is your weakest.
