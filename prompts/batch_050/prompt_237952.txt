A friend in my reading group reconstructed Pascal's wager as a general argument-form and claims it proves too much, and I can't decide whether he's found a flaw or just rediscovered the many-gods objection in a nicer suit. His version: (1) In decisions under uncertainty, you should maximize expected value. (2) Any act with a non-zero probability of an infinite payoff has infinite expected value, and so beats every act with merely finite stakes. (3) Believing in God has a non-zero probability of infinite reward, so I ought to believe. (4) But there is also some conceivable god who infinitely rewards disbelief and punishes believers; its existence likewise has non-zero probability, so by the same reasoning I ought to disbelieve. (5) More generally, for any course of action whatsoever I can describe a possible being who attaches an infinite payoff to it, so this form of reasoning indifferently licenses everything and therefore licenses nothing. (6) Hence something in (1)–(3) must go. I grant that (4) and (5) feel like reductio fuel, but I don't want to abandon expected-value reasoning wholesale, and I'm unsure whether the wager itself is the casualty or whether the infection is in how infinities and tiny probabilities are being handled. Tell me which premise or step I should reject, why it looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs -- including whether assigning probabilities of zero, or capping utilities, or restricting the argument to "live options" is a principled fix or an ad hoc one, and rank the exits from least to most expensive.
