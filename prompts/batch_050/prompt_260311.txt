I am a philosophy professor, and for the past three years I have chaired my university's Student Hardship Fund panel. We meet monthly, decide roughly thirty applications per meeting, and have about ninety minutes of deliberation in total, so each case gets perhaps three minutes. Each applicant submits a standardized financial statement and a one-page account of their circumstances; we have no capacity to investigate beyond what is on those two documents. Awards range from £200 to £2,500, the fund is exhausted most months, and our decisions are effectively final -- the bursary office disburses whatever we approve, and there is no appeal. The other two panel members are a student-services administrator and an elected student representative, neither with any philosophical training.

Our standing, unproductive disagreement is about the currency of hardship. The administrator ranks applications by financial shortfall against a standard student budget; the student representative ranks them by how much distress the applicant's statement conveys; and I keep encountering cases where both rankings seem wrong -- a student with adequate funds whose disability makes an ordinary student life cost far more, a student in evident misery whose finances are unremarkable, a student whose shortfall results from an expensive choice made with open eyes. I want to stop re-litigating the metric case by case.

Write a decision procedure the panel can actually run, grounded in the "equality of what?" debate: welfarism, resourcism in something like Dworkin's ambition-sensitive and endowment-insensitive form, the capability approach associated with Sen, and the standard objections each faces -- cheap and expensive tastes, adaptive preferences, the problem of measuring welfare from a one-page statement, and Anderson's charge that luck-egalitarian distributions institutionalize humiliating pity. I recognize that this literature concerns the metric of egalitarian justice across a society, whereas we are doing triage with a fixed budget; part of what I am asking is what, if anything, survives that translation, and your procedure should say so explicitly rather than paper over it.

Specifically, I want:

1. A sequence of questions the panel asks of each application, in order, each with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer given only our two documents, and an instruction for what to do when the information a question requires simply is not available -- because it usually will not be.

2. An explicit choice of primary metric, with the reasoning, plus the precise points at which the other two metrics may override or modulate it. Tell me which distinctions from the literature must survive translation into a three-minute checklist -- responsibility and expensive tastes, differential conversion of resources into functionings, adaptive preferences -- and which can be dropped without much loss, and why.

3. An honest marking of the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and of the failure modes: the kinds of case this procedure will get wrong systematically, given our information constraints, and what the panel should do about that residual error.

4. Worked applications to three cases: (a) a final-year student with chronic depression, whose finances match the standard budget, requesting funds for private therapy because the university counselling waitlist is eight months; (b) a student with a mobility impairment whose stipend equals her peers' but whose accessible housing and transport costs consume most of it; (c) a student who declined a well-paid summer job to take an unpaid internship in his intended profession and now cannot make rent. At least one of these should be a case where the procedure delivers a verdict my panel will strongly dislike; for that case, say whether the dislike is a reason to revise the procedure or a reason to distrust the panel's instincts, and how one would tell the difference.
