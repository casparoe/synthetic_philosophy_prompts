So I'm a junior and I somehow ended up as the one person on student council's events committee who "likes thinking about this stuff" (my advisor's words), so I got stuck writing the decision memo for our spring fair venue problem. Here's the situation: we have to pick between renting the big gym ($400) or just using the cafeteria (basically free), and we have NO idea how many people will show up. It's the first time we've run this event, last year's fair got cancelled for covid reasons, and there's honestly no good way to guess attendance. Could be 30 people, could be 150.

We made a rough table of how good/bad each choice turns out (in points, 0 = break even-ish):

- Low turnout (~30 people): gym = -8 (we blew the budget on a mostly empty room, looks bad, principal is annoyed), cafeteria = +5
- High turnout (~150 people): gym = +20, cafeteria = +5 (way too cramped, we have to turn people away, feels like a wasted chance)

Two people on the committee are fighting over it. Maya says we should use "maximin" — pick the option whose worst case is least bad, so cafeteria. Jay says use "minimax regret" — figure out how much you'd kick yourself in each scenario compared to the best you could've done, and pick whichever keeps your biggest regret small, which he says comes out to the gym (regret of 13 vs 15, if we did the math right). Meanwhile our treasurer keeps saying "just average the two outcomes and pick the higher number," but I'm pretty sure that secretly assumes both turnouts are equally likely, which we don't actually know?? That feels like cheating.

Write the memo I'll bring to Thursday's meeting. It should: recommend one option as the bottom line up front, explain the winning rule in plain language, give the losing rule its strongest possible case instead of strawmanning it (the committee has to vote, so I can't just steamroll them), and deal with the treasurer's averaging idea. Keep it under 400 words because nobody reads past that and I know it.
