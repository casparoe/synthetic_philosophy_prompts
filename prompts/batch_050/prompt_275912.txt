I was at a conference dinner last week and ended up eavesdropping on two academics from another department (Nadia and Tom) having a proper row about their PhD admissions committe. Here's what happened, as best I can reconstruct it.

Background: their committee admits a candidate iff two things hold -- the research proposal is strong enough, and funding is available for them. Three members voted on one applicant. Member A: strong proposal yes, funding yes, so admit. Member B: proposal yes, funding no, so reject. Member C: proposal no, funding yes, so reject. The chair aggregated premise by premise: "strong proposal" passed 2-1, "funding available" passed 2-1, so the committee admitted the student -- even though B and C, asked the bottom-line question, both voted to reject her.

Then the argument kicked off:

(1) Nadia said the chair got it badly wrong. The department has now admitted a student that two of three members wanted to turn away. Worse, it's a decision nobody owns: if the student struggles, every member can honestly say "I never voted to admit her." An admission that no actual person is willing to stand behind isn't a real collective decision, it's an artefact of the tallying method.

(2) Tom said conclusion-based voting is far worse. On that method the committee goes on record affirming "the proposal is strong", affirming "funding is available", and denying admission -- which flatly contradicts its own published criterion. The minutes would describe a body that doesn't follow its own rules, and any applicant or faculty board reading them would see that. Premise-by-premise voting is the only way the group's judgements stay coherent, and dispersed responsibility is a price every voting rule charges somewhere, not a defect of this one.

(3) Nadia replied that the "contradiction" only exists if you insist on treating three people's ticks on a ballot as a single mind that must be internally consistent. Nobody acts on "the group's beliefs" -- people act on the admission decision, and that decision should be one a majority of actual humans actually endorse. She also claimed premise-based voting under conjunctive criteria has a structural yes-bias: as panels get bigger, candidates almost nobody wants overall keep getting waved through, because each premise passes on a different rotating majority while every voter rejects the package for their own reason.

(4) Tom came back that her bias argument proves too much: by exactly the same rotating-majority logic, conclusion-based voting has a structural no-bias, so she's not defending a neutral procedure, just picking the direction of error she finds more palatable. If the department thinks admission errors are costlier than rejections, the honest fix is to change the criterion or demand a supermajority on premises -- not to adopt a rule whose official record is incoherent and then hope nobody reads the minutes.

Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing (e.g. options neither considered, or assumptions both are making). Finish with an overall call on who gets the better of the dispute.
