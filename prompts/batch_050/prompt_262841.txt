I'm doing a reading group with some coworkers and we've gotten onto Buddhism-adjacent stuff. One guy, who's read a couple of popular books on it, gave us this explanation of karma that he swears is the standard view, and I wrote it down because something about it bothers me but I can't put my finger on it: "Karma is the universe's moral accounting system. Every action you do generates a karmic debt or credit, and the universe guarantees the books balance -- do good and good will come back to you, do harm and you'll suffer for it, in this life or a future one. That's why Buddhists say that if something terrible happens to you, like an illness or losing everything, it's the fruition of something you did before, possibly in a past life, so in a sense you earned it. The flip side is that karma is basically fate: what's coming to you is already fixed by your past, so the tradition counsels acceptance rather than fighting your circumstances. And because karma is a universal law like gravity, it doesn't care about your intentions -- a bad outcome is a bad outcome, whether you meant it or not."

Now, the "do good, get good" part I buy as at least a decent first approximation, and I know rebirth is in the picture for the traditional versions. But three things nag at me. The fate bit seems to make effort pointless, which can't be right for a tradition that's literally built around a practice you have to do. The "you earned your illness" bit seems both morally ugly and, I vaguely recall, something the Buddha actually pushed back on somewhere, though maybe I'm inventing that memory. And the intentions bit seems backwards -- I thought intention was the whole ballgame in Buddhist ethics. But my coworker is usually reliable and he says this is just what the view is, victim-blaming implications and all, and that Westerners water it down because they find the real thing harsh.

So: is his summary fair? Please break it down piece by piece -- what's accurate as a gloss of the traditional view(s), what's a common-but-contested simplification, and what would a Buddhist philosopher (or a Hindu one, if they differ in relevant ways) flatly reject. Also, if parts of it are wrong, does that rescue the view from the victim-blaming objection, or does some version of that objection survive even the corrected account? I'd rather know if the harsh reading is the honest one than get a sanitized version.
