I've been a moderator on a rationalist-adjacent discussion forum for several years, and we run structured debates: two participants, an agreed proposition, and me as referee empowered to rule on whether a claimed entailment actually holds, so the debate can move on rather than stall. My recurring nightmare is a particular species of dispute that I think of as the tortoise manoeuvre, after Lewis Carroll's 1895 dialogue in which Achilles cannot make the tortoise accept a conclusion because the tortoise keeps granting each premise while withholding acceptance of the step from premises to conclusion, demanding that each such step be written down as a further premise. Most of my debaters are not doing anything so pure, but the manoeuvre shades into legitimate challenges, and I have no principled way to sort one from the other. I make these rulings perhaps a dozen times a month; getting them wrong in one direction kills a live debate, and in the other lets a bad actor filibuster indefinitely.

Please write me a decision procedure I can actually apply at the table, grounded in whatever the philosophical literature on the regress of deduction genuinely supports. Concretely, I want:

1. An ordered sequence of questions to put to a challenger who refuses to grant an inferential step, designed to locate exactly where their refusal sits: whether they dispute a premise, dispute that the step is an instance of a rule they otherwise accept, dispute the rule itself across the board, or are demanding a justification of deduction in general. For each question, what counts as a sufficient answer.

2. A classification of the answers, and for each class, what the correct referee ruling is: rule the entailment valid and move on, rule the step out of bounds and require a rewrite, suspend the debate as resting on a genuine disagreement about logic, or eject the challenge as a tortoise move. I especially want the criterion that separates a sincere logical revisionist (someone who rejects a rule everywhere, on principle, and pays the costs) from a selective tortoise (someone who rejects a rule only where it hurts their position), including a behavioural test I can actually administer mid-debate.

3. Explicit marking of the points where the procedure must hand off to my judgement, because the question is not one a checklist can settle, and an honest statement of what the procedure cannot do -- in particular, I assume it cannot itself supply a non-circular justification of deduction, and I'd like you to say why that limitation does or doesn't undermine my authority as referee.

Then run the procedure on three cases I've genuinely faced: (a) a challenger who grants both premises of a simple modus ponens but declines to affirm the conclusion, saying only that they don't see that it follows; (b) a challenger who accepts the inferential step but argues the conditional premise is only supported probabilistically, so the conclusion inherits less than certainty; (c) a challenger who announces, in a debate about whether contradictions can be tolerated, that they reject the rule that anything follows from a contradiction, and applies this consistently. I want the worked rulings, and I want you to flag whichever ruling you expect I'll find most uncomfortable and why.

Please write in British English throughout.
