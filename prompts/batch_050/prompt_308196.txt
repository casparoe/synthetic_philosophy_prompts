I run a reading group on animal minds (a few philosophers, a couple of psych grad students) and our fiction pick this round is Flowers for Algernon. I want to read it against Morgans canon — the old comparative psychology rule that you shouldn't explain an animal's behavior by appeal to a higher mental faculty when a lower one will do — and I want help building the reading before I have to lead it.

The thing I keep circling: the novel seems to run the canon in both directions and convict it either way. Charlie attributes all sorts of things to Algernon — spite in the maze races, understanding during the regression, something like knowing what's coming — and a strict Morganite would strike most of that as projection. But the scientists effectively run the canon on Charlie: Nemur talks as if pre-op Charlie wasn't really a person yet, the lab treats Charlie and Algernon as the same category of experimental material, and that under-attribution is depicted as the actual horror of the book. So does the novel think parsimony about minds is a fine rule that everyone in it applies badly, or that the rule itself is the problem because in practice it only ever cuts one way, against whoever's already being treated as less than a full subject?

Some pressure points I want worked through:

- the maze races and whether Charlie reading Algernon as a rival is anthropomorphism or just accurate, given what was done to both of them
- the Chicago conference, where Charlie lets Algernon out of the cage and takes off with him — Charlie clearly sees a fellow prisoner making a break for it. Is there any version of the canon that licenses that reading, maybe because Charlie has a basis for the analogy to Algernon's mind that no outside observer could have, since he's literally living Algernon's future? This intrests me most: does shared fate do epistemic work that the standard argument from analogy can't?
- the deterioration, the burial, the closing request that someone put flowers on the grave — is that ending sentimental anthropomorphism, exactly what the canon exists to block, or is it the novel's thesis about when attribution is earned?
- the diner scene with the disabled busboy, where the crowd laughs and Charlie catches himself laughing too — that scene isn't about an animal at all, so tell me honestly whether it belongs in this reading or whether I'm forcing it

And push back where the reading fails. The canon rests on a ladder of faculties, higher over lower, but the novel buys its own ladder (intelligence as a single scale you climb and then slide down), so maybe the book is a bad vehicle for criticizing the canon since it shares the same picture. I also don't want to reduce it to an illustration — it's obviously about disability and research ethics before it's about mice, so flag the scenes that resist the animal-minds frame. End with whether the book actually adds anything to the comparative psych debate (it predates the whole anthropomorphism vs anthropodenial fight by decades) or just dramatizes a dilemma the literature already had. No need for exact quotes, just point me at scenes.
