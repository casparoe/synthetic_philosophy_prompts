I teach an upper-level philosophy of math course, and one of my sharper grad students came to office hours genuinely rattled by a popular math book she'd picked up over break. The author's gloss on Skolem's paradox goes roughly like this: "Assuming ZFC is consistent, the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem hands us a countable model of set theory. But ZFC proves Cantor's theorem, so this countable model contains a set that it regards as uncountable — the set really is countable, seen from outside, and the diagonal argument only appears to succeed inside the model because the model happens to be missing the relevant bijection. So either first-order set theory is inconsistent, or uncountability was never a real feature of the mathematical universe at all — just an artifact of looking at structures from the inside, which means Cantor didn't discover anything deep about infinity." My student has concluded she has to choose between abandoning Cantor and abandoning first-order logic, and the book is confident enough that I can see why she's spooked.

My own sense is that the summary is mostly fine as a statement of the technical situation but goes wrong somewhere in the moral it draws — the "either inconsistent or uncountability is unreal" fork smells like a false dichotomy to me, and I suspect the author is sliding between a set being countable and a set being countable-according-to-a-model as if that were costless. But I haven't thought carefully about the Löwenheim–Skolem theorems since grad school, and I'd rather not wing it in front of students. Could you go through the summary and tell me which parts a set theorist would accept as accurate, which parts are distortions (and of what — is he conflating two claims? overstating what the theorem shows? treating a standard resolution as if it didn't exist?), and whether his final verdict about Cantor survives once the errors are corrected? If part of the issue is that the conclusion depends on a contested philosophical assumption — say, that first-order logic is the right framework for set theory — I'd like that flagged too, since that's actually the most teachable moment for my seminar.
