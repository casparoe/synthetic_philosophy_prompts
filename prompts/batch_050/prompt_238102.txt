I am a novelist at work on a book whose narrator, a concert pianist in her fifties, develops pain asymbolia after a small stroke: she continues to feel pains, can locate and describe them, and still withdraws her hand from a hot stove, but reports with evident sincerity that pain no longer bothers her in the least. The emotional core of the book is her uncertainty about whether anything of value has been lost — she is, in one sense, freer than the rest of us, and yet she keeps burning herself without noticing. I want her scattered reflections on pain to rest on a defensible philosophy, or at least to fail only where I have chosen for them to fail. Over a year of notebook entries I have settled on the following commitments, which I now suspect do not all sit comfortably together.

First, the badness of pain is intrinsic to the experience itself, a matter of how it feels from the inside, and does not consist in the frustration of the subject's desires or preferences; a creature with no relevant desires could still be wronged by being made to hurt. Second, what is bad about pain is specifically its aversive dimension rather than its sensory dimension, so that a pain whose sensory character remained fully intact but which the subject experienced with perfect indifference would not be bad for her at all. Third, my narrator's condition is best understood as a genuine dissociation between the sensory and aversive components of pain, and not as her having ceased to feel pain altogether. Fourth, torturing someone would remain gravely wrong even if the victim had first been given a drug that left the sensory side of pain untouched while rendering them wholly unmoved by it — and part of what makes it wrong, I want to say, is that their pain is still bad for them, not merely that their autonomy has been violated or their trust abused. Fifth, a subject's sincere and considered testimony that her own pain does not feel bad to her settles the question; there is no external standpoint from which the rest of us could overrule her about the quality of her own experience. Sixth, the pains of infants and of non-human animals can be genuinely bad for them even though they are incapable of offering any testimony whatsoever.

Before I let the narrator commit to this position on the page, I would like an audit. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, and which pairs only appear to conflict before dissolving on closer inspection? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating every friction as equally serious, and for each one say precisely what would have to give. Finally, identify the least costly revision that restores consistency — and tell me what the novel, and the narrator's way of understanding her own condition, would lose if I made it.
