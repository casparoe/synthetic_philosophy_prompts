I am a software engineer at a firm that deploys fairly autonomous trading agents, and I have been dragged into an internal policy discussion about liability when one of them causes a loss. A colleague's memo argues that legal personhood for AI is, in his words, a solved problem, on the grounds that the law already made persons of corporations. His analogy does double duty. First, as an existence proof: corporations are non-human legal persons, so there is no conceptual barrier to doing the same for a model. Second, as a practical scheme: register each agent as a person, require it to hold a capital reserve and insurance, and counterparties can simply sue the agent itself rather than chasing developers, operators, or users through a fog of contractual indemnities. I pushed back with a patch of my own: some scholars have proposed housing autonomous systems inside limited liability companies with no human members, which seemed to me to concede that the bare analogy is not enough. My colleague replied that the very availability of such a structure shows the analogy holds, since the corporate form already stretches to fit.

I have no philosophy background and no legal training, so I would like a proper audit of this analogy rather than more intuitions. Please give me the correspondence table first: what, on the AI side, is supposed to play the role of shareholders, directors, the registered office, limited liability, the capital reserve, dissolution, and veil-piercing. Then the disanalogies, and here I would like you to be specific rather than gestural. Some that occur to me: a corporation's rights and duties seem to bottom out in human interests somewhere, whereas the agent has no one inside it; accountability normally runs through officers who can themselves be sanctioned; fining a corporation deters by hurting people who care about money, and it is unclear what fining an agent deters; and a corporation's assets cannot simply be copied to another jurisdiction overnight. Please say which of these actually matter for my colleague's two conclusions and which are superficial.

I would also like you to use the standard theories of what a corporation even is — roughly, that it is a creature of state concession, an aggregate of its contracting participants, or a genuinely real entity that acts — and to test the analogy under each, since it strikes me that the analogy's strength may depend entirely on which of these pictures you start from, and my colleague never says which he assumes. In particular: if personhood for corporations is purely instrumental, granted for the sake of the humans behind them, does importing it to AI quietly foreclose the question of whether an agent could have interests of its own, or does it instead make that question unavoidable?

Finally: which of his two conclusions, if either, survives the audit, and if the corporate analogy cannot carry the load, is there a better legal-historical analogy — I have dimly heard that ships can be sued as if they were defendants, and that trusts hold assets without being persons — and what would that better analogy license that his does not?
