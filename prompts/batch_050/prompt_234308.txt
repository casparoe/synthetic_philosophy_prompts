I've been running a small reading group on Indian epistemology (we're amateurs, one philosopher and a bunch of curious physicists), and we just finished the Nyāya material on the pramāṇas — perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison (upamāna) and testimony (śabda). At the pub afterwards someone said the Galileo affair is basically a pramāṇa dispute, and the idea has been rattling around my head ever since. So: I'd like a proper analysis of the Galileo controversy through the lens of Nyāya pramāṇa theory, running in both directions — the theory illuminating the episode, and the episode stress-testing the theory.

The facts I have in mind are the standard ones, so don't worry about obscure scholarship: Galileo turns the telescope skyward around 1609-10, reports the moons of Jupiter, the phases of Venus, lunar mountains and sunspots; some contemporaries refuse to look through the instrument or distrust what it shows; the Church treats certain scriptural passages as bearing on celestial matters; there's the 1616 warning, the Dialogue in 1632, and the trial and house arrest in 1633. Galileo also argued, following Augustine, that where scripture seems to conflict with what has been demonstrated about nature, scripture should get a non-literal reading, since it was written to accomodate ordinary understanding.

Here's what I want, roughly in order. First, classify the telescopic observations: are they pratyakṣa — perception by means of an instrument, the way seeing through a window or wearing spectacles presumably still counts as seeing — or are they really anumāna, an inference from terrestrial tests of the telescope to celestial conclusions? Say what turns on this, given that Nyāya inference is supposed to rest on previously perceived regularities. Second, the people who declined to look: is there any pramāṇa-theoretically respectable defence of that refusal, or is it just irrationality? Third, scripture as śabda: Nyāya treats testimony as knowledge when it comes from a trustworthy speaker, an āpta, so analyse the collision between śabda and perception-plus-inference here. Does Nyāya have the resources to vindicate Galileo's hermeneutic move, the Church's insistence on the literal reading, or neither? Fourth, the part I care most about: what does the episode do to the theory? Nyāya is usually read as a pluralist about the pramāṇas — they cooperate and can converge on the same object. Does the affair confirm that picture, or does it push towards the rival view that each pramāṇa has its own exclusive domain, which would defuse the whole conflict by ruling scripture silent on the heavens? And is the telescope itself a case classical Nyāya never anticipated — if perception requires sense-object contact, what exactly is in contact with what when you observe Jupiter?

Keep the answer in American English please (my reading group is mostly American and they'll tease me otherwise), and I don't want a bibliography or citations — just the analysis. A few pages' worth is fine; don't pad it.
