I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a philosophy of physics seminar, and we have just spent two sessions on Henri Poincaré's Science and Method, in the Halsted translation collected in The Foundations of Science. In the chapter where Poincaré reconstructs his own invention of the Fuchsian functions, he argues, in rough paraphrase, that the unconscious mind generates enormous numbers of combinations of ideas, nearly all of them sterile, and that a specifically aesthetic sensibility — an attunement to harmony, elegance, and economy of structure — functions as a kind of sieve, allowing only the promising combinations to surface into consciousness. Our seminar split cleanly in two over how to read this passage. Half the room insisted that Poincaré is offering psychology of discovery and nothing more: the sieve explains where good ideas come from, and carries no implication that beauty is a sign of truth. The other half insisted that the sieve metaphor only makes sense if the aesthetic sense reliably discriminates fertile combinations from sterile ones, which smuggles in an evidential claim — that elegance tracks fruitfulness, and perhaps truth, closely enough to merit the trust mathematicians repose in it. Both readings seem textually defensible, and our professor declined to arbitrate.

It then struck me that this classroom standoff mirrors a much older and larger stalemate. Figures like Weyl and Dirac treated mathematical beauty as close to a sufficient mark of a sound physical theory; Steven Weinberg argued that a truly beautiful theory has a kind of inevitability that nature respects; James McAllister's Beauty and Revolution in Science defends a calibrated, historically grounded version of the idea; and Sabine Hossenfelder's Lost in Math contends that aesthetic criteria such as naturalness and symmetry have recently been a systematically misleading guide in fundamental physics. What baffles me is that all parties seem to accept the same historical episodes — Maxwell's equations, general relativity, and the Dirac equation on one side; supersymmetry's non-appearance and various beautiful dead ends on the other — yet nobody's confidence ever moves. My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the debate is stalled for three layered reasons: first, 'beauty' picks out different properties for different speakers (symmetry, simplicity, unifying power, inevitability of structure), so some exchanges are merely verbal; second, underneath that there is a genuine but unresolved empirical disagreement about base rates, since neither side has an agreed denominator of beauty-guided attempts against which to score the famous successes; third, the two camps may be answering different questions under one heading, one about the rationality of trusting aesthetic judgment as a research heuristic, the other about whether aesthetic features confer genuine evidential support on a theory.
