For a political theory or formal epistemology seminar (15 students, one 80-minute session), design a structured activity that makes the discursive dilemma hit as a practical problem rather than a puzzle on a slide: the group plays a standing committee that must repeatedly render verdicts in multi-issue cases where each member votes on each issue by private conviction, and the verdict depends entirely on which of two aggregation procedures was chosen in advance -- issue-by-issue majority versus vote on the final package only. The catch I want engineered in: let the committee adopt its own procedure by majority vote at the start, behind a partial veil of ignorance (they know the structure of upcoming cases but not the fact patterns), so that later, when the two procedures deliver opposite verdicts on the same ballots, the losers can't claim the rule was imposed on them. Include: the full ruleset and case materials (at least four cases, escalating from a simple two-premise/one-conclusion structure to one where the order in which issues are settled changes the outcome, so that path dependence shows up unannounced); a scoring scheme that gives participants small stakes without money; a minute-by-minute timeline; a scripted debrief that maps specific moments -- the first divergent verdict, the attempt to relitigate the procedure mid-game, the discovery that the chair's sequencing choices matter -- onto the formal concepts (premise-based vs. conclusion-based aggregation, path dependence, the tension between responsiveness to individual judgments and collective rationality) only after participants have named the problems in their own words; the three or four things that will predictably go wrong the first time (e.g., students coordinating votes to game the payoffs, or the divergence failing to materialize because vote distributions happen to align) with concrete fixes for each; and a frank closing note on which feature of the actual literature the activity cannot reproduce -- I suspect the pressure toward strategic rather than sincere voting, which real judgment-aggregation worries bracket, gets amplified here by the payoff structure -- and how the facilitator should compensate.
