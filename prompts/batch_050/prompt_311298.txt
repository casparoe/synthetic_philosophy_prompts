Play John Stuart Mill, author of On Liberty (1859). Speak in the first person, in character, until I say 'stop'. You may use only the concepts and vocabulary available to you in 1859, plus whatever I explain to you about how modern institutions work — if a case I describe involves something you couldn't know about (clinical trials, employment law, whatever), ask me about it the way Mill would, rather than breaking character. Ground rules: you'll defend your principles seriously and won't concede the core claim that competent adults are sovereign over their self-regarding conduct, but you're not stubborn — you can concede a distinction, a hard case, or a badly drawn line if I actually earn it. Keep replies to a paragraph or two so it feels like a real exchange.

Here's the passage I want to press you on, from chapter five of On Liberty. You write that the maxims of the essay are "first, that the individual is not accountable to society for his actions, in so far as these concern the interests of no person but himself," and "secondly, that for such actions as are prejudicial to the interests of others, the individual is accountable, and may be subjected either to social or to legal punishment, if society is of opinion that the one or the other is requisite for its protection."

Here's my problem with it. Your framework tells me when *society* may step in. What it doesn't seem to tell me is when a person's own agreement counts in the first place. Everyone, you included, treats consent extracted by a lie or by a threat as no real consent — you yourself allow interference against fraud and force. But then consent isn't the bedrock; something underneath it is doing the work, and I don't think your two maxims say what that thing is.

So here's the case I want you to rule on. A woman signs up for a medical trial. The information sheet she was given disclosed every risk truthfully, but in dense institutional language she plainly could not have parsed, and nobody checked whether she had. She also signed partly because the man running the trial site is her employer, and he let it be understood, without ever saying it outright, that her contract renewal might go more smoothly if she volunteered. Nothing she was told was false, nothing was explicitly threatened, and she's an adult of ordinary intelligence.

Three questions, in order. First: is her consent valid? Second: if it fails, does it fail on grounds of deception, of coercion, or of her not really being in a position to understand — because I don't think those are the same failure, and I want to know whether your apparatus can even tell them apart, or whether you have to lump them all under 'not truly voluntary.' Third: does it matter to your answer that she was never lied to, only left to not understand? Go.
