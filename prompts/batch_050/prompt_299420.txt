Ok so my roommate walked me into this over drinks and I've been stuck on it for a week. It started as the classic argument against cyclic preferences but it seems to prove way too much. Here's the argument as we've written it on the whiteboard:

(1) Suppose someone has preferences that go in a circle: they'd rather have wine than whiskey, rather have beer than wine, and rather have whiskey than beer. Same person, same moment.

(2) Hand them the whiskey. Since they prefer wine to whiskey, they'll happily pay a dime to swap whiskey for wine. Since they prefer beer to wine, they'll pay a dime to swap wine for beer. Since they prefer whiskey to beer, they'll pay a dime to swap back. Each trade is one they endorse, given their own preferences.

(3) Now they're holding exactly what they started with, thirty cents poorer. And there's nothing stopping the bartender from running the loop again. And again. Until they're broke.

(4) Nobody rational can be arranged so that a series of choices they themselves endorse reliably transfers everything they own to someone else in exchange for literally nothing.

(5) So cyclic preferences are irrational. Fine so far, I was on board.

But then my roommate pointed out:

(6) Nothing in steps (2) and (3) actually required the preferences to be held at the same time. Suppose I prefer whiskey to wine on Mondays and wine to whiskey on Fridays, because my tastes just shift over the week, the way tastes do. The bartender sells me wine on Monday for my whiskey plus a dime, and buys it back on Friday for a dime less than I paid. Same pump, spread across the calender instead of running in one afternoon.

(7) But changing your tastes over time is obviously not irrational. People acquire a taste for things, get sick of things, grow up. If (6) works, then anyone whose preferences ever change is irrational, which is absurd.

(8) So either the original money pump argument is broken too, or there's some difference between the synchronic and diachronic cases that does all the work, and nobody at our table could say what it is.

Here's the thing: I really want to keep both that cyclic preferences are irrational AND that changing your mind over time is fine. So what I want from you is: which step do I reject, or where does the parallel between (2)-(3) and (6) quietly break? And whatever principle you use to separate the two cases, tell me what it costs -- like, does saving the money pump argument mean committing to some strong claim about how rational agents have to plan ahead, or treat their future selves, that has its own weird consequences? Because I suspect the fix is going to be something like "the cyclic guy can just refuse the third trade," but then I don't see why the pump ever got off the ground in the first place.
