I am a novelist, two years into a book set in the seventeenth-century Indian Ocean: a Gujarati merchant house, a Dutch trading post, a Swahili port city. The structural problem that has defeated three drafts is, I have come to realize, the same problem on which an entire field of historical scholarship has been stuck for a generation: whether the past can be told from everywhere at once, or whether every telling picks a center, openly or by stealth.

The scholarly dispute, as I have absorbed it through my research, runs like this. For thirty years or so, global historians have tried to write against Eurocentrism: tracing connections rather than comparing sealed-off civilizations, treating Europe as one region among several, and showing that much of Asia matched Europe in prosperity and commercial sophistication until quite late. The critics reply that the enterprise recenters Europe even as it disowns it. The master question is still why Europe industrialized first — the 'great divergence' question — which quietly installs Europe's trajectory as the norm and everyone else's as a deviation in need of special explanation. The working categories — market, state, capital, modernity, even 'the economy' as a thing a society has — are European exports; so is the periodization that still organizes the shelves. And the archives, being largely the records of European companies and chanceries, decide in advance who gets to speak. The global historians answer that comparison and connection are the only alternatives to nationalist storytelling, that inherited categories can be revised in the using, and that declining the big question on account of its pedigree abandons it to the triumphalists. There the matter rests. The facts are largely shared, the books keep appearing, and nobody has moved.

My working hypothesis comes from my own trade rather than theirs: narrative form is the hidden variable. A novel cannot be told from everywhere at once, because telling requires selection, selection requires a criterion, and any criterion a writer actually applies — whose agency matters, which changes count as transformations, what the story is finally about — is inherited from the tradition that trained her. Even polyphony is staged by a single hand. If I am right, the historians are conducting a dispute about whether form can be neutral while almost never discussing form, and no quantity of new archival material will settle it. A rival diagnosis I would bet less on but cannot rule out: there are really two disputes here, a tractable empirical one about the timing and causes of industrialization and an intractable normative one about which stories deserve the center, and the field's exhaustion comes from running both under one heading.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict and not a survey of schools. What kind of disagreement is this, at bottom: about the facts, about methods, about the categories themselves, about whether form can be innocent, or about the political stakes of the question — and which of these is doing the real work when the arguments are actually exchanged? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a new archive, a new metric, a new narrative form, a distinction nobody has yet drawn, or the admission that the question as posed has no answer? Tell me honestly, too, whether my novelist's hypothesis is an insight historians could use or a confusion I am importing from a different craft. And if your answer has consequences for what I should do with my manuscript — choose a center and own it, multiply centers and accept the cost, or abandon the omniscient frame altogether — I will take those as well.

No citations and no reading list, please; the diagnosis is the deliverable. Please write in American English.
