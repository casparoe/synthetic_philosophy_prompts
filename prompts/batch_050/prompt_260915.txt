After years of reading rationalist blogs, I keep running into the same unresolved crux: discussions of bounded rationality treat logical uncertainty as obviously real and obviously tractable, while the philosophy I've read on the problem of logical omniscience mostly treats it as a defect of our formalisms that no one has convincingly repaired. I want to pressure-test my own view with your help.

Here is the proposition I'd like your credence on: there exists a principled, non-ad-hoc account of rational belief for computationally bounded agents that (1) permits assigning intermediate probabilities to conjectures whose proofs have not yet been found, without thereby licensing Dutch books against the agent on theorems it has already verified; (2) reduces to something recognizably Bayesian in the limit of unbounded computation, so the bounded account is a generalization rather than a replacement; and (3) does not smuggle in an oracle, i.e., does not quietly assume the agent can already check any relevant inference step for free. Note that I'm asking about the existence of such an account in principle, not about whether any particular published proposal succeeds.

Give me your own number, but more importantly, decompose the question. The sub-questions I'd suggest starting from: whether logical omniscience is a genuine commitment of ideal Bayesian rationality or merely an artifact of modeling belief as a probability distribution over possible worlds; whether uncertainty about logical consequences can be reduced to ordinary empirical uncertainty about the output of one's own reasoning processes, or whether something irreducibly distinct remains; whether notions of coherence can be extended to bounded agents in a way that yields constraints rather than triviality; and whether the demand in clause (3) is even well-posed, given that the line between "free inference steps" and "costly ones" may have no principled location. Assign a rough probability to each, state how they combine into your final credence (are they conjunctive, or can failures in one be compensated elsewhere?), and identify which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to.

For context: my credence is about 0.35, mostly because I suspect clause (3) hides an infinite regress. A friend puts it at 0.9, on the grounds that bounded agents demonstrably do reason sensibly about unproven conjectures, so an adequate account must exist even if no one has written it down. Tell me which sub-question the two of us most likely disagree about, and what argument or result would move your number the most in either direction.
