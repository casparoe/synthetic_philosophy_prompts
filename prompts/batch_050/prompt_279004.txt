I sit on the grants panel of a mid-sized private science funder, and we're deadlocked over how to score two proposals and I need to get us unstuck.

Quick version of the fight. Both teams work on the same messy longitudinal dataset (think: thirty years of regional economic indicators, we're keeping the field vague on purpose). Team A built their model *after* the full dataset was public, and it fits the known data gorgeously — every wiggle explained, beautiful posterior fit, reviewers agree it's technically superb. Team B's model is clunkier on that same data, but two years ago, before the most recent wave of numbers came in, they stuck their neck out and predicted in advance roughly what the new figures would show, and they got it basically right.

Half the panel says Team A's model is strongly confirmed by the data — the data really is way more likely given their model than given its rivals, so why should the order of events matter? The other half says Team A's success is nearly worthless as evidence because the model was engineered to fit exactly that data, so of course it fits, and only Team B has actually earned any confirmation. There's a further wrinkle: Team A's lead researcher swears (plausibly, imo) that the model's structure came from theory they developed for other reasons, not from curve-fitting — but nobody can verify that from the outside.

The panel needs a written policy, not just a verdict on these two, since this keeps coming up. Draft the decision memo I'd circulate: recommend how our scoring rubric should treat a model's fit with data that was already public when the model was built, relative to successful advance prediction. Give us criteria reviewers can actually apply (e.g. what should count as evidence that a model wasn't just reverse-engineered from the data?). Include an honest statement of the strongest argument for the side you don't pick — if you say discount Team A, deal seriously with the point that likelihood ratios don't come with timestamps; if you say score them on fit alone, deal with the worry that we'd be rewarding whatever has enough free parameters. Also say what we should do about unverifiable sincerity claims like Team A's, since we can't audit people's brains.

Casual tone is fine but keep it structured enough that a board could vote on it. At the very end, give me the bottom line as \answer{...} containing one of: score-on-fit-alone, discount-accommodated-evidence, or case-by-case-with-criteria — plus a five-words-or-less gloss.
