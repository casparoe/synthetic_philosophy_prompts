I cover neuroscience and AI for a general-interest magazine, and there's a pattern I've now seen maybe two hundred times that I want to ask about, because I genuinely can't tell if it's a known thing with a name or just a bee in my own bonnet.

The pattern: a field will officially, loudly reject some picture of how things work — the experts roll their eyes if you even raise it — and then that same picture keeps sneaking back in through the field's everyday working language.

Consciousness research is where I see it most. Every neuroscientist I interview insists there's no little observer sitting in the head watching the senses like a screen — they bring this up unprompted, it's basically a reflex by now. And then ten minutes later the same person is telling me that at around 300 milliseconds the stimulus "enters conscious awareness," or that the brain "binds" color and motion into a single experience "presented to the subject." Enters into what, though? Presented to who? Nobody means it literally, fine, but the sentences only really parse if there's somewhere the information arrives and someone it arrives for. I've started a file of these. "Covert awareness" in vegetative-state patients. The moment a signal "reaches" consciousness. Even the AI skeptics do it — "the model has no access to what it's saying." Access for whom?

And it's not just consciousness people. Evolutionary biologists officially reject purpose in nature, then talk nonstop about what a gene is "for" and what the cell "wants" — but here's the thing, they *know* they're doing it and will tell you it's just shorthand. The consciousness people, as far as I can tell, mostly don't notice, and a couple of them got genuinely annoyed when I asked. I don't know if that difference matters but it feels like it might.

So: is there a name for this — for a refuted picture surviving in the metaphors of the people who refuted it? It's not plain metaphor, metaphors are usually innocent. It's not hypocrisy either, they sincerely reject the picture. It's more like the concept got executed but the grammar kept the body. If there's a literature on this, point me at it — but don't just hand me a label, tell me how this thing differs from nearby stuff like theories contaminating the words used to test them, or ordinary loose talk, or the Lakoff-style claim that all abstract thought is metaphor anyway, because I suspect it's none of those exactly. And if nobody's named it, what would you call it, and where are the edges: when is smuggled-in language a symptom that the old picture is still secretly doing the explanatory work, versus harmless shorthand everyone could cash out if pressed? Who gets to decide which one you're looking at?
