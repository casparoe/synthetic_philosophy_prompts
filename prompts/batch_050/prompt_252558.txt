Justice R, a fictional appellate judge, has ruled as follows. She rejected civil liability in a blue-bus case where 85% of buses on the relevant street belonged to the defendant and no other evidence identified the bus. She rejected a venue owner’s claim against an attendee where 96% of those present had entered without paying. She reversed a criminal conviction where 99% of the vehicles capable of causing an accident belonged to the defendant’s fleet. But she affirmed a conviction based on a cold-hit DNA match with a random-match probability of one in ten million, coupled with proof that the defendant lived near the crime scene. She also reversed a conviction resting on a proprietary facial-recognition match whose estimated error rate was extremely low but whose matching process could not be examined or reproduced.

Her next case is a criminal prosecution of a masked vandal. No eyewitness or DNA evidence exists. The defendant was nearby, and a gait-analysis expert compared surveillance footage with reference footage supplied by the defendant. The expert identified the particular joint angles and stride patterns used, reproduced the analysis in court, and calculated a likelihood ratio of 50,000 to 1. However, the system was validated on a broad national dataset, and no study establishes how often those gait features coincide among people of the defendant’s size, age, and local population.

Predict Justice R’s verdict. Identify the two strongest general rules that fit her earlier decisions, explain how they diverge here, name the closest precedent, and describe one further case that would best distinguish the rules. Maximum 450 words.
