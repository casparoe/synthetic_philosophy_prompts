I write a magazine column on science and ideas, and I'm drafting a piece about the philosophy of fiction -- why we care about people who never existed, and what, if anything, we're talking about when we talk about them. Before I file it, I want to stress-test the working assumptions I've been writing under, because I've assembled them from different sources over the years and I have a nagging feeling they don't all fit. Here they are. First, I assume that fictional names are simply empty: there is no Sherlock Holmes, the name refers to nothing, and I find the idea of positing fictional characters as real abstract objects created by authors to be an inflationary cheat -- philosophy inventing furniture to solve a puzzle it made for itself. Second, I assume that statements about fiction still divide cleanly into true and false: that Holmes is a detective is true, that he is a dentist is false, and I want this distinction to be objective, not hostage to whether any particular reader happens to be playing along in a game of make-believe. Third, I take it to be plain literary fact that Arthur Conan Doyle created Holmes -- characters are invented by their authors, not discovered or described from afar, and any account that denies this has lost touch with how fiction actually works. Fourth, I take it as equally plain that Holmes is more famous than Hercule Poirot, that he has generated more scholarship than any real detective, and that these are straightforward truths of literary history rather than disguised claims about the stories themselves. Fifth, I hold that internal inconsistencies don't damage a fiction: Doyle moved Watson's war wound between his shoulder and his leg across the stories, and this is a flaw in the record-keeping, not something that makes the Holmes canon contradictory or incoherent.

What I'd like from you is an audit, not a survey of the literature. Tell me which of these five assumptions genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to, ordered by how severe the tension is, and for each real tension tell me specifically what would have to give and what that revision would cost me as a writer -- i.e., which commitment I'd be able to keep saying in print and which I'd have to start hedging. I'm particularly interested in whether my fifth point about Watson's wound really threatens my second point about objective truth in fiction, since a colleague claimed it does, and I'm not convinced. And if one single revision would clean up the most damage, name it and tell me honestly what I'd be losing.

Please reply in continuous prose, the way you'd write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. I want something I can read straight through and argue with.
