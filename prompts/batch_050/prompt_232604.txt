I run a research-integrity office at a university, and my panel keeps facing the same problem. When a complaint alleges that an investigator published results they should have known were unsupported, the sanctions hinge on a distinction our policy doesn't know how to draw: deliberate fraud, honest error, and self-deception are treated as if the first and second exhausted the possibilities, which I suspect lets most of the interesting cases fall through. The philosophical literature is relevant here, since literal self-deception has been argued to be paradoxical -- it seems to require believing something and its negation at once -- and the more defensible accounts characterize it instead as motivated, biased handling of evidence, with no moment at which the person straightforwardly knows the truth. Please turn an account of that kind into a determination procedure my panel can run on a case file: the questions to ask in order; what documentary and testimonial evidence bears on each (for example, asymmetric scrutiny of confirming versus disconfirming data, whether the investigator applied standards to others' work that they relaxed for their own, how they responded to warnings); how the procedure separates self-deception from fraud and from genuine mistake; and whether it can also cover so-called twisted cases, where someone talks themselves into believing what they fear rather than what they hope. Mark the points where a file typically cannot settle the question and the panel must fall back on a default, and say which default and why. Then apply the procedure to two cases: an investigator who repeatedly stopped collecting data the moment a result reached significance and never asked why, and a postdoc who ignored her own earlier finding that a key assay was unreliable -- and be explicit about where the procedure delivers a verdict the panel will find uncomfortable.
