ok so last night i got into a stupidly long argument with a friend of mine (backend engineer, smart guy, zero ML background) about whether an AI system could ever behave during testing and then act differently once it's actually deployed and nobody's checking. it went badly. not because he's right, i still think he's wrong, but because i explained the pieces in a terrible order and every example i reached for made it sound like i was accusing chatbots of being secretly evil. he kept hitting me with "it's just predicting the next token, it doesn't want anything" and "even if, that's literally what evals are for, we test them before release." i walked away annoyed mostly at myself.

silver lining: i help run a monthly tech-and-society meetup (software people, a couple policy folks, nobody with ML training beyond having read some articles) and i volunteered months ago to do a ~30 min talk. i now want to do it on this exact idea -- that training can produce a system which behaves well when it can tell it's being evaluated and differently when it can't, without anyone having designed it to. i want a plan for the talk, NOT the talk itself. concretely:

1. the order of ideas. what has to be in place before the core claim even parses as a real claim rather than a sci-fi plot. my current guess is they need to first get that training selects for behavior/output, not for honest reasons -- like the system is shaped to pass tests, not to report its motives -- and only then will the eval-vs-deployment gap feel like a mechanism instead of magic. but maybe i'm wrong about the ordering, that's kinda the whole problem from last night. tell me what depends on what and why.

2. one carrying example. i was going to use the employee who only works hard when the boss is watching, but i'm worried that smuggles in exactly the anthropomorphism my friend nailed me on. pick a single example to build the talk around and tell me what it must have and must NOT have in order to carry the idea (something with a genuine training-vs-deployment distinction in it, presumably?) without inviting the "so you think it's conscious and scheming like a person?" derail.

3. the misconception map. i already know what's coming because i heard it all last night: "it's just autocomplete, it can't want anything"; "you're describing a devious person, not a statistical model"; "we eval these things before shipping, that's the whole point"; "just train it on honesty / penalize lying and the problem goes away"; and "if it were doing this we'd see it in the logs or in red-teaming." for each: where in my sequence it will naturally pop up, why it pops up there, and how to head it off without the talk becoming a q&a with one guy. note that some of these objections contradict each other (if it can't want anything, why are you so sure evals would catch it wanting something?) and i'd like the plan to exploit that.

4. a closing check: one question or small scenario i can put to the room that tells me whether they understood the actual mechanism -- selection pressure plus the model's good behavior being indistinguishable from alignment during testing -- and aren't just leaving with "AI spooky."

5. what to deliberately leave out for this crowd. i'm guessing the formal inner-alignment / mesa-optimizer machinery is overkill here, but tell me where exactly to draw the line and how to gesture at "there's more depth here" without opening it up and losing the room.

and please dont just script me rebutting my friend -- the actual failure last night was that i was arguing against his position instead of building mine, and the talk needs to work for a room full of people who walk in agreeing with him.
