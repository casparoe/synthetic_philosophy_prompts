I teach an upper-level seminar in moral epistemology and want a handout that sorts the positions in the debate over moral testimony — the debate about what, if anything, is epistemically defective about coming to believe that an act is wrong simply because someone tells you so. Please build the handout as a decision tree.

Root the tree in a shared datum everyone in the debate accepts: people constantly acquire moral beliefs by being told (a child told that cruelty is wrong, a citizen told that some policy is unjust), and no party to the debate wants a theory on which all such belief-formation is epistemically worthless. From there, the branch nodes should be these splitting questions, each phrased as a question a student can answer before knowing which position it leads to — no node may presuppose the vocabulary of any camp:

1. Does the felt oddity of taking someone's word on a moral question show that testimony cannot transmit moral knowledge at all, or only that something less than knowledge is missing?
2. Is there a genuine asymmetry between moral and non-moral testimony that needs explaining — and if so, is it located in the subject matter, in the hearer, or in what the hearer is entitled to pass on to others?
3. Must uncritical deference (accepting a moral verdict on someone's say-so without being able to say why it is true) be treated separately from learning by testimony more broadly, so that a position might bless one and condemn the other?
4. Is what is lost in deference epistemic at all, or is it rather moral understanding — the capacity to grasp why the right act is right and to extend that grasp to new cases?
5. Does apparent moral testimony reduce to non-moral testimony (about the testifier's reliability, their evidence, or the underlying non-moral facts) combined with the recipient's own moral judgment — and if it does, does the puzzle survive the reduction?

Aim for roughly six to nine leaves. Each leaf should carry a position, with the standard label where the literature has one (optimism and pessimism about moral testimony, asymmetric or restricted optimism, understanding-based accounts, reductionist or hybrid views) and "unnamed" where it does not, plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment — for instance, what a pessimist must say about ordinary moral education, or what an optimist must say about the lingering sense that pure moral deference is second-rate.

Note explicitly any combination of answers the tree makes available that nobody in the debate actually occupies, and diagnose briefly whether that region is empty because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot genuinely separate, rather than forcing a verbal distinction between them.

Close the handout with a short epistemic health warning: separate what is broadly agreed among people who work on this topic (for example, that uncritical moral deference at least appears second-rate, and that some explanation of the appearance is owed) from what is genuinely contested (whether the defect is epistemic, where exactly it lies, and whether the moral/non-moral asymmetry is real), and do not present any one response to the puzzle as the consensus view.
