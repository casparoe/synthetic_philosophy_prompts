I am writing a long critical essay on the misuse of historical analogy in foreign-policy debate, and I want a ranking I can argue with rather than a list I can nod along to. Below are six analogies that recur whenever commentators interpret a new international crisis; for each I have stated the lesson people typically draw from it.

1. Munich 1938: negotiating with an expansionist adversary is appeasement; concessions feed aggression rather than sate it.
2. Vietnam: a limited intervention in a peripheral conflict will escalate into a quagmire that domestic opinion cannot sustain.
3. The Thucydides trap: a rising power and an established power are structurally prone to war, whatever either side intends.
4. July 1914: a multipolar crisis can escalate through mobilization timetables and alliance commitments faster than any participant wants.
5. The Great Depression: a financial collapse demands immediate, massive state intervention, on pain of a decade of stagnation.
6. The Second World War: a diffuse ideological enemy must be confronted as a global war rather than managed as a policing or containment problem.

For each analogy, give me two scores between 0 and 1:

- Selection asymmetry: the degree to which the analogy is invoked because it is vivid and rhetorically available rather than because the template and the present case share decision-relevant structure. Score 1 if the analogy is almost always reached for first and justified later.
- Compression of disanalogy: the degree to which deploying the analogy silently flattens features that distinguish the present case from the template and that would matter to the decision. Score 1 if the typical deployment erases nearly all such differences.

Call the average of the two scores the analogy's drag. Rank the six by drag and group them into three tiers: usually illuminating, genuinely mixed, and usually misleading. Because an analogy's drag depends on what it is applied to, score each one as it is most commonly deployed in public debate, and flag any case where your score would change substantially for a different target application. For every item, name at least one concrete decision-relevant difference that its typical deployment compresses — for instance, what gets flattened when the Depression template was applied to the 2008 financial crisis, or what the Munich template assumes about an adversary's ultimate aims.

I want the ranking to be falsifiable rather than decorative, so end with two things: first, for each analogy, the single historical consideration or structural argument that would most change your scores and move it across a tier boundary; second, an honest note on where the scoring scheme itself is most likely to mislead — for example, whether it unfairly penalizes vividness when vividness sometimes tracks real structural similarity, or whether drag misses the ways analogies mislead by omission rather than by false comparison. If you think one of my six does not belong on the list because its typical use is mostly sound, say so and explain what would prove you wrong.
