I spent forty years as a population geneticist before retiring, and over the past two years I have been reading philosophy of biology and auditing an upper-level undergraduate seminar at my former university. The instructor has kindly invited me to contribute one essay question to this year's final examination, on the material closest to my old work: the charge that "survival of the fittest" is tautological, and the propensity interpretation of fitness that is standardly offered in reply.

The standard exam question on this material is too easily answered by rote: state the tautology objection, define fitness as expected reproductive success rather than actual survival, and declare the problem dissolved. I want a question that cannot be answered this way. Concretely, I want candidates forced to engage a worry I have not been able to settle to my own satisfaction: whether the propensity interpretation resolves the circularity or merely relocates it. The question should require candidates to take a position on at least the following. First, the explanatory cost: if fitness is a propensity rather than an outcome, then the fitter type can fail to leave more offspring by sheer chance, so it is unclear in what sense fitness explains what actually happens in any particular population, as opposed to describing a statistical tendency. Second, the epistemic circularity: propensities cannot be observed directly, and in practice — as I know from decades of counting offspring — fitness is estimated from realized reproductive success, which looks uncomfortably like inferring a quantity from the very phenomenon it is then invoked to explain. A good answer must judge whether this pattern is vicious or benign, and I would like candidates pushed toward the obvious comparison class: physical dispositions such as electrical charge or fragility, where we likewise infer the disposition from its manifestations and then explain the manifestations by it, apparently without scandal. If candidates think fitness differs, they should say exactly where the disanalogy lies — environment-relativity, the absence of anything like an intrinsic categorical basis, or the fact that an individual organism reproduces only once while a propensity seems to characterize an ensemble of possible trials. Third, the question should permit any of several verdicts — that the interpretation succeeds outright, fails, or succeeds only at the price of making selection explanations irreducibly statistical, a price that itself needs defending — provided the verdict is argued rather than asserted.

Please deliver: (1) one essay question, suitable for a 75-minute slot in a three-hour exam and answerable in roughly 800 words, with any necessary setup included in the question itself so that a candidate who has done the readings needs no further context; (2) a model answer of about 600 words that takes a definite position rather than surveying the options; and (3) a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, stating the marks each is worth out of a total of 20, with a one-line note on the characteristic way a weaker candidate mishandles each point, plus a closing paragraph naming the specific moves that separate an A answer from a B answer. The scheme should reward the quality of the argument rather than the recitation of positions, and should not require name-checking particular philosophers.

Begin your response with the exam question itself, with no preface, framing, or commentary.
