Last night a friend and I spent several hours arguing about moral testimony — about whether one can ever simply take another person's word that something is right or wrong, the way one takes a doctor's word about a diagnosis. The argument broke off unresolved over a case that surfaced only at the very end, and I want help reconstructing where her view actually commits her.

Over the course of the evening she gave me these verdicts, as nearly as I can reproduce them:

- Whether the restaurant she likes sources its meat from factory farms, and whether eating there is therefore wrong: she was happy to defer to a well-read mutual friend, and has stopped going on his say-so.
- Whether lying is ever permissible, as a general principle: she said she would never adopt a principle like that on anyone's authority, not even someone she took to be morally wiser than herself — she would want the arguments.
- Whether the city's new zoning policy is unjust: she deferred to a housing-policy researcher she trusts and saw nothing even slightly odd in doing so.
- Whether she should forgive her estranged brother: she said flatly that no one else's verdict could settle this for her; if a wise counselor told her to forgive and she complied on that basis alone, whatever she did would not be forgiveness.
- Whether a joke she made at a dinner last month was cruel to a colleague who was present: she said that even here she could not reach the conclusion by testimony, and that apologizing because someone told her to would be going through the motions.
- Whether to vote for a complicated ballot measure funding homeless services through a tax change: she voted as a policy-savvy friend advised and felt entirely comfortable about it.

Then came the case that stalled us. Her former mentor — to whom she owes a great deal and with whom she has remained close — has been credibly accused of serious misconduct toward students. A colleague whose moral judgment she admires more than anyone's told her, without laying out reasoning she could fully assess, that continuing the friendship is wrong and that she is obligated to cut off contact. I predicted she would refuse to defer on this one; she herself was unsure, and we left it there.

Please do the following. First, predict her verdict on the mentor case and say which single rule, fit to all six earlier verdicts, delivers that prediction. Second, construct the strongest alternative rule that fits the six earlier verdicts exactly as well but delivers the opposite verdict on the mentor case — I suspect the two rules will agree on where testimony fails her (forgiveness, apology, principled conviction) while disagreeing about why, and I'd like that disagreement made precise. Third, adjudicate: say which attribution is more plausible given the details of her verdicts, including any wording of hers that tips the balance. Finally, describe a further case, and her verdict on it, that would discriminate between the two rules — a case on which they clearly diverge, stated concretely enough that I could actually put it to her.
