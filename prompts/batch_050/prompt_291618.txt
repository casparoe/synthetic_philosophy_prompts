For a graduate seminar on the ethics of political violence, I am building a session around the July 20, 1944 attempt on Hitler's life — Stauffenberg's bomb at the Wolf's Lair, the planned seizure of the government under Operation Valkyrie, the plot's failure, and the execution of the conspirators — and I want the session to stage a genuinely two-way encounter with Aquinas's teaching on tyrannicide rather than treating Aquinas as a decorative citation.

Let me lay out the material from Aquinas that I take to be relevant, so the analysis works from these texts as I understand them and not from a stock summary. In his early commentary on the Sentences, Aquinas appears to condone the killing of a ruler who governs for his own advantage rather than the common good, at least where there is no recourse to a superior authority. In De regno he is notably more cautious: he rehearses the practical dangers — attempts often fail, successful ones frequently install a worse tyrant, and would-be liberators are often moved by ambition rather than zeal for the common good — and he prefers that tyrants be removed through public authority where such authority exists. In the Summa (II-II, q. 42) he holds that overthrowing a tyrannical regime is not, strictly speaking, sedition, since a government ordered to private advantage is not just and it is rather the tyrant who disturbs the civic order; yet he qualifies this where resistance would produce greater harm to the multitude than the tyranny itself. Two further pieces of his framework matter here: the distinction between a tyrant by usurpation of title and a ruler legitimate in title but tyrannical in exercise, and his doctrine of oaths, on which a sworn promise cannot bind anyone to wrongdoing.

Here is what I want. First, a disciplined mapping. Classify Hitler's regime within Aquinas's scheme — he acceded through legal forms but ruled tyrannically — and say whether the usurpation/exercise distinction does any work here and whether it matters for the permissibility question. Determine who, on Aquinas's view, may act: the plot was a conspiracy of senior officers and a handful of civilians, not an act of public authority or of the multitude, so does it fall under the apparently permissive early teaching, or does De regno's preference for public remedies exclude it? Apply the no-greater-harm condition: the plotters gambled against the risk of civil war and state collapse, and in the event the failure brought reprisals without liberation. And address the personal oath of unconditional loyalty the officer corps had sworn to Hitler, a scruple some plotters themselves felt keenly — does Aquinas's doctrine of oaths dissolve it as cleanly as it first appears?

Second, an honest account of where the framework strains or falls silent. Aquinas's conditions seem to presuppose a polity in which some public authority or multitude could in principle act, and in which an agent could estimate the disorder his act will cause. Consider whether a modern totalitarian state — which consists precisely in the systematic destruction of such public space — is a case his scheme anticipates or one that exposes its limits, and in particular whether the no-greater-harm condition is usable at all by agents reasoning ex ante under radical uncertainty.

Third, run the analysis in the other direction. The plot failed, the regime's terror intensified, and yet the conspirators are now almost universally honored. Say what this outcome does to Aquinas: does it vindicate De regno's caution, does the moral consensus in the plotters' favor tell against that caution, or does the episode reveal that the permissive and cautious strands of his teaching were addressed to different questions all along? I want your considered judgment about which strand the case actually tests, not an even-handed summary.
