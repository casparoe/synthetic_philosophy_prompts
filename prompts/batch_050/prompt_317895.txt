So I'm in an epistemology seminar this semester and we just started the unit on peer disagreement and higher-order evidence. On a friend's recommendation I also read this popular book about rationality (one of those airport-bookstore ones, I won't name it) that has a chapter basically summarizing the philosophy literature for a general audience. Here's how it puts the main result, roughly: "When you find out that an epistemic peer -- someone just as smart as you, with access to the same information -- disagrees with you about something, the only rational response is to meet them halfway. It doesn't matter how confident you were or how carefully you checked your reasoning beforehand: evidence about your own possible unreliability overrides whatever got you to your answer in the first place. And this is why philosophers think it's flat-out incoherent to believe something while also believing you have no good evidence for it -- rationality requires your confidence to always line up with the total balance of evidence, and since evidence about your own fallibility is part of that balance, the second-guessing basically always wins."

The book then draws a pretty strong practical moral: whenever you're in a dispute with someone roughly as informed as you, just defer or at least heavily discount your own view, and anyone who "stands their ground" after discovering disagreement is exhibiting bias, full stop. It also briefly mentions the restaurant-check case where two people do the math and get different totals, as if that settles things for disagreement in general.

Something about this feels too tidy to me -- like, if higher-order evidence always wins, wouldn't that make the original disagreement impossible to even identify, since deciding someone is my peer is itself first-order reasoning? But the author seems confident and clearly read the literature. Can you audit the summary for me? Which parts are a fair representation of what conciliationists actually claim, which parts overstate or conflate things (I'm suspicious of the "meet them halfway" framing and the "always overrides" bit), and would defenders of the steadfast view or people who think epistemic akrasia can be rational agree that their positions have been refuted rather than just disagreed with? And once the corrections are in, does the book's practical advice -- when in doubt, defer -- still follow?
