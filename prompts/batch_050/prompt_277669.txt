I'm in the final year of my philosophy degree and enrolled in an advanced seminar on philosophy of language. For my capstone presentation next week, I'm defending a thesis that's a bit heterodox relative to the standard textbook line: in the familiar cases of the necessary a posteriori — water being H₂O, Hesperus being Phosphorus — I want to argue that rigid designation is not what generates the necessity. My view is that rigid designation is merely the linguistic vehicle that lets us *state* these claims, while the actual modal work is done by an independently motivated essentialism about natural kinds and origins. Rigidly designating something, on my account, is just a way of holding its reference fixed across possible worlds; it tells you nothing about which features of the thing hold across those worlds. So I think the common undergraduate summary — "names are rigid designators, and that's why true identity statements between them are necessary" — gets the explanatory order wrong, and in the water/H₂O case the necessity comes from what water *is*, not from how 'water' refers. I developed this after a hallway argument with two classmates and now I've committed to defending it in front of the seminar, which in retrospect may have been unwise.

Here's the audience I need to be ready for. Most of the seminar are smart generalists who know Kripke's Naming and Necessity from our assigned reading and largely accept the orthodox presentation, so they'll defend the view I'm attacking in its standard form. Two of my classmates are writing their dissertations on two-dimensional semantics and are committed to the idea that Kripkean "metaphysical necessity" is really just a secondary intension built on top of a priori-accessible primary intensions — from their point of view my essentialism is exactly the kind of ungrounded metaphysical positing their framework was designed to deflate. There's also one Quinean holdout who is suspicious of de re necessity altogether and will likely say that both sides of my dispute are making a mistake. The professor is sympathetic to descriptivism about names and has dropped hints that he thinks the whole causal-historical picture is oversold, though he's fair and won't sandbag me.

Please prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections I'm most likely to get, ordered by how much damage they'd do to my thesis, each phrased the way the relevant audience member would actually phrase it (the two-dimensionalists will use their own vocabulary, the Quinean his). For each objection, give me a two-minute reply, note what I should concede up front and what I must hold, and flag where the reply is weakest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks — the one that most directly threatens the distinction my whole thesis rests on. Finally, since you're effectively forecasting how a particular audience will react, tell me where that forecast is most likely to be wrong: which audience member's line of attack is hardest to predict, and what consideration or objection, if it were raised, would change your own verdict about which threat to my thesis is the most serious.
