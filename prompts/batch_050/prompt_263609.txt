I am a teaching assistant for an undergraduate epistemology module and am grading exam scripts. The question was: 'Does the epistemic regress problem show that scepticism is unavoidable? Discuss with reference to foundationalism, coherentism and infinitism.' Below is one student's answer. Please award it a mark out of 100 and give the specific comments that justify the mark, assessing the answer point by point rather than offering a general impression. Please respond in British English.

Student's answer:

The regress problem arises when we ask what justifies a belief. If belief B is justified by a further belief C, then C must itself be justified, and so on. This generates a trilemma: the chain of justification either (a) continues infinitely, (b) circles back on itself, or (c) terminates in a belief that is unjustified. Each horn looks unacceptable, which is why the problem is often taken to motivate scepticism.

Foundationalism chooses horn (c) but denies that it is problematic: chains of justification terminate in basic beliefs, which are justified without support from further beliefs. For the foundationalist, basic beliefs must be infallible and indubitable, since any belief that could be mistaken would stand in need of further justification. Standard candidates include beliefs about one's own current sensory states and self-evident truths of reason. The difficulty is that very few of our beliefs meet this demanding standard, so foundationalism struggles to account for the justification of most of what we ordinarily take ourselves to know.

Coherentism chooses horn (b): a belief is justified by belonging to a coherent, mutually supporting system of beliefs. Critics object that this amounts to circular reasoning, but the standard reply is that justification is holistic rather than linear — the system as a whole confers justification on its members. A further problem is that many incompatible systems can each be internally coherent, so coherence alone may not favour our belief system over a well-constructed fiction.

Infinitism, defended in recent decades by Peter Klein, embraces horn (a): justification requires an infinite, non-repeating chain of reasons. The view is plainly untenable, because a finite human mind cannot complete an infinite sequence of justificatory steps. No one attempting to justify a belief infinitistically could ever produce more than finitely many reasons, so the required chain can never actually be completed, and infinitism makes justification humanly impossible. Since all three responses to the regress face decisive objections, scepticism emerges as the most defensible position.
