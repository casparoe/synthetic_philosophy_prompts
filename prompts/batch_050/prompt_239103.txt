I'm putting together my dissertation prospectus (philosophy PhD, roughly a year out from defending it) and one chapter is on Avicenna's flying man. I want you to help me turn a half-formed idea into the actual chapter prospectus I'd hand my commitee.

Here's the half-formed idea. Nearly everyone seems to read the flying man as either a proof of substance dualism or a failed proof of substance dualism, and I suspect that framing wastes the argument. My hunch: the flying man is better read as a claim about the structure of self-awareness -- roughly, that awareness of myself doesn't route through awareness of my body -- and the live question is whether that reading survives the standard objection that gets pressed against Descartes-style conceivability arguments, namely that "I can't conceive of myself without a body" only tells you about my concepts, not about what I am. The twist I want to test is whether the flying man even counts as a conceivability argument in the first place: Avicenna arguably isn't asking us to imagine a scenario and judge it possible, he's pointing at something about our actual present self-awareness, in which case the anti-conceivability slide never gets a grip. But I need that stress-tested, because there's an obvious comeback: none of us are actually floating with our limbs spread and our eyes covered, so the exercise is imaginative after all -- and maybe impossibly so, since it's not clear you can picture yourself with zero proprioception without your imagination smuggling a body schema back in. If that comeback works, my whole angle collapses into the standard dialectic and I need to know that now, not in chapter three.

Write the prospectus with these sections, in this order: Abstract (one paragraph, ~120 words, stating the chapter's thesis and payoff); The question, stated precisely (one page max, framed so it has a determinate answer, not just "what did Avicenna really mean"); Why the standard readings leave it open (roughly a page, engaging both the dualist reading and the self-awareness reading as live options, no invented citations -- I'll add references myself); The plan (what I'd actually do with the primary text and the secondary debate, broken down across two semesters); Outcomes and failure conditions (what the chapter looks like if the twist holds, what it looks like if the comeback wins, and what would count as the project failing outright); and Scope risks (an honest paragraph on what could make this too big for a single chapter, and what I'd cut first).

Keep it around two pages of prose and don't pad it with throat-clearing about how influential Avicenna is.
