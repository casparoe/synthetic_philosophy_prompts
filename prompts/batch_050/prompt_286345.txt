I'm working on a paper about the moral asymmetry between suffering and happiness, and I keep hitting the same frustration: positions in this area seem to be adopted by brandishing a single intuition — the agony that no amount of bliss could excuse, or the claim that enough mild headaches obviously add up to something torture-like — rather than by meeting any shared standard. Before I commit to a view, I want the standard itself written out. So: write the desiderata that any adequate theory of the comparative moral weight of pleasure and pain must meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether it strikes you as non-negotiable or as a camp-specific prejudice dressed up as common sense.

The list should cover, at minimum: how the theory must handle aggregation in both directions (many trivial pains versus one severe pain, and vast pleasure versus severe pain); whether it must respect transitivity of moral betterness and what goes wrong if trade-off judgements cycle; whether it owes us an explanation of the felt discontinuity between ordinary discomfort and agony rather than simply stipulating one; what exactly a claim like "no pleasure compensates for this suffering" has to mean — no pleasure at all, or no finite amount — and what each reading commits the theory to when outcomes are uncertain rather than guaranteed; whether it can distinguish the badness of a pain from its moral priority for action without collapsing the two; and whether it must remain consistent with the plausible phenomenological point that pleasure and pain do not present themselves as positions on a single shared felt scale. If some subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly and show why — that is the part I most care about, since an impossible specification is itself a result about the debate. Also tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding contenders.

Then, briefly and only after the criteria are settled, score three views against the list: classical total utilitarianism, an unrestricted negative utilitarianism on which suffering counts and pleasure does not, and a lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism on which suffering below some severity can be offset by pleasure while suffering above it cannot. For each failure, note whether the view can bite the bullet with dignity or whether the failure looks like a genuine refutation. Please keep the emphasis on the requirements and their motivating cases rather than the scorecard — I can do the scoring myself once the standard is clear. Write the answer in British English.
