I am a retired experimental physicist who has lately been reading philosophy of mind, and I have run into a dispute that I find genuinely puzzling. In his 1959 book Dreaming, Norman Malcolm argued, following Wittgenstein, that a dream is not an experience had while asleep at all: there are no independent criteria for what a sleeper is experiencing during sleep, so the dream report made on waking is, on his view, all there is to the dream -- the question of when during the night the experience occurred is, he thinks, without sense. Daniel Dennett, in his 1976 paper Are Dreams Experiences?, defended a related but distinct picture, sometimes called the cassette theory: dream narratives may be composed and stored in advance and loaded into memory at the moment of waking, so that what feel like long, temporally extended dream experiences may never have been experienced at all, only remembered. Against both stands the naive view I had always assumed: dreams are conscious episodes, temporally located in the night, which we later recall more or less well.

I would like Christopher Nolan's Inception read through this dispute. The film is, I take it, committed to the naive view almost throughout: Cobb and his team share experiences inside dreams, converse, plan, suffer, and form memories of what happens there, and the time-dilation conceit -- minutes up here, years down there -- presupposes that dream experience has a real duration to dilate. But several elements seem to me to cut the other way. The dreamers can never recall how a dream began; Cobb presses this on Ariadne as a diagnostic of dreaming, and it looks rather like the kind of fact Dennett's composition-on-waking picture would predict, since a precanned narrative need only supply a middle. The architect's job of fabricating a world in advance, stocked with detail the dreamer accepts without noticing its construction, resembles a cassette being prepared for insertion. And Mal's fatal conviction that her experienced world is a dream is precisely a crisis about which of two rival narratives to trust, not about which of two experiences she is having.

So my questions are these. First, work through the film's central devices -- dream-sharing, the inability to remember a dream's beginning, time dilation, the kick, limbo -- and say for each whether it makes better sense on the naive view, on Malcolm's view, or on Dennett's, and where a device simply refuses to sit on any of the three. Second, consider the ending: Cobb's spinning top, and his walking away from it without waiting to see it fall. Malcolm holds that there is no fact of the matter, beyond the report, about whether a seeming-experience occurred in sleep; does the film's final gesture endorse something like that, replacing the question is this real with the question does Cobb still need an answer, or is that a sentimental evasion of the question the film spent two hours raising? Third, and most important to me: the film assumes a realist answer to the very question Malcolm thought ill-formed -- it treats where Cobb is as a matter of fact that the top can detect. Is Inception thereby a straightforward counter-argument to Malcolm in fictional dress, or does it expose what makes his position so hard to refute, namely that no test conducted from inside an experience can settle whether the experience was had? Please take the philosophy as seriously as the film: I do not want Inception used as a mere peg on which to hang a summary of two articles, nor the articles flattened so the film seems to illustrate them. Where the film resists the theories, or the theories make the film look naive, say so plainly, and close by telling me what, if anything, a cinematic treatment contributes to this dispute that the journal articles cannot -- or whether it contributes only confusion.
