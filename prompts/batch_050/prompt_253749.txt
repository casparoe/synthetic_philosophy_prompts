The exam question was:

"Describe the Tarskian hierarchical response to the Liar paradox and Kripke's fixed-point theory of truth, and explain how each fares against the strengthened Liar ('this sentence is not true'). Then state the dialetheist response and what is usually taken to be its main cost."

The student's answer:

"The Liar paradox comes from the T-schema ('P' is true iff P) together with a sentence that refers to itself. Tarski's answer is to forbid a language from containing its own truth predicate: the truth predicate for a language L has to live in a metalanguage, so no sentence of L can predicate truth of itself, and he showed that this lets us make natural languages like English consistent by sorting their expressions into levels. Kripke keeps a single language with its own truth predicate but builds the truth predicate up in stages starting from grounded sentences; in the minimal fixed point the Liar is ungrounded and receives no truth value rather than a contradictory one. Kripke's theory also handles the strengthened Liar without further trouble, since 'this sentence is not true' is ungrounded as well, so we can simply conclude that it is not true, which is just what the sentence says of itself. Dialetheism goes the opposite way and accepts that the Liar is both true and false. Since it happily accepts contradictions, it is the one theory on the table with no revenge problem at all; its main cost is that it forces the abandonment of classical logic, which many find too high a price."
