I am preparing a graduate seminar on Du Bois's aesthetics and have run into an interpretive difficulty I would like help working through. The text is the address he gave in June 1926 at the Spingarn Medal celebration for Carter G. Woodson, published in The Crisis, Vol. 32, October 1926, pp. 290-297, under the title "Criteria of Negro Art." Midway through, Du Bois imagines his audience granted everything the movement is fighting for, and writes:

"If you tonight suddenly should become full-fledged Americans; if your color faded, or the color line here in Chicago was miraculously forgotten; suppose, too, you became at the same time rich and powerful; -- what is it that you would want? What would you immediately seek? Would you buy the most powerful of motor cars and outrace Cook County? Would you buy the most elaborate estate on the North Shore? Would you be a Rotarian or a Lion or a What-not of the very last degree? Would you wear the most striking clothes, give the richest dinners, and buy the longest press notices?

Even as you visualize such ideals you know in your hearts that these are not the things you really want. You realize this sooner than the average white American because, pushed aside as we have been in America, there has come to us not only a certain distaste for the tawdry and flamboyant but a vision of what the world could be if it were really a beautiful world; if we had the true spirit; if we had the Seeing Eye, the Cunning Hand, the Feeling Heart; if we had, to be sure, not perfect happiness, but plenty of good hard work, the inevitable suffering that always comes with life; sacrifice and waiting, all that -- but, nevertheless, lived in a world where men know, where men create, where they realize themselves and where they enjoy life. It is that sort of a world we want to create for ourselves and for all America."

Just before this passage, Du Bois describes American tourists overrunning the Scottish lake he had known through Scott's poetry, and says of them: "They struck a note not evil but wrong. They carried, perhaps, a sense of strength and accomplishment, but their hearts had no conception of the beauty which pervaded this holy place." And earlier still, he remarks: "We who are dark can see America in a way that white Americans cannot."

Here is my difficulty. In the essay's better-known concluding movement, Du Bois declares that all art is propaganda and insists that Black art must serve truth, justice, and the right rather than beauty for its own sake. My students typically read that conclusion as subordinating aesthetics to politics. But this earlier passage seems to run the argument in the opposite direction: political equality, wealth, and full citizenship are treated as the hollow alternatives, and the beautiful world as that for the sake of which the fight is conducted. So the essay appears to contain both an instrumentalization of art by politics and an instrumentalization of politics by aesthetics, and I am not satisfied with the usual resolution that Du Bois is simply being rhetorically inconsistent.

Could you help me reconstruct the argumentative structure here? Specifically: (1) What is the logical grammar of the hypothetical? Is Du Bois arguing that civic equality is merely a means to the beautiful world, or that the beautiful world is constitutive of what equality is for -- and does the distinction matter for how we read the propaganda claim? (2) When he calls the tourists' conduct "not evil but wrong," what work is the word "wrong" doing? Is this an aesthetic predicate doing moral work, a moral predicate short of evil, or a third category -- and can you construct a coherent account of wrongness-without-evil that would let the essay hold that a society can be just and yet still defective in the way the tourists are? (3) How should we understand the epistemic privilege claimed in "We who are dark can see America in a way that white Americans cannot" -- is the advantage claimed here perceptual (a trained eye for beauty), moral (knowledge of justice gained through injustice), or sociological (a vantage on American ideals unavailable from inside them), and how does it relate to the "second sight" of the double-consciousness passages in his earlier work? (4) Finally, is there a reading on which the propaganda conclusion actually presupposes this earlier vision -- so that propaganda for Du Bois means art in service of a world-picture, and the dispute with the purists is not about whether art serves ends but about which ends are available to be served? I would rather have a careful analysis of where the text resists systematization than a tidy reconciliation imposed on it.
