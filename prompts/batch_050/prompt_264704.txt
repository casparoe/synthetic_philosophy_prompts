I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm drafting a piece on the backlash against philosophical thought experiments — trolley problems, violinists, experience machines, all that. A colleague of mine, who has no patience for any of it, made a debunking argument over coffee that I found really persuasive until I tried to write it up, at which point I noticed it seems to prove way more than he wants it to. Here it is as best I can reconstruct it. (1) A thought experiment gets its grip precisely by stripping a situation down — it throws out almost everything true of real cases and keeps only the handful of features the author chose to keep. (2) Which features get kept is the author's decision, and authors reliably decide in whatever way makes the intended verdict feel obvious; change the incidental details and the audience's intuition flips, which shows the intuition was tracking the packaging, not the problem. (3) A conclusion that depends entirely on which details its presenter chose to include is evidence about the presenter's choices, not about the subject matter. (4) So thought experiments have no evidential value at all — they're rhetoric wearing a narrative costume, and philosophers who treat them as data are fooling themselves. Fine so far, I thought. But then: (5) controlled idealization in science works exactly as described in (1) — frictionless planes, ideal gasses, point masses, infinite populations in evolutionary models, all of them throw out nearly everything and keep a few variables the modeler selected, and the modeler selects them partly because they make the math come out a certain way. (6) So by (3), idealized models are evidence only about the modelers' choices, and physics and evolutionary biology are rhetoric in a lab coat. (7) That's nuts, so something above has to give. Here's my problem: I'm actually pretty sympathetic to my colleague's debunking of the trolley-style cases — I'd love to be able to say in the piece that those particular intuitions are manufactured artifacts — but I obviously can't print an argument whose logic also dissolves the ideal gas law. So where does this go wrong, if it does? Is there a real, principled difference between the physicist's frictionless plane and the philosopher's runaway trolley that lets the argument stop at (4) instead of rolling on to (6)? Or is my colleague only entitled to some much weaker claim, like "thought experiments are easier to rig than models" — and if so, weaker how, exactly? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep the skepticism about trolley intuitions than his sweeping premise (3), but I want to know what that choice costs me.
