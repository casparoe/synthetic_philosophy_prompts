I serve on the drafting committee for a new national charter of rights, and one member keeps pressing Bentham's complaint that natural rights are "nonsense upon stilts" -- that talk of rights existing prior to and independent of law dresses up someone's view of what the law ought to be as if it were a discovery about what already exists. I do not want to concede the point entirely, but I do want to take it seriously. Write a procedure the committee could apply to any proposed clause to determine whether it is vulnerable to the Benthamite objection and, if so, what would be needed to make it survive: the questions to ask in order (Is there an identifiable duty-bearer? Is there a mechanism of enforcement, or at least a specifiable one? Does the clause assert a claim someone could press, or merely express an aspiration? Would anything be lost if it were redrafted as a directive to the legislature?), what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure delivers a verdict, and where it can only flag that the committee faces a genuine disagreement about moral foundations that no drafting technique will settle. Then run the procedure on three candidate clauses: a right not to be arbitrarily detained, a right to adequate housing, and a right of future generations to a minimally intact environment. At least one run should produce a verdict the committee's majority will dislike, and you should not soften it. Close with an honest account of what the procedure assumes Bentham got right, and what it quietly concedes to his opponents by letting a clause pass.
