Ok so my roommate is a philosophy grad student and he hit me with an argument last week that's been bugging me ever since. It's about full-information / ideal-advisor theories of reasons -- the idea that what I have reason to do is whatever some ideally rational, fully informed version of me would want my actual self to do. He thinks the whole approach collapses, and I hate to admit it but the steps each look fine on their own. Roughly as he gave it:

(1) What I have most reason to do = whatever my fully informed, procedurally rational counterpart (call her Me+, though she'd insist on better branding) would want my actual self to do.

(2) Being fully informed includes knowing, vividly and from the inside, exactly what every option open to me would be like -- what it feels like to free solo a cliff, to live as a monk, to raise a kid, whatever.

(3) But that kind of knowledge isn't inert. Actually living through things changes what you want, and vivid total information does the same work: Me+'s desires are partly shaped by experiences no actual deliberator, me included, has ever had or could have without already being transformed by them. So Me+ isn't just me plus some facts. She's a different person in the respects that matter.

(4) If Me+ is a different person in the respects that matter, then what she wants for me could be somthing I could never come to be motivated by, no matter how carefully I reflect from where I actually stand -- her standpoint just isn't reachable from mine by any deliberation I could actually carry out.

(5) A consideration can only be a reason for me if it could motivate me via some possible route of good deliberation starting from where I actually am.

(6) So nothing that depends on Me+'s transformed standpoint is a reason for me. But on (1), all of my reasons run through her standpoint. So the account implies I have no reasons at all -- and since every actual agent is in the same boat, there are no reasons for anyone who isn't already ideal.

His verdict: the ideal-advisor picture proves too much, scrap it. I'd really like to keep (5) if I can -- a reason that couldn't possibly move me feels like no reason at all -- and I'd like to keep somthing in the neighborhood of (1) too, because where else are reasons supposed to come from? But I can't find the loose thread. Which step do I reject, and why did it look fine in the first place? And what does each repair cost: biting the bullet on (5), reformulating the idealization in (1), or restricting what "fully informed" in (2) is even allowed to include?
