Having retired from a career in experimental biology, I now help design assessments for an interdisciplinary honors seminar on neuroscience and free will at my local university. I would like one essay examination question on Libet-style experiments and their implications for free will, answerable in 75 minutes, that cannot be answered merely by reciting the standard timeline of the readiness potential and the reported onset of conscious intention. The question should instead force students to confront what the experiments could actually establish: for instance, whether showing that a brain state predicts an arbitrary, reason-free urge bears on actions chosen for reasons; whether a capacity to veto an initiated act rescues or abandons the notion of conscious will; and whether a predictive correlation observed before a reported intention warrants the inference that the decision was causally settled at that point. Please supply the question itself, a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 that identifies the specific moves separating an excellent answer from a competent one — including at least three common errors that should cost marks, with a brief note on why each is an error. Kindly write everything in American English.
