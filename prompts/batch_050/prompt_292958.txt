I am a materials engineer responsible for certifying two safety products, and I am in a dispute with a colleague about how the certification paperwork should describe them. I would like help thinking it through.

Product 1 is a glass-lined chemical flask. The glass liner is of a kind that ordinarily shatters when dropped onto concrete. However, the flask ships inside a fitted foam-and-steel casing that absorbs impacts, and in our drop tests the liner has never broken. If you removed the casing and dropped the bare liner, it would break on the first try.

Product 2 is an experimental "smart" flask made of a polymer with embedded sensors. In its resting state the polymer is brittle: a sharp blow to a resting sample cracks it. But the sensors register the vibration signature of an incoming impact and, within milliseconds, activate a heating element that softens the polymer, so that by the time the blow lands the material flexes rather than cracks. In every drop test so far, the flask has survived. If the sensor or heater fails, or if the impact arrives too quickly to be detected, the flask shatters.

Our certification standard says only: "must not break when dropped from two meters onto concrete." Both products pass. My colleague argues that a pass is a pass, that the certificate should describe both as "unbreakable when dropped," and that any further qualification is pointless pedantry. Marketing agrees with him. I objected that the two products are genuinely different, but when pressed I found it difficult to say exactly what the difference consists in — after all, it seems true of both that if they were dropped, they would not break.

So, my questions. First, is my colleague right? If not, what exactly distinguishes the two cases, and does the distinction matter for how the certificate should read, or only for the fine print? Second, consider the marketing line proposed for Product 2: "never breaks when dropped." This has been true in every test to date and may well remain true forever. Is there nonetheless something misleading or otherwise defective about printing it? Third, looking forward, I would like to revise the certification standard itself. Is there a principled way to write such requirements so that they distinguish products that genuinely withstand the dangerous condition from products that merely ensure the condition never gets a fair trial? I would welcome both conceptual clarification and practical suggestions.
