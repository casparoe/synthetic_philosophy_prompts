In my graduate seminar on the philosophy of mathematics, a student defended an argument that has been bothering me for weeks, because it seems to prove far more than any of us can accept. I will reconstruct it as fairly as I can. (1) Any first-order theory that has an infinite model has a countably infinite model; this is a theorem, not a conjecture. (2) Assuming first-order set theory is consistent, it has a model, and hence a countable one, M. (3) M satisfies the sentence asserting the existence of an uncountable set, even though M contains only countably many objects. (4) The only way to reconcile this is that what M calls uncountable is uncountable only relative to M: the enumeration witnessing its countability exists outside M but is unavailable inside it. (5) Whatever we, working mathematicians, can determinately say about sets is exhausted by the first-order theory we could in principle write down; we have no access to any structure beyond what our theory pins down. (6) So our own talk of the uncountable inherits the relativity: there is no absolute fact of the matter that the real numbers are uncountable, only that no enumeration of them appears within our theory. (7) If that is right, then Cantor's theorem does not establish anything absolute about the sizes of infinity; it establishes only an internal unrepresentability claim, and the entire received picture of a hierarchy of infinite cardinalities is an artifact of our standpoint. (8) Worse, the conclusion is stated in a metalanguage that says M is countable, and by the argument's own lights that metalanguage cannot determinately express countability either, since it too is just another first-order theory subject to the same theorem -- so the conclusion saws off the branch it sits on.

My student takes this to show that first-order foundations collapse into a kind of cardinality relativism that even its proponents cannot coherently state. I suspect something has slipped between (4) and (6), but when pressed I cannot say exactly what, and (5) in particular has a plausible learnability motivation: a language that could only be mastered by finite beings, the thought goes, cannot latch onto anything its sentences do not fix. Which step should I reject, and why does that step look so natural given only elementary model theory? Please also price the exits: what it costs to deny that our mathematical language is exhausted by a first-order theory, what it costs to insist on an absolute, standpoint-independent notion of countability while keeping first-order set theory as one's foundation, and what it costs to embrace the relativist conclusion but deny that it is self-undermining. If forced, I would rather preserve the absolute reading of Cantor's theorem than preserve premise (5), but I want to know what I am paying either way.
