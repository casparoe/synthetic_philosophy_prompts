For several semesters my graduate seminar in rational choice has stalled over a version of Newcomb’s problem that I call Ledger Newcomb, and I would like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another defense of one-boxing or two-boxing.

The setup is this. Before the agent chooses, the predictor has already placed either nothing or a large sum in the opaque box, while the transparent box always contains a small sum. The predictor makes no causal intervention after the deposit, and everyone in the seminar accepts that there is no backward causation. The unusual feature is that the predictor is highly reliable in a specifically even-handed way: it is equally accurate about agents who arrive at one-boxing and agents who arrive at two-boxing, because it predicts the final choice from the agent’s whole deliberative pattern rather than from the presence of any one argument. The agents know all of this. They also know that some two-boxers leave rich because the predictor expected them to two-box only half-heartedly, while some one-boxers leave poor because the predictor saw through their last-minute evidential rhetoric. Still, conditional on actually being predicted to one-box, one-boxing does better; conditional on either prediction already being fixed, two-boxing adds the small sum.

The room divides into four camps. The causalists say that, at the moment of choice, the money is where it is, so taking both boxes is the only act that dominates; anything else mistakes a welcome correlation for a lever. The evidentialists reply that the agent does not know which world she is in, and choosing both boxes is excellent evidence that she is in the unfavorable one; rational choice should be sensitive to what the act indicates, not only to what it causes. The policy theorists insist that both sides are answering the wrong question: the rational unit is not the token act but the disposition or deliberative policy that makes one the sort of agent the predictor rewards, so the real choice was effectively made earlier, when one became a certain kind of deliberator. Finally, the quietists say the predicate rational is being applied equivocally to an act, a policy, a character trait, and an ex post outcome, and that once these are separated the paradox dissolves rather than gets solved.

My own hypothesis is that the dispute is not primarily empirical, because all sides concede the predictor’s reliability, nor purely formal, because each camp can reproduce the others’ calculations. I suspect it concerns the proper object and temporal standpoint of rational assessment: guidance at the moment of action versus evaluation of the agent one has become. But I am not confident this explains why the camps keep talking past one another even after accepting the same ledger of results.

Please diagnose the disagreement. Is it substantive or partly verbal? If substantive, locate the precise fork: facts, standards of evidence, the individuation of acts and policies, the reference class for evidential support, or rival bedrock intuitions about guidance and control. If partly verbal, say which shared term is doing the damage and how to regiment it without trivializing the dispute. Then state what, given your diagnosis, would count as genuine progress: a distinction, a theorem, a decision-procedure constraint, an experiment about deliberation, or an argument that no resolution should be expected. Avoid a survey of familiar positions; engage the structure of this particular stalemate.
