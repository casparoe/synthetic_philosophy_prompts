Write a satirical excerpt from a fictional self-help bestseller: the introduction to a book called Live Like the Wasp: The Last Productivity System You Will Ever Need. Aim for roughly 700–900 words, in American English, in the breathless, confiding, second-person-heavy voice of a peak-performance guru addressing burned-out professionals.

The conceit is as follows. The author has discovered the digger wasp, which hunts by paralyzing a cricket, dragging it to the entrance of its burrow, leaving it at the threshold, ducking inside to check that all is well, and only then emerging to pull the cricket in. Naturalists have observed that if someone nudges the cricket a short distance away while the wasp is underground, the wasp comes up, retrieves the cricket, deposits it at the threshold once more, and goes inside to inspect the burrow all over again — and this can be repeated on the same wasp many times, without the wasp ever simply seizing the cricket where it now lies and dragging it straight in. Where others see a disturbing mechanical loop, the author sees the apex of existence: an organism with zero decision fatigue, zero procrastination, perfect consistency between trigger and response, and no energy wasted on deliberation. He has built his entire system on becoming more like her.

The humor must come from taking the genre's real logic to completion rather than inventing absurdities. Mainstream productivity writing already promises to automate behavior, script the morning, eliminate choice, and free the mind by making action frictionless; this author simply keeps every one of those promises in full, with a straight face. He should celebrate habits that never misfire, routines with no exits, and a calendar so complete that surprise is structurally impossible, all in the genre's actual idiom — its testimonials, its numbered laws, its conspiratorial questions to the reader.

Include one passage where the author anticipates the obvious reader objection — what happens when circumstances change? — and answers it with serene confidence by describing his contingency protocol, which is itself a single fixed routine that would fail in exactly the wasp's way, though he narrates it as his system's crown jewel. End with his recommended daily sequence rendered as a checklist whose shape unmistakably mirrors the wasp's threshold ritual, presented as the book's gift to the reader.

Constraints: stay deadpan throughout; the author never winks, never doubts himself, and never uses the words free will, determinism, or philosophy. Nothing should mark the piece as satire except the logic itself. By the final line, though, an attentive reader should feel the genuine question pressing through the comedy: whether a life of flawlessly optimized routine is liberation from deliberation or merely a more elaborate rigidity — and what, if anything, distinguishes the reader's scripted morning from the wasp's forty-first trip back to the threshold.
