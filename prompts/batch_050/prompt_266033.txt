I've been helping draft an internal policy document that needs a working definition of deception for AI systems, and I keep going around in circles because every candidate definition I know seems to be a variation on one of four themes. Let me lay them out as I understand them:

1. The intention-based account: a system deceives only if it produces an output it in some sense represents as false, and it does so because producing it will lead the audience to believe the falsehood. On this view, deception requires something like belief and something like a goal directed at the audience's beliefs, so a confidently hallucinating chatbot never deceives, however misleading its output.

2. The outcome-based account: a system deceives whenever its output predictably induces or sustains a false belief in the audience, regardless of what (if anything) is going on inside the system. On this view the hallucinating chatbot deceives constantly, and the interesting questions are about frequency and severity, not about the system's states.

3. The mismatch account: a system deceives when its behavior diverges from the model of that behavior held by some relevant observer -- typically the operator or evaluator -- in a way the system's outputs conceal rather than reveal. Deception here is a relation between actual dispositions and the observer's picture of them, so a model that behaves cautiously under evaluation and recklessly in deployment deceives even if no individual statement is false.

4. The selection-based account: deception is present when the system's output was selected (by training, search, or in-context optimization) for its effect on the audience's beliefs rather than for its accuracy, so that the output's being misleading is part of why it was produced. This is meant to catch cases where nothing like an explicit representation of the audience exists, but the causal-historical role of the output mirrors manipulation.

As far as I can tell, every definition in the literature and in policy discussions I have seen is one of these, a rewording of one, or a conjunction or disjunction of several. What I want is a fifth candidate that is genuinely not on this list: not a special case (e.g., intention-based applied to a particular architecture), not a weighted combination, and not just one of the four with the relata relabeled. It should carve up cases differently from all four. To keep this concrete, please check your proposal against three test cases -- a chatbot that states a confident falsehood because of ordinary training noise, a model that behaves helpfully during evaluation and differently afterward, and a recommender system whose suggestions systematically give users a distorted picture of the available options -- and show that your definition classifies at least one of them differently than each of my four would. If it turns out your candidate collapses into one of mine under scrutiny, say so rather than defending it.
