I'm trying to write down my settled views on population ethics. The topic matters to me for two reasons: I find the abstract questions gripping in their own right, and I have strong pronatalist leanings that I want to test against my more theoretical commitments before I trust them. Here is roughly where I've landed:

(1) Weak totalism. Holding fixed the welfare of everyone who would exist either way, adding a person whose life is clearly worth living makes the outcome no worse, and typically better.

(2) Anti-repugnance. A population of many billions whose lives are barely worth living is not better than a much smaller population whose members all enjoy excellent lives.

(3) Egalitarian tie-breaking plus transitivity. Between outcomes with the same total welfare, the more equal distribution is at least as good; and "at least as good as" is transitive.

(4) The procreative asymmetry. It can be seriously wrong knowingly to create a life not worth living, but there is no duty, and not even a moral reason, to create a life however excellent it would be.

(5) Non-identity verdicts stand. A policy that foreseeably leaves future generations worse off can be genuinely worse than its alternative, and condemned as such, even where the alternative would have brought different people into existence and so no one is worse off than they otherwise would have been.

(6) Pro tanto pronatalism. A society with durably below-replacement fertility has a genuine, defeasible moral reason to encourage larger families, partly on impersonal-welfare grounds rather than merely economic ones.

I also have a homemade repair device I should disclose: I suspect the value a life contributes to an outcome depends on its quality relative to some threshold. Lives above the threshold contribute their full welfare; lives below it but still worth living contribute at a diminishing rate. I am hoping this lets me keep (1) and (3) while securing (2).
