I'm a second-year philosophy undergraduate, and in my philosophy of language seminar we just finished a unit on the sorites paradox. Our professor presented epistemicism — the view associated with Timothy Williamson — as one of the serious solutions on offer: vague predicates like "heap," "bald," and "tall" really do draw sharp boundaries, and the paradox dissolves once we accept that these boundaries exist even though we don't know where they lie. On this view, there is a genuine fact of the matter about whether the 9,999th grain turns a non-heap into a heap; it's simply a fact we haven't discovered yet.

Here's where I'm stuck. If the boundary genuinely exists, then vagueness looks like a purely empirical problem, and empirical problems yield to better measurement. When astronomers didn't know the value of some constant, they built better telescopes; nobody concluded the constant was unknowable. So why couldn't we locate the sharp boundary of "heap" in principle — say, by aggregating the considered judgments of enough competent speakers, or mining a large enough corpus of usage, or running sufficiently fine-grained psycholinguistic experiments — and thereby determine that the heap/non-heap line falls exactly here rather than there? If the epistemicist replies that we can't find it, that sounds less like a deep philosophical point and more like an oddly defeatist attitude toward what is essentially a data-collection problem. I asked my professor about this and she said something brief about the boundary depending on our own dispositions, which I confess I didn't follow.

A related worry occurred to me while reading for a constitutional law class. If vague terms all have sharp thresholds, then legal vagueness should be eliminable in the same spirit: "reasonable doubt," "reckless disregard," and "cruel and unusual" would all have true precise extensions that courts simply need to investigate more carefully, perhaps with survey evidence about community usage. Some of my classmates found this suggestion obviously confused but couldn't articulate why. What kind of fact is the boundary supposed to be on the epistemicist picture, and why would the measurement strategies I've described fail to reveal it — or would they?
