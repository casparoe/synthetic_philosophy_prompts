I've been trying to sort out my intuitions about the paradox of blackmail — the puzzle of why it can be a crime to threaten to do something you have a perfect legal right to do (publish true information, file a lawsuit, walk off the job) if the threat is paired with a demand for money. Here are my gut verdicts on cases. Demanding $10,000 from a stranger in exchange for not telling his wife about his affair: clearly wrong. Telling a restaurant I'll post a truthful one-star review unless they refund a genuinely bad meal: pushy but fine. A lawyer threatening to file a meritorious lawsuit unless the other side settles: completely fine, it's how litigation works. A union threatening an otherwise lawful strike unless wages rise: fine. A tabloid quietly offering a celebrity the chance to pay to kill a true story: wrong, maybe worse than the affair case. A witness demanding payment in exchange for not reporting a crime to the police: wrong. What principle, if any, captures all or most of these? I'm especially stuck on the pair of the lawyer and the stranger — both are threatening to do something they're entitled to do unless paid — and on whether the strike case really belongs with the lawyer or whether my approval of it is doing the work. Then apply whatever principle you land on to the case I can't settle: a laid-off engineer who tells her former employer she'll send truthful, non-confidential documentation of safety violations to regulators unless they raise her severance. Blackmail, hard bargaining, or something else?
