I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting a long feature on a puzzle that sits awkwardly between economics and epistemology: why financial markets, which are supposed to be the crown jewels of applied rationality, seem to be something theory says shouldn't exist. The standard results I have in mind are the no-trade theorem in the tradition of Milgrom and Stokey -- roughly, that rational agents with common priors and common knowledge of rationality will not trade with one another on the basis of private information -- together with the older adverse-selection logic of Akerlof's market for lemons, where the mere willingness of a counterparty to deal is itself bad news. To organize the piece, I wrote down the strongest version of the skeptical argument I could build, and it seems to prove that my own profession's subject matter -- a global market turning over trillions daily -- cannot exist. Here it is. (1) A voluntary trade occurs only if each party expects to be made better off by it. (2) When rationality is common knowledge, a counterparty's willingness to trade is itself evidence: if an informed, rational agent is eager to sell me an asset at this price, the most natural explanation is that the asset is worth less than the price. (3) A rational agent updates on that evidence before accepting, which lowers her willingness to pay; but her hesitation is likewise visible to the seller, who updates in turn. (4) Iterating this mutual updating, every proposed price unravels: each side reads the other's acceptance as proof the deal favors the other side, until no price survives at which both would transact. (5) Therefore, among rational agents with common knowledge of rationality, there is essentially no trading. (6) But there plainly is trading, on a staggering scale. (7) So either market participants are systematically irrational, or the equilibrium-and-common-knowledge machinery is the wrong tool for describing real markets.

The trouble is that the conclusion feels too strong in the wrong way -- an argument that proves this much has presumably proved too much, and I suspect the rot is somewhere in (2) through (4), but I can't localize it. Please tell me which premise or step you would reject, and be specific about the diagnosis: if there is an equivocation, a slide between different readings of a premise, or an assumption smuggled in about why agents want to trade in the first place, name it and explain why that step looked innocuous. I would also like the alternatives priced. If I have to sacrifice something, I'd most like to keep (1) and (6) -- giving up gains-from-trade as the motive for exchange, or denying the existence of markets, seems like curing the disease by killing the patient -- but I want to know what retaining them costs: does rescuing trade require abandoning common priors, importing unexplained differences in beliefs or risk appetite, positing traders who transact for reasons orthogonal to information (liquidity, hedging, differing needs), or treating real traders as boundedly rational in a way that does the explanatory work? Finally, I'd like to know how the defenders of the no-trade result itself would respond -- my understanding is that they would insist the theorem was never about all trade but only about a certain kind of trade, so please tell me exactly what restriction of scope saves the theorem, and whether that restriction leaves the argument above as a genuine critique of how the result gets popularized (including, perhaps, in articles like the one I am writing) rather than of the result itself.
