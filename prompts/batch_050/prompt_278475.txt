I teach a small graduate seminar on mathematical methodology, aimed at a mixed audience of philosophy and mathematics students, and I am putting together the take-home final. One recurring theme of the course has been heuristic reasoning in number theory: the practice of estimating how often some arithmetic event occurs by pretending the integers behave randomly and treating distinct arithmetic events as independent — what we have been calling the presumption of independence — and then repairing the estimate when the presumption fails. The students have seen Cramér-style random models of the primes and the Hardy–Littlewood correction factors, but in every exercise the corrections were handed to them; they never had to decide for themselves when the presumption is legitimate in the first place. That decision is what I want the exam to test, because it is where the philosophy actually lives.

Please write one substantial essay-cum-problem question built around the following three scenarios, which the question should present to students: (i) the pair (n, n+1), for which naive independence predicts infinitely many cases where both are prime, while in fact there is exactly one; (ii) the pair (n²+1, n²+4), which looks like two independent nearby-prime events but in fact can essentially never both be prime, because one of them is always divisible by 5 (the obstruction is hidden and students must find it unaided); and (iii) the triple (n, n+2, n+6), where naive independence gives the right rate of growth but the wrong constant, because of a partial correlation at the prime 3. The question should ask students, in connected prose, to: compute what the naive presumption predicts in each case; diagnose exactly where and why independence fails in each; state a general criterion for when the presumption of independence is justified (ideally recognizing that independence is exact precisely when the events are fixed by congruences at pairwise coprime moduli, via the Chinese remainder theorem, and that corrections are needed precisely when the events share a small modulus as a common cause); and finally discuss whether this criterion can itself be fully formalized or remains a heuristic, and what the pattern of successes and failures implies about the epistemic status of heuristic reasoning — in particular whether the corrected, constant-adjusted heuristic rests on the very presumption it corrects.

I would like your response organized into exactly the following sections, in this order. First, "The Question Paper" (roughly 250 words): the question exactly as students would see it, including a brief preamble for students, the three scenarios, the tasks, the mark allocation (out of 40), and a stated time allowance of two hours. Second, "Model Answer" (roughly 600–700 words): what a genuinely excellent answer looks like, written in connected prose as a student would write it, including the correct computations and the diagnosis of the hidden mod-5 obstruction. Third, "Marking Scheme" (roughly 400 words): a breakdown of the 40 marks that names the specific moves an A-level answer must contain (for instance, finding the mod-5 obstruction unprompted, computing the local correction factor at 3 for the triple case, and articulating the common-cause criterion rather than merely gesturing at correlation), and that explicitly lists the identifiable errors that should cost marks (for instance, assuming correlations can only lower a predicted constant, or conflating statistical independence within a random model with the deterministic truth about integers). The marking scheme should make it possible for a grader to see precisely what separates an A from a B.
