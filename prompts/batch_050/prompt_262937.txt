I do freelance editing for a couple of pop-science publishers and a small natural history museum, and I keep running into the same fight with authors: how much "information" language about DNA can we let through. Everyone writes stuff like the genome is a blueprint, genes carry instructions, a gene "for" cystic fibrosis, cells "read" the code, epigenetic marks "reprogram" expression. Some of this strikes me as fine shorthand and some of it strikes me as actually wrong, but I can't articulate the line, and my gut reactions are inconsistant -- I'll flag "blueprint of life" and then wave through "the gene for BRCA1-related breast cancer" without being able to say why.

So here's what I want: a checklist or decision procedure I can run on any sentence of this kind, grounded in what the philosophy-of-biology debate about whether DNA is really a code actually establishes, as opposed to vibes. My rough understanding is that there's a narrow sense in which the genetic code genuinely is a code (triplet codons map to amino acids via a rule that could have been otherwise), but that this gets stretched when people talk as if the genome encodes the whole organism, or a trait, the way a message encodes a meaning -- and that critics say at that point the "information" is doing no real work, since the developmental environment carries just as much. Please turn that dispute, corrected if I've garbled it, into ordered steps: what questions to ask about a sentence, in what order, what answers mean the sentence passes, what answers mean it has to be rewritten, and where the procedure bottoms out in an editorial judgment call that no checklist can settle. It needs to be runnable by a copyeditor with no bio background in a few minutes per sentence.

Then apply it to at least four concrete sentences: the genome is the blueprint of life; she carries the gene for cystic fibrosis; epigenetic changes reprogram the cell; and one more you think is the hardest borderline case. I'd like at least one worked application where the procedure tells me to cut or rewrite something I would have let through, so I can see where my instincts are miscalibrated, and a short note on where the whole procedure breaks down -- e.g., contexts like headlines or museum wall text where there's no room for the careful version.
