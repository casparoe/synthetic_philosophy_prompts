ok so there's an assumption that floats around in ai-strategy and effective altruism circles, sometimes called the strategy-stealing assumption: roughly, for any plan the "other side" could carry out to get what they want, your side could adopt enough of the same plan to capture at least as much of what you want. people wheel it out to justify all sorts of things -- taking a job you find iffy because if you dont take it someone worse will and at least you can steer from the inside, earning-to-give in industries you disapprove of, matching an opponent's tactics while calling it value-neutral. i don't want to litigate whether the assumption is true. i want you to make fun of it.

Write a satirical piece, around 500-650 words, in the form of a chapter from a business self-help bestseller aimed at ambitious young altruists -- give it a cheesy title like "Steal This Strategy: The Winner's Guide to Letting Your Enemies Do the Planning." Deadpan guru voice, numbered principles, one invented anecdote about a client whose career the guru transformed, maybe a little worksheet at the end. The humor has to come from the actual structure of the assumption, not a strawman. Some pressure points you could mine: the assumption says nothing about whose values get loaded into the stolen plan; it quietly requires that you can observe the other side's strategy well enough to steal it (good luck); "at least as good by my lights" does a lot of work while the side effects on everyone else go unmentioned; and both sides can invoke it simultaneously, each thereby assured of victory. The guru should never notice any of this -- the reader has to. Keep it deadpan and legible to someone who's never heard the term.

Then, out of character, add a short note (100-150 words) flagging where the satire stands on things that are genuinely contested: which of the guru's claims are faithful exaggerations of the assumption as its defenders actually state it, and which are distortions you've added for comic effect (e.g. whether proponents apply it to ends or only to means, and whether anyone serious claims it holds in full generality). Don't present one side's critique of the assumption as settled consensus -- if the piece leans on a contested reading, say so.
