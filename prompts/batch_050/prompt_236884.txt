I help run a ~3,000-member housing co-op, and over the last couple years we've started filling some roles by lottery instead of election -- mostly because nobody would run for things like the maintenance committee, and the same six people kept winning everything else anyway. Now the board wants me to draft a standing rule for when we use a lot, when we hold an election, and when we just appoint someone (the accountant, say -- nobody is suggesting we lottery that). People keep saying "like a citizens' assembly" without anyone being able to explain why those are supposed to work, and I want the rule grounded in the actual arguments rather than vibes: what sortition is supposed to be good for (representativeness, breaking up cliques and capture, rotation, legitimizing hard calls) and what elections are supposed to buy you instead (accountability, the ability to kick a bad actor out, a mandate), and where competence just trumps all of it.

So: write me a decision procedure I can run on any role or decision body in about fifteen minutes. The questions in order, what counts as a good answer to each, where the procedure defaults to lottery vs. election vs. appointment, and which questions are genuine judgment calls where it has to stop and hand things back to the board to argue about. It needs to handle explicitly: what to do when the role needs skills the median member lacks (and whether a training period changes the answer), what to do when a bad pick can do real damage before any recall or rotation kicks in, how term length and re-eligibility interact with the choice, and whether the fact that nobody volunteers for a role is itself an argument for sortition or actually a warning sign about the role.

Then run the procedure on three of our real open questions: the seven-seat maintenance oversight commitee (currently by lot, one-year terms), the treasurer (elected, currently a headache), and a one-off five-member panel to decide whether to sell our parking lot to a developer -- a decision members disagree about bitterly and where whoever decides will instantly be accused of being bought. I suspect at least one of these is currently structured wrong, and I'd rather the procedure sometimes give answers I won't like than be tuned to confirm what we already do. Also say what the procedure flat-out can't settle.
