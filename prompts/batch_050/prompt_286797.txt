My reading group (mixed philosophers and bio people, plus a few neither) got stuck for two whole meetings on whether "survival of the fittest" is a tautology, and I noticed everyone kept talking past each other because they'd self-sorted into camps by label. So I want a decision tree / sorting quiz that finds out where someone actually stands on fitness and the tautology objection, purely from their verdicts on concrete cases.

The passage that kicked it all off is from chapter IV of Darwin's On the Origin of Species (6th edition, 1872):

"It has been said that I speak of natural selection as an active power or Deity; but who objects to an author speaking of the attraction of gravity as ruling the movements of the planets? Every one knows what is meant and is implied by such metaphorical expressions; and they are almost necessary for brevity."

Make this the root question of the tree: does Darwin's "it's just a metaphor, like physicists talking about gravity ruling the planets" move settle the worry, or does it dodge it — i.e. is the real complaint that the metaphor is loose talk, or that "the fittest survive" comes out true no matter what happens in nature? The first branch of the tree splits on how people react to this passage.

Then the details:

- 8–10 questions total, root one included. Every question after the root has to be a concrete case, not a conceptual poll. Cases I want worked in (adapt freely): two genetically identical gazelles, one eaten by a lion through sheer bad luck before reproducing — did they differ in fitness?; a moth with perfect camouflage for its forest, killed by a lightning strike before ever mating; a neutral allele that drifts to fixation — did selection happen here?; an organism that out-reproduces its rivals only because a useless trait is stuck to a useful one — is it fitter?; a bacterium carrying a costly resistance gene in an enviroment that never contains antibiotics.
- The items must be answerable by someone who has never heard the word "propensity." No jargon inside the questions; the sorting happens through case verdicts, not through which label sounds nice.
- Leaves (each must be reachable by some answer pattern): (a) fitness is just actual reproductive output, and talk of what would have happened is heuristic fluff; (b) the propensity view — fitness is a disposition or expectation that luck can frustrate; (c) "yes, it's definitional, but harmlessly": the theory's real content is the empirical claim that heritable trait differences cause reproductive differences, and "the fittest survive" was never load-bearing; (d) the statisticalist/instrumentalist view — selection is a pattern in the data and fitness a bookkeeping variable, so the whole causal/circularity fight is misconceived; (e) the grim leaf: the tautology objection lands, naive formulations really are circular, and the theory needs rebuilding rather than reinterpretation. Add leaves if the tree forces them.
- Where a combination of answers corresponds to a position nobody holds (or could coherently hold), mark it and give a one-line diagnosis: incoherent, just unattractive, or genuinely unexplored.
- If two leaves answer every case identically, say so explicitly and tell me what extra question would split them — don't pretend the tree separates what it can't.
- No citations or reading lists, please. Naming the positions is enough.

Format, in this order:
1. Root question — the Darwin item rephrased for the group, with its answer options (~half a page)
2. The tree — the full branching structure, each question with its case(s) and which answers lead where (~1–2 pages)
3. The leaves — each end position named, with a 3–4 sentence sketch of what someone parked there believes (~half page each)
4. Empty branches — the unoccupied answer combinations with a one-line diagnosis each (~half a page)
5. Blind spots — any positions the tree can't tell apart, plus the extra question that would (~half a page)
