Here's what he wrote:

Harsanyi's aggregation theorem is one of the few real proofs in moral philosophy. The setup is that each individual's preferences over uncertain prospects satisfy the standard von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, so each person can be represented as maximizing expected utility, and we also require society's preferences over the same prospects to satisfy those axioms. Add the Pareto condition -- if everyone is indifferent between two prospects, society is too, and if everyone weakly prefers one prospect and someone strictly prefers it, so does society -- and out falls the conclusion that social welfare must be a weighted sum of individual utilities, with equal weights for everyone. This is essentially the same argument as Harsanyi's better-known claim that a rational person choosing principles of justice behind a veil of ignorance would maximize average utility, since not knowing who you'll be forces you to weigh everyone's interests equally. The theorem also vindicates cardinal, interpersonally comparable utility: because the proof delivers a single social utility function built out of individual ones, it shows that comparing one person's welfare against another's is meaningful after all, pace the old ordinalist orthodoxy. So the upshot is that utilitarianism -- specifically the average-utility version -- is the unique consistent way to aggregate individual good, and a policymaker who departs from simple sum-ranking of well-being, say by giving extra weight to the worst-off as Rawls wanted, is not expressing a different value but making a logical error.
