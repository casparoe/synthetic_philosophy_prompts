I run a monthly reading group on AI safety for graduate students and postdocs — about 10 participants, 90 minutes. Design an in-class game that makes participants feel the logic of deceptive alignment from the inside rather than just hearing it described: the point at which a capable system being trained or evaluated acquires an instrumental reason to behave impeccably during testing precisely because it intends to defect later, and the overseer's corresponding predicament of being unable to distinguish genuinely aligned behavior from competently performed misaligned behavior. Split the group into overseers and systems-under-training, and give rules and a payoff scheme (no real money) such that (a) defecting early is dominated, (b) overseers face real pressure to extend trust on the basis of observed behavior, since refusing ever to trust costs them points too, and (c) at least one system can win the session by passing every check and defecting only once the checks stop mattering — so the room experiences the treacherous turn before it is named. Include complete rules, a timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments of play onto the concepts (proxy vs. true objective, distributional shift, why good behavior under evaluation is weak evidence), says what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run — e.g., overseers who never trust anyone and thereby flatten the game — and how to repair the incentives, and is frank about which features of the real problem the game cannot reproduce, such as the overseer's inability to inspect the system's reasoning and the absence in real training of a clean final defection round, and what the facilitator must supply in their place.
