I write a science-and-ideas column and about once a month a press release lands in my inbox claiming some new study proves fish or crabs or shrimp "feel pain" -- or, from the other side, an industry group sending me a study claiming to prove they don't. My editors want fast turnaround, I'm not a neuroscientist, and I've been burned twice now: once I covered a zebrafish study that turned out to show nothing more than basic reflex withdrawal, once I dismissed a crab study as "just reflexes" and then had to quietly update the piece when colleagues pointed out the animals were doing cost-benefit trade-offs, which reflexes famously don't do.

So I want a reusable triage procedure I can run in about 45 minutes when one of these studies lands, to decide (a) whether the finding actually says something about felt suffering rather than nociception alone, and (b) whether it's worth a story. Ground it in the actual criteria researchers use to argue for sentience in animals -- things like whether the behavior persists beyond the stimulus, whether the animals will pay a cost to avoid the stimulus, whether painkillers or anxiety drugs change the behavior in the specific way you'd predict if there's a felt state driving it, whether they can learn and remember which places or stimuli to avoid. Write it as an ordered sequence of questions: what I'm asking at each step, what counts as a pass versus a fail, where the procedure terminates in "no story" or "this is reflex-level, don't oversell it," and where it has to hand off to a judgment call because the question is about how much evidence is enough, which is a values/threshold question not a fact question. I'd also like it to handle the asymmetry problem -- a study failing my criteria doesn't show absence of pain, and I need language for the piece that doesn't say "crabs proven not to feel pain."

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical studies I'll make up: (1) crabs avoid a shelter where they previously got a mild electric shock, and will give up a preferred dark shelter to avoid it; (2) fish injected with vinegar rock back and forth, and the rocking stops when they're given a painkiller, but the rocking also stops when they're given a sedative that doesn't touch pain; (3) shrimp rub a spot treated with an irritant but show no change in feeding or avoidance learning afterward. At least one of these should come out with a verdict I'd find annoying as a journalist, and tell me what the honest headline would be anyway. Also tell me where the procedure will misfire -- cases where following it mechanically gets the wrong answer -- and which distinctions I absolutely cannot collapse when writing for a general audience, even though it'll cost me words.
