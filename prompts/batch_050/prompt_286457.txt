Last week a close friend and I argued until two in the morning about institutional reform. Her position: deference to inherited rules is usually just inertia wearing a costume of wisdom, and the burden of proof should always fall on whoever wants to keep a practice, not whoever wants to scrap it. Mine, roughly Burkean, was that long-standing practices often encode knowledge their current stewards can't articulate -- Chesterton's fence, the idea that you shouldn't tear down a fence until you understand why it was built -- and that the burden should sometimes fall on the reformer. We didn't settle it, but the argument made me realize I need something more than a disposition.

Here's my situation. I've just become operations director of a mid-sized charitable foundation that has run for sixty years, and I inherit dozens of standing rules, review steps, and customs whose origins are undocumented. Over the next two years I will face roughly one proposal a month to abolish or streamline one of them, usually from smart staff who find the rule pointless. The stakes vary: some changes are reversible in an afternoon, others would be nearly impossible to undo. I have perhaps three hours to spend on any single decision, no access to the people who instituted most of these practices, and final sign-off rests with a board that meets quarterly and dislikes surprises.

What I want is a reusable decision procedure for fence-removal proposals -- one that operationalizes the Burkean insight without collapsing into "never change anything," and that is honest about where my friend's position is right. Please structure your answer in the following sections, in this order:

1. Summary (about 100 words): the procedure in a paragraph, so I can put it in a memo to staff.
2. The Procedure (about 500-600 words): the tests to run in order on any proposal -- for instance, how to investigate a practice's rationale when no one remembers it, how to weigh the costs of reversal against the costs of persistence, what role the practice's age and survival should play, and when a provisional or reversible change may substitute for a full removal. State what counts as a sufficient answer to each test, and where the procedure stops with a default answer.
3. Judgment Points (about 200 words): the steps where no checklist can substitute for a decision, and what kind of judgment each requires.
4. Worked Applications (about 400-500 words): run the procedure on three proposals: (a) abolishing a rule that every grant over a certain size requires a second signature from a semi-retired senior officer who mostly rubber-stamps; (b) discontinuing an annual in-person meeting with grantees that consumes a month of staff time and that attendees describe as pointless but keep attending; (c) rewriting from scratch a legacy record-keeping system that works but that no current employee fully understands. I want at least one of these to come out against the change I'd instinctively favor -- I'll admit I want to abolish (a) and (c) -- if that's what the procedure honestly yields.
5. The Procedure's Limits (about 150 words): the kinds of cases where the Burkean framework itself is the wrong tool -- where inherited practice deserves no presumption at all -- and what I'd say to my friend about those.

Keep the philosophy in the background: I want the operational content of Burke and the fence principle, not an exposition of their texts, and no citations or reading lists.
