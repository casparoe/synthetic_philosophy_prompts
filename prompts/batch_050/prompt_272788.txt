So I just spent like two hours at a bar watching two friends go at it over something that started as a joke about one of them buying a 12 dollar shirt, and now I genuinely can't tell who won. Here's the dispute as best I can reconstruct it. Mateo bought a shirt from some fast fashion brand and Dana said he's complicit in sweatshop labor. Mateo's defense: his one purchase changes literally nothing. The factory runs the same shifts whether he buys or not, no worker is worse off because of him, and if your action neither causes nor risks any harm then you haven't done anything wrong, end of story. Dana's comeback: complicity was never about causing harm. If you pay into, profit from, or knowingly keep a practice going, you're on the hook even when the outcome was fixed anyway. She gave an example: a firing squad with twenty shooters where ten bullets would already be plenty -- the eleventh shooter didn't cause the death, but nobody would say he's clean.

Then it got interesting. Mateo said her test proves way too much: by that standard paying taxes, charging a phone full of mined cobalt, buying gas, eating anything shipped by truck, all of it is complicity, and a concept that condemns literally every ordinary act isn't doing any work, it's just a way to feel bad forever. Dana bit the bullet a bit and said maybe we are all somewhat complicit in things and the discomfort isn't a refutation, but she also tried to draw lines: maybe it matters how directly you benefit, whether the wrong actually depends on collective demand (clothes sales, unlike taxes, are the whole reason the practice exists), and whether you had decent alternatives. Mateo answered that in a market with millions of buyers the 'collective demand' point is doing the sneaky work -- no individual buyer tips anything, and a tiny chance of tipping a production threshold can't ground real blame, otherwise lotteries would make us responsible for everything. Dana said that's exactly the move she distrusts: everyone hides inside the crowd and nobody is responsible for a thing the crowd very obviously does.

They both turned to me at closing time and I blanked. So: who is right about which specific point? I want a point-by-point verdict -- is Mateo right that no causal difference means no wrongdoing, is Dana right that complicity doesn't require causing, whose line-drawing attempts survive, and is there anything both of them are missing? Please also tell me how confident you are in each part of the verdict, and single out the single point you're least sure about and why.
