I am a high school junior preparing for a debate tournament, and one of the motions is roughly whether present-day citizens of a country can owe apologies or reparations for atrocities committed by their state generations before they were born. In a prep packet our coach circulated, a podcast episode about postwar Germany is summarized as follows:

"Writing in the years just after the Second World War, Karl Jaspers argued that every German was guilty, but in four distinct senses: criminal guilt for those who committed or ordered crimes, political guilt shared by all citizens of a state simply in virtue of their citizenship, moral guilt before each person's own conscience, and metaphysical guilt before God. Since guilt operates at all four levels, Jaspers concluded that the German people as a whole stood guilty, not merely a criminal minority. And because a nation is a continuing moral agent, this collective guilt does not expire with the generation that committed the crimes: later generations inherit a standing obligation to confess and atone for what their nation did."

Something in this bothers me — the inheritance claim at the end seems to sit oddly with the earlier insistence that moral guilt is a matter for each individual conscience — but the summary is written confidently, and my teammates want to cite Jaspers for our side. How much of this would Jaspers actually accept? Please separate what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what reverses his point, paying particular attention to which of the four kinds of guilt (if any) he thought could attach to a whole people rather than to individuals, and to whether guilt of any of the four kinds can pass to people not yet born when the crimes occurred. If the inheritance claim does not survive correction, I would also like to know whether anything true in the summary supports a weaker claim we could honestly use — for instance, that a continuing state, as distinct from its current citizens, can still owe something for its past.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
