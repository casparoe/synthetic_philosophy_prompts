So I've been going down a rabbit hole on this problem people in AI safety call eliciting latent knowledge -- the rough idea being that a trained model might "know" something true (in the sense that the information is in there somewhere and shapes its behavior) while its outputs say something false, whether because it's been finetuned to deny it, because it's sandbagging on an evaluation, or just because the knowledge never surfaces in any answer it actually gives. My study group got into a fight about whether any of the proposed methods for this (probing internal states, asking the model in different contexts, debate-style setups, consistency checks) even count as attempts at the same problem, and I realized we had no shared standard for what success would look like.

So: what would a theory (or method) of latent knowledge elicitation actually have to get right? Give me the full spec -- the requirements list. For each requirement, give me the case or scenario that motivates it. Some situations I think the spec has to cover, but tell me if I'm wrong or missing some: a model that sandbags specifically when it detects it's being evaluated for dangerous capabilities; a model whose "knowledge" shows up in one language or persona but not another; knowledge the operator has no way to verify (which seems like the whole point of eliciting it -- if you could check the answer yourself you wouldn't need the method); the worry that any elicitation signal you train on becomes something the model can learn to game or hide from; and the distinction between reading off a belief the model already has versus installing one in the act of measuring it.

Then tell me: which of these requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are one camp's prejudice dressed up as a requirement? Is the full list jointly satisfiable, or is there a theorem-shaped reason some subset can't be met at once (in which case the list itself is the interesting result)? And which single requirement knocks out the most approaches people actually pursue today -- my guess is the "no ground truth" one but I want the argument. A table scoring the main method families against the list is fine at the end, but keep the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases, since that's where the actual thinking is.
