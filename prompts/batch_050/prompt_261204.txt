I'm a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and I've been asked to run one session of an upper-level undergraduate seminar on phenomenology and embodied cognition (14 students, 75 minutes, ordinary seminar room with tables). The session is on Heidegger's analysis of equipment in Being and Time — the contrast between readiness-to-hand and presence-at-hand, and what breakdown reveals. Design a staged activity for the session that lets the students undergo the shift from absorbed tool-use to deliberate inspection of the tool before anyone names it, rather than hearing a lecture about it. No Heidegger terminology may be used until the debrief.

Here is the structure I'm after. The activity should have three rounds built around one simple, learnable manual task (your choice, but it must use a physical tool and be something students can become reasonably fluent at within a few minutes, so that transparent coping has a chance to set in). Each round should be engineered to produce one of the three disturbance modes Heidegger distinguishes in §16: the tool malfunctioning in use, the tool turning out to be missing or unavailable when reached for, and the task being blocked by something standing in the way. I want the three rounds ordered and designed so that, by the end, the group has generated all three experiences without being told that this taxonomy is the point. Crucially, the failures must be located in the equipment, not in the participants' skill: I want to avoid the lazy version of this activity where you just make people use their non-dominant hand, because clumsiness of the agent is not what the analysis is about. Specify exactly how you'll sabotage, remove, or obstruct the tools so that the disruption reads as the tool's failure, and how you'll do it covertly enough that students don't treat the session as a prank — if they get suspicious, the absorption you're trying to break was never genuine.

Give me: the task and why you chose it; a materials list limited to things I can get for under twenty dollars; a minute-by-minute timeline including how fluency is established before the first disruption; instructions for a brief first-person log students keep during the activity (two or three sentences written at each disruption, capturing where their attention went), since the debrief will lean on their own words; and the debrief itself, mapping the three rounds onto Heidegger's three modes and onto the larger claim that breakdown makes the tool's network of involvements visible precisely by interrupting it — the debrief should use quotations from the students' own logs, so tell me what to listen for in them.

Then two honest closing notes. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: I'm particularly worried about students detecting the sabotage, about skill differences making round one uneven, and about the three disruptions collapsing into one undifferentiated experience of annoyance — tell me which of these is most likely and how to recover. Second, which feature of Heidegger's account the activity cannot reproduce and I will simply have to state in the debrief: I suspect the staged character of the breakdown and the shallowness of ten-minute fluency both matter here, but tell me what you'd flag.
