So I've been following the cognitive penetrability debate for a while, mostly from the sidelines as a rationalist-adjacent person who cares a lot about motivated reasoning and whether you can actually trust what you see. And the state of the literature genuinely confuses me. On one side you've got Pylyshyn-style arguments that early vision is encapsulated, plus that big Firestone & Scholl critique from the mid-2010s that went through basically all the claimed top-down effects and argued they were judgment effects, memory effects, demand characteristics, attention, task demands -- anything but perception itself. On the other side people keep producing new studies -- race affecting weapon detection, moral disgust changing lightness perception, ambiguous figures, the old New Look stuff like poor kids overestimating coin sizes -- and both sides look at the same experiments and one sees perception being warped and the other sees post-perceptual cognition being mislabeled.

My hypothesis, which I hold loosely: this isn't an empirical disagreement at all anymore, it's a disagreement about where the seeing/thinking boundary goes, which means both sides can grant every result and just sort it differently. The encapsulation side has a narrow notion of perception, the penetration side a broad one, and "does cognition affect perception" ends up meaning different things to each. But I'm not sure that's right, because the defenders of penetration insist they're making a real empirical claim, and some of them explicitly accept the methodological critiques and say the newer paradigms avoid them.

So: what kind of disagreement is this actually? Verbal dispute about the word "perception," a hidden empirical question that one well-designed experiment could settle, or something deeper -- like the two sides having different background pictures of what a perceptual system even is (roughly modular feedforward boxes vs. predictive-processing-style brains where there's no clean feedforward pass to point at)? Note that if you take the predictive processing picture seriously, the whole debate starts to look ill-posed, and I'd like you to address that directly rather than dodge it.

I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the studies. And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a specific experimental design that both sides would accept in advance as decisive, a conceptual distinction, or an argument that the impasse is permanent because the question itself is malformed.
