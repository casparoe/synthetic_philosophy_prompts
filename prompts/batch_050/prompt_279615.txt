I'm in my final year of high school, and for our modern history unit on nationalism I'm designing a class activity: a one-page flowchart that sorts each of my classmates into a position on the question "How old are nations, and what makes one real?" I'd like you to build the flowchart as a decision tree.

Our starting text is Ernest Renan's 1882 Sorbonne lecture What is a Nation?, where he argues, roughly, that nobody is bound into a nation by race, language, religion, or the shape of rivers and mountain ranges, and that a nation instead rests on two things at once: a shared inheritance of memories (including memories of things the nation has collectively forgotten) and a continually renewed agreement, in the present, to keep living together. Your tree should be built around this: the very first question should test, by means of a concrete case and without naming Renan, whether the respondent thinks facts like ancestry, language, and borders can make people a nation whether or not anyone feels or chooses it. Then, at the end, I want you to place Renan himself on the finished tree using only that position — say which leaf he lands on, and identify the one question he could not answer cleanly (since he insists on both inherited memory and present consent), rewording it so he can pass through without contradiction.

Requirements:

- The tree should have ten to fourteen yes/no questions and fit on one page. Every branch node must be phrased as a concrete case or claim a classmate could answer without knowing any of the position labels — for example, "France was already a nation when Joan of Arc fought for it" rather than "Do you accept modernism?" Names of positions and thinkers may appear only at the leaves.
- Exactly six leaves: primordialism (nations are natural, ancient, given); perennialism (nations are old and keep recurring, but aren't facts of nature); ethnosymbolism (modern nations are real but built out of older ethnic myths, symbols, and memories); industrial-society modernism (a standardized national culture is something a modern economy needs and therefore produces); constructivist modernism (nations are imagined communities, assembled recently through things like print, mass schooling, and deliberately invented traditions); and civic voluntarism (nationhood is ongoing collective choice). Work from the logical space first: figure out which combinations of answers these positions occupy, then place them.
- At least three of your questions must use specific cases I've chosen: a medieval kingdom whose elites shared a chronicle, a law code, and a patron saint; a postcolonial state whose borders, flag, and founding heroes were all settled within living memory; and a diaspora whose members have never lived in the territory they call their homeland.
- Note at least two answer-combinations your tree reaches that no actual position occupies, and say briefly whether each is empty because it's incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.
- Flag any pair of leaves the tree can't separate. In particular, try hard to find one concrete case on which the industrial-society modernist and the constructivist modernist come apart (both say nations are new and made, so attitude questions won't split them); if you can't find one, say so honestly and present them as one leaf with two nameplates rather than forcing a fake distinction.
- Finish with a worked example: trace one path down the tree from the first question to a leaf, as if filled in by a classmate who thinks their own nation is a thousand years old.

The audience is classmates who have read nothing on nationalism, so keep the node questions plain, concrete, and free of jargon.
