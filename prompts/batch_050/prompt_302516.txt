Write a dialogue between two economists, Maya and Tomas, who are the only two active bidders in a dollar auction: an auctioneer is selling a one-dollar bill to the highest bidder, with the twist that both the highest and the second-highest bidder must pay their final bids. The bidding currently stands at 60 cents (Maya) against 55 cents (Tomas), and it is Tomas's turn. Maya has just announced that she intends to quit if Tomas bids again, on the grounds that continuing past the point where the two bids sum to more than a dollar is pure loss for the bidders collectively and that any rational player should have foreseen this and never entered. Tomas replies that her reasoning proves too much: at every individual step, bidding 65 cents to win a dollar dominates quitting and losing 55 cents for certain, and if her backward-induction argument were decisive, no one could ever rationally make the profitable opening bid of 5 cents against an opponent known to be rational. Have the two work through whether Tomas's local rationality and Maya's global rationality can be reconciled — for instance, whether a publicly announced stopping rule could be credible, whether the trap lies in the auction's structure or in the bidders' reasoning, and what each would do differently at the opening bid. Both characters should be equally competent and must respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talk past each other; the dialogue should end without a clear winner, but with each side having conceded at least one point.
