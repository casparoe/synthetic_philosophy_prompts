I'm in a history-and-philosophy reading group and we've been circling the same fight all semester about how moral norms actually die. The stock cases are slavery and dueling: the moral arguments against both were around for ages before anything changed, and then both collapsed fairly fast in the 19th century. One half of the group says the arguments eventually won because they were right and people caught up. The other half -- basically following Appiah's line in The Honor Code -- says the arguments were already known and already sound, so they can't explain the timing; what changed was something like honor, national respectability, or economic interest, and the arguments just gave the winners something to say afterwards. Nobody's moved, and I suspect the two sides aren't even disagreeing about the same thing: one is asking what justified the change, the other what caused it.

So: what kind of disagreement is this actually? A factual dispute about the historical record that more archives could settle? A conceptual confusion where "the arguments worked" means different things to each side? Or a bedrock split about what it would even take for reasons to cause social change, which no amount of history could adjudicate? Give me your diagnosis with reasons, and then tell me what progress would even look like -- a distinction, a new case study, a different question -- or argue that the impasse is permanent and why.

Format: "What each side is really claiming" (~150 words), "My diagnosis" (~200 words), "What would count as progress" (~100 words), "Where my reading could be wrong" (~100 words).
