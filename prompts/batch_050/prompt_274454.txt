I’ve foolishly agreed to mediate a family argument at Christmas. My uncle, a retired engineer, calls himself a “strict finitist”: he says endlessly continuing a process is fine, but a completed infinity is just mathematicians mistaking a useful notation for a thing. My cousin, who is studying maths, keeps wheeling out Cantor and Hilbert’s hotel as though they settle the matter. I suspect they are lumping together several different claims: that finite beings can reason reliably about infinity, that infinite collections are legitimate mathematical objects, and that anything infinite exists outside mathematics.

Please rank the following six considerations from strongest to weakest as arguments against my uncle’s position: ordinary induction over all natural numbers; Hilbert’s hotel; the power set of the natural numbers; Cantor’s diagonal argument; the mathematical continuum; and the apparent continuity of physical space or time. Give each two scores out of 10—one for dialectical force against strict finitism and one for the danger that it equivocates between mathematical existence and physical existence—then calculate a final score of force minus half the danger. Explain each ranking in no more than two sentences. Finally, identify the first rung of this ladder where my uncle can coherently get off without contradicting ordinary school-level mathematics, and say exactly what he would have to give up. No citations or reading lists, please; I need the argument itself.
