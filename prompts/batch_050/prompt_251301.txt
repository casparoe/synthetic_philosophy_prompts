I teach an intro ethics seminar, about 20 undergrads, 75-minute session, and we're about to do moral luck. I want a classroom activity, not a lecture, that makes them *feel* the pull of resultant luck before anyone utters the words "Nagel" or "control principle." Here's my rough seed of an idea, which you should develop or replace if you have something better: split the class into "drivers" and "jurors." Every driver faces the exact same mildly negligent choice (drive home exhausted after a double shift, or pay for a cab they can't really afford), they all choose to drive, and then outcome cards or dice decide whether they get home fine or a kid steps out into the road. The jurors then have to assign actual-ish punishment — small real stakes like losing participation points or taking on an extra reading response, not just tokens.

What I need from you:

1. Full runnable rules. How many rounds, what the jurors are and aren't told (crucially: do they know the drivers all made the identical choice, and do the drivers know dice are involved?), how the punishment gets assigned, and how to keep the "nothing happened" drivers from just being boring bystanders.
2. A way to smuggle in circumstantial and constitutive luck in later rounds without students clocking the trick too early — e.g. some drivers were assigned cars with bad brakes they didn't know about, or some got dealt "naturally reckless personality" cards.
3. A debrief plan that maps specific moments from the game onto the taxonomy (resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, causal) and onto the tension with the control principle — but the game has to generate the intuition first. No mini-lecture in the middle. The students should discover they've been blaming people for dice rolls before I say anything.
4. What will predictably go wrong when I actually run it. My guesses: students treat it as pure theater and game the votes, the jurors go soft because they like each other, or some smartass immediately says "it's just dice, obviously I wouldn't punish the unlucky one" and then does anyway without noticing. Tell me how to handle these.
5. An honest paragraph on what the activity structurally can't reproduce and I'll have to just supply in discussion. I assume the big one is actual guilt and harm, since nobody really gets hurt and so the "punish the unlucky driver" instinct might be an artifact of it being a game — but if you think something else is the bigger gap (like the fact that real negligence isn't chosen from a menu), say so instead.
