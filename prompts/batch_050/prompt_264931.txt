I've settled on a position about collective harms that I'd like stress-tested. The view: individual consumer choices in large anonymous markets -- buying meat, booking flights, running the air conditioning -- make no morally relevant causal difference to the harms those markets produce, and arguments from expected marginal impact don't rescue them. I accept the expected-value reasoning in principle: if my purchase has a one-in-n chance of triggering a batch-level production adjustment of size n, the expected impact equals the average. But I hold that actual supply chains are buffered -- inventory slack, fixed contracts, aggregated orders, lagged price signals -- so that the probability of any single purchase changing anything downstream is far lower than the symmetrical triggering story assumes, and the expected impact shrinks to something morally negligible. What would count against this view? I want the defeaters mapped and ranked: which empirical facts about how markets actually respond to small demand shifts would refute it, which arguments (for instance, that buffering redistributes rather than eliminates sensitivity, or that thresholds exist somewhere in the chain even if I can't locate them) it must answer, and which considerations it can absorb without damage. Also address two harder questions: does the position generalize in ways I won't like -- to voting, to contributions to tiny thresholds in funding or activism -- and has it been insulated by its appeal to empirical complexity into something no realistic evidence could dislodge? Distinguish what critics would say counts against the view from what a reasonable holder of it should concede, and end with an honest verdict on how exposed the position actually is.
