A colleague of mine uses "sphexish" — after the digger wasp that mechanically repeats its nest-inspection routine whenever its prey is moved — for behavior that merely mimics intelligence. Her verdicts so far: the wasp itself is sphexish; a chess engine that loses to the same opening trap every time, no matter how often it recurs, is sphexish; a metronome is not, since it doesn't even purport to be intelligent; a GPS that fluidly reroutes around any closure is not, despite doing nothing but routing; and a star student who reproduces the textbook's arguments with varied wording but collapses on any problem of a slightly novel kind is sphexish.

Now consider two new cases. First, a student who has memorized such an enormous bank of worked solutions that every exam question, however "novel," happens to be covered — he succeeds flexibly on every test, but only ever by lookup. Second, a clinician whose diagnostic judgments are flexible, well-calibrated, and responsive to genuinely new presentations, yet who, on one rare symptom pattern she was mis-trained on decades ago, makes the identical wrong call every single time and never learns from the corrections.

What will my colleague say about each, and what should she say for consistency? I'm less interested in the verdicts than in the candidate rules her track record supports, what each rule predicts for these two cases, and what further case would best discriminate between the rules you can't yet separate.
