I teach a seminar on irrationality, and every year the unit on self-deception dissolves into the same fight: half the class thinks self-deception is straightforwardly impossible (a person can't simultaneously believe p and not-p, and can't set out to trick herself without already knowing the trick), and the other half thinks it obviously happens all the time and the paradoxes must be pseudo-problems. Both halves are right about something and I can no longer tell what a theory of self-deception would even have to look like to satisfy the phenomenon instead of explaining it away. So, before we read another paper, write the desiderata: the specification any adequate philosophical account of self-deception must meet.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. I want at minimum: the stock of cases it must classify correctly (including the ones usually ignored, e.g., the person who deceives herself into believing something true, or into believing something she did not want to believe); the contrasts it must preserve rather than collapse (self-deception versus ordinary wishful thinking, versus being mistaken, versus weakness of will, versus delusion); the commitments it must honor about belief itself (whatever your account says self-deception is, it has to be consistent with beliefs being the kind of thing sensitive to evidence at all); and the constraint that the mechanism it posits has to be psychologically possible -- it cannot require a faculty for concealing intentions from oneself so effective that the account becomes indistinguishable from simply positing a second person in the head.

Two further instructions. First, the part I most want: identify which subsets of your desiderata look jointly unsatisfiable, and diagnose why. My suspicion is that the more an account honors the intuition that the self-deceiver genuinely gets herself to believe against the evidence, the less it can honor the intuition that she remains in some sense aware of what she is doing -- but I want this tension stated precisely, not gestured at, and I want to know whether it is a genuine inconsistency or just a pair of intuitions from different camps that cannot both be fully satisfied. Second, once the list exists, run the two standard strategies against it in a paragraph each: intentionalist accounts that divide the mind into cooperating parts, and deflationary accounts that treat self-deception as motivationally biased belief formation requiring no intention to deceive. Tell me which single desideratum does the most damage to each.

Mark which requirements you take to be non-negotiable (anything failing them has changed the subject rather than solved the problem) and which are really the intuitions of one faction dressed up as data. And if you think my framing is off -- for instance, if you think there is no unified phenomenon here for one theory to be a theory of, or that asking for desiderata before choosing between intentionalism and deflationism already rigs the game -- say that plainly at the start and tell me what I should have asked instead. I would rather be corrected than accommodated.
