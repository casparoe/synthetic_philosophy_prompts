My brother and I have been going round in circles on this one and I want a verdict. Say you believe there's a genuine rule against killing an innocent person — not a rule of thumb, a real line you don't cross. Now:

(1) Killing an innocent person is wrong, full stop, whatever good might come of it.
(2) If killing innocent people is wrong, then a situation with five innocent people killed is worse than a situation with one killed.
(3) If one outcome is worse than another and you can affect which happens, you ought to steer toward the less bad one.
(4) Suppose I can kill one innocent person or stand back while someone else kills five. If I stand back, the worse outcome from (2) happens, and I could have affected it.
(5) So by (3) I ought to kill the one.
(6) But (1) says I mustn't, whatever good comes of it.

So the "absolute" rule seems to refute itself — the moment you take the wrongness of killing seriously enough to care about five deaths versus one, it orders you to kill. My brother reckons this proves all moral rules are just about minimising bad outcomes in disguise. I think that's mad, but I can't find the loose screw. Which step do I actually have to reject, why does it look so reasonable, and what does rejecting it cost me? 250 words max, and tell me the bottom line up front.
