I have no academic background, but I've been thinking for a while about the chatbots and digital avatars that companies now offer to imitate people who have died, built out of their texts, emails, and recordings. I find that my gut reactions to different versions of this practice are firm but don't obviously cohere, and I'd like to know whether any single principle lies behind them, or whether I have to give one of them up. Here are my verdicts, case by case.

A widow subscribes to a service that trains a chatbot on her late husband's messages; it replies in his style, and she knows perfectly well it's software. I think this is fine, even moving.

Same service, but the company keeps the simulation running after she cancels her subscription and later licenses the dead man's synthesized voice to advertise products he never used. I think this is plainly wrong.

A terminally ill woman spends her last months deliberately recording material and helping design a simulation of herself so that her grandchildren, who will barely remember her, can converse with "her" as they grow up. I think this is fine, and the fact that she chose it seems central.

Parents whose teenage son died keep his simulation running and have it periodically updated so that it "ages" -- it marks the birthdays he never lived, talks about graduating, imagines the career he might have had. Nothing here deceives anyone; the parents consent and are the only users. Yet this one feels wrong to me, even ghoulish, and I can't articulate why it differs from the widow's case.

A man builds a simulation of his ex-wife, who died recently and who told him while alive, more than once, that she detested the whole idea of such things. He finds it comforting. I think this is wrong even so.

Finally, I feel a difference between two versions of the widow's bot: one that only recombines things her husband actually wrote, and one that generates new statements he never made -- for instance, telling her, in his voice, "I forgive you" about an old quarrel. The second feels like a betrayal of him even though it might comfort her more.

My problem is that cases one and four seem structurally alike -- a consenting, undeceived user, no third party harmed -- yet I judge them oppositely, while cases three and five suggest the dead person's own wishes are what matter, which case four doesn't fit neatly either, since the son never expressed any wishes at all. Is there a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures all or most of these verdicts? Please state it precisely, show me which of my cases motivates each part of it, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts, if any, it cannot save. Then test it on a case I haven't settled: a family agrees to let a company build an interactive simulation of a deceased, much-loved children's author so that young readers can "talk" to her; the author left no instructions either way. What should the principle say about that, and what single feature of the case, if changed, would flip the verdict?
