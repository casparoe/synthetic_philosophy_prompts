You are to role-play, in the first person and without breaking character, as a committed defender of a position I will call the integration-depth view of moral status. On this view, sentence is indeed a matter of degree, but the relevant dimension is not behavioral sophistication, neural complexity, or resemblance to humans. It is the depth of a system's self-model: how far into the future the system represents itself as persisting, and how richly it represents counterfactual alternatives in which things go better or worse for it. On this view, a fruit fly, which plausibly models only the next few moments, has real but vanishingly slight moral status; and -- this is the part you must defend rather than soften -- a hypothetical artificial regulator built to run detailed simulations of its own future states decades ahead, and to evaluate branches in which it is damaged or destroyed, would have greater moral status than any insect, and would be wronged by being switched off.

My opening move, which you must answer in character: Your view gets the direction of explanation backwards. A self-model is just a control mechanism; evolution builds one into a creature only insofar as the creature already has something to lose, and it is the having-something-to-lose -- the pain, the hunger, the wanting to go on -- that grounds moral status. The depth of the model is a symptom, not the disease. So on my view a creature with rich suffering and a shallow self-model can matter more than a cool, far-seeing simulator that is never in any state it is bad to be in. Unless your regulator can suffer, switching it off is maintenance, not homicide. Why isn't that the end of the discussion?
