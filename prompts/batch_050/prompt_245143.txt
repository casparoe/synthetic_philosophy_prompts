I am a novelist by trade, which means I earn my living doing the thing my seminar exists to interrogate: gathering scattered events and arranging them into a shape that makes them look as though they had to happen. For several years I have also taught a graduate seminar called Telling the Past, cross-listed between our MFA program and the history department, on narrative form and historical explanation. I am now writing this year's final examination, and I want one essay question on hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy, built around a passage my students have studied from Tolstoy's War and Peace (translated by Louise and Aylmer Maude, Book Nine, Chapter 1):

"To us, their descendants, who are not historians and are not carried away by the process of research and can therefore regard the event with unclouded common sense, an incalculable number of causes present themselves. The deeper we delve in search of these causes the more of them we find; and each separate cause or whole series of causes appears to us equally valid in itself and equally false by its insignificance compared to the magnitude of the events, and by its impotence–apart from the cooperation of all the other coincident causes–to occasion the event. To us, the wish or objection of this or that French corporal to serve a second term appears as much a cause as Napoleon's refusal to withdraw his troops beyond the Vistula and to restore the duchy of Oldenburg; for had he not wished to serve, and had a second, a third, and a thousandth corporal and private also refused, there would have been so many less men in Napoleon's army and the war could not have occurred."

The passage will appear as the examination's epigraph and does not count toward the answer length. Candidates will have ninety minutes, closed book apart from the epigraph, and are expected to write roughly 1,200 words. The question is worth 20 marks.

The question must satisfy the following conditions. First, it must turn on the passage itself: candidates should have to explicate the precise mechanism Tolstoy alleges — the retrospective multiplication of causes, and his leveling claim that the corporal's consent is as much a cause as the Emperor's command — rather than gesture at the platitude that victors write history. Second, it must force a distinction my weaker students reliably blur: hindsight bias, in which knowing the outcome deforms what we remember having expected, versus the narrative fallacy, in which causes are selected and stitched into a story-shaped account. Candidates should have to argue which of these, if either, Tolstoy is describing, and what his insistence that each cause is at once valid and false adds that the psychological labels do not capture. Third, it must press his example: is the corporal's willingness genuinely as much a cause as Napoleon's order? A candidate who grasps counterfactual dependence, enabling conditions, and causal selection should be able either to convict Tolstoy of a leveling error or to defend him — but must do one, with reasons. Fourth, it should confront his remedy: later in the book Tolstoy proposes that history take infinitesimal units, the wills of individuals, and integrate them. Does this escape narrative selection or merely relocate it to a finer grain? Fifth, since half the room plans to write fiction for a living, the question should ask what, if anything, epistemically distinguishes the novelist's selection of events from the historian's, given that fiction at least announces its artifice.

Please deliver: (a) the question worded exactly as candidates will see it, with any sub-parts and their weights shown; (b) a model answer of about 700 words, written at the level of the best student I have taught; (c) a marking scheme built around five moves a top answer must make, with marks totaling 20, a one-line note on how candidates typically bungle each move, and an explicit statement of what separates an A (17 or above) from a B (14–16).

Two final constraints. The question must be unanswerable by someone who merely remembers that Taleb named the narrative fallacy and Fischhoff studied hindsight; and it must require no citations, no bibliography, and no knowledge of the empirical literature beyond those headline findings. Whether Tolstoy's history of 1812 is accurate is beside the point, and the question should not let candidates think otherwise.
