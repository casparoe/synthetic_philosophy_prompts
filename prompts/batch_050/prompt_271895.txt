I run a grad epistemology seminar, and after last week's meeting two of my students, Maya and Denis, got into a genuinely heated argument about the dogmatism paradox while packing up. I mostly stayed out of it, but I've been turning it over since and I want a referee. Could you put on your "professional epistemologist" hat, think this through carefully (feel free to brainstorm the dialectic before committing), and then adjudicate?

Here's the exchange as best I remember it. Maya opened by stating the paradox: if I know that p, then I know that any future evidence against p is misleading evidence, and if I know a piece of evidence is misleading, I seem justified in disregarding it — so knowledge licenses me to toss counterevidence in the bin unread, which is absurd. Her diagnosis: closure of justification under known entailment has to go. Denis pushed back that this conflates two different moments. Before I encounter the evidence, sure, I'm justified in predicting that whatever anti-p evidence shows up will be misleading. But the moment the evidence actually arrives, my total evidence has changed, and if it's strong enough I simply stop knowing p — there's no stable standpoint from which I both know p and am staring at the counterevidence. Maya said that can't be the whole story, because it seems to license a wierd kind of epistemic inoculation: knowing her paper's central result is correct, she could glance at the referee report's first line — "the argument in section 3 is fallacious" — and file it under misleading without reading further, on the grounds that her knowledge was in place before she looked. Denis replied that the inoculation story cheats by smuggling in the assumption that she retains knowledge while deliberately avoiding the evidence, and that avoiding evidence is exactly the sort of thing that undermines the knowledge you'd need for the move; plus, if the referee is actually right, then her "knowledge" was never there, and you can't build a paradox on cases where p is secretly false. Maya's last move was to distinguish being justified in believing the evidence is misleading from being justified in ignoring it: a scientist who knows her scale is off by two grams doesn't smash the scale, she recalibrates — the rational response to evidence you believe is misleading is often to investigate it, not discard it, so the paradox trades on conflating those two. Denis muttered that this just relocates the problem, since on her picture it's still the prior knowledge that's doing the normative work, but he didn't have a clean comeback and we all had to leave.

So: go through the exchange move by move and tell me who is right about what. Specifically — does the paradox really threaten closure, or is Denis right that there's no stable standpoint for the puzzle? Is the "inoculation" case a fair restatement of the paradox or does it smuggle something in? Does Maya's believe-it's-misleading vs. ignore-it distinction actually dissolve the puzzle, or does it just kick it one level up? And if there's something both of them are missing entirely — a distinction or reframe neither one reaches for — say what it is. I want a verdict on each move, not just an overall winner, though you can give one of those too.
