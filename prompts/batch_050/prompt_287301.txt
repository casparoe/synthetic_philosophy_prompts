Exam question: "Explain how the Dutch book argument is supposed to establish that rational degrees of belief must obey the probability axioms. State carefully what the argument actually proves and what assumptions it depends on, and assess whether it succeeds as a *pragmatic* justification of probabilism."

Student's answer:

"The Dutch book argument, due to Ramsey and de Finetti, works as follows. Suppose your degrees of belief violate the probability axioms — say you believe p to degree 0.6 and also believe not-p to degree 0.6. Then a clever bookie can sell you bets at prices matching your degrees of belief such that, taken together, you lose money no matter what happens. Since a guaranteed loss is irrational, rational credences must conform to the probability calculus.

The argument therefore proves that anyone whose beliefs violate the probability axioms will in fact lose money, which is why we call violating the axioms 'incoherent.' It also shows that rational agents must update by conditionalization, since a bookie can exploit any other updating rule in exactly the same way.

The key assumption is that your degree of belief in a proposition equals the betting odds you regard as fair, i.e., the price at which you'd be indifferent between buying and selling a bet on that proposition. Ramsey argued this is just what degrees of belief are: behavioral dispositions measured by betting behavior.

One objection is that the argument is pragmatic rather than epistemic: it shows incoherent agents can be exploited, but not that their beliefs are inaccurate or unjustified. So it establishes at most that coherence is instrumentally required, not that it's an epistemic norm. I conclude that the argument succeeds as a pragmatic justification, since avoiding sure loss is a genuine practical requirement, and it moreover grounds the full Bayesian picture: once your credences are probabilistic, expected utility maximization follows directly."
