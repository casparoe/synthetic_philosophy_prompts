I'm part of a small reading group that works through papers on neural network interpretability, and we keep running aground on a question that I suspect has no canonical answer but several defensible ones: when do two components of two networks — say, a circuit in a base model and a superficially similar one in a fine-tuned descendant — count as the *same mechanism*? This matters to us because "mechanistic anomaly detection" only makes sense if you can say what a model's mechanisms are supposed to do: you can't flag a behavior as anomalous relative to a mechanism unless you can first decide whether the mechanism you identified last week is still the one producing behavior today, after fine-tuning, pruning, or even just a global rescaling of weights. In our discussions people slide between very different individuation standards — "same weights, same circuit," "same input-output function on the data we care about," "same causal role under interventions," "same algorithm, different implementation" — without noticing they've changed the subject.

Please write a decision tree that takes someone from the starting commitment "I have identified a candidate circuit in model A, and I want to know whether model B contains the same mechanism" to a named position on mechanism individuation. Each branch node should be a concrete diagnostic question the person could answer by running or imagining an experiment — for instance, whether an interchange intervention (splicing an activation from A into B mid-computation) preserves B's output, or whether the circuit survives arbitrary basis rotations within its own activation space — and never a question that presupposes knowing which position it leads to. As leaves, your tree must at minimum reach: strict implementational identity; behavioral equivalence restricted to a fixed input distribution; equivalence under interchange interventions at some explicitly chosen level of abstraction (and the tree should force the user to confront the fact that this criterion is only as objective as the choice of abstraction level); an "algorithmic sameness" view that tolerates radically different implementations; and a deflationary leaf on which "the same mechanism" is simply not a well-posed relation, only similarity-for-a-purpose is. Where two leaves produce identical verdicts on every diagnostic question you pose, flag them as inseparable by your instrument rather than inventing a spurious split.

Two further requests. First, build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: derive the questions, enumerate the answer combinations they permit, locate the positions in that space, and note any unoccupied combination with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether it's incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored. Second, illustrate exactly one branch point of your choosing with a fully worked toy example — I'd suggest a tiny network that computes some simple function twice over, once directly and once under an internal symmetry such as a basis rotation or a sign flip absorbed by downstream weights — showing precisely which diagnostic questions the two implementations answer differently and which they answer the same. The toy example should do real work: if it can't separate any two leaves, replace it with one that can.
