I'd like your help refereeing a dispute between two members of my fiction workshop, Elena and Marcus, which I witnessed as a bystander and now genuinely can't settle for myself. Please take on the role of a philosopher of art with particular expertise in the philosophy of fiction. Before giving any verdict, first reconstruct the strongest version of each side's position, and only then work through the exchange point by point.

Background: Elena circulated a short story narrated by a village elder in a harsh fictional society. The narration presents it as simply true, and simply good, in the world of the story that exposing surplus infants on the hillside is an act of generosity, and the story's emotional climax depends on the reader feeling the nobility of one such exposure. Nearly everyone in the group could not feel it, and the climax fell flat. The disagreement concerned what this failure shows.

(1) Elena's opening position: The failure is in the story, and it is an aesthetic failure. Fiction enjoys a kind of narrative authority — whatever the narrator asserts becomes true in the fiction — but that authority has limits, and moral evaluations presented as straightforwardly true sit at the limit. A reader who cannot bring herself to imagine that cruelty is generosity is in the same position as a reader who spots a plot hole: the text asked for something it had not earned. And just as an author can fix an implausible twist by laying groundwork, she can fix this by laying moral groundwork — rendering the practice from inside the characters' form of life so that the evaluation comes to feel inevitable. So this is a craft defect, and my job as a critic is to point to the missing groundwork.

(2) Marcus's reply: That proves too much. We grant fiction authority over physics — dragons, time travel — and over bizarre social arrangements constantly, and readers routinely inhabit the moral worlds of older literature they reject without those works failing aesthetically. What varies is the reader's moral investment, not the story's authority. The same story would land for a reader with different moral priors, and plausibly did land for members of some real historical society. So the flatness you felt is a fact about you and your convictions — perhaps even a virtuous refusal on your part — but not an aesthetic defect in the text. Calling it one conflates moral disagreement with aesthetic failure.

(3) Elena's counter: The dragons comparison misses the asymmetry. Nobody resists dragons, because nothing hinges on dragons being true outside the story. We resist the infant exposure precisely because the story wants us to export the evaluation — to feel, here and now, that something noble occurred, and to carry some of that feeling back out of the fiction. Resistance spikes exactly when a fiction claims authority over what we are to feel and think in actuality. That is also why framing matters: put the same practice in a story clearly presented as one character's deluded outlook and nobody balks. So it is not my moral priors doing the work; it is the story's bid to govern my actual responses, and a story can fail at that bid the way an argument can fail to persuade.

(4) Marcus's counter: But then your view collapses into mine. If the defect is that the story asks me to endorse what I will not endorse, then whether there is a defect still depends on me and my refusal, and a reader disposed to accept the evaluation will find no flaw. You have merely relocated the subjectivity from "the reader refuses" to "the story demands and the reader refuses." Worse, your own craft remedy cuts against you: if groundwork can in principle always earn the authority, then there is nothing special about morality at all — no principled limit, only harder cases, which is exactly my position.

They have both asked me to rule, and I keep flip-flopping. Please adjudicate: assess each of the four moves — where it succeeds, where it overreaches, and where it concedes too much — then say who is more right overall, or whether the disagreement dissolves once a distinction (say, between what is true in the fiction and what the fiction asks us to feel in actuality) is properly drawn. Note also what, if anything, both parties are missing. Finally, state how confident you are in your overall verdict and in each move-by-move assessment, and single out the one claim in your own analysis that you are least sure of, explaining why.
