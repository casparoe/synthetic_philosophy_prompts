For the next issue of my little science newsletter I'm building one of those internet personality-quiz sorters, except instead of sorting readers into Hogwarts houses it sorts them into positions on what an organism even is. Design it for me properly.

Audience is general readers with zero philosophy of biology. Eight questions, each one a concrete case with two or three plain-language verdict options, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions. The quiz has to sort by what people say about the cases, so no question may mention theories, jargon, or ask anyone to self-label.

The cases I want used (one question each, though you can split or merge if the logic demands it): a lichen; a Portuguese man o' war; a honeybee hive; you plus your gut microbes; the Pando aspen grove (one root system, tens of thousands of trunks, single genotype); a cellular slime mold where free-living amoebae pile up into a fruiting body and the stalk cells die so the others can spread; a deep-sea anglerfish where the male permanently fuses into the female's body; and a human chimera who absorbed a fraternal twin in the womb and now carries two genomes.

The leaves the key has to be able to land people on, described by content, not name:

1. One-bottleneck view: an organism is whatever develops from a single founding cell or clonal lineage — one genotype all the way through.
2. Immune-boundary view: an organism is whatever its immune system consistently tolerates as self.
3. Units-of-selection view: an organism is whatever can stand in parent-offspring lineages and have fitness of its own — and this comes in degrees.
4. Holobiont view: an organism is the host plus the symbionts it can't develop or function without.
5. Deflationist: "is it one organism?" is a verbal question; integration comes in degrees and that's all there is to say.

The part I actually need your brain for: build the logical space before the quiz. First lay out which combinations of verdicts across the eight cases are even available, then locate the five leaves in that space, then show me the empty combinations and say for each whether nobody's there because the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the quiz can't seperate — my suspicion is that leaves 1 and 2 agree on almost everything except the anglerfish and the chimera, and I honestly don't know what leaf 2 is even supposed to say about a lichen, which doesn't have an immune system. If a case can't split anything, cut it or justify keeping it as flavor.

Also: every question has to split at least two leaves; the scoring key should be readable as patterns ("if you said 'one' on 2, 5 and 8…") rather than point totals; and at the end tell me which single question does the most sorting work, and which answer option anywhere in the quiz is the trap where gut instinct and every serious theory come apart.
