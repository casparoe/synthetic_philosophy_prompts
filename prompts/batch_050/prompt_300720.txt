I am a novelist, and in the spring I am to deliver the closing address at a university humanities center's annual conference on philosophy and literature. The invitation asked for something on the writing life and its meaning, and I have chosen — rashly, perhaps — to defend a thesis I have adapted from Moritz Schlick's 1927 essay On the Meaning of Life. My reading of that essay, in brief: Schlick holds that activity governed by purposes can never confer meaning, because a purpose locates all value in a future state not yet attained, so that a life organized by purposes is a chain of means in which value is perpetually deferred and never actually lived; meaning belongs instead to activities that carry their value within themselves, for which Schlick's paradigm is play, and his essay ends by associating a meaningful existence with youth, since the young play where the old merely work toward ends. My own talk extends this into a claim about my craft: that the novel as a form enacts Schlick's distinction, because plot is purpose — every event a means to the ending — while everything that actually rewards a reader, the texture of sentence, scene, and voice, is intrinsically valued activity; and that the novelist's working life, which outsiders imagine as driven by the purpose of finishing books, is in fact meaningful only insofar as the daily activity is its own justification. I will concede in the talk that my extension of Schlick is mine and not his.

The audience will be about sixty people, and I know roughly who they are: a majority of analytic philosophers, including several value theorists who work on meaningfulness in lives; at least one Kantian ethicist; a couple of utilitarians; some philosophers of art who will be wary of a novelist making claims about the novel as a form; a scattering of literary scholars who admire Schlick's essay mainly as a period piece of the Vienna Circle; and graduate students. There will be no Schlick specialists, but two or three people will know the essay well and will notice if I have misread him.

Prepare me a Q&A prep sheet. Give me the objections I will actually receive from this audience — not the objections a literary audience would raise — ordered roughly by the damage each would do to the talk if I answered it badly. Phrase each objection the way its likely source would actually phrase it: a value theorist who thinks meaningfulness is a sui generis category irreducible to affective states will not put the point the way the utilitarian will, and neither will put it the way the Kantian will when pressing me on whether play can bear the weight of a life's worth. For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, and mark plainly where that reply is soft — where a sharp follow-up would push it over. Include the objections I should simply concede, with the most graceful form the concession could take without abandoning the thesis. Tell me which single reply has to land for the talk to survive. And include the question I am hoping nobody asks — which I suspect concerns whether Schlick's argument proves too much, since it seems to make the idle amusements of a comfortable life more meaningful than sacrifice, labor, or love undertaken for another's sake.
