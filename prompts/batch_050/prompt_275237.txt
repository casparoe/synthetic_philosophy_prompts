I teach a mixed undergrad seminar on political language, about 18 students, one 90-min session, half politics majors half philosophy. Almost all of them have skimmed Orwell's essay on politics and the English language (or had it assigned in high school) and they file it under "style advice: prefer short words." I want an activity that breaks that -- something that makes them feel, in their own fingers, the claim he makes there and then pushes much further in Nineteen Eighty-Four: that lazy, inflated, euphemistic language isn't just a symptom of muddled or brutal thinking, it feeds back into it, until eventually certain thoughts get hard to think at all.

What I'm imagining (improve freely): a staged thing in rounds, where early on they have to produce language about a morally loaded scenario under constraints that reward vagueness, passives, abstractions, committee-speak -- and then in later rounds they, or a different group of students, have to reason and decide things using only the language the earlier round produced, so they catch themselves literally unable to see the thing anymore. The feedback loop has to happen to them before anyone names it. No lecture up front, and I don't want them to hear Orwell's name until the debrief, because once they know where it's going half the class will just perform agreement with him.

So give me: the scenario or scenarios to use (fictional is fine, maybe better than real so it doesn't turn into a debate about the underlying politics); the exact rules per round -- what constraints get imposed, in what order, and some payoff or points scheme so the sanitizing feels like winning rather than just an arbitrary instruction; materials; a minute-by-minute timeline; and a debrief that maps each round onto the two different claims -- the weaker one, that bad language makes bad thought easy and comfortable, and the stronger one, that a language can make some thoughts close to unthinkable -- and forces them to argue about which one the activity actually demonstrated, because I genuinely think it only shows the first and that gap is pedagogically interesting.

Also: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. I assume some students will turn it into a comedy exercise (who can write the most ridiculous bureaucratese), and I need to know how to keep the stakes feeling real without any actual stakes. And how to handle the two or three students who've definately read 1984 and will recognize the game in round one and spoil it. Finally be honest about the limits: Orwell is describing whole speech communities over years, and 90 minutes with willing volunteers probably can't touch the strongest version of the claim -- tell me which part of the idea I just have to say out loud in the debrief because no game can produce it.
