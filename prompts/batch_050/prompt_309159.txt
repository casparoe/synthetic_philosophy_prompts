I put together the following argument for a philosophy of physics reading group, and it appears to prove something absurd. I'd like a diagnosis of where it goes wrong -- or, if it doesn't go wrong anywhere, an account of which premise is cheapest to give up and what that costs.

Setup: consider the two electrons in a ground-state helium atom, or if you prefer, the two electrons of a spin singlet prepared in a laboratory. Their joint state is an entangled, antisymmetrized state of the pair, and quantum mechanics assigns each electron the very same reduced state -- for the singlet, a maximally mixed spin state with no definite direction of spin on either side.

The argument:

(1) If x and y are numerically distinct, there is some property possessed by one and not by the other. Equivalently, things that share all their properties are identical.

(2) Intrinsic properties do not distinguish the two electrons: same mass, same charge, same spin magnitude.

(3) State-dependent properties do not distinguish them either: each electron individually is in exactly the same mixed state, so every possible measurement on either one yields the same probability distribution.

(4) Relational properties do not distinguish them: each bears the same spatial relations to the nucleus (the joint spatial wavefunction is symmetric), the same coupling to external fields, and whatever relations each bears to the other -- being entangled with, being correlated with, being some distance from -- the other bears to it in turn, by symmetry.

(5) From (1) through (4): the two electrons share all their properties, so they are numerically identical. Helium contains one electron, not two.

(6) The reasoning generalizes to any collection of same-type particles in a symmetrized or antisymmetrized joint state -- which is to say, all of them. So the universe contains at most one electron.

(7) But chemistry and spectroscopy require two electrons in helium's ground state. Shell structure, the exclusion principle, and the periodic table all presuppose that we can count electrons and get numbers greater than one.

Two friends have offered conflicting diagnoses. One says the argument equivocates on the notion of a property somewhere between (2) and (4). The other says the trouble is earlier, in the move from facts about the joint state of the pair to claims about the properties of each member. I don't know which is right, or whether both are. For my part, (2) through (4) seem as close to empirical facts as philosophical premises get, and I'd give up almost anything before (1), since I don't know how to think about distinctness at all without some version of it -- though I'm open to a restricted version surviving in place of (1) as stated.

What I want: identify the step or steps where the argument fails, or say which premise is least costly to reject if it is valid; explain why the failing step looked fine; and price the main alternatives, including at least (a) restricting (1) to qualitative or non-relational properties, (b) denying that entangled particles possess individual properties at all, (c) holding that a relation such as having opposite spin to can distinguish two particles even when no monadic property does, and (d) rejecting the assumption that the state of the pair fixes the properties of its members.

Format: give the diagnosis in prose first. Then append a JSON object with these fields: "failing_step" (a premise number, an inference between named steps, or "none -- the argument is valid"); "why_it_looked_fine" (one or two sentences); "alternatives" (an array of objects, each with fields "option", "what_you_give_up", and "cost_rating" of low, medium, or high); and "recommended_fix" (the cheapest viable repair and the residual cost of adopting it).
