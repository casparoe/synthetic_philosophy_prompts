Next month I'm presenting at an economic history workshop and I need to survive the Q&A. My paper defends a fairly modest version of the institutionalist thesis on why some countries got rich: not that institutions are the only thing that matters, but that once you have a decent measure of how extractive a country's institutions were around, say, 1800, that variable predicts long-run income differences better than any geographic variable we've been able to construct, and that the correlation survives the obvious reverse-causality worries better than the skeptics admit. Basically the inclusive/extractive story, hedged.

The audience is the problem. It's about fifteen people: a core of applied econometricians who cut their teeth on the critique literature around the settler-mortality instrument (the measurement error issues, the fact that the instrument is barely measured, the overidentification doubts), two cliometricians who think in terms of persistence studies and think most big-cause regressions are under-identified narrative dressing, one historical geographer who thinks the whole institutions-vs-geography framing is a false dichotomy since institutions are partly downstream of disease environments and crop suitability anyway, and a few PhD students who will ask whatever's fashionable this year. Nobody there is a culture person, thankfully, so I probably don't need to defend against that flank, though someone might bring it up to be difficult.

What I want is a prep sheet, roughly the objections in the order they'll actually come up, phrased the way those specific people would phrase them (the econometricians will talk about exclusion restrictions and weak instruments, the cliometricians will talk about how many effective observations you really have when everything clusters by continent, the geographer will accuse me of treating endowments as randomly assigned). For each one: do I concede it, and if so how much, or do I push back, and if I push back what's my two-minute answer. Also tell me where each of my replies is soft, because I'd rather know now. And be honest about the weakest point of the hedged thesis itself -- my sense is it's the word "extractive," which I suspect I can't define in a way that's both non-circular and measurable, but maybe there's somewhere softer I havent noticed. Finally, tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks, so I can at least have something ready if one of the students asks it.
