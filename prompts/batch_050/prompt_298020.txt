After my father entered hospice last month, I became his healthcare proxy. Our family now disagrees less about whether his preferences matter than about how to distinguish a genuine preference from a bias. He refuses morphine because he associates it with moral weakness, prefers some aides to others for reasons he cannot explain, and recently changed his view about treatment after watching an online video. Any of these responses might express insight, fear, social pressure, prejudice, or a stable value. I am preparing a workshop for relatives and hospice staff and want a classification instrument that clarifies the philosophical positions without recommending what I should do in any particular case.

Construct a decision tree that begins with this situation: an adult expresses a morally significant preference, meaning a desire or aversion that the person treats as normatively significant or that imposes morally relevant costs on someone else. The tree should classify positions on when that preference should be honored, corrected, discounted, or overridden.

Build the tree from the logical space of possible answers rather than from a list of established philosophical labels. Use no more than ten decision points and eight to ten leaves. Phrase every branch as a concrete question that a hospice worker or family member could answer without already knowing which position it supports. The decision points should require respondents to distinguish:

- deference to a person’s current preference from appeal to what the person would endorse under calmer, better-informed, or more reflective conditions;
- objections to a preference’s causal origin from objections to its content or reliability;
- self-regarding preferences from preferences that impose burdens on third parties;
- social influence that contaminates judgment from social influence that partly constitutes a person’s values;
- instability caused by temporary incapacity from instability that merely reveals an unresolved conflict;
- an independent moral standard from a purely procedural standard such as equal concern;
- the stakes and reversibility of intervention from the underlying classification of the preference;
- the laundering trap, in which an allegedly neutral filtering procedure smuggles in the evaluator’s own values, and the symmetry problem, in which the evidence used to expose another person’s bias might equally expose the evaluator’s.

Do not let offensiveness, disagreement, social influence, instability, or a psychiatric diagnosis function as conclusive evidence by itself.

Include leaves for at least these positions, using these descriptive labels or better ones: present-preference sovereignty; idealized endorsement; causal-reliability filtering; independent-reasons filtering; narrative or identity endorsement; procedural equal regard; stakes-sensitive contextualism; and no-principled-line skepticism. For each leaf, provide a one-sentence definition, the test that places someone there, the practical form of intervention it permits, and one sentence identifying its most awkward commitment. Do not attribute the positions to named philosophers.

After the tree, apply every leaf to the following five cases in a compact matrix:

1. My father refuses morphine after watching a video portraying pain medication as moral weakness.
2. He rejects an otherwise competent aide because of the aide’s race while describing the reaction as a matter of personal comfort.
3. He adopts a vegan diet after watching a documentary, and relatives disagree about whether he has been enlightened or manipulated.
4. During a period of delirium he asks to stop all treatment, although he consistently reaffirms the treatment plan when lucid.
5. He asks to remove his gay grandson from his will but cannot explain what principle justifies the change.

Where a position gives no determinate answer without an additional fact, identify the missing fact rather than forcing an outcome. Then identify at least three coherent combinations of answers that occupy no leaf in the tree and explain whether each is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot reliably separate and state what further diagnostic question would be needed.

Please write the entire response in American English.
