Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of three hours over Book XI of Augustine's Confessions — in particular, over whether Augustine's conclusion that time is a distention of the mind commits him to holding that time is unreal, or only to a thesis about how intervals of time get measured. Neither of us conceded anything. As it happens, I am grading midterm examinations this week for my survey course in the history of philosophy, and one of the questions on the paper bears directly on our dispute. I will not say which side of it I defended, since I do not want to prime your assessment; precisely because the argument is still fresh, I do not trust my own first reaction to the answer below. I would like an impartial grade and the reasoning behind it.

The question as set:

Augustine observes that we measure time — we call one interval long and another short, and we compare them — yet the past no longer exists, the future does not yet exist, and the present, if it is truly present, has no extent. Reconstruct this puzzle as Augustine presents it in Book XI of the Confessions, explain his solution, and assess whether the solution commits him to the view that time is merely subjective.

The paper is marked out of 20: 5 points for the reconstruction of the puzzle, 4 for the treatment of the astronomical definition Augustine rejects, 6 for the positive solution, and 5 for the final assessment.

The student's answer, in full:

Augustine's puzzle is that we measure time — we say one lecture lasted twice as long as another — yet there seems to be nothing there to measure. The past no longer exists, the future does not yet exist, and the present has no extent: any present with duration, whether a century or a second, divides into a part already past and a part still to come, leaving only an indivisible instant, and an instant without extent cannot be long or short. So the long and short times we confidently measure are nowhere to be found.

Augustine considers the suggestion that time just is the movement of the heavenly bodies and rejects it. A body's movement itself takes time; the same interval could contain more or less movement, since a body may move faster or slower; and time would continue even if the heavens stood still. Time therefore cannot be defined by any motion.

His positive solution relocates the measurable object. What is present to us of the past is memory; of the present, direct attention; of the future, expectation. When we measure an interval we do not measure the things that have passed, which are gone, but the impression they leave in the mind, which remains after them. Time, Augustine concludes, is a distention (distentio animi) of the mind itself, and it is in the mind that times are measured. Reciting a hymn from memory shows the structure: expectation of the verses to come passes through attention into memory, and the whole action is stretched out between the two.

This solution shows that Augustine takes time to be merely subjective. Since neither past nor future exists, and time is nothing but a condition of the soul, temporal succession has no reality outside the mind: passage is something we experience, not something that happens. In this Augustine anticipates McTaggart's conclusion that time is unreal. Nor does Augustine leave time any footing in the physical world, since his refutation of the astronomical definition shows that time is independent of all physical change; it is a form the mind imposes on experience, much as in Kant. Augustine also holds that there was no time before the creation of the world, time having come to be together with created things.
