I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, and I've ended up as the de facto ethics person on a procurement project, which is how I got into a months-long argument with a coworker. Our company contracts with overseas manufacturers whose factory conditions would horrify most of our employees, and the question on the table is whether we should impose a wage floor on our suppliers (with the risk they simply relocate or subcontract further down the chain). My coworker circulated a short argument in our internal wiki that she says settles the moral side of it. The argument convinces me right up until it doesn't, because by the end it seems to prove that price-gouging hurricane evacuees is fine, and I can't accept that. Here it is, essentially verbatim in structure:

(1) If two competent adults voluntarily enter a transaction, neither is deceived or coerced, and each ends up better off than they would have been had the transaction not occurred, then neither wrongs the other by transacting -- whatever one might separately say about third parties or background institutions.
(2) Sweatshop workers choose factory employment over the alternatives actually available to them -- subsistence farming, scavenging, informal day labor -- and they stay in it when they could leave. So by their own revealed judgment, the job beats the alternative, which is just what "better off than without the transaction" amounts to here.
(3) So sweatshop owners do not wrong their workers by employing them at prevailing wages and conditions. (From 1 and 2.)
(4) But nothing in this reasoning is specific to factories. The shopkeeper who quintuples generator prices as a hurricane approaches sells only to willing buyers, each of whom prefers an overpriced generator to none. The payday lender's borrower prefers a 400% loan to eviction. The private clinic's patient prefers an extortionate operation to no operation.
(5) So the price-gouger, the payday lender, and the clinic wrong no one either. (From 1 and 4.)
(6) Hurricane price-gouging is wrong. That is about as solid a moral judgment as I have, more solid than any premise in this argument.
(7) So the argument proves too much -- unless I'm wrong about (6), which I'm not prepared to accept.

Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd like to keep both (3) -- I do think the factory job can be permissible even at wages I'd consider insulting -- and (6), so if those two are in genuine tension I'd like that shown rather than assumed. Something feels slippery about the phrase "better off than they would have been had the transaction not occurred," as if it quietly builds in an answer to the question of what the comparison point should be, but I can't articulate the objection and I might be fooling myself. Please tell me why the bad step looks fine (they all look fine, which is the problem), and price the main repair strategies: what extra machinery do I need if I want the sweatshop case and the hurricane case to come out differently?

Two follow-ups I'd like folded into the answer. First, my coworker's response when I pressed her was that the comparison point itself can be rigged: if the region's commons were enclosed a century ago, or the employer lobbies against minimum-wage law and union organizing, then the job only looks good relative to a baseline the employer's side helped create, and premise (2) quietly stops being true in the relevant sense. Does that reply actually rescue the argument, relocate the flaw, or just change the subject? Second, suppose it turns out the owner does wrong the workers in some sense. Does anything follow about what we should do -- boycott, wage floors, withdrawal -- given that the workers themselves would rather we keep buying? I have a nagging sense that "the owner acts wrongly" and "we ought to intervene" are being run together in our internal debate, but I can't get the separation clean either.
