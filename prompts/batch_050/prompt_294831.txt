A few months ago I stumbled on a thought experiment in an online puzzle forum -- I later learned it's sometimes called the Pasadena game -- and it has cost me a friendship's worth of arguments since. Here's the setup as I understand it. A fair coin is flipped until it comes up heads. If the first heads appears on flip n, you receive a payoff of 2^n/n dollars, positive if n is odd and negative if n is even (so +2, -2, +8/3, -4, +32/5, and so on, with each outcome having probability 1/2^n). When you try to compute the expected value in the obvious way, you get the series 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which two features make treacherous: the positive terms alone diverge to infinity and the negative terms alone to minus infinity, so the expectation is undefined in the standard sense; and by rearranging the order of the terms, the series can be made to sum to any number whatsoever (or to diverge), even though summed in its natural order it settles at about 0.693.

My friend and I have settled into two fixed positions, and I'd like you to draft the document we should have produced instead of arguing. My position, put neutrally: the game has a definite fair price of about 0.693 dollars, because there is a privileged order -- the order in which the outcomes are indexed and would in any real playing actually be settled -- and taking the limit of the partial expected values in that order is not an arbitrary convention but the natural extension of expected value to this case. My friend's position, put neutrally: the game has no value, full stop; the rearrangement freedom is not a bookkeeping nuisance but a sign that the question "what is this game worth?" has no answer, and any number anyone assigns has been smuggled in by a convention that does no work beyond hiding the arbitrariness.

Draft the joint statement we could both sign after a proper adversarial collaboration. Specifically, I want:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. These should include the plain arithmetic facts (the divergence of the two halves of the series, the rearrangement result, the unboundedness of the payoffs), but also substantive agreements if there are any -- for instance, whether we can both agree the game is preferable to some definite alternatives and dispreferable to others even without agreeing it has a value, and whether ordinary dominance reasoning survives here.

2. A precise statement of each remaining disagreement, phrased so that each of us would accept the wording of our own side. Please be alert to the possibility that part of our dispute is verbal -- for example, I suspect we may use "the value of the game" to mean different things, and if so I want that isolated and named rather than smoothed over.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that resolving them would force at least one of us to move. One I want included is the sequential-settlement variant: suppose the game is not a lump-sum bet but is genuinely played out flip by flip, with each round's payoff credited or debited as it occurs, so the natural order is fixed by time rather than chosen by an accountant. Does that variant have a definite expected value even by my friend's lights, and if it does, does that concede anything about the original one-shot game? For each crux, name the case or argument we would both accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each of us predicts about it and what we would each have to concede if the prediction fails.

Please don't adjudicate between us, and don't soften either position into something easier to defend. The deliverable is a text we could both put our names to, with the residue of genuine disagreement stated as exactly as the arithmetic allows.
