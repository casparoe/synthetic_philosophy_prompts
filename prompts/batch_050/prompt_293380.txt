So I've been reading LessWrong and adjacent blogs since basically the sequences era, and the standard rationalist package on death (anti-deathism, "death is a tragedy not a transition," cryonics, all that) is something I bought into years ago and never really stress-tested. But a friend of mine who's into ancient philosophy keeps poking at me about Epicurus, and I finally sat down and read the Letter to Menoeceus properly. It's not the famous "when we are, death is not come" line that got me — it's this bit right after:

"The wise person does not deprecate life nor does he fear the cessation of life. The thought of life is no offense to him, nor is the cessation of life regarded as an evil. And even as people choose of food not merely and simply the larger portion, but the more pleasant, so the wise seek to enjoy the time which is most pleasant and not merely that which is longest."

(Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus, in the Hicks translation.)

Anyway, here's roughly where I've landed, and before I embarass myself in front of said friend I'd like to know whether it hangs together:

1. Death is bad for the person who dies, when and because it deprives them of good life they would otherwise have had. So the Epicurean conclusion is wrong, even if some of his arguments are fine.
2. The passage above is basically correct as life advice and I endorse it: what matters is how good your time is, not how much of it you get.
3. Curing aging would be one of the best things that could happen to me personally; living to 500 in good health would be enormously good for me, way better than dying at 80.
4. Epicurus's timing point stands: there is no time at which being dead is bad for me — none while I'm alive, none after. That's why I don't fear being dead. But preferring more life doesn't require fearing its absence, so this doesn't touch (1).
5. Nothing that happens after my death can be bad for me. If my partner breaks a promise to me after I'm gone, or my life's work gets deleted, that's a loss for the world, but it doesn't harm me — there's no me around to be harmed.

My friend claims 1 and 5 contradict each other but refuses to say why, which is annoying and I think wrong. My own guess is that if there's a real problem it's between 2 and 3. So: which pairs genuinely conflict, which only look like they do, and how severe is each? For each real tension, what's the cheapest fix and what does it cost? And if I could only revise one commitment to clean up the most, which should it be?

One more thing, since alot hinges on it: am I even reading the passage right in (2)? Does "not merely that which is longest" actually commit me to being indifferent to duration once life is pleasant enough, or is Epicurus only saying something about tradeoffs? Because if it's the former I may have to rethink more than I thought.
