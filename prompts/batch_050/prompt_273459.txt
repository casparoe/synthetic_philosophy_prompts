I'm a high school senior taking an ethics elective, and for the final unit each of us has to submit a short statement of our views on a chosen topic and then defend it against objections from the class. My topic is when a person is in a position to blame someone else — the question of who has the standing to criticize whom. The topic isn't abstract for us: last semester a student on the honor council publicly called out a classmate for copying homework, and it then came out that he had done the same thing earlier in the year without ever reporting himself. Most of my friends thought he had no business saying anything; a few insisted that wrong is wrong no matter who says so. After that argument I wrote down what I found myself actually believing, and before I hand it in I want to know whether the set hangs together. Here it is:

(a) Standing to blame is real and can be forfeited: someone who is himself guilty of the very conduct he condemns, and has not owned up to it, is in no position to deliver that condemnation, even if every word of it is accurate.
(b) The moral status of an act does not depend on who is speaking. Copying homework is wrong, and stays wrong, whether the person saying so is a saint or a repeat offender.
(c) What makes hypocritical blame objectionable is the dishonesty in it: the hypocrite implicitly presents himself as clean, and that pretense is what we resent.
(d) Someone who committed the same wrong in the past but has confessed, made amends, and genuinely changed has full standing to blame — arguably more than a person who was never tempted, because he speaks from experience.
(e) Standing cannot require a spotless record, since no one has one; if any past wrongdoing disqualified a blamer, moral criticism would become impossible and no community could enforce its own standards.

What I'm asking for is an audit, not an overview of the topic. Tell me which pairs of these commitments genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to at first glance, and order the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. For each real tension, spell out exactly where one claim's rationale undercuts another's — including how it plays out in concrete cases like a wrongdoer who is completely open about his own record and keeps blaming anyway — and state what the least costly fix would be and what it would force me to concede about the honor council case. Then tell me which single revision would make the whole set consistent at the lowest overall cost. I haven't decided which commitment I'd sacrifice, so please don't assume one is untouchable; I want the price of each option laid out clearly.
