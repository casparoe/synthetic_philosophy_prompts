In my advocacy and professional ethics seminar (fourteen final-year law undergraduates, one 80-minute workshop) we have just spent two sessions on perjury and on the legal line between lying and misleading: in many jurisdictions a witness who says something literally true, knowing it will send the jury in the wrong direction, has not committed perjury. My students accept the distinction on paper but plainly do not feel it; they keep insisting the misleading witness "basically lied", and the discussion goes in circles. I want a game that makes them feel the gap between what a speaker says and what a speaker means — Grice's distinction, though I would like them to meet the phenomenon before they meet the terminology, so neither his name nor the word "implicature" should come up until the debrief.

Design the game, with these constraints. First, communication must happen under a restriction so that literal encoding is impossible or expensive and players must rely on what their words suggest in context rather than on what they strictly say — but not a variant of Taboo or charades where the winning strategy is just synonyms; the decisive moves should exploit the hearer's reasoning about what the speaker could have said and chose not to (for instance, saying "some" in a situation where the hearer knows the speaker would have said "all" if they could). Second, at least one round should make a literally true, relevant utterance the losing move — it misleads a trusting partner — so the room has a concrete incident to argue about afterwards. Third, run it on points rather than money, and keep all fourteen students active rather than watching two volunteers. Fourth, if you can manage it, build in an asymmetry in what the two sides know about the game's own rules and expectations, because I suspect whatever breaks down there is the philosophically interesting part; if you include this, explain why you designed it that way.

Then give me the debrief: map specific moments of play onto the said/implicated distinction, onto the maxims (quantity and relevance at minimum), and onto the difference between openly flouting a maxim and quietly violating one; connect it back to the lying/misleading line the students kept resisting; list two or three ways this will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (a hearer who refuses to draw any inference that is not logically guaranteed, a sender who thinks the aim is simply to be obscure, or whatever you actually expect) and how to handle each; and be candid about which feature of Grice's account the game cannot reproduce — my guess is the open-endedness of real conversational context, or cancellability, but tell me which one genuinely breaks under the game's fixed rules and why. Please answer in British English.
