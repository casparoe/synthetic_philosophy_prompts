I chair the acquisitions and digitization committee of a small historical society. We have the budget to take on exactly one project this year, and I want our decision to cohere with our own record rather than be relitigated from scratch. Our recent verdicts:

1. We digitized a collection of deteriorating cassette interviews with retired textile workers. Research demand was low, the tapes were physically decaying, the workers speak in their own voices, and almost nothing else documents their working lives.
2. We accepted the admission ledgers of a shuttered psychiatric hospital that was about to dispose of them. The patients authored nothing in them; the entries were written entirely by staff, but the ledgers are nearly the only surviving record of those patients' lives.
3. We declined the business archive of a nineteenth-century shipping magnate, offered in excellent condition. He and his firm are already extensively documented in other repositories.
4. We declined to digitize a county's parish registers. Genealogists request them constantly, but they are already microfilmed and consultable at a state archive.
5. We accepted, with access restrictions, police surveillance files on a 1960s tenants' organization, found molding in a basement. The perspective is hostile to the group, but the files are the fullest surviving record of its activities.

The new case: descendants of people enslaved on a local plantation have asked us to digitize the estate's ledger books. These are the only surviving documents that record those individuals' names, births, and the breaking of their families, though every word was written by the enslaving family. The originals are already held in stable conditions by a university archive three hours away, which has microfilmed them; consulting them requires an in-person research visit.

Two of our precedents seem to pull in opposite directions, and I want you to do the adjudicating. Please: give your recommendation; identify which precedent is the closest analog and why; state the two most plausible rules that each fit all five earlier verdicts, show that they diverge on this case, and say which one our track record better supports; then state honestly what principle we would be adopting under each possible decision, including what that principle would commit us to in the awkward direction (e.g., toward records kept by the powerful about the powerless, or toward letting preservation risk dictate everything); finally, describe one hypothetical future case that would most cleanly discriminate between the two rules. If you think a third rule fits the precedents as well as the two you selected, note it briefly.

Open with your recommendation in a single sentence, and keep the whole response under 450 words.
