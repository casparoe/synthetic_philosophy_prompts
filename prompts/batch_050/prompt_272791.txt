I work as a site reliability engineer, and a running joke at my company is that every complex system failure gets the same treatment: a blameless postmortem, a "five whys" root-cause analysis, and a list of action items nobody completes. Over the holidays I fell down a Wikipedia rabbit hole on societal collapse -- the Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya, Rapa Nui -- and I couldn't stop reading the scholarship through that lens. Tainter says collapses happen when complexity's returns diminish; Diamond leans on ecocide; the McAnany and Yoffee crowd push back that "collapse" usually means political reorganization while ordinary people carry on, and that calling it failure is a value judgment smuggled in as a description. Meanwhile my industry's whole epistemology of failure insists that there is never a single root cause, that "human error" is an explanation-stopper, and that the system was doing exactly what it was designed to do. I want to see these two discourses collide.

Write a parody incident postmortem, roughly 700 words, treating the Late Bronze Age collapse (with the Classic Maya and Rapa Nui as prior incidents referenced in a "similar past incidents" section) as a production outage reviewed by a blameless SRE team. Use real postmortem structure: Summary, Impact, Timeline, Root Cause Analysis, What Went Well, Lessons Learned, Action Items. The comedy should come from applying the genre's conventions faithfully to the actual historiographical disputes: the root-cause section must refuse to pick between drought, the Sea Peoples, trade-network fragility, and systems complexity because "incidents have contributing factors, not causes"; the Impact section must awkwardly confront the fact that most "users" experienced no outage -- population continued, successor polities emerged, the alphabet spread -- forcing the team to downgrade the severity rating mid-document; the What Went Well section should be disturbingly easy to fill in. The running irony is that blameless-postmortem methodology accidentally lands on the revisionist position: if no one is at fault and the "failure" was the system adapting, then collapse stops looking like an incident at all, and the Action Items (e.g., "reduce palace-economy single points of failure," "monitor topsoil metrics") should read as both sensible and obviously useless centuries later. Keep the tone deadpan corporate throughout -- no winking, no breaking format -- and include at least one reviewer comment thread where an engineer insists the Sea Peoples were "just traffic" and gets overruled. A reader who knows the collapse literature should feel the satire is fair to both the doom narratives and the resilience revisionists; a reader who only knows tech should still find it funny.
