Write, for my seminar on practical rationality, a dialogue between two bidders caught mid-auction in a dollar auction. The setup: an auctioneer is selling a $20 bill; the highest bidder wins the bill, but both the highest and the second-highest bidder must pay whatever they bid. The bidding now stands at $20 from Ana and $19 from Ben, and it is Ben's turn to act. Ana argues that it is transparently irrational to bid more than $20 for a $20 bill, that Ben should accept his $19 loss, and that her position is therefore unassailable; Ben replies that losing $1 beats losing $19, that his committed $19 is a sunk cost no sound theory of rationality may invoke, and that Ana's injunction to "just stop" would, applied symmetrically, tell her to stop as well — so it cannot justify her winning. Have them genuinely engage each other's reasoning rather than trade slogans: they should dispute whether the rationality of the next bid depends on predicting the other player's rationality, whether a sequence of locally rational moves can be irrational as a whole, whether any policy adopted in advance (never enter, cap bids at face value, randomize) could survive a rival who knew you had adopted it, and precisely where the mistake occurred — the first bid, or only the eleventh. Neither bidder is a fool; each should press at least one objection the other cannot easily parry, and the dialogue should end with Ben making his move and both of them grasping what it reveals.
