I've been reading around infinite ethics for a philosophy seminar — the cluster of problems that arises for aggregative consequentialism once you take seriously the possibility that the universe contains infinitely many sentient beings, spread through an infinite future or an infinite space. The bit that stuck with me is the worry sometimes called infinitarian paralysis: if the total sum of welfare is infinite no matter what I do, then expected-value reasoning seems to lose its grip entirely, and every action — donating to charity, napping, arson — comes out tied. What I find darkly comic is that the literature on this is written in a tone of perfect administrative calm, as if the collapse of moral arithmetic were an accounting inconvenience to be patched with footnotes about hyperreal numbers. It seems ripe for satire, and I'd like you to write it.

Write a mock charity-evaluation report, in the style of a scrupulously rigorous cost-effectiveness outfit, assessing a fictional nonprofit — something like the Unbounded Impact Fund — whose claimed intervention affects infinitely many beneficiaries. Around 600-700 words, deadpan throughout, formatted with the genre's usual apparatus: an executive summary, a cost-effectiveness estimate with a confidence interval, a section on room for more funding, and reservations confined to footnotes. The humor must come from applying the evaluator's standard toolkit to genuinely infinite quantities and refusing to blink: the analyst patiently explaining that the objection "two infinite expected values are equal" conflates cardinality with measure; DALYs averted expressed in non-standard analysis; a steep discount rate for beneficiaries at unbounded spatiotemporal distance defended as consistent with industry practice; the solemn bottom line that nearby, finite suffering rounds to zero in the final column. The target is the complacency of total-view expected-value reasoning when stretched past the domain where its tools were calibrated — not any named person, and not effective altruism as such. The satire should be fair enough that someone who works on infinite population ethics recognizes real positions being exaggerated (paralysis worries, spatiotemporal discounting proposals, non-Archimedean utility) rather than strawmen, while remaining legible to a reader who knows no philosophy: the comedy of a bureaucracy calmly filing its paperwork at the end of arithmetic should carry the piece on its own.
