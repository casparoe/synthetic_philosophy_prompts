For a collection of essays on the metaphysics of categories, I want to commission a specification before any theory is defended. The problem I have in mind is the identity of letterforms: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? The lowercase letters on this page, the ones on the street sign outside, a child's wobbly first attempt, an elaborate illuminated capital, and the letterforms of a typeface so stylised it is barely legible are all recognisably instances of the same twenty-six shapes, yet any two of them may share almost no visual features. To keep the problem tractable, imagine also a constrained testbed: an artificial alphabet in which every letterform must be built from straight segments drawn on a fixed grid of a few dozen points, and the question becomes which of the vast number of grid-permissible drawings count as a given letter, and which fail despite being geometrically near-misses.

Write the desiderata a theory of letterform identity must meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it: the phenomena it must explain (why competent readers can usually classify a letterform in an unfamiliar style at a glance; why some near-misses in the grid testbed read as the target letter and others as nothing at all; why a stroke can be essential in one style and a mere flourish in another), the distinctions it must not collapse (for example, between a letter as a role in a writing system, a letterform as a shape type, a token as a physical mark, and a style as a transferable way of rendering all the letters at once; and between the psychological question of how recognition works and the normative question of whether a given shape genuinely is an instance), and the results it must be consistent with (that entirely new styles can be invented and immediately understood; that the sameness in question survives changes of size, orientation within limits, and medium; that there are genuinely borderline cases). Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular approach, and explain the marking.

The part I care about most: identify any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say why. In particular, I suspect a tension between giving a purely geometric criterion of letterhood (which the grid testbed seems to invite) and explaining how humans track letter-identity across unlimited stylistic variation, and a second tension between treating style as secondary decoration and the observation that style is precisely what is copied when someone imitates a whole typeface. Whether these are real inconsistencies or only apparent ones should fall out of the list itself. Finally, and briefly, score the obvious contender families against the list — template-matching accounts, structural or relational descriptions, exemplar-based similarity, and any account that treats letters as carrying an analogical or essential core — but keep the weight on the criteria; I shall draw my own conclusions about the winner. A table for the desiderata themselves is welcome.
