I'm a software engineer at an AI lab with no formal philosophy background, and next week I'm presenting to our internal safety reading group (about fifteen people: mostly ML researchers from deep learning and RL, plus two members who read philosophy of mind seriously and one former academic philosopher). My talk defends this thesis: HCH (Humans Consulting HCH) and the factored cognition hypothesis are best understood not as engineering proposals but as an idealization of human deliberation — a reference model playing the same role for reasoning that the Turing machine plays for computation: deliberately unrealistic, but useful because it separates what a deliberative process can in principle reach from how efficiently any real system reaches it.

My argument rests on three supporting claims:

1. HCH is a fixed-point construction: a human consulting copies of itself, taken to the limit, defines what that human's reflective judgment converges toward. On my reading, that is a claim about deliberation itself, not about any buildable system.
2. Factored cognition is the analogue of a Church–Turing-style assumption: the bet that decomposing questions into sub-questions does not change which answers are reachable in principle, only the cost of reaching them.
3. The standard failure modes (memorization substituting for decomposition, the obfuscated-argument problem, drift under iterated amplification) are implementation-level concerns, and therefore do not bear on the idealization.
