Ok so I'm in a decision theory seminar this term and last week we did the absent-minded driver puzzle (the one my prof credited to Piccione and Rubinstein). Quick version in case you need it: you're driving home on a highway with two exits. Taking the first exit gets you payoff 0, taking the second gets you 4, and missing both and ending up at the beach gets you 1. The catch is you're absent-minded: at any exit you can't remember whether you've already passed one, and you can't tell the exits apart, so whatever you do, it has to be the same policy at each exit. Working it out ex ante, the best plan is to exit with probability 1/3 at each exit you reach. But -- and this is the famous part -- once you're actually sitting at an exit, your own recommended plan seems to tell you you should be at the second exit more often than the first, and suddenly exiting looks better than the plan says. You can't rationally follow your own optimal plan.

Fine, weird puzzle, whatever. But my flatmate (who's in the class too and loves this stuff way too much) turned it into an argument that honestly seems to prove way too much, and neither of us can find the hole. Here's his argument, numbered:

(1) Rational choice means: at the moment you act, pick the action that maximizes expected value given your beliefs at that moment.

(2) In the driver case, the plan that maximizes expected value from the planning standpoint (exit w.p. 1/3) is not what maximizes expected value from the at-the-exit standpoint, given the beliefs you rationally form there. So the driver rationally cannot execute his own best plan.

(3) Nothing about this depends on memory loss specifically. What drives it is just that your information when you execute a plan differs from your information when you made it.

(4) But execution always comes after planning, and time passing always changes your information at least a little. You always know more later -- even if it's just that the plan was made, that you didn't die in the meantime, etc.

(5) So for any plan whatsoever, the you who executes it has different information than the you who made it, and so (by 1-3) the executing-you can rationally find reason to deviate.

(6) Therefore rationality makes planning pointless: no rational agent can ever be counted on -- even by themselves -- to follow through. Promises, diets, putting the alarm clock across the room, Ulysses and the sirens, all of it is irrational self-delusion.

(7) But that conclusion is insane. Planning obviously works. People do follow through, and it's often rational to. So something above is wrong.
