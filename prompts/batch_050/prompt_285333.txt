I am a retired molecular biologist who has, over the past two years, been working my way through the philosophy of mind, and I keep running into the same problem when I try to explain the debate over philosophical zombies to friends from the laboratory: the positions are almost always presented as a list of -isms rather than as a sequence of forced choices. I would like to fix that. Please write a decision tree that takes a reader from the starting commitment "I grant that a perfect physical duplicate of me lacking conscious experience is genuinely conceivable, not just falsely imaginable" to a named position on the conceivability argument against physicalism.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from the names, in roughly the following way:

1. Each branch node should be one of the inferential steps the argument needs, phrased as a yes/no question the reader can answer without already knowing where the answer leads: whether conceivability in this idealized sense suffices for metaphysical possibility (and what would have to be true of the link between the two if it does not); whether, if a zombie world is metaphysically possible, consciousness must be something over and above the physical facts; whether the epistemic gap between physical and phenomenal description is itself an ontological gap or merely a feature of our concepts; and whether phenomenal facts must make a causal difference to anything if they exist at all. You may add or split nodes if the argument really requires it, but say why.

2. Each leaf should be a named position: property dualism of the sort associated with the conceivability argument, epiphenomenalism, type-A materialism (the view that zombies are not genuinely conceivable, e.g. eliminativist or illusionist treatments), type-B materialism (the view that the gap is conceptual but not ontological), and panpsychism. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves the tree cannot truly separate — I suspect epiphenomenalism and interactionist dualism, at minimum, will come apart only if you include the causal-efficacy node.

3. Note any combination of answers that lands in unoccupied territory and diagnose briefly whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

The audience is scientists, not philosophers, so each node should carry one sentence of motivation in plain terms (an analogy from chemistry or molecular biology is welcome) rather than a citation. The tree itself should have no more than a dozen leaves and should be accompanied by a short worked example tracing a single path from the root to property dualism, so that the reader can see how the instrument is meant to be used.
