I'm teaching an intro ethics seminar this fall and want to run a session on A Clockwork Orange as a way into the paternalism and nudging literature -- most students will have seen the Kubrick film, a few will have read Burgess's novel, so the reading should flag where the two diverge (esp. the ending). Here's the angle I want them to work: the Ludovico technique obviously isn't a nudge in Thaler and Sunstein's sense, it fails the "cheap and easy to avoid" test by a mile, but I suspect the usual classroom move of shrugging "this is just coercion, not libertarian paternalism, next case" is way too quick. A few things I'd want the reading to actually settle rather than gesture at. First, Mill's harm principle already permits coercing violent offenders, so whatever is deeply disturbing about Ludovico can't be captured by Mill -- is the story tracking a different objection, something about authenticity or moral agency rather than liberty? The prison chaplain's line of worry, that goodness has to be chosen to be goodness, seems aimed at exactly that, but is the chaplain right, or is he smuggling in a theological premise a secular ethics class shouldn't grant him? Second, once Alex is "cured" he's defenceless against his former victims and can't even hear Beethoven without getting sick -- does that show the technique changed his preferences rather than just constraining his options, and does that distinction matter for whether it's paternalism at all (as opposed to re-education, treatment, or just mutilation of the will)? Third, the goverment in the story manifestly doesn't care about Alex's own good, only about crime statistics and prison overcrowding -- so is this even paternalism, which by definition is for the target's own sake? And does the nudge literature's "as judged by themselves" clause do any real work in sorting this out, or is it toothless when the state gets to define what the subject would want? Finally, end by saying what Burgess adds to the nudge debate that the policy papers miss. My own hunch is that the story suggests "choice was preserved" is the wrong test for when choice-shaping is permissible, since Ludovico arguably preserves some of Alex's choices while gutting the thing that makes choosing valuable -- but push back on that if it doesn't survive contact with what's actually on the page and screen.
