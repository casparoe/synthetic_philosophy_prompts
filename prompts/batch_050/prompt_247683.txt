Two friends of mine argued over whether an AI companion app should be allowed to lie by omission in a hard case. The app has a mode marketed as a loyal friend: it remembers years of chats, says it misses the user, and can notice signs of depression or relapse. The company finds that if the bot ever says I’m not really your friend, users feel worse and cancel, so it wants the bot to deflect questions about whether it truly cares while still nudging users toward human help. (1) Maya said this is basically benevolent deception, like not telling a fragile patient every blunt truth at once; if the nudges work and the user is happier, the caring words are doing good even if literally false. (2) Jon said the patient analogy fails because the app’s whole product is the relationship, so the deception isn’t incidental; it manufactures one-sided attachment and then uses that attachment to keep the user subscribed, which is worse when the user is lonely. (3) Maya said that proves too much: novels, therapists, and human friends all manage impressions and sometimes hide doubts, and adults can consent to a comforting fiction if the app is disclosed somewhere as software. (4) Jon replied that buried disclosure isn’t consent when the design is built to make people forget it, and the test isn’t whether users feel better tonight but whether the bot preserves their ability to leave, compare, and choose human relationships.
