I teach a second-year undergraduate epistemology module (sixteen students, one eighty-minute seminar) and we are approaching the week on rational belief and inconsistency. Rather than presenting the lottery paradox and the preface paradox on slides, I want an activity in which the students produce the paradoxes themselves before either is named. My rough idea is to exploit the fact that the two are usually treated as cousins — one concerning a single raffle, the other a corpus of claims an author stands behind — and to stage them as two rounds of the same game, so that the class notices for itself that the second round is essentially the first one in different clothing. Please design the full activity: rules, roles, materials, a points scheme with small genuine stakes that does not involve money changing hands, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the debrief.

A few constraints and desiderata. First, the mechanism should put pressure on outright, flat-out belief rather than letting students hide behind probabilities: a student who answers "well, I am 95 per cent confident" should find that this does not get them out of the bind, because I want them to feel the gap between high confidence and belief rather than to dissolve it by stipulation. Second, the two rounds should run back to back with no lecture in between, and the moment of recognition — that the class has contradicted itself, or has refused to assert collectively what every member asserts individually — should be allowed to land before I say anything; please script how I should stay quiet without being obtuse. Third, the debrief should map specific moments of the session onto the logical structure: the rationality of believing of each ticket that it loses, the impossibility of believing that they all lose, the author's humble preface, and the principle that rational belief is closed under conjunction, ending with a menu of live options for what to surrender and what each surrender costs. Fourth, be candid about which feature of the philosophical problem the game cannot reproduce: I suspect that any payoff scheme turns it into a game about what it pays to say rather than what it is rational to believe, and I want an honest note on how much that matters and what I must supply in words afterwards. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students colluding, someone computing expected values out loud and spoiling the bind, the whole class agreeing to assert nothing at all — and how to handle each failure mode without breaking the spell.
