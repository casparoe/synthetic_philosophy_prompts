I have no training in philosophy, law, or history; I have simply been following the news in my country for years, and I have become stuck on the question of when governments owe apologies for things done long ago. I would like help thinking through my own government's record, because I cannot see the pattern, and a new case is coming up for decision. Here is the record as I understand it. During a war about eighty years ago, the government interned thousands of citizens of one ethnic minority under an emergency decree, without trials; many people at the time protested that this was lawless, and the government formally apologized some decades later, while a number of former internees were still alive. About fifty years ago, state agencies pressured unmarried mothers into giving up their children for adoption, and the government apologized for that too, with many of those mothers and their children still living. Roughly 150 years ago there was a famine that the government's trade and relief policies made far worse, and the government has repeatedly refused to apologize, saying that the officials involved acted according to the accepted thinking of their day. About a century ago a system of indentured labor, entirely legal at the time, ended, and the laborers' descendants are still a visibly disadvantaged community; that community has campaigned for an apology and has been refused. Finally, when serious physical abuse in state-run boarding schools came to light decades after the fact, the government apologized; some victims were alive, and the abuse had been criminal even when it occurred. The new case is this: about 140 years ago, government soldiers massacred an indigenous community, and an official inquiry at the time condemned the killings and punished some of the officers, so that even by the standards of that era the act was treated as a crime. No one alive today experienced it, but descendants of the community are asking for an official apology, and the government must decide. What I want to know is, first, what rule or principle best accounts for the five earlier verdicts, and what other rules fit them equally well; second, what each of those candidate rules would say about the massacre case, since I suspect they do not all agree; third, your best guess as to what the government will in fact do, and what it should do if it wants to be consistent with its own record, including which of the earlier cases is the closest analog and what principle it would implicitly be adopting whichever way it goes; and fourth, if the record genuinely underdetermines the answer, what further case, real or hypothetical, would tell the candidate rules apart. Please write your answer as continuous prose, like a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
