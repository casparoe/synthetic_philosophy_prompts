**Why your math proof might get "falsified" next Tuesday**

We like to think math is the one place where once you've proved something, you're done. Finished. Whereas science is this endless mess where today's theory is tomorrow's cautionary tale. I think this contrast is mostly wrong, and the person who convinced me is Imre Lakatos, who wrote a book structured as a classroom dialogue about Euler's formula for polyhedra (V - E + F = 2, the thing where vertices minus edges plus faces equals two). What Lakatos shows is that people kept finding polyhedra that broke the formula -- cubes with holes drilled through them, picture frames, that sort of thing -- and instead of binning the theorem, mathematicians did all these sneaky moves: they narrowed what counts as a polyhedron to exclude the troublemakers, they patched the theorem to apply to fewer cases, they rewrote the proofs. So the history of one theorem looks almost exactly like the history of a scientific theory getting battered by experiments.

My own claim goes a bit further than I think Lakatos goes. Everyone says: math differs from science because in science you have the tribunal of experience, nature gets a vote, whereas in math there's nothing outside the system to vote against you. But I think that's wrong. A counterexample to a proof is itself a piece of mathematics -- someone had to construct that picture-frame polyhedron and verify it satisfies the relevant conditions. So mathematics does have a tribunal, it's just staffed by more mathematics. Refutation in math is internal, not absent. And that's why Lakatos is right to call math "quasi-empirical": not because math secretly needs telescopes, but because its method -- conjecture, refutation, patch, repeat -- is the same method science uses, run on materials that happen to be homemade.

One more thing. Notice which direction the repairs go. In science, when the experiment disagrees with the theory, you fix the theory and keep the phenomenon -- nobody deletes the phenomenon. In math it's the reverse: you keep the theorem (or its spirit) and revise the concepts, or you mutilate the theorem's scope to save the concept. The theorem is the phenomenon, the definitions are the theory. If that analogy holds, then definitions in math play the role that hypotheses play in physics -- which would mean definitions, of all things, are the empirically vulnerable part of mathematics. I find that hilarious.
