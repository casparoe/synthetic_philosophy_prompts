I'm a second-year graduate student in philosophy, and I need a two-page proposal for a one-semester independent study whose final product would be a paper of roughly 8,000 words. The project grew out of an argument with a friend of mine, an economist, that started when I mentioned I had declined a free genetic screening offered through my university. He invoked Good's theorem on the value of information: for an expected-utility maximizer who updates by conditionalization, acquiring free information before making a decision can never make things worse in expectation, so turning down costless information is simply irrational, full stop. I countered with what I took to be counterexamples: someone who declines genetic knowledge because disclosing results could affect their insurability; a hiring committee that votes not to look at a leaked reference letter; a person prone to temptation who would rather not learn that there is cake in the house; a negotiator who prefers not to know certain facts because being known to know them would change the other side's behavior. His response, in each case, was that I had smuggled in a hidden cost, or a strategic environment, or an agent who fails to conditionalize properly — so the theorem itself survives untouched, and my intuitions were just misclassifications. My rejoinder was that if the theorem can only be defended by building every exception into its premises, then it may be true but nearly without application, and the interesting question is not whether it holds but where, if anywhere, it actually has normative force for real agents facing real decisions.

My half-formed idea is to make the argument itself the object of study: to take the standard roster of cases in which apparently rational agents decline free information, classify each one according to which premise of the theorem it actually violates (costliness of acquisition, imperfect updating, a decision problem that changes once the information arrives, a strategic setting in which others reason about what you know, dynamic inconsistency of the agent), and then ask whether anything is left over — whether there is a residual class of cases where every premise genuinely holds and declining the information still seems defensible. The proposal should state that residual question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, explain why the two positions my friend and I took — the theorem settles everything versus the theorem is vacuous — both leave it open, and lay out the method concretely: what the taxonomy would look like, which two or three cases I would work through in detail and why those, and why this premise-by-premise approach is better than the obvious alternatives (a general literature survey, or a purely formal treatment of the theorem's proof). It should also say what the possible conclusions are — for instance, that the theorem is a substantive norm with a delimited but real scope, or that it is best read as a partial definition of ideal rationality that licenses no advice to actual agents — and which considerations would decide between them. Please include what would count as the project failing, and an honest assessment of what could make it too big for one semester. No citations or bibliography: I'll add the references myself, so refer to positions and arguments by description rather than by author and year.
