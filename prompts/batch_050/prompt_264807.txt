I am a third-year philosophy student working as a teaching assistant for an undergraduate metaphysics course. Next week I have a single fifty-minute section to teach the failure of the simple conditional analysis of dispositions — the problems of finks and masks — to students who encountered the idea of a disposition (fragility, solubility) only last week and who have no formal background in counterfactuals.

Please act as an experienced teacher of metaphysics. Think carefully about the dependency structure of the material, and begin your answer with a brief brainstorm of the conceptual pieces the students must have in place before giving me the plan itself — I want the plan, not the explanation. Specifically:

1. The order of ideas. Should masks come before finks or the reverse? Please argue for one ordering rather than simply choosing it, and note at which point the plan should make explicit that one failure mode attacks the necessity of the conditional and the other its sufficiency, since I suspect students will not see this symmetry unaided.

2. The carrying examples. Can one example (say, the fragile glass) carry both problems, or do I need two? If two, specify what the fink example must have that a masking case lacks — removal of the disposition's causal basis at the moment of stimulus versus downstream interference with the causal process — and what it must lack so as not to smuggle in distracting physics.

3. The misconception map. I expect at least these: that a mask shows the disposition was absent after all; that finks and masks are the same phenomenon described twice; and that the analysis can be saved by appending a nothing-interferes rider. For each, indicate where exactly in the sequence it will arise and how to head it off, including why the rescue clause courts vacuity.

4. A closing question that tests whether they grasp the fink/mask distinction rather than having memorized the examples.

5. What I should openly decline to cover at this level (e.g., causal-basis metaphysics, dispositional essentialism) and how to decline without dampening interest.

The whole plan must fit fifty minutes, ten of them reserved for questions.
