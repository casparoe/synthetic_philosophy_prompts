I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on philosophy of mind: 14 students, one 100-minute session, seminar room with tables, no special equipment beyond paper, index cards, and small prizes (say, chocolates). Design an in-class activity that makes the extended mind thesis — the claim that some of an agent's cognitive states and processes literally extend beyond the skull into notebooks, devices, or the environment — feel like a live hypothesis rather than a catchy slogan. Crucially, students must not hear the thesis named or be told what the session is about until the debrief; they should encounter the phenomenon in their own behavior first and only afterward be given the theoretical vocabulary for it.

The activity should be built around the following arc, which you should design in detail:

1. A competitive task phase in which pairs of students work on a series of tasks where externalizing information into a physical scaffold (a notebook, laid-out index cards, tokens arranged on the table) confers a real advantage — for example, tasks involving tracking a changing set of facts, remembering instructions received several rounds ago, or planning under partial information. The scoring scheme should give small but felt stakes (points convertible into the prize pool) so that pairs that build fluent, habitual routines with their scaffold genuinely outperform pairs that keep everything in their heads. The rules should be arranged so that successful pairs stop consulting their scaffold deliberately and start using it automatically, the way one consults a memory.

2. A disruption phase in which the facilitator, citing some plausible pretense (equipment malfunction, a rule violation by another pair), confiscates or degrades each pair's scaffold mid-task. This should be engineered so that at least one pair experiences the loss not as losing a tool but as losing part of their working cognition — ideally mid-sentence or mid-plan, so the moment is visceral. Specify how to engineer this reliably: what state the task must be in, what the facilitator says, and what reactions to watch for and record.

3. A tampering round in which, with consent given in advance in general terms, the facilitator or a designated student secretly alters some pairs' scaffolds — introducing small errors into what is written down. Some pairs should catch the alteration and some should confidently act on corrupted information. This phase is meant to make students feel the force of the standard objection that externally stored information is too fragile, distrusted, or unendorsed to count as belief — and, on reflection, the force of the reply that biological memory has the same vulnerabilities. Specify how to run this without poisoning trust in the room for the rest of the session.

For each phase, give: the exact rules as you would state them to the class (wording matters, since students must not infer the point prematurely), the materials, a minute-by-minute timeline fitting the 100 minutes, and the payoff structure.

Then provide a debrief plan (roughly 25 minutes of the session) that: names the thesis only after eliciting students' own descriptions of what the disruption phase felt like; maps specific moments from the activity onto the criteria usually proposed for when an external resource counts as part of a cognitive system (constant availability, fluency of use, automatic endorsement, easy access); and stages the boundary question the tampering round raises — if a well-worn notebook counts as part of one's mind, does the internet, and what principled line separates the two? Give the facilitator concrete prompts for pushing students who answer too quickly in either direction.

Finally, include two candid notes. First, a failure-mode forecast: what will predictably go wrong the first time this activity is run (students who refuse to outsource and thereby ruin the comparison, pairs who figure out the theme early and start performing, the disruption landing as annoying rather than uncanny, the tampering round breeding resentment) and how to handle each. Second, an honest statement of which features of the philosophical position the activity cannot reproduce — for instance, that the thesis concerns standing dispositional states maintained over years rather than a scaffold adopted for an hour, and that no classroom exercise can settle whether functional parity is sufficient for genuine belief — and what the facilitator must supply by discussion to compensate.
