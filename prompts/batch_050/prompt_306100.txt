I sit on the board of a worker-owned manufacturer with about 350 employees, and at our last meeting a long-simmering argument finally came to a head, so I would like the question settled as rigorously as it can be. For fifteen years we have allocated our shared resources — machine-shop hours, warehouse space, the forklift fleet, and the annual equipment budget — through a quarterly planning council where department delegates negotiate a schedule and a spending plan. Growth has made this process slow, adversarial, and dominated by whoever argues longest. Management has now proposed replacing the council with an internal market: each department would receive an annual endowment of internal credits, bid for resources through a clearing algorithm, and watch prices adjust automatically as demand shifts. One faction on the board calls this market socialism in miniature and invokes Lange's trial-and-error pricing as proof it can work; the other invokes Mises and Hayek and insists the scheme must collapse under the same problems that doomed central planning. Two details matter. First, we are not an island: we buy our inputs and sell our output on ordinary markets, so genuine external prices exist for nearly everything except the internal services themselves. Second, more than half our revenue comes from bespoke orders, and much of the knowledge that determines real costs lives in the heads of senior machinists who neither attend meetings nor trust the algorithm to represent what they know.

Please describe the most important implications of the socialist calculation debate for this decision. I want a committed answer, not a survey: state in your first sentence whether we should adopt the internal market, reject it, or adopt a specified modified version of it, and then defend that verdict. In doing so, address whether Hayek's knowledge problem and Mises's calculation argument actually bind at the scale of a single firm embedded in a price system, whether Lange's mechanism of parametric prices adjusted by trial and error is even the right model for an internal clearing algorithm, and what follows specifically from the fact that our internal credits are not convertible into anything outside the firm. No preamble and no restating the question; lead with the verdict.
