I'm a retired molecular biologist (forty years in the lab, hung up my pipettes last spring) and since retiring I've gotten into philosophy the way other people take up golf. My reading group — three other retirees and one very patient grad student — has been stuck for two months on the Meno problem: why knowledge should be worth more than mere true belief, when a true belief gets you to Larissa just as well.

The group has definately hardened into two camps, and I want you to write the document they'd produce if they ran a proper adversarial collaboration. Camp one is basically reliabilist: knowledge is true belief produced by a dependable method, and whatever extra value it has comes from the method's reliability — though they keep getting hammered with the swamping problem, i.e. once the belief is true, the reliability of the source seems to add nothing, the same way a great espresso machine doesn't improve a cup of coffee that's identical to one a lousy machine made. Camp two is the credit crowd: knowledge is a success for which the believer deserves credit, and achievements are worth more than their products, like a goal you actually scored versus one that bounced in off the referee.

Here's what I want the joint statement to contain, in this order:

1. Agreed claims: a numbered list of claims both camps can honestly sign (about lucky guesses, about what true belief suffices for, that sort of thing).
2. Disagreements: each remaining disagreement stated in one or two neutral sentences, worded so each side would accept the formulation of its own view. If part of their fighting has just been about words, say so and dissolve it rather than dignifying it.
3. Cruxes: the two to four sub-questions where settling the answer would genuinely move at least one camp. For each crux, give one concrete case or thought experiment both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts the case shows. I'm used to lab meetings where you state your prediction before you run the gel, so don't let either side weasel out of that part.

Candidate cases you could use or replace: the road to Larissa, a stopped-clock style Gettier case, and a chemist who lands on the right answer through a bungled calculation.

Keep the whole thing under 700 words, write it in American English, and phrase it as a document both camps could actually put their names to — not a verdict on who's right. Where a disagreement can't be dissolved or tested, mark it plainly as residual instead of papering over it.
