DRAFT: "Two problems, not one"

Everyone agrees Gettier cases refute the justified-true-belief analysis of knowledge, but I think the literature conflates two genuinely different problems, and that once you separate them, each half becomes tractable on its own.

Half one: inferential cases. Gettier's own 1963 cases work like this. You have justified belief in some falsehood, and from it you infer a truth by competent deduction. Consider a new case of the same shape: Maya checks the staff directory, which says Devon is in office 412. Unknown to her, the directory is outdated and Devon officially moved to 418 months ago -- but by sheer coincidence, Devon is sitting in 412 today for a one-off meeting. Maya's belief "Devon is in 412" is justified and true, but it isn't knowledge. The diagnosis seems easy: her justification runs through a false lemma ("the directory is current"). So condition one: knowledge excludes justification that passes through a falsehood at any inferential remove. Call this the chain condition.

Half two: non-inferential cases. Fake barn country is the standard example: you look at the one real barn in a valley of barn facades, form the belief "that's a barn," and you're right, but it isn't knowledge. No inference, no false lemma -- the chain condition doesn't apply. What's sometimes called environmental luck (following Pritchard) has a different structure from the intervening luck in the directory case: nothing goes wrong *in* your reasoning; the world around you is just arranged so that your usual method could easily have failed. So condition two has to be modal, not doxastic: in nearby worlds where you use the same method and the target fact doesn't hold, you don't believe it. A safety-style clause, roughly.

So the proposal: knowledge = true belief + justification + chain condition + safety clause, and the two conditions are not doing the same work, because they catch different luck structures. The mistake was trying to find one condition that handles both.

The case I'm least sure about is Russell's stopped clock. You glance at a stopped clock that happens to show the right time. There's no inference through a falsehood -- you just read the clock. So it threatens the chain condition's coverage. My fix: extend the chain condition from "no falsehood in the inference" to "the justifier must be fact-connected," where a justifier is fact-connected iff the state of affairs doing the justifying (the clock's display) stands in a non-accidental explanatory relation to the fact believed. In the stopped clock case the display is explanatorily disconnected from the actual time, so it fails. In the barn case the justifier IS fact-connected (you're seeing a real barn), which is why we need the separate safety clause for it.

Conclusion: Gettier cases are two families with two fixes, and the clock case is where the two families touch.

Okay, comments I'd specifically like:

1. The big claim is the two-family taxonomy. Is it actually stable? I.e., can you construct a case that resists both the chain condition (extended) and the safety clause, or one that sits awkwardly between the families? That's the thing a referee would kill me with.
2. The "fact-connected" extension for the stopped clock feels like the weakest move in the draft. Is it circular -- does "non-accidental explanatory relation to the truth" just smuggle knowledge back in? If so, is there a better way to state it, or should I just concede the clock case to the safety clause and drop the extension entirely? Tell me which of those you'd advise.
3. My directory case: is it genuinely a Gettier case, or does someone wiggle out by saying Maya's belief is really "the directory says 412" and her inference to "Devon is in 412" fails? Does that response work for Gettier's origional cases too, and if it does, does it break my whole "inferential family" framing?
4. Have I misdrawn the environmental/intervening luck distinction, or overstated how cleanly it maps onto my two conditions? I lean on Pritchard here but only from memory, so flag anything that sounds off.
5. More boring but important: is the taxonomy claim even interesting, or is "Gettier cases come in structurally different types" something everyone already accepts and I'm dressing up as news? And what should I cut if this has to shrink to 1500 words?

Be blunt. Especially on 2.
