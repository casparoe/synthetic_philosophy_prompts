So here's the situation. I TA an upper-level undergrad seminar called Minds, Messages, and Meaning, and the prof just told me I get to write one question for this year's final exam. The timing is funny, because last month I was cleaning out my late grandfather's workshop and found a reel-to-reel tape with a label written in his private shorthand -- the tape might be his old band's recordings, or lectures he taped off the radio, or nothing at all, and since nobody alive reads his shorthand I've been stuck on the question of where the "meaning" of this thing actually resides. Is it in the grooves? In the label? In decoding conventions that died with him? I dug out my old copy of Gödel, Escher, Bach and reread the chapter where Hofstadter splits any message into three layers: the outer message (whatever tips you off that there IS a message and how to get at it), the frame message (the rules or key that do the decoding), and the inner message (the intended content itself). Now I want to build the exam question around that.

Write me one essay question, answerable in about 60 minutes, that forces students to actually use the three-layer analysis rather than recite it. The question should present a case where the tidy three-tier picture gets strained -- my seed idea is a message engineered so that one layer does work it's "not supposed to," like an outer message that's deliberately misleading, or a recording whose frame message has been destroyed while everything else survives intact, but if you can construct a better crux, go with that instead. The question should push students to take a stand on things like: if the frame message is unrecoverable, is the inner message's meaning destroyed too, or merely inaccessible? Can something count as a message if no possible audience could ever be tipped off that it is one? Students who memorized the definitions should flounder; students who actually get it should be able to seperate "the meaning is gone" from "we can't get at the meaning anymore."

Then give me a model answer and a marking scheme. The scheme should be built around five must-hit points, with marks for each adding up to 20, and for each point a one-line note on the specific way students typically blow it (e.g., treating the frame message as part of the content, or assuming meaning must physically reside somewhere in the artifact itself). One firm request: commit. I don't want a model answer that lays out three possible positions and declines to choose -- pick a definite stance on the hard case (I lean toward "the meaning survives the loss of the decoding key; only our access to it dies," but argue me out of it if you think that's wrong) and give reasons. Wishy-washy model answers make for wishy-washy grading, and I have forty of these to mark.
