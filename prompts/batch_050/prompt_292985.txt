so i'm in a reading group on divine foreknowledge and free will and we're doing molinism this week, and i kind of fell in love with the structure of the view even if i'm not sure i buy it. for context: the idea is that before deciding what world to create, God knows three kinds of stuff -- what could happen (natural knowledge), what will happen in the world he actually picks (free knowledge), and in between, "middle knowledge": for any possible person and any possible circumstance, what that person *would freely do* if placed there. the crucial bit is that these counterfactual truths aren't up to God and aren't up to us either, they just sort of... sit there, prior to any decree, constraining which worlds are even on the menu. so God doesn't cause my free choices, but he also doesn't merely watch them unfold -- he picks the world partly *because* of what he middle-knows i'd do in it.

anyway, i want you to act as a poet who's also a careful philosopher of religion (think carefully about the metaphysics first, maybe sketch your ideas before versifying, then write the poem). write me a villanelle about middle knowledge. the reason i want a villanelle specifically is that the two refrains should be the two recurring counterfactual truths -- the same lines returning in stanza after stanza the way the same fact about what i'd freely do returns in circumstance after circumstance, true in every possible world God considers, unchangeable by the decree that's coming. one refrain should carry the "in any circumstance, here's what you'd freely do" idea and the other should carry the "true before God ever willed it" idea, and the way they land differently each time should mirror how God's knowledge of them works differently pre-decree vs. post-decree. i'd love the final quatrain to lean into the slightly vertiginous thing that struck me in our discussion: that God knows what i'd do, yet i do it freely -- so the refrains meeting each other at the end should feel like the puzzle snapping shut rather than dissolving.

nineteen lines, five tercets plus a quatrain, proper villanelle rhyme scheme (aba aba aba aba aba abaa). please don't just name-drop molina or use the phrase "middle knowledge" in the poem itself -- the concept should be *in the form*, so that someone who knows the view reads it and goes "oh, that's exactly it," and someone who doesn't still gets a good poem about being known that thoroughly.
