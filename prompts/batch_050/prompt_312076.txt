I'm a software engineer on the reliability team of a payments platform. We run an automated mitigation service that, roughly two hundred times a day, detects an anomaly -- elevated error rates, a dependency timing out, a node behaving strangely -- and picks one of three responses: do nothing and keep monitoring, shift traffic into a degraded-but-safe mode, or trigger a full failover to a secondary region. Each response has uncertain outcomes. A failover done unnecessarily costs us money and customer trust; a failover skipped when it was needed can corrupt transaction data, which for us is close to the worst thing that can happen. Decisions must be made in milliseconds, with no human in the loop, though a staff engineer reviews a sample of decisions weekly and has to sign off on any change to the decision logic.

Right now the service compares options by plain expected value: it assigns each possible outcome a utility score, weights by estimated probability, sums, and picks the maximum. My manager has asked me to make the system "more conservative," and the current approach is to bolt on ad hoc penalty terms for scary outcomes. The result is inconsistent: sometimes the system is reckless, sometimes it fails over at the slightest provocation, and nobody can articulate the rule it's actually implementing.

While reading around for a principled alternative, I came across the idea of rank-dependent risk weighting, developed in recent decision theory as an alternative to expected utility. As I understand it, the idea is: instead of weighting each outcome purely by its probability, you first rank the possible outcomes of an action from worst to best, and then let the decision-maker's attitude to risk distort the weights, so that worse outcomes can count for more than their raw probability and better outcomes for less, with the degree of distortion set by a single risk function. A very cautious agent has a risk function that heavily up-weights the bad tail; a neutral agent's risk function leaves probabilities untouched and recovers ordinary expected value.

I want to turn this into a procedure my team can actually implement and operate. Write me that procedure. Specifically:

1. A step-by-step decision procedure, in pseudocode or a numbered flowchart in words, that takes as input (a) a set of candidate responses, (b) for each response a probability distribution over outcomes, and (c) a utility scale for outcomes, and outputs a choice. It should be explicit about the order of operations: ranking outcomes, applying the risk function, aggregating, comparing across responses.

2. Guidance for the parts that need engineering judgment. How should we pick the risk function in the first place, and who should own that choice -- is it a parameter an engineer tunes, a policy decision leadership must sign off on, or something that can be inferred from our historical decisions? Give us concrete questions to walk stakeholders through to elicit it, since nobody here can introspect a mathematical function directly.

3. How to handle the known awkward cases. In particular: outcomes that resist a single utility scale (a dollar loss vs. a data-corruption event vs. reputational damage); tail risks with tiny estimated probabilities where our probability estimates are themselves shaky; and the fact that we make hundreds of these decisions a day, so a choice rule applied per-decision may recommend something different from the same rule applied to the whole day's portfolio of decisions. Tell us which of these the framework genuinely settles and which it merely relocates.

4. Worked applications to three cases I'll supply: (a) a routine anomaly where all options are low-stakes, (b) an anomaly carrying a small probability of data corruption, and (c) a case where the most probable good outcome and the catastrophic tail belong to the same response. You may invent plausible numbers. I'd like at least one worked case where the procedure delivers a recommendation my manager will dislike -- either flagging a favorite response as unacceptable or waving through something that looks alarming -- so I can see the friction before we ship it.

5. An honest limitations section: where does this procedure misfire, what does it assume that our setting might violate, and at what points is judgment unavoidable no matter how the math is set up?

Assume I've never taken a philosophy or decision theory course; explain any technical vocabulary you can't avoid, and don't point me at readings I can't evaluate.
