Okay, I need help with a structural problem in a novel I'm writing. It's set in the early 2030s after two back-to-back catastrophic heatwaves, and the plot runs on the fight over whether to start injecting sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere to cool the planet down fast. I've got maybe eight characters who argue about this — a climate modeler, a US senator, a young activist whose group wants to just do it themselves with balloons, a diplomat from a low-lying island country, a fossil fuel lobbyist, a disaster insurance analyst, an indigenous leader whose land the proposed launch site sits on — and my editors note was brutal but fair: they all sound like the same person wearing different hats. Their disagreements are volume knobs, not real disagreements. If you swapped their lines around mid-scene nobody would notice, and that's death for the big dinner-party argument scene in chapter nine that the whole book hinges on.

So I want to build the cast on purpose this time. Please write me a decision tree I can run each character through, starting from roughly "we could measurably cool the planet within a few years for relatively little money" and ending at named positions on solar geoengineering. The fault lines my plot actually runs on, so the ones the tree needs to separate, are: (1) whether even small outdoor experiments are already a step too far, or only full deployment is; (2) the moral hazard worry — that the mere existence of a cheap fix lets everyone off the hook for cutting emissions, and whether that's an argument about the technology or just an argument about our own psychology; (3) the termination problem — once you start, stopping suddenly whipsaws the climate worse than gradual warming would have, so is starting without a credible decades-long continuation plan worse than never starting at all; and (4) who gets to decide — everyone affected, which is literally the whole planet, some subset, or whoever has the planes. Phrase each branch node as a question or a little concrete scenario a character could answer without knowing where it leads, the way "would you rather" questions work — not "are you a techno-optimist," which sorts nobody.

Then map the leaves onto positions that genuinely exist in this debate — ban all research, research-but-never-deploy, deploy only with near-global consent, deploy now as an emergency measure, sovereign right of any nation to deploy unilaterally, whatever else is actually out there — and for any combination of answers that nobody seems to occupy, tell me whether the seat is empty because the view is incoherent or just unpopular. That matters because one of my characters needs to be the weirdo holding a position nobody else in the room holds, and I want to know whether he can be written coherently or whether I'd be putting a contradiction in a trench coat. If two leaves can't really be told apart by any question, flag that rather than fudging it. Somewhere around 6-9 questions deep is fine. I'll be using the tree to check the chapter nine scene for real stakes, so accuracy about where these views genuinely come apart matters way more to me than covering every fringe position.
