I am taking a course in philosophy of mathematics, and at a recent guest lecture a speaker used Gödel's first incompleteness theorem as the basis of an extended analogy. Any consistent formal system rich enough to express arithmetic, he reminded us, contains a true sentence that the system itself cannot prove; yet a human mathematician, considering the system from outside, can recognise that this Gödel sentence is true. He then proposed that a human mind stands to any formal system as the mathematician stands to the axioms on the page, and concluded that no machine could ever fully reproduce human mathematical understanding — there would always be some truth we could grasp that it, being a formal system at bottom, could not. I gather this is a version of an argument associated with J. R. Lucas and, more recently, Roger Penrose.

I would like you to audit the analogy rather than simply endorse or dismiss it. Please give me an explicit table of correspondences: what, in the mind, is supposed to play the role of the axioms, the inference rules, the Gödel sentence, and the outside vantage point from which its truth is "seen". Then identify where the mapping breaks down — for instance, whether the mind must be assumed consistent for the argument to go through, whether it has anything like a fixed axiom set, and whether "seeing" the truth of the Gödel sentence is a capacity a machine lacks in principle or merely one the construction does not grant it in a single step. Distinguish the disanalogies that actually bear on the anti-mechanist conclusion from those that are merely cosmetic, say whether the conclusion survives once the serious disanalogies are on the table, and, if you think a different analogy better captures what the theorem genuinely shows about minds and machines, state what it is.
