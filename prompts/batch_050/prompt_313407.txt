okay so i'm running a reading group on Wigner's 1960 essay about the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics, and before we start arguing about actual explanations (we've got a platonist, a nominalist, one guy pushing the "it's all selection bias, we'd only be here to notice regularities where math happens to grip" line, and someone who's read too much Tegmark) i want the group to agree first on what an explanation of the phenomenon would even have to DO. otherwise everyone just shows up with their pet theory and talks past each other for two hours.

act as a philosopher of mathematics who's also genuinely comfortable with the physics examples. think carefully, and brainstorm before you commit to the list -- i'd rather you throw out ten candidate requirements and prune to six than hand me the same five everyone recites.

write me the desiderata: what an account of why math works so unreasonably well in physics has to deliver, with the case or phenomenon behind each requirement. here's what i think belongs on the list, but refine it or push back where i'm wrong:

- it has to handle the "pure math first, physics decades later" cases: the geometry of curved spaces developed in the 1800s for purely internal mathematical reasons turning out to be exactly what a theory of gravity needed; a number system invented to make polynomial equations solvable becoming unavoidable in quantum mechanics; symmetry math cooked up to study algebraic equations becoming the organizing language of particle physics. any account treating math as just a tool we consciously build to fit data looks dead here unless it has a clever escape hatch -- spell out what the escape hatch would have to look like
- it has to distinguish kinds of effectiveness: math as mere notation, math as compression of results we already had, math as an engine that predicts things nobody went looking for (the particle that shows up in the equation before it shows up in the lab). an explanation that covers the first two and quietly lets you think it covered the third is cheating
- it has to explain the failures too, or at least not predict success everywhere. plenty of sciences are swimming in data and short on crisp mathematizable laws. if your view is that the universe just is mathematical at bottom, you owe a story about why whole fields didn't get the memo
- it can't just relocate the mystery. "mathematicians' sense of beauty is attuned to the deep structure of reality" is not an explanation, it's the mystery wearing a fake mustache -- unless you can say what did the attuning
- it should have something to say about why math hangs together internally -- concepts built for one corner of the subject keep paying off in totally unrelated corners -- since half my group thinks that's the same mystery as the physics one and half thinks it's a different one entirely

for each requirement: say what failing it does to a theory, and whether that's a genuine knock-out or one camp's hobbyhorse dressed up as neutral ground. then: (1) which single requirement eliminates the most live candidates, (2) is any subset of the list jointly unsatisfiable -- my suspicion is you can't have both "naturalistically kosher, no spooky pre-established harmony between human math and nature" and "explains the pure-math-first cases" without falling back on the selection-effect move, and that move has its own problems, but tell me if i'm wrong, and (3) which requirements a nominalist will just flatly deny describe real phenomena in need of explanation rather than real desiderata.

short scorecard at the end against 3-4 named families of views is fine, but keep it to a paragraph each -- the criteria are the deliverable, not the scoring.
