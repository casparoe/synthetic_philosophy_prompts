A few months ago I gave a short talk on Maxwell's demon at an informal ideas-and-science discussion circle I help run, and the evening ended in a standoff between our two most regular attendees that has now dragged on for three meetings. One of them, an engineer by training, holds the view I had taken to be settled: that the demon is defeated by the physics of information -- in the tradition running from Szilard's one-molecule engine through Landauer and Bennett -- because the demon must record its measurement somewhere, and erasing that record dissipates at least kT ln 2 of heat per bit, which is exactly enough to save the second law. The other member has been reading the critical literature (he keeps mentioning John Norton) and insists this whole resolution is built on sand: that Landauer's principle has never been properly proven from statistical mechanics, that every step of a measurement-and-erasure cycle can in principle be done with arbitrarily little dissipation, and that invoking the principle to rescue the second law is circular, since the principle itself is usually justified by appeal to the second law. I have some sympathy with both, which is why they have now jointly appointed me to draft something.

What I would like from you is the document they should be able to sign together -- an adversarial-collaboration joint statement, written as if drafted by the two of them in good faith rather than a verdict declaring one the winner. Please give me, first, a numbered list of claims both sides can genuinely accept: what the demon thought experiment is actually asking, what both grant about the impossibility of a second-kind perpetual motion machine, what both accept about the demon being a physical system with finite memory rather than an abstract oracle, and whatever both would concede about the kT ln 2 figure itself. Second, a table of the precise residual disagreements, each formulated so that the side holding it would endorse the wording -- my suspicion is that one real fault line is whether erasure is thermodynamically irreversible in principle or merely in practice, and another is whether the Bennett-style analysis quietly assumes what it is meant to prove. Third, for each disagreement, identify the crux: the sub-question such that settling it would force at least one of them to move, and any concrete case, model calculation, or thought experiment both would agree bears on that crux, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Finally, if you conclude that some portion of their quarrel is verbal -- for instance, a slide between "dissipation is required by the laws of physics" and "dissipation is required by any physically realistic implementation" -- please flag exactly which portion and explain why.

I should add that neither of them is a working physicist, and I am not academically trained at all, so the document needs to be precise without presupposing fluency in statistical mechanics jargon; where a technical term is unavoidable, a one-line gloss would be appreciated. I would also like, at the end, a short note addressed to me on what each of them is most likely to feel is misrepresented in the draft, so I can defend it when we meet next.
