I'm teaching a little seminar on time in literature and philosophy, and next week we're pairing the opening of Book XI of Augustine's Confessions with Ted Chiang's "Story of Your Life" (the story Arrival was based on; most of them saw the movie). Right at the start of Book XI, in the old Pusey translation, Augustine asks God:

"O Lord, since eternity is Yours, are You ignorant of the things which I say unto You? Or see Thou at the time that which comes to pass in time? Why, therefore, do I place before You so many relations of things? Not surely that You might know them through me, but that I may awaken my own love and that of my readers towards You."

So Augustine's whole setup is: God doesn't learn anything by watching events unfold; he doesn't see things "at the time" they happen, the way we do. Later in the book Augustine develops the idea that for us the past and future only exist as a kind of present -- memory of what's gone, attention to what's now, expectation of what's coming -- while God alone has the whole span at once. And notice the point of the passage above: prayer and confession still make sense for creatures like us precisely because we're stuck inside time and don't know what's comming.

Here's my question for the class. Louise, learning the heptapod language, ends up with something like the view Augustine reserves for God: she experiences her whole life, including her daughter's death, all at once, and has the child anyway. Does the story vindicate Augustine's division of labor -- God sees time whole, humans live it moment by moment -- or does it complicate it? Specifically: Augustine thinks the only reason confession, hope, and prayer are coherent activities is that the future is genuinely hidden from us. Does Chiang agree that a human who took up the God's-eye view would stop being the kind of creature who could choose, hope, or grieve -- or is Louise shown doing exactly those things from that view? And be honest about where the story resists the reading: Augustine would presumably say God's seeing everything doesn't cause anything, so is Louise's knowledge of the future actually different in kind from foreknowledge, or is the story quietly sliding between knowing the future and the future being fixed? Which scenes or moments would you point the class to, and is there anything in Augustine's picture that the story gets more right than the philosophy seminar version of him does?
