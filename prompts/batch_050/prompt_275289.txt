I teach a second-year philosophy of mind module, and this term's seminar on irrationality ended in an unresolved argument about Alfred Mele's deflationary account of self-deception -- roughly, that self-deception requires no intention to deceive oneself and no simultaneous contradictory beliefs, just a motivationally biased treatment of evidence that lands one in a false belief one would not otherwise have formed -- versus older intentionalist pictures in the spirit of Davidson, on which genuine self-deception involves a part of the mind intentionally bringing it about that one believes what one somehow knows not to be true. My students kept generating examples that either side could absorb without strain, and nobody could build a case that actually forced the theories apart. So here is what I want you to do for next term's exam paper: construct a single realistic scenario, no longer than one paragraph, drawn from ordinary professional or domestic life (no hypnosis, no amnesia, no split-brain patients, no stipulating what anyone "deep down" believes), in which Mele-style deflationism is obliged to classify the subject as self-deceived but an intentionalist is obliged to deny it -- where the intentionalist's denial must follow from the structure of their theory rather than from mere squeamishness about the word.

After the scenario, do the verification work. State the subject's belief, the motivation in play, and the pattern of evidence-handling. Then give each theory's verdict in turn and derive it from the theory's commitments -- do not simply assert that the case fits. Identify the single feature of the scenario that makes the two theories diverge, and say what happens if that feature is removed: does the case collapse back into something the intentionalist can count as self-deception, or into ordinary wishful thinking that even the deflationist will not count? Then take a third pass and construct, in a couple of sentences, a minimal variant of your own case that even the deflationist must refuse to classify as self-deception, and explain where the boundary falls -- this is the part my students found hardest, because it turns out to be easy to describe motivated reasoning and much harder to say why it merits the deception label at all.

Please write in British English. I would also like you to be explicit about your own epistemic position on the case: say which of the two theories you think your scenario in fact favours, if either, identify the point in your construction where the argument is most likely to go wrong -- for instance, where you may have smuggled in an intention under the guise of a "motivation", or vice versa -- and state what consideration or counterexample would lead you to revise your verdict.
