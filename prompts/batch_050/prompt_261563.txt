I've been a LW/OB reader for years and spend a fair amount of time thinking about what kinds of arguments can actually produce justified certainty, and I keep circling back to computer-assisted proofs as a stress test for my views. I'm drafting a position statement for an epistemology discussion group and, before I present it, I'd like an audit of whether my commitments actually hang together. Here they are. First, I hold that what distinguishes mathematical knowledge from empirical knowledge is that a proof is, at least in principle, surveyable: an individual mathematician can work through the argument and see why the conclusion follows, and the social institution of mathematics exists to distribute and check that surveyability. Second, I hold that the four-colour theorem is simply proven -- it would be unreasonable, at this point, to withhold assent from it. Third, I hold that when a program produces output I cannot check by hand, my grounds for trusting that output are inductive and empirical: the reliability of the hardware, the care of the programmers, agreement across independent reimplementations and reruns. Fourth, I hold that Hales's proof of the Kepler conjecture, especially after the Flyspeck project completed its formal verification, settled the conjecture as thoroughly as any theorem in the literature. Fifth, I hold that the distinction I drew in my first commitment is doing real work -- it marks a genuine epistemic kind, not just a sociological difference in how two communities happen to behave.

I've stared at these for a while and can't locate a contradiction, but I have the nagging sense that I've smuggled in two different notions of "checking" somewhere and am equivocating between them. What I'd like from you: go through the commitments pairwise and identify which are in genuine tension and which only appear to be. Rank any real tensions by severity, and for each one state precisely what would have to give -- not just "reject commitment two," but what that rejection actually costs me, including whether the cost falls on my philosophy of mathematics or merely on my verdicts about particular theorems. I'm also suspicious that my second and fourth commitments, which I currently treat as the same kind of case (a computer was used, we trust the result), might differ in a way that matters for my first commitment, and I'd like that possibility examined rather than assumed.

If the set turns out to be consistent as stated, say so and show the reconciliation explicitly -- I don't want a clean bill of health that papers over a real problem for the sake of a tidy answer. But if there is a genuine inconsistency, please end by telling me which single revision would restore consistency at the lowest total cost to the rest of my view, and flag any commitments I hold that would become unmotivated -- rather than false, just unanchored -- after that revision.
