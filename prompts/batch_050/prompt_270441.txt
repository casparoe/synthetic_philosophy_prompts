For my school's history-and-ethics club I have to write a short position statement on how we should judge historical figures, and before I submit it I want to know whether my views actually cohere. Here is what I currently hold:

(1) The "man of his time" defence fails for Jefferson: abolitionist arguments were available and made by his contemporaries, so he cannot plead ignorance of the moral case against owning slaves.

(2) Even so, we should not hold historical figures to precisely our own moral standards -- moral progress is real, and part of judging fairly is taking account of what it was epistemically reasonable for someone to see at the time.

(3) Churchill deserves to keep his public honours (statues, buildings, his place in the national story) despite the Bengal famine and his racial views, because his overall legacy is admirable.

(4) Our own descendants will be right to judge us harshly for things we treat as normal -- factory farming, for instance -- because the arguments against these practices are already available to us now.

My worry is that (1) and (3) may be doing different things with the same test, and that (1) and (2) might contradict each other outright. Please audit the set rather than survey the options: tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank the real tensions by severity, and for each one commit to a definite verdict about which commitment should give way and why -- don't leave the choice to me.
