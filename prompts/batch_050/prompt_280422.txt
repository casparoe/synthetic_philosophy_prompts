Two friends of mine argued for most of an evening about Montaigne's essay Of Cannibals, and I would like a verdict I cannot reach myself. They agree on the essay's contents: it describes the Tupinambá of Brazil — their valor in war, their practice of killing and ritually eating captured enemies as an act of supreme revenge rather than nourishment — and it repeatedly turns the mirror back on sixteenth-century France, whose inhabitants recoil at cannibalism while practicing judicial torture and religious massacre.

Clara's position is that Montaigne is a genuine cultural relativist. On her reading, the essay's core claim is that every people calls barbarous whatever falls outside its own habits, that judgments of custom have no culture-independent ground, and that Montaigne accordingly refuses to condemn the Tupinambá's practices. His praise of their courage and candor, and his insistence that the label of barbarity reflects unfamiliarity rather than any defect in the people so described, show a thinker extending outward the same interpretive charity he demands of his readers.

Daniel reads the essay as a rhetorical maneuver rather than a relativist manifesto. On his view, Montaigne never actually suspends moral judgment: he plainly condemns European cruelty — tormenting people to death, burning them over doctrinal disputes — and he praises the cannibals by standards such as valor, honesty, and constancy that he assumes his French readers share. If all judgment were custom-bound, Daniel argues, Montaigne's own condemnations would be as groundless as the horror of cannibalism he mocks, so the essay must presuppose a universal standard — roughly, that cruelty is worse than unfamiliarity — which makes Montaigne a moral universalist in relativist dress.

Clara answered that the comparison with European cruelty is not a condemnation but a further demonstration that horror tracks custom: we are more disturbed by eating the dead than by tormenting the living only because the latter is familiar, and the essay's aim throughout is to destabilize confidence in our judgments rather than to assert any universal truth. Even the closing dialogue, in which the Tupinambá visitors to France marvel that well-fed men tolerate beggars at their doors, can be read as one custom-bound people judging another by its own lights — which is exactly her point.

Daniel replied that this renders the essay self-undermining: if Montaigne's judgments are mere expressions of French custom, his readers have no reason to be moved by them, yet the essay plainly intends to shame those readers into seeing themselves differently. He added that the essay's famous closing quip — to the effect that, all this notwithstanding, the cannibals still wear no breeches — shows Montaigne wryly signaling the limits of his own exercise, which sits oddly with serious relativism.

Each thinks the other has missed the essay's point entirely, and I cannot settle it. Please adjudicate the dispute move by move: say what each side gets right and wrong at each step, which reading of the essay is better supported overall, and what, if anything, both are missing — including whether "relativist" versus "universalist" is even the right axis on which to locate Montaigne, given that a skeptic who suspends all judgment can look like a relativist without being one. Open with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the dispute. Please also indicate how confident you are in your adjudication, and single out the one point on which you are least sure.
