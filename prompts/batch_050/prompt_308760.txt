I am a retired experimental physicist who, three years into retirement, joined a philosophy reading circle at our public library, and it has fallen to me to lead next month's session. The circle — eight of us, mostly former engineers and schoolteachers plus one retired GP — has been working through classic texts on probability and judgment. This month's reading was the chapter on moral hope in Laplace's A Philosophical Essay on Probabilities, in the old Truscott and Emory translation. In it Laplace describes the familiar game in which a coin is tossed until it first lands heads, the player receiving two francs if this happens on the first toss, four if on the second, eight if on the third, and so on; he observes that the mathematical expectation is infinite even though no sensible person would pay more than a trifle for a ticket, and he offers two ways out: that any real adversary's fortune is finite, which truncates the game, and that the worth of a sum of money depends on what its recipient already has, so one should compute the moral rather than the mathematical expectation, which comes out finite.

My intention is to run the session as a single arc, beginning with Laplace's game and ending with a variant I have prepared that appears to defeat both of his remedies at once. The variant keeps the same coin and the same stopping rule but alternates the sign of the payout: the player receives 2 francs if the first heads falls on toss one, pays 2 if on toss two, receives 8/3 if on toss three, pays 4 if on toss four, receives 32/5 if on toss five, and in general receives (−1)^(k−1)·2^k/k francs when the first heads falls on toss k. Each term of the expectation is then (−1)^(k−1)/k, and the series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + … converges only conditionally: in the natural order it sums to ln 2, but its terms can be reordered to yield any preassigned value, or none. The fair price of this game therefore seems not infinite, nor even counterintuitive, but simply undefined. Moreover the payouts can be stipulated in units of the player's utility rather than in money, so Laplace's logarithmic remedy does not touch it.

What I want from you is not the explanation itself but a plan for the ninety minutes. Specifically: the order in which the pieces must be introduced and what depends on what (I assume I must re-establish expected value as a fair price before any game will surprise anyone, but you may persuade me otherwise); the carrying example — I should prefer to keep Laplace's coin on the table throughout and change only the payout schedule rather than introduce a second game — and what that example must have and must lack if it is to bear the whole weight of the evening; the misconceptions I should expect, which I take to include the convictions that the infinite expectation must be an arithmetic slip since probabilities sum to one, that truncating at the bank's fortune dissolves the paradox rather than substituting a different, finite game, that a convergent series has one sum however its terms are added, and that an undefined price simply means a worthless game; the exact point in the sequence at which each of these should be headed off rather than merely answered when raised; one closing task that would show the idea has landed, something the members must produce rather than assent to, such as devising a payout schedule of their own whose expectation is undefined, or stating why the chronological order of the tosses has no special claim when the outcomes are summed; and finally what I should openly declare out of bounds for this group — the measure-theoretic machinery, certainly, but I want your judgment on what else to fence off and how to say so without sounding evasive.

About 700 words, please, and no reading lists: the circle has Laplace's chapter and my payout schedule, and that is all they will ever read.
