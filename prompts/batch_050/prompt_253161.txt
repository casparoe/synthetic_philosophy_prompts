I am a PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on the grounds of promissory obligation, and I am trying to make sure my taxonomy of existing accounts is complete before I defend my own view. Here are the accounts I have catalogued so far:

(1) The expectation-based account: a promise binds because it deliberately induces the promisee to expect performance, and disappointing an expectation one has intentionally created is a harm; the obligation is thus a special case of a general duty not to harm.

(2) The conventionalist or practice-based account: promising is a rule-governed social practice, and to make a promise is to invoke that practice's rules, thereby acquiring whatever obligations the rules assign; the obligation binds the way the rules of a game bind a player who voluntarily plays.

(3) The assurance or trust account: a promise is an invitation to rely on the promisor's assurance, given in full knowledge that the promisee needs reason to trust; reneging is wrong because it abuses a trust one has actively solicited, and this wrongs the promisee even where no material harm results.

(4) The autonomy or will-based account: in promising, the promisor deliberately takes on a new obligation by an exercise of normative power, and the obligation binds because rational agents have the standing capacity to bind themselves, much as they can consent or transfer rights; the duty is grounded in respecting the promisor as the author of her own obligations.

(5) The Kantian account: breaking a promise involves a maxim that cannot be universalized without undermining the very institution of promising that the lie presupposes, so promise-breaking is ruled out as a contradiction in conception.

My supervisor insists there must be at least one further account that is genuinely distinct from these five, and I am stuck. Please propose one. To count as distinct, it must not be a restatement, special case, or hybrid of the above: it should identify a different ground for the obligation, not merely a different description of when promises happen to be wrong to break. In particular, it should ground the obligation in something other than disappointed expectations, practice rules, abused trust, self-legislation, or universalizability.

Once you have stated the account, please do two further things. First, test it against three problem cases: a promise made to someone who is now dead, a promise to do something mildly immoral, and a promise extracted under duress -- a good account should deliver plausible verdicts on all three, or explain honestly why its counterintuitive verdict is a price worth paying. Second, explain which of my five listed accounts yours is closest to, and where exactly the two come apart.
