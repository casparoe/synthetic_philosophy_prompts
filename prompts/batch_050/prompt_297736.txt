I teach the early modern survey at a small liberal arts college, and this semester I made the mistake of letting my seminar students push me on Kant's lying promise discussion in the Groundwork (around 4:402). Since I'm working from memory here, I'll paraphrase rather than quote: Kant imagines someone in financial trouble who needs a loan he knows he can't repay, and asks whether he may promise repayment anyway. Kant's answer is that the man can will the lie, but cannot will that everyone act on that maxim, because universal false promising would make promising itself impossible — nobody would believe promises anymore, so the maxim, as a universal law, destroys the very practice it exploits. Fine so far. But one of my students built the following argument out of that passage, and it seems to prove way too much:

(1) An action is wrong if its maxim, willed as a universal law, undermines the practice that the action itself depends on (that's the promising test above).
(2) Lying to the murderer at the door depends on his trusting your assertion; universal lying would destroy that trust and the practice of assertion; so by (1), lying to him is wrong.
(3) Pulling your savings out of a bank you suspect is insolvent depends on most other depositors not doing the same; universal withdraw-on-suspicion would make ordinary banking impossible; so by (1), moving your own money is wrong.
(4) Taking the empty back road to beat trafic depends on its staying empty; if everyone took it whenever the highway slowed, it would jam too and your plan self-defeats; so by (1), the shortcut is wrong.
(5) But (3) and (4) are absurd — it's my money, and it's a public road.
(6) So either (1) isn't really Kant's test, or something in how (3) and (4) apply it is broken, and whatever fixes them had better not also save (2), unless I want to swallow Kant's 1797 murderer-at-the-door conclusion wholesale.

My problem: I want to keep (1), because it's the only version of the promising argument I find remotely persuasive, and I half-suspect the rot is in the slide from "the practice would collapse" to "my advantage would disappear" — but I can't state that difference in a way that doesn't also rehabilitate the lie to the murderer. Answer as someone who actually works on Kant's ethics, and think carefully rather than handing me the textbook paragraph: brainstorm the candidate faults if you like, but land on one — which step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, tell me whether the fix forces me into the view that maxims can be redescribed so specifically that anything universalizes. Hard limit: 350 words. The Kant paraphrase and the numbered steps don't count toward it.
