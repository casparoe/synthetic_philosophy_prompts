I'm a high school junior, and something came up across my classes this year that I can't stop thinking about. In biology, we learned about periodical cicadas that emerge every 13 or 17 years, and our teacher presented it as a genuinely good explanation of those life cycles that 13 and 17 are prime numbers, because primes minimize how often the cicadas' emergence can synchronize with the cycles of predators. Similarly, we learned that honeybees build hexagonal cells, and the explanation given was that hexagons partition a surface into equal cells with the least wax -- a fact about geometry, not about bee physiology or evolution in the usual sense. Meanwhile, in physics, our teacher casually said things like "the law of conservation of energy explains why the roller coaster can't reach a higher point than it started," where the explanation seems to be almost entirely a piece of math.

Here is what puzzles me. If I ask why the water boiled, the answer cites molecules and heat -- actual stuff in the world doing things. But when I ask why the cicadas' cycle is 13 years rather than 12, the answer seems to cite a property of the number 13. My biology teacher says this is a real explanation, as good as any other in the course. My math teacher, when I asked, shrugged and said numbers are just tools we invented for counting and calculating. But those two attitudes seem to be in tension: if numbers are merely tools in our heads, how can a fact about one of them explain the life cycle of an insect that existed long before anyone counted anything?

So my question is: what does the practice of science, as I see it in these examples, actually presuppose about mathematics? When scientists accept "13 is prime" as the explanation of a biological fact, are they committed to numbers being real things that exist independently of us and somehow constrain the physical world? Or can you fully accept the cicada and honeycomb explanations while insisting math is just a human-made language, with the real explanation being something else entirely? If the second, I want to know what the something else is supposed to be, stated concretely for the cicada case, not just in general terms. If the first, I want to know how strange a commitment that really is -- does it commit scientists to believing in an invisible realm of mathematical objects, or is there a milder reading?
