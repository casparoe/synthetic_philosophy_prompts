You are a political philosopher with expertise in legitimacy theory and comparative political institutions. Think carefully and work through the problem step by step before settling on a verdict.

In debates about whether prosperity and order can justify authoritarian rule, defenders of systems like Singapore's or China's frequently reach for a corporate analogy: a state, they say, is like a large company. The government is the management team, appointed for competence rather than popularity; citizens are like shareholders or employees, who care chiefly about results -- rising living standards, safety, efficient services -- rather than about voting on every decision; and just as nobody demands that a successful CEO face competitive elections, nobody should demand that a successful government do so. Critics of this analogy sometimes reply with a different one: a state is not a company but more like a family or a marriage, a relationship you cannot simply quit, in which voice and consent matter precisely because exit is not a real option.

Audit both analogies as rigorously as you can. Specifically:

1. Lay out the correspondence tables explicitly: in the corporate analogy, what plays the role of the shareholders, the board, the stock price, the option to sell your shares, a hostile takeover, and a firm's competitors? In the family analogy, what plays the role of divorce, of the children (who never consented to the arrangement at all), and of household decisions made by one spouse alone?

2. Identify where each mapping breaks down, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended argument from the ones that are merely superficial. In particular: shareholders who dislike management can sell their shares without leaving their home, language, and community behind -- can citizens do anything comparable? A firm's shareholders hold a fungible financial claim, while a citizen's stake in a state involves identity, rights, and coercive subjection -- does this difference undercut the corporate analogy, or only complicate it? Conversely, does the family analogy smuggle in an assumption that political relationships must be permanent, and is that assumption any better supported than the corporate one?

3. Ask what each analogy quietly imports. Does the corporate analogy presuppose that the goals of a state are as given and measurable as profit, thereby begging the question against the idea that contestation is needed to define collective goals in the first place? Does the family analogy presuppose an intimacy and small scale that a state of millions cannot have?

4. Render a verdict: can either analogy actually carry the weight placed on it -- namely, justifying or condemning rule that delivers prosperity without electoral accountability -- or does each merely restate the conclusion it was meant to argue for? If both fail, is there a better analogy (or a better non-analogical framework) for thinking about what performance can and cannot legitimate?
