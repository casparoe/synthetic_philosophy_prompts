I chair the appointments committee for a mid-sized academic department. We vote on candidates sequentially -- each member states their assessment aloud in turn before we deliberate -- and I've become convinced this procedure manufactures false consensus: once two or three senior members have spoken positively, everyone after them seems to agree, including people who privately tell me afterward that they had doubts. My understanding is that the literature on information cascades and rational herding explains exactly this: later members rationally discount their own private judgment in favor of the signal carried by earlier votes, so the group's final view can reflect the order of speaking rather than the information in the room.

Write a procedure I can actually implement for structuring our committee's assessment of candidates -- the rules for eliciting and aggregating judgments, in what order, and at what points -- that preserves the genuine informational value of hearing each other's reasoning while blocking the cascade dynamics. Specify: what to collect privately and in writing before any discussion, how and when to reveal it, what the chair should and should not do with their own opinion, and what to do when a junior member's private judgment conflicts with the apparent consensus. Be explicit about which features of the procedure are grounded in the logic of rational herding and which are institutional judgment calls on my part that the theory doesn't dictate. Then apply the procedure to two scenarios: one where a candidate looks strong on paper and the two most senior members are enthusiastic, and one where the committee's written pre-scores, once revealed, turn out to be bimodal. At least one application should produce an outcome I'd find uncomfortable. Finally, say how confident you are that the procedure would actually change outcomes rather than just relabel our existing dynamics, and identify the single design element you're least sure about.
