I chair the Commemoration and Naming Review Committee at a public university, and next month we must decide the fate of Vale Hall and the bronze statue of Josiah Vale that stands in front of it. The historical facts are not contested. A commissioned report, accepted by all parties, establishes the following. Vale, an alumnus and the university's largest early benefactor, made his fortune in mining and served from 1921 to 1933 on his state's Board of Social Hygiene, where he voted consistently for the compulsory sterilization of persons the statutes of that era classified as feeble-minded, and defended the program in published essays. The same record shows that his gifts to the university endowed its first scholarships open to Black students and to women, which he insisted on over the objections of several trustees. One further fact matters: neither the statue nor the building name dates from Vale's lifetime. Both were created in 1961, at the urging of a retiring university president who admired him, and student newspapers from that spring show the dedication ceremony was boycotted by the campus chapter of the NAACP.

The committee has narrowed its options to three: (1) retain the name and statue unchanged; (2) remove both and rename the hall; or (3) what our counsel calls decommemoration — the university formally revokes the honor, renames the hall, and relocates the statue to the campus museum alongside the report's findings, so the object is preserved as evidence rather than displayed as tribute. Descendants of the state's sterilization victims, whom we consulted, are themselves divided: some want the statue gone from public view, others want it retained precisely so the history cannot be tidied away. Vale's descendants point to a 1958 gift agreement in which the university promised to maintain the name in perpetuity; our general counsel considers the clause probably unenforceable and in any event morally irrelevant, and I am inclined to agree, but the memo should address it.

Draft the committee's decision memo. State the recommendation in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; do not restate the record, which the committee has read. The memo should take a position on the questions the decision actually turns on: whether a building name and statue are a standing act of honor the institution performs continuously (and so answerable to present judgment) or a completed historical fact it merely inherits; what follows from the 1961 dating, given that the common defense that such monuments reflect their own era here cuts in an unusual direction; whether Vale's mixed record changes the verdict or only its weight; and whether the middle option dissolves the dispute or merely relocates it, since a museum label is itself a form of commemoration. Give the strongest argument for the option you reject, and close by stating what single further fact, if the report had found it, would have changed the recommendation.
