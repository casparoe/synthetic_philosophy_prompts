A colleague in my metaphysics reading group has been pressing the following argument, and although every step looks defensible I cannot accept the conclusion, so something must give. (1) It is essential to any biological organism what it developed from: no human being could have originated from wholly different material, say from a union between two strangers on another continent. (2) A human organism develops from exactly one zygote, formed by the fusion of one sperm and one egg. (3) A zygote is individuated by the gametes that fused to form it: had a different sperm fertilized that egg, the result would have been a numerically different zygote, not this very zygote with a different genetic endowment. (4) A numerically different zygote would have developed into a numerically different organism. (5) Therefore you could not have come from a different sperm and egg. (6) But the individuation principle in (3) cannot be restricted to whole gametes: a zygote is what it is in virtue of the matter constituting it, so had even one molecule of that sperm been different, the resulting zygote would likewise have been numerically different. (7) Hence had anything differed at your conception -- one molecule rearranged, the sperm marginally altered by an hour's delay -- you would never have existed; someone else would have. (8) That is absurd: there are nearby worlds differing from actuality only trivially at your conception in which you plainly exist. (9) So (1) is false, and origin essentialism collapses. He takes this to show that necessity-of-origins intuitions rest on an untenable hyper-essentialism. I suspect the damage is localized somewhere between (3) and (7), since I would much rather surrender (6) than (1) -- but I cannot explain why (3) may be held at the level of whole gametes while (6) fails at the molecular level without it looking like an ad hoc cutoff, nor am I sure (3) itself is licensed by anything in (1) and (2). Which step should I reject, why does it look compelling, and what does rejecting it cost?
