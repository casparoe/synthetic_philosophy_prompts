i've been reading LW/SSC-adjacent stuff for years, and Moloch has slowly turned into a universal explanation: bad schooling, bad media, AI races, workism, housing, whatever -- just say coordintion failure and gesture at perverse incentives. That can't be right. Sometimes the problem really is distributed selection pressure, sometimes it's one greedy actor, sometimes people simply disagree about what counts as good, and sometimes the alleged trap is actually the least-bad equilibrium available.

Write the desiderata for an adequate theory of Molochian lock-in. By that I mean a theory that explains when individually defensible moves produce a stable, collectively worse outcome because unilateral restraint gets punished -- and, just as importantly, when that is not what's happening. The point of the theory is practical: given a proposed intervention, it should help identify which variable the intervention changes, whether the equilibrium can actually move, and how the system might adapt around the intervention.

Don't write a general essay on Moloch. Give me the spec. I'd like roughly 10-14 requirements, each with:

- the requirement itself
- a toy case showing why it matters
- what goes wrong if the theory lacks it
- whether you consider it non-negotiable or negotiable

The list should at least force distinctions between:

- a bad Nash equilibrium, a selection process, path dependence, and an ordinary collective-action problem
- externalities, positional competition, information problems, enforcement problems, and genuine value conflict
- a race to the bottom and a race to some local optimum that outsiders dislike
- agents choosing badly and agents being replaced by less restrained competitors
- instability, metastability, and an actual ratchet
- Moloch-as-metaphor and a mechanism specific enough to be wrong

It should also say what a theory must do with adaptation and Goodharting: e.g. if you tax the visible margin of competition, agents may shift to a less visible margin; if you mandate safety, firms may redefine safety; if you make restraint cheap for one group, another group may enter and take its market share. I want criteria for when an intervention reduces total pressure versus merely rerouting it.

Use these test cases somewhere in the requirements, and add one or two of your own if useful:

1. Several AI labs would all prefer a slower development regime, but each expects restraint to lose talent, funding, and strategic position unless the others also slow down.
2. Creators adopt increasingly provocative formats because attention adapts; last year's outrageous is this year's baseline, while creators who refuse lose distribution.
3. Employers demand ever more credentials mostly to differentiate applicants. Most workers and employers would prefer a cheaper sorting system, but no one can safely stop using the existing signals.
4. A monopoly extracts wealth from customers. Bad outcome, but is this Moloch at all, or does calling it that erase the relevant agency?
5. A competitive process produces something locally ugly but globally useful -- say punishing standards that destroy weak projects quickly. A decent theory should be able to call this beneficial selection rather than automatically treating every relentless pressure as demonic.

Flag any desiderata that seem jointly unsatisfiable -- for example, maybe a theory cannot simultaneously be mechanism-specific, domain-general, morally neutral, and intervention-ready without giving something up. If there is such a tension, say which horn you would choose. Also identify the single requirement that eliminates the most lazy Moloch explanations.

After the requirements, briefly score four broad approaches against them:

- game theory / mechanism design
- evolutionary game theory and organizational selection
- public choice / institutional economics
- cybernetic or complex-systems feedback models

Keep the scoring subordinate to the desiderata; don't turn it into a history-of-economic-thought exercise or a bibliography. Toy payoffs are welcome where they clarify a distinction. End by saying which approach is the best skeleton for a general theory, which missing component it most needs to borrow from the others, and what observation would convince you that a supposed Moloch trap had been misdiagnosed.
