Since I retired from my job as a fisheries biologist I've been spending too much time in a philosophy reading group at the library, and we're currently on personal identity and essentialism, which I initially thought would be navel-gazing but has properly got its hooks in me. One of the group floated the idea that where you come from biologically -- the particular sperm and egg -- is essential to you being you, and I find I have strong gut reactions about cases but they don't obviously hang together. Let me lay out my verdicts and you tell me what principle (if any) I'm actually committed to.

Case one: my parents have a child two years before I was actually conceived, from entirely different sperm and egg, and by sheer coincidence the kid grows up to be temperamentally and physically a dead ringer for me. Verdict: that's not me, that's just my brother. Case two: same sperm, same egg, but my mother emigrates while pregnant and I'm raised abroad under a different name in a different language. Verdict: still me, obviously. Case three, and this one matters because I actually have an identical twin: whatever combination of sperm and egg produced us produced him too, and he is definitely not me. Case four: suppose a stray cosmic ray had knocked out one base pair in the zygote that became me. Verdict: still me, that kind of thing presumably happens all the time. Case five: suppose my parents had used a sperm donor, and the donor happened to be my father's identical twin brother, so the resulting child would have been a near genetic match to me. Verdict: my gut says no, not me, though I admit I squirm a bit here. Case six: the embryo I came from gets frozen for twenty years and I'm born in the 80s instead of the 60s. Verdict: still me, just born late.

So here's my tentative principle, which probably needs work: you're essentially tied to the particular sperm-and-egg pair you came from, plus unbroken continuity of the organism from there. But case three seems to break that immediately -- my twin satisfies both clauses and isn't me. Maybe the fix is to tie identity to the zygote rather than the gametes, but then I don't know what to say when a zygote splits: was the zygote never identical to either of us? And cases one and four pull in opposite directions in a way I can't articulate -- a whole different sperm is disqualifying but a one-base change isn't, and I'd like to know whether there's a non-arbitrary line there or whether my verdicts are just tracking quantity of genetic change, which seems philosophically embarassing. Please give me a principle (or a couple of candidates) stated precisely, flag which of my six verdicts it can't save, and tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: mitochondrial replacement IVF, where the nucleus comes from the same sperm and egg as mine but the egg's cytoplasm and mitochondria come from a donor. Would that still be me?
