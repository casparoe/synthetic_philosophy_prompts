You are a philosopher of biology who specializes in the ontology of organisms, serving as an outside adviser to a state biodiversity review council. Before drafting, think through the case carefully: consider several plausible framings of what is at stake, and only then settle on a recommendation.

I chair the council. Under our statute, certain development permits require mitigation scaled to the number of "individual organisms" of a protected species that a project would destroy. The statute nowhere defines "individual."

Here is the pending case. A developer wants to build on a slope occupied by a large clonal aspen stand. Genetic sampling indicates the entire stand — roughly 4,000 stems spread over about 40 hectares — is a single genet: one genotype, propagated vegetatively from a common root system, plausibly thousands of years old. The root network is partly discontinuous, and arborists testify that severed sections could persist on their own. The project would remove about 2,600 stems; the remaining stand would survive, and the genet as a whole would likely resprout and continue.

The developer's consulting biologist argues that the stand is one individual organism, so the project kills zero individuals — at most it "trims" one, no different in principle from pruning a tree or mowing a clonal lawn — and therefore triggers no per-individual mitigation at all. Our staff ecologist argues that counting by genets is biologically perverse in this context: the stems are physiologically semi-autonomous, respond to local conditions, die one at a time, and function as the ecologically relevant units, so on her view the project destroys thousands of organisms; that they share a genotype is, she says, no more relevant than the fact that identical twins do.

Both sides can appeal to real biology. Botanists do standardly distinguish genets from ramets; there is genuine dispute about whether colonies of clonally produced units are themselves Darwinian individuals; and "organism" has been variously defined by physiological integration, by immune or germ-soma boundaries, by being a unit of selection, and by other criteria. But the council votes next month, and whatever line we take will become precedent for a queue of similar cases: coral fragments, fungal mats, meadow-forming seagrasses, and a chimeric sponge colony awaiting review.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should:

- Recommend a concrete counting rule for permit purposes and state what it implies here — full mitigation, partial mitigation, none, or some hybrid such as mitigation for lost ecological function rather than lost individuals.
- Identify which facts about the stand actually carry the weight in your analysis (shared genotype, physical connection, capacity to survive severance, reproductive autonomy of the stems), so that the opinion generalizes beyond this case.
- Take the losing side's strongest argument seriously and answer it, rather than caricaturing it.
- Address the precedent problem in both directions: how does your rule treat cases where the physiological and evolutionary notions of individuality diverge the other way, e.g., a physically continuous but genetically chimeric colony?

One request about calibration: please be explicit about what is genuinely settled among philosophers of biology and working biologists (for instance, that the genet/ramet distinction is standard conceptual equipment), what is genuinely contested (for instance, whether there is a single correct answer to the question of what counts as one organism, or whether the concept must be relativized to explanatory purpose), and where your recommendation ultimately rests on a normative judgment about what the statute is for rather than on any biological fact. Do not present a contested position on biological individuality as though it were the consensus.
