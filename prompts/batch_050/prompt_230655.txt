I'm a software engineer who spends a lot of time with proof assistants and formal verification, and I recently read a popular account of the Lucas–Penrose argument that minds can't be machines. I reconstruct it like this. (1) For any consistent formal system F rich enough to do arithmetic, there is a sentence G(F) which is true but not provable in F; this is just Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, and I don't dispute it. (2) If my mind just is some machine — equivalently, if everything I can come to know by mathematical reasoning is captured by some formal system F — then anything I can genuinely know by reasoning must be provable in F. (3) But hand me any candidate F, and by understanding how G(F) is constructed I can see that G(F) is true, i.e., I can come to know something F cannot prove. (4) Therefore no formal system captures my mathematical knowledge, so my mind is not a machine. My problem is that the argument seems to prove too much. If (3) is right, it seems to follow that no formalizable practice whatsoever could exhaust mathematical knowledge — but the actual, public activity of mathematicians looks exactly like a formalizable practice, and my day job suggests you can mechanize proof-checking very far indeed. Worse, an AI could recite this very argument about itself and "conclude" it is not a machine while being one; and if I run the argument on the human mathematical community as a whole, I seem to get that mathematicians collectively know something inconsistent with their own consistency, which I don't believe. So something has slipped, but I genuinely can't locate it — is the culprit (2), (3), or the step from (1)–(3) to (4)? Please name the exact step you'd reject, explain why it looked innocent (I suspect something about what it takes to "see" G(F) as true, but I can't make that precise), and then price the main alternatives: what does it cost Lucas and Penrose to insist on the step, and what does it cost the mechanist to deny it?
