I write novels for a living, and the book I'm stuck on has forced a philosophical position on me that I didn't expect to end up holding. The setup: two men drive home from the same party, equally tired, equally one-drink-over, both glance at a phone at the same stretch of road. One hits a kid who ran out after a ball; the other doesn't, because no kid ran out. Everything about the two men is the same except the world. And yet in my draft the one who killed the child is destroyed -- by the town, by himself, by me as author -- and the other gets to forget the night ever happened. A philosopher friend told me this is basically the problem of moral luck and pointed me at the chapter in Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments where he discusses the influence of fortune on how we judge people. Paraphrasing, Smith's claim is roughly that our tendency to judge by outcomes rather than intentions is an "irregularity" of our sentiments but not a mistake -- it's useful, even quasi-providential: it makes people care about actually doing good rather than merely meaning well, it turns mere negligence into something we treat seriously, and it stops everyone from pleading good intentions. Here's the thing: I've come to accept Smith's defense. I think the outcome-sensitivity of blame is a feature, and my novel kind of depends on that.

So what I want is: what would count against this view? Not just the stock observation that it's unfair (Smith knew that), but the actual defeaters. Which intuitions about cases would a committed Smithian have to give up, and which can they absorb? Does it stand or fall with empirical claims -- e.g., if it turned out that outcome-based blame doesn't actually make people more careful, or that people can't be trained to punish negligence more than bad luck, does the defense collapse or just get dented? What about the reverse direction: if it turned out we psychologically can't help judging by outcomes, does that help Smith (nature knows best) or hurt him (no point defending a compulsion)? I'd like the potential defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with a note on which ones a real defender of Smith's position would concede as counting against them versus dismiss as missing the point. And if your honest answer is that the view has been insulated from refutation -- that any awkward result can be absorbed by the "it's useful" move -- say that, and say whether that's a vice for a view like this or just what moral psychology always looks like. Bonus: tell me what your answer implies for how I should write the second driver, the one who got lucky. Right now I'm letting him off too easy and I can feel it.
