I'm a philosophy professor (metaphysics, mostly) and through a series of bad decisions on my part I've ended up as the token academic on the advisory commitee of a consumer-product testing body — the people who decide whether a claim printed on packaging is defensible or misleading. Claims like "waterproof," "shatterproof," "non-flammable," "biodegradable." We review maybe thirty of these a month, roughly 45 minutes of discussion each, and the stakes are real: companies get fined, products get recalled, people occasionally get hurt.

The committee's working rule is basically: run the test, and if the product passes, the claim is fine. I keep making myself unpopular by pointing out that passing a test doesn't settle what a product is — a waterproof watch is still waterproof sitting in a dry drawer, and a fabric that catches fire because the lab tech preheated it strangely hasn't thereby been shown to be flammable in the relevant sense. My colleagues got sick of these objections and told me to write them a procedure instead of philosophizing at them. So here we are.

Write me a decision procedure the committee can actually run on a proposed label claim, grounded in what philosophers actually know about dispositions: the link between stimulus conditions and manifestation, why something can possess a disposition it never manifests, and especially finks and masks and the difference between them — an external arrangement that would block the manifestation (the way packing foam protects a fragile glass) is not the same as something that would strip away the disposition itself the moment the stimulus arrives, and a testing regime built on "just test it" confuses both of these with the plain absence of the disposition. The procedure has to be usable by chemists and engineers with zero philosophy background, so no jargon unless you define it on the spot.

Concretely I need:
- the questions to ask in order, and for each one, what counts as a good enough answer to move on to the next;
- how the procedure should pin down the relevant stimulus conditions, and how it should decide whether something (a coating, a case, a sacrificial component, an automatic cutoff) counts as part of the object being described or as an external interferer;
- where the procedure terminates in a verdict and where it has to hand things back to a person because the question is one of policy or values rather than fact — mark those handoff points explicitly, since our lawyers have to sign off on exactly those;
- then run it on three claims we've genuinely argued about: (1) "biodegradable" on packaging that only breaks down in industrial composting facilities at high temperatures; (2) "non-flammable" on upholstery fabric whose protective treatment has to be re-applied every couple of years; (3) "shatterproof" on a phone screen that only survives drops when it's inside the manufacturer's own case. For each, give the verdict the procedure actually produces, and tell me which of the three verdicts the committee will hate most and why I should stand by it anyway;
- finally, be honest about where this procedure will misfire — cases where the philosophy itself runs out, not just cases where the answer is unpopular.
