For a column on why legal thresholds never seem to sit where we can see them, build me a thought experiment to the following specifications. Take a predicate drawn from institutional rather than observational life — not "heap" or "red," but something like "financially dependent," "unfit to stand trial," or "too frail to live alone" — and arrange a finite, ordered series of cases that differ only in one continuously varying quantity, with everything else held fixed, such that the tolerance premise (if case n satisfies the predicate, so does case n+1) is maximally compelling at every step, while the two endpoints clearly differ in whether the predicate applies. The whole setup should fit in a paragraph, should involve a decision with real stakes for the people in the series, and must not stipulate where any boundary lies.

Then run the check. Select a sentence of the form "case k qualifies and case k+1 does not" from the middle of your series, and demonstrate that three theories of vagueness deliver three pairwise-distinct verdicts on it: the epistemicist must hold that the sentence is flatly true or flatly false — and that exactly one such sentence in the series is true, though we cannot know which; the supervaluationist must hold that no particular sentence of this form is true, even while the general claim that some step marks the cutoff remains true; the degree theorist must assign the sentence an intermediate truth value. For each verdict, show that it follows from the theory's machinery rather than being merely asserted. Close by identifying which single feature of the case does the work in keeping the three verdicts apart, stating what would happen to the disagreement if that feature were removed, and giving the best reply a supervaluationist could offer to the charge that their view absurdly lets "some step is the boundary" be true while every step is exonerated.
