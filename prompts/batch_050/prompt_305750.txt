I've been thinking about Kavka's toxin puzzle and I've landed on a pretty strong take: the puzzle proves that the rationality of intending something can genuinely come apart from the rationality of doing it. On my reading, you're rational to intend to drink the toxin (that's what wins the million) even though drinking it at 8am would be flatly irrational, and any theory of intention that can't make sense of that split is just wrong. So: what would count against my view? I want the serious defeaters, not the lazy ones. E.g. does the view collapse if you deny the case is coherent in the first place -- if no human could actually form the intention, does that undercut the lesson or reinforce it? What if it turns out there's a way to intend without expecting to act (resolutions, self-manipulation, hiring a hypnotist) -- does that dissolve the split I'm relying on, or relocate it? Also: is my conclusion hostage to something broader, like evidential vs. causal decision theory, or the claim that reasons for attitudes always reduce to reasons for what the attitude is about? Rank the threats from fatal to merely annoying, and for each one say whether someone holding my position would concede it counts against them or would just shrug it off. Also honestly assess whether my view actually risks anything, or whether I've stated it in a way that can absorb any counterexample.
