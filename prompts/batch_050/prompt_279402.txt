I've been going down a bit of a rabbit hole lately after listening to a podcast about medieval philosophers -- no background in any of this, I work in logistics, but I got hooked on this guy Abelard and his idea that sin is all in the consent, not the actual deed. From what I can gather (mostly Wikipedia and a very old book I found in a charity shop), his line in his Ethics was basically: the outward act is morally neither here nor there, what matters is what you inwardly agreed to do. He even says stuff like a woman who accidentally smothers her baby while trying to keep it warm has committed no sin because she never consented to any harm. That part I love. But I tried to build his position into a proper argument and it seems to prove something insane, and I can't find the leak. Here it is:

(1) The only thing fully up to me is what I consent to in my own mind -- my body, the circumstances, whether I succeed or get interrupted, all that's partly luck, and it would be unfair for morality to depend on luck.
(2) So my guilt or innocence is fixed entirely at the moment of consent, before anything outward happens.
(3) But I can only consent to something under the description I believe it falls under -- I can't agree to "defy God" unless I believe the thing I'm about to do defies God.
(4) A person who sincerely, whole-heartedly believes their terrible act is what God wants therefore never consents to defying God, even while doing it.
(5) So by (2) and (4), the sincere fanatic -- the inquisitor who genuinely thinks torture saves souls, say -- commits no sin at all, whereas a squeamish person who does something tiny against their own conscience does sin.
(6) But that's just wrong, surely the sincere fanatic is among the worst cases, not the innocent ones.
(7) So one of the earlier steps has to go.

Now, Abelard apparently bit this bullet pretty hard -- I gather he said the people who persecuted the martyrs would have sinned MORE if they'd gone against their own consciences -- which makes me think maybe the argument is fine and the problem is my intuitions in (6). But I really don't want to give up (1) either, the whole appeal of the view is that morality shouldn't be a lottery. So: where does the argument actually go wrong, if it does? And if I insist on keeping (1) and (2), what exactly am I forced to say about the inquisitor -- is there some way his consent is defective that isn't just smuggling the outward act back in? I'd rather be told which premise is the cheapest to drop and what it costs than be given a mushy "it's complicated". Also, if Abelard himself had an answer to this I'd love to know roughly what it was, but mostly I want the diagnosis. Oh and please reply in British English, the American spellings in my charity shop book keep throwing me off.
