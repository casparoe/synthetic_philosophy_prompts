Two friends of mine, both of whom took the same cognitive science seminar, got into a long argument about the Libet experiments, and I'd like an outside ruling.

(1) Marcus's opening move: in Libet-style experiments, a buildup of brain activity (the readiness potential) begins several hundred milliseconds before subjects report becoming aware of their urge to move, so the brain settles on the action before consciousness gets there. The feeling of deciding, he concluded, is a post-hoc narration; conscious will doesn't do any causal work.

(2) Dana replied that this overreads the readiness potential. On the accumulation-to-threshold picture, the brain is constantly producing small fluctuations of motor noise, and a movement happens when that noise drifts past a threshold. The "readiness potential" is then just the average of those ramp-ups backward from the movement, not a signature of a decision being made. Nothing was decided early; the conscious urge can be exactly when the decision happens.

(3) Marcus conceded the point about the ramp not being a decision, but said it doesn't save her. Even if consciousness shows up right at the threshold crossing, the crossing itself is driven by subpersonal noise plus ramping, so the conscious urge is still the output of the process, not its driver. And the standard escape hatch — Libet's own suggestion that we retain a conscious veto in the final 150–200 milliseconds, a "free won't" — just relocates the problem: any veto would itself be a neural event, presumably preceded by its own buildup, so invoking it starts a regress.

(4) Dana's final move: both his framing and the regress charge assume that for free will to be real, a conscious inner agent must stand before the neural machinery and initiate it. But if the person simply is the whole process — the noise, the ramping, the threshold, and the awareness of the urge — then asking whether the conscious part "caused" the movement, as if it were a separate operator who arrived too late, is a category mistake rather than a discovery.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four moves, say whether it's sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and why — paying attention to whether Marcus's move (3) really survives conceding the accumulation interpretation, and whether Dana's move (4) answers the regress worry or merely changes the subject. Finally, is there anything important about these experiments — for instance, the kind of spontaneous, reason-free decision the task involves — that both of them are missing?
