Two colleagues of mine argued through lunch about whether "democratic backsliding" names a real phenomenon or is mostly a partisan label. Mara opened with Hungary: an elected government rewrote the constitution, restructured the courts, saw most major media pass into friendly hands, and repeatedly adjusted electoral rules to its own advantage. None of that is ordinary politics, she said; it's the piecemeal dismantling of checks on power from within, by legal means, and we need a term for exactly that pattern. Daniel replied that everything on her list was done by governments with electoral mandates, through legal procedures, and voters remain free to remove them -- the opposition still wins local races. If using a mandate to reshape institutions counts as backsliding, he said, then nearly every ambitious reform government qualifies, and in practice the label gets applied to right-wing governments while comparable institutional hardball elsewhere gets called "reform." Mara answered that the distinctive mark isn't legality but direction: each change weakens the constraints that would govern the *next* election, so "voters can still remove them" becomes less true with every round -- and the charge that the term is sometimes misapplied cuts both ways, since refusing to name a real pattern because it's occasionally abused is its own bias. Daniel's last move was to ask who gets to decide when a constraint has been "weakened" versus merely "rebalanced": the standard democracy indices rely on expert coders with their own politics, so the concept can't do the neutral, above-the-fray work she wants, and we'd do better to condemn specific acts on their merits than to wield a label that does the condemning for us. I stayed out of it.
