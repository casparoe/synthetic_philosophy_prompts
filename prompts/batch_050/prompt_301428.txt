So I've been circling around this debate for a while now, first in an undergrad seminar and now in a reading group, and I'm hoping someone can just tell me what's actually going on. The debate is about complicity: cases where you participate in some collective wrongdoing but your own contribution makes no causal difference. Think of the classic examples -- buying factory-farmed chicken when the industry is too coarse-grained for your purchase to change anything, voting for a bad policy in an election decided by millions, working a mid-level job at a company doing something terrible where if you quit they'd hire someone identical by Friday. Some philosophers say participation itself is wrong even without causal impact (there's talk of "participating in a collective wrong," of complicity as its own category, of sharing in the group's reasons or intentions), and others say if you didn't make a difference, you didn't do anything wrong, full stop -- the complicity talk is just moral guilt-by-association dressed up as ethics. Both sides have been writing papers at each other for decades and as far as I can tell nobody has converted anyone.

My hypothesis, which might be totally wrong, is that this isn't really an ethical disagreement at all but a disagreement about what moral theory is *for*. The causal-skeptics seem to treat morality as answering "what difference do I, this one agent, make?" while the complicity people treat it as answering something more like "what should we be doing, and where do I stand in relation to that?" -- so the two sides are arguably passing each other with different questions under one label. But maybe that's too tidy and there's a real empirical or formal question hiding underneath, like whether "collective action" even makes sense as a unit of moral assessment, or whether expected-value reasoning quietly settles the chicken case and people are just squeamish about the answer. I genuinely can't tell if the impasse means the question is ill-posed or just hard.

Give me your diagnosis as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no intro or conclusion. I want to know what kind of disagreement this really is -- verbal vs substantive, facts vs intuitions vs standards of evidence -- and for each point, what would actually count as progress (a distinction, an argument form, a case both sides must agree on), or if the answer is "no progress is coming," say which point that applies to and why.
