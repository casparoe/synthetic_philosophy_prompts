So I work at a mid-sized software company and last week a chunk of our internal design docs ended up on a competitor's Slack, screenshots and all. We share files with two outside contractors, and access logs show the docs were pulled through one of their API keys, but because of how our logging pipeline was set up (long story, bad decisions) we can't tell which vendor's key was actually used. Here's where it got spicy at lunch. Vendor A handles roughly 90% of the file traffic through that pipeline, Vendor B handles 10%. Our security lead, Marcus, said we should drop Vendor A: if the leak came through one of their keys and A accounts for 90% of the traffic, there's a 90% chance it was them, and you don't keep working with a vendor you're 90% sure leaked your docs. Priya, one of our backend engineers, said that's insane and that a 90% market-share number is not evidence against anyone in particular. Marcus said it's literally just probability, the same math we use for fraud scoring and spam filters every day, and nobody calls those insane.

Then it went back and forth and I lost track of who was winning. (1) Priya said courts have dealt with exactly this — if a company runs 80% of the buses on a road, that alone can't prove their bus caused an accident — and the reason is that bare statistics don't single anyone out, they're "naked." Marcus fired back that (2) we're not a court and don't need proof beyond a reasonable doubt, we just need a good business decision, and if a hospital found out 90% of infections traced to one supplier, nobody would say keep ordering from both because the evidence is too naked. (3) Priya's counter was that the 90% number doesn't even mean what Marcus thinks: traffic share is only the leak probability if both vendors are equally careless, which is exactly the thing we don't know, so the number is either assuming what it's trying to prove or it's useless. (4) Marcus said fine, then it goes the other way too — if A actually leaked, there'd presumably be *some* specific evidence of it, and the fact that all we have is a market-share number is itself suspicious, so maybe neither vendor did anything and our logging is just broken. Priya said (5) that last move actually gives away the game, because he's now admitting specific evidence matters, and the original "90%, drop them" argument never had any.

I genuinely can't tell who's right at each step — Marcus seems right that businesses act on base rates all the time, but Priya's point about the number assuming equal carelessness feels load-bearing. Can you go through moves (1) through (5) and say who's right at each one and why, and then say who's more right overall? Also: is there anything both of them are missing, like some way the market-share stat could legitimately count as evidence even if Priya's objections land?
