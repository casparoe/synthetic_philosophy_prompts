Roughly thirty undergraduates, mostly not philosophy majors. They will arrive having read a short piece arguing that a symphony is not identical with any performance of it, but the reading only glances at recordings. Plan how I should run the explanation rather than giving me the explanation itself: what has to be established first (I'm assuming some preliminary notion of repeatability, or of multiple correct instances of one thing?), what the one carrying example should be that runs through the whole session, and why it should or shouldn't be a famous canonical work versus, say, a cover song, a jazz standard with no definitive score, or a work that exists only as a studio recording with no performable score at all.

Where I specifically want help is the misconception map and its timing. I can already predict some of what students will do: they'll treat the score as the work itself, or treat the work as the set of all its performances, or -- the one I'm most worried about -- treat the famous recording they grew up with as the work, such that an alternative performance counts as a version of the recording rather than of the piece. Tell me which of these will actually arise, in what order, given the audience; at what point in the sequence each should be headed off rather than allowed to form; and which wrong turn is actually pedagogically useful and should be let to run for a few minutes before being closed down. Finally: tell me what to deliberately leave out at this level and say out loud that I'm leaving out -- I'm guessing questions about what makes a performance count as a performance of this work at all, and whether the composer's intentions fix the type, are rabbit holes for this audience -- and give me one closing question or quick written exercise that would let me tell whether they've grasped that the work is something performances and recordings are of, in different and asymmetric ways, rather than just having memorized the vocabulary. Assume a whiteboard and no audio equipment, so the example has to work entirely through description.
