I serve on the independent review panel of a parcel-delivery company whose routing software was introduced two years ago to reduce costs and fuel use. The system sends trucks through whichever legal routes best satisfy its delivery targets. No employee chooses particular streets, but engineers set the system’s priorities, managers decide which warnings require attention, executives establish delivery deadlines, and the board rewards each division for speed and cost. An engineer once filed a report warning that the software might disproportionately route heavy trucks through villages with narrow roads; the report was classified as low priority because it did not identify a definite increase in accident risk. Executives later saw regional safety statistics, but not in a form that highlighted particular communities. Drivers simply followed the routes assigned to them.

Over eighteen months, three serious accidents occurred in the same village, and a statistical review has now made the routing pattern unmistakable. Yet everyone involved can plausibly disown the outcome: the engineer raised the concern and had no authority to delay deployment; the safety manager followed the company’s escalation rules; the executives approved a general efficiency policy rather than these routes; the board never reviewed the relevant data; and the drivers broke no traffic laws. The company’s lawyers propose paying legally required damages and announcing technical improvements, while insisting that neither any employee nor the company itself acted wrongly. Residents want the company to apologize in its own voice, compensate them beyond its legal obligation, and accept responsibility rather than treat the accidents as an unintended side effect.

How should our panel think about this? I am not asking for public-relations advice. I want to know what would have to be true for the company itself, and not merely some collection of employees, to be at fault when no individual decision seems sufficient to produce the harm. How should we distinguish mere causal contribution, individual negligence, and responsibility belonging to the organization as an organized decision-maker? If the company can properly be blamed, what responses would actually express that blame—an apology, compensation, sanctions against individuals, changes to incentives and authority, or something else—and who is capable of apologizing on its behalf? Please also assess the two tempting shortcuts: blaming each employee in proportion to seniority, and concluding that because no one intended the accidents, responsibility has simply vanished.
