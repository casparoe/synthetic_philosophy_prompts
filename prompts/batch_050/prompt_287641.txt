Last night my flatmate and I got into a three-hour argument about the statue-and-the-clay problem that ended, rather unsatisfyingly, with both of us agreeing that the standard case — sculptor forms a lump into a statue, someone later squashes it flat, statue gone but lump intact — has been discussed to death and proves less than people think. The squashing is a single, clean destructive event, and every theory of material constitution seems to have a pre-packaged response to it. What neither of us could do off the cuff was construct a case where the theories actually pull apart in a less rehearsed way. So I am asking you to do what we could not: build me a thought experiment, to specifications, in which the verdicts of three positions on material constitution genuinely diverge.

Here are the specifications. The scenario should involve an ordinary artifact made of ordinary matter — a workshop, a kitchen, a foundry, not a philosophy-of-physics lab; no teleporters, no gods, no fissioning persons, and no ship of Theseus. Crucially, the case must involve the same portion of matter first becoming an artifact, then undergoing a gradual change, and then being worked into an artifact again, so that the question "is the thing at the end identical to the thing at the start?" cannot be settled by pointing to one decisive crushing moment. One paragraph for the scenario itself, no more. The case must be engineered so that (a) a constitution theorist — who holds that the statue and the lump are two distinct objects coinciding in one place, related by constitution rather than identity — and (b) a four-dimensionalist or perdurantist — who holds that objects are extended across time as worms or stages — come to opposite verdicts on whether the artifact existing at the end is numerically identical to the artifact at the beginning, and so that (c) a nihilist or eliminativist about artifacts refuses the question's presupposition and denies there were ever any statues in the story at all. The verdicts must fall out of the theories; a case that simply stipulates who says what does not count, since I intend to use this in a reading group where at least one member defends each position.

After the scenario, do the audit. Walk through the reasoning each of the three positions gives and show that each verdict is generated rather than assumed. Then tell me which single feature of your case is doing the philosophical work — my suspicion is that gradualness alone is not enough and that the reuse of the very same matter in the second artifact is what actually forces the constitution theorist and the perdurantist apart — and say what happens to the divergence if that feature is removed and the case collapses back into a one-artifact story. Finally, identify which of the three positions comes out looking worst on your case, and give the strongest counterattack its defender could mount: does she challenge the case's coherence, deny one of its stipulated facts, or bite the bullet, and at what cost?
