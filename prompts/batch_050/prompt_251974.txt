I teach an undergraduate seminar in formal methods for philosophy students -- 24 students, one 75-minute session, no prior economics assumed. I want an in-class game that makes them feel, in their own balance sheets, the difference between an ensemble average and a time average, before either term is mentioned.

The mathematical core I'd like the game built around is a repeated multiplicative gamble of roughly this shape: each round, a player stakes their entire current balance on a fair coin flip; heads multiplies the balance by 1.5, tails multiplies it by 0.6. The per-round expected multiplier is positive (1.05), yet a player who keeps playing almost surely trends toward zero, since the typical path loses about 5% per round. Crucially, I want the design to make visible, live in the room, the divergence between the class as a collective and its members: the summed wealth of all 24 students should keep climbing (driven by one or two lucky outliers) while the large majority of individual students go bust -- so that "the average student is getting richer" and "nearly every student is getting poorer" are both true at once, on the same projected numbers.

Please specify: the full rules and round structure; how to run 20-30 rounds fast with 24 students (flips done individually, in parallel, or one shared flip -- recommend and justify); a scoring scheme with real but small stakes that doesn't involve money; and a way to display the aggregate-vs-individual divergence as it emerges. Then I'd like a second phase built in: students are offered the chance to pool -- e.g., groups of four split each round's gains and losses equally -- and the rules should be arranged so that pooling flips the typical individual trajectory from shrinking to growing, so participants discover that cooperation here is not altruism but a way of converting a time-average problem into something closer to an ensemble average.

Include a debrief plan that names the concepts only afterwards and maps specific moments of the session onto them (including the moment the room realizes the pooled groups' gains came partly from the bankrupt players' coins), a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and a frank statement of which feature of the ergodicity critique of expected-value reasoning the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in discussion.
