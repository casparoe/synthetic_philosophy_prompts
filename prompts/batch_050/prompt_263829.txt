After forty years in experimental physics, I have spent my retirement working through philosophy and game theory, and I keep returning to the prisoner's dilemma -- specifically, to the fact that the same two players, facing the same payoffs, should rationally defect if they meet once and yet can rationally cooperate if they expect to meet again. I would like you to write me a villanelle that takes this contrast as its entire subject.

The form should do real work. Let the first refrain state the one-shot logic: whatever the other prisoner does, defection leaves one better off, and so reason compels it. Let the second refrain state the iterated logic: when today's choice returns to greet one tomorrow, restraint can be the policy that pays. Because a villanelle's refrains recur throughout the poem, the form itself enacts repetition -- the very condition under which the second refrain becomes true -- and I would like the final quatrain, where the two refrains stand side by side for the only time, to bring out that the difference between the two situations is nothing but the shadow of the future: lengthen it and cooperation emerges, fix the final round in advance and the whole structure unravels backward to mutual defection on the first move.

Please keep to the traditional nineteen lines in iambic pentameter with the standard ABA rhyme scheme and refrain pattern. The content should be accurate enough that someone who knows Axelrod's tournaments and the folk theorem would nod rather than wince: defection's dominance in the one-shot game, the success of a strategy that opens cooperatively, answers defection in kind, but is quick to forgive, and the fact that cooperation in finitely repeated play with a known endpoint collapses by backward induction. No name-dropping of theorists in the poem itself, though -- the ideas should be recognizable without labels.
