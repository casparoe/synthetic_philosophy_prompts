I chair the intellectual-property committee of my university, a body whose formal charter is thin and whose real law is its own string of past rulings. I intend to use our docket in my graduate seminar on property theory, and I want a careful analysis first. Our precedents to date:

1. A historian wrote a trade book during a sabbatical, using her office, the library, and a small research fund. We held the copyright belongs to her; ordinary academic support does not create a university claim.
2. A member of the IT staff wrote a room-scheduling system as part of his assigned duties. We held the university owns it outright.
3. A doctoral student, paid from a research grant, synthesized a compound in the principal investigator's laboratory. We held the university owns the patent, with a royalty share flowing to the named inventors.
4. A professor designed an online course on her own initiative but recorded the lectures in the university's professional media studio. We held she owns the course, while granting the university a perpetual, nonexclusive license to use the recordings.
5. A staff photographer's images, shot on assignment for university publications, we held to be university property; his personal portfolio shots, taken with university equipment on weekends, we held to be his.

The case now before us: a tenured computer scientist, acting outside any assigned duty, has built a forecasting model that she proposes to license commercially. Three facts complicate it. First, the training data includes hundreds of hours of her own lectures, recorded over the years in the media studio under exactly the arrangement described in case 4. Second, it includes problem sets written by her teaching assistants, who produced them in the ordinary course of their teaching duties and were never asked about this use. Third, the training runs consumed a large allocation on the university's high-performance computing cluster, awarded to her on a competitive internal grant and far beyond ordinary support.

Please do four things, in order. First, state the two or three principles each of which is individually consistent with all five precedents, stating each precisely enough that it can decide cases. Second, show that at least two of these principles diverge on the new case, and say what each would have us decide and why. I am particularly interested in whether the model is better analogized to the expressive works of cases 1 and 4 or to the invention of case 3, and in whether case 5 implies that part of the training data belongs to the university rather than to her in the first place. Third, recommend the ruling that best coheres with our record, identify the closest precedent, and state honestly which principle we would be ratifying if we ruled the other way. Fourth, describe the future hypothetical case whose resolution would finally force the committee to choose between the two live principles. If you find that some pair of our precedents is inconsistent under any principle whatsoever, say so and identify the offending pair rather than papering over it.
