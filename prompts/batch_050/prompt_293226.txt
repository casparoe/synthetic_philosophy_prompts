Two frameworks are routinely offered for the same everyday phenomenon — acting against one's own better judgment — and I want to know how they relate, and whether one of them actually explains what the other merely describes.

The first is the philosophical puzzle of akrasia. Socrates is said to have denied that anyone ever acts against what they judge best: genuine knowledge of the good, on this view, simply issues in action, so apparent cases of weakness of will must really be cases of ignorance or confused judgment. The later philosophical tradition has largely taken the opposite line, treating clear-eyed akrasia — judging that one ought to do A, while freely doing B, with full awareness — as possible and in need of explanation. The puzzle is that such action seems to violate plausible principles about the connection between judgment, reason, and motivation: if you sincerely judge A better all things considered, and nothing compels or impedes you, how does B happen?

The second is the economic model of hyperbolic (or more generally present-biased) discounting. On this model, agents discount future rewards at a rate that is not constant over delay: immediate rewards loom disproportionately large relative to later ones. Because preferences over the same pair of outcomes can reverse as time passes, an agent may sincerely prefer the distant good option when both options are far off, yet choose the nearer, worse option when it becomes available. Behavioral economists treat this as a well-confirmed empirical regularity, and some present it as dissolving the old puzzle: weakness of will is just what dynamically inconsistent time preferences look like from the inside.

Here is what I want you to adjudicate. First, are these two frameworks even describing the same phenomenon? The economic model describes a predictable, systematic pattern of preference reversal across time; the philosophical puzzle concerns a breakdown between judgment and action at a single moment, which need not involve temporal discounting at all (consider acting on a passing rage against your standing judgment, or procrastinating about something where no immediate reward tempts you). So one possibility is that hyperbolic discounting explains a subset of akratic-looking cases and leaves the core puzzle untouched. Another is that the philosophical puzzle was always confused, and once you see action as the output of competing valuation systems rather than the deliverance of an all-things-considered judgment, there is nothing left to explain. A third is that the economic model covertly presupposes an answer to the philosophical question — for instance, about which momentary preference represents the agent's "real" judgment — and so cannot be used to settle it.

Second, whichever way you come down on that, tell me which framework comes closer to a complete account of weakness of will, and what "complete" should mean here: predictive accuracy, psychological realism, normative guidance about what to do, or resolution of the conceptual puzzle. I want your verdict, not a balanced survey.
