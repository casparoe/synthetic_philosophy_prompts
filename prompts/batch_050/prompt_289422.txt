I'm writing an essay on M. Night Shyamalan's The Village for a political theory reading group, and I want to read it through Burkean conservatism and Chesterton's fence. On its face the film looks like a fable built out of the fence principle: the villagers obey the rules (never cross the boundary, fear the creatures in the woods, avoid the bad color) without knowing the reason, and the one character who does cross, Ivy, discovers what's actually going on. But the twist complicates everything: the fence's keepers are its builders. The elders didn't inherit a tradition whose rationale got lost over generations, they fabricated one in a single generation, complete with invented monsters, and they decide the young people can't be let in on the reason.

So: read the film seriously against both Burke and Chesterton. Does it expose a hole in the fence principle -- namely that Chesterton's rule assumes the reformer can go find out why the fence was built, but here the reason can't survive being made public, and the people who know it have an interest in keeping it secret? Is a manufactured tradition a corruption of what Burke valued, or does the film work as a reductio of Burke, since on his own account legitimate authority always rests on a kind of useful fiction that institutions grew rather than were chosen by somebody? And what does the ending, where the elders double down even after everything, suggest about whether a fence founded on a lie can ever settle into a genuine tradition? Be honest about where the film resists the reading rather than forcing it, and say what the film dramatizes about inherited institutions that the standard Burke/Chesterton arguments don't capture.
