Before I commit to any particular view about how small harms stack up against severe ones, I want to fix the standard by which I will judge the views on offer. The motivating puzzle is the familiar one: is it better that one person undergo fifty years of excruciating torture than that some astronomically large number of people each suffer a single dust speck in the eye — and if, as many feel, no multitude of specks ever outweighs the torture, what feature of harms explains that? I am not asking you to solve the puzzle directly. I am asking what any adequate theory of the aggregation of harms must be able to do in order to count as a serious candidate.

The purpose constraining the desiderata is this: the theory must be good enough to guide real policy trade-offs in which a small number of severe burdens are weighed against vast numbers of trivial ones (speed limits that predictably cost some lives while saving everyone time, safety regulations, resource triage). For that purpose the theory must reckon with several wrinkles the bare dust-specks case conceals: some trivial harms are consciously noticed while others fall below the threshold of awareness altogether (imagine a device that silently shaves one second of restful sleep off every citizen each night); some trivial harms are the only harm their victim suffers, while others land on people already badly off; and trivial harms can cascade, each speck generating further specks. A theory may answer these cases differently, but it may not pass over them in silence.

Write the desiderata: which verdicts the theory must deliver in which cases, which distinctions it must respect (for instance, between aggregation across persons and aggregation across moments within a single life, and between harms that are felt and harms that merely register), and which structural properties it must exhibit (transitivity across long chains of pairwise trade-offs, consistent treatment of the millionth speck and the first, stability when tiny harms cascade). For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether it is negotiable given the stated purpose or not. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Then, briefly, score three contenders against the list: additive total utilitarianism, a lexical-threshold view on which some harms outrank any number of lesser ones, and a significance-floor view on which harms below a certain weight simply do not count.

Present your answer as a numbered list of exactly six items, with no introduction and no conclusion: items 1 through 5 are desiderata, each stated in one or two sentences together with its motivating case and a note on whether it is negotiable; item 6 is the brief scoring of the three contenders.
