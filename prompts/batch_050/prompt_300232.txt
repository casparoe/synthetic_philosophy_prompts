I'm one of three trustees of a small family foundation (about $300k a year in grants, $6M in an invested endowment). The other two trustees disagree morally: Marta thinks factory farming is the great moral emergency of our time, Jon thinks it's obviously malaria and global health. Two decisions are on the table for the December meeting: this year's grant budget (Marta wants to fund a cage-free corporate campaign, Jon wants a malaria charity) and whether to divest the endowment from agribusiness stocks, which Marta cares about far more than the grants since the endowment dwarfs them, and which Jon considers a symbolic waste of time. Marta has proposed a deal: she'll vote for Jon's malaria grant if he votes for divestment, and she points out, correctly I think, that this leaves both of them better off in terms of what each actually values. Jon is refusing, on grounds he's bad at articulating: roughly, that you don't horse-trade your votes on matters of right and wrong, and something has gone wrong the moment a moral conviction becomes a bargaining chip. I chair the board and have to write the memo we'll vote on. Draft it: recommend accepting the deal, rejecting it, or some third arrangement, with real reasons -- is Marta's deal just ordinary compromise, or the shady kind of logrolling people object to in politics, and is that objection actually any good? Is "we each just vote our conscious on every question" really the neutral default it feels like? And take Jon's integrity worry seriously, because I suspect he's wrong but can't quite say why.
