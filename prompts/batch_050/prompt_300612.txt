Read King Lear through Jane English's friendship model of filial obligation -- her view that grown children owe their parents not repayment of a debt for past sacrifices but the duties of an ongoing friendship, grounded in present love rather than in accounts of who gave what. Lear seems to run the opposing, debt-based model from the first scene: he demands that love be declared, measured, and exchanged for territory, and he experiences Cordelia's refusal to bid as ingratitude rather than as a refusal to treat love as a transaction. Does the play vindicate English -- showing that the debt model corrupts precisely the thing it tries to secure, so that the daughters who speak its language speak falsely and the one who won't speak it loves best? Or does the play complicate her: Cordelia's nothing is itself a kind of pride, Kent and the Fool show that loyal service can look like insolence, and Lear's late wish to be forgiven suggests something between debt and friendship that neither model cleanly names? I'd like a reading that takes English's account seriously as philosophy, not just as a label -- where it illuminates the love test, the storm scenes, and the reconciliation, where Lear's own position is more defensible than the model allows, and what, if anything, Shakespeare's version of a family that cannot say what it owes shows about the limits of treating filial duty as friendship.
