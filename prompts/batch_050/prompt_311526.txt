I'm a hobby beekeeper, no philosophy or physics training, and something's been bugging me since I read a bunch of pop-science stuff about electrons over the winter.

Here's the setup as I understand it (correct me if I'm wrong). In physics, when you count the possible arrangements of a bunch of particles, swapping two electrons doesn't count as a new arrangement. Like, two boxes with an electron each: physics says there's exactly one state, not "electron A left, B right" vs "electron B left, A right." Apparently this really matters — if you mix two tanks of the same gas, the entropy doesn't go up, but if you mix two different gases it does, and the whole counting system of modern physics depends on treating same-kind particles this way. Meanwhile in ordinary life we count the other way: two identical bee suits hanging on two pegs are still two suits, and if I swapped them I'd say something changed, even if nobody could ever tell.

Around the same time I picked up a translation of Leibniz's Monadology (from 1714), and near the start he claims that in nature there can never be two beings that are perfectly alike, that any two real things must differ in some internal way, otherwise they'd be one and the same thing. I couldn't find two identical leaves in my garden to disprove him, so fine, maybe.

But now I'm stuck, because the physicists' counting practice seems to do something weird relative to Leibniz. On one reading, electrons are exactly the counterexample he said couldn't exist: two electrons, perfectly alike, differing only in that there are two of them. On another reading, the counting practice is secretly Leibnizian — it refuses to treat the swapped cases as different states precisely because there's no difference between them, so "which electron is which" isn't even a real question.

So what I want to know is: what does this counting practice actually presuppose about what it means to be a thing? Does it only make sense if Leibniz was right (no true difference, no true twoness)? Or does it presuppose he was wrong (there really are two electrons, physics just declines to track which is which)? Or is the practice neutral between those, with several different pictures of identity all licensing the same counting? And practically: if you committed to one of these pictures for real, would anything about how you count, measure, or talk about particles have to change, or would the math stay identical and only the story around it shift?

Please keep it accessible — I don't know the jargon, so explain terms when you use them, and tell me where you think the question is genuinely undecided rather than just picking a side for me.
