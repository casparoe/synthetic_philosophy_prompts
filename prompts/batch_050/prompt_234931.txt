Construct a triage case, for use in a seminar on health resource allocation, in which three allocation principles that are often treated as roughly coincident come apart: saving the greatest number of lives, maximizing QALYs, and a fair-innings principle giving priority to those who have had the least life so far. Constraints: a realistic setting (e.g., a single ICU during a surge, or a fixed stock of a scarce treatment); at most three patient groups; the scenario itself limited to one paragraph; and numbers small and explicit enough that a reader can verify each verdict by hand. The three-way divergence must arise from the structure of the case — from differences in prognosis and in the years of life at stake — and not from any built-in judgment about the quality of patients' lives, which would let a critic dismiss the case rather than engage it. After the scenario: state each principle's verdict and show the arithmetic behind it; identify the single parameter that does the work; explain how the ordering changes if that parameter is equalized across groups; and give the strongest objection available to a defender of whichever principle ends up looking worst.
