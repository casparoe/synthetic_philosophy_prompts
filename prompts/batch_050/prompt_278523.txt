For a meeting of my school's philosophy society I put together the following argument, which appears to show that promises never create obligations; none of us has been able to locate the flaw, though we are fairly sure there must be one.

(1) Suppose I promise to do something I already have sufficient reason to do, such as repaying a debt. Then the promise is idle: the obligation existed before I spoke, and my uttering the words adds no new duty.
(2) Suppose instead I promise to do something I do not otherwise have sufficient reason to do. Then any obligation must be created by the utterance itself. But merely saying words cannot change what I have reason to do; if the act was not obligatory beforehand, declaring it so cannot make it so.
(3) Every promise falls under one of these two cases.
(4) Therefore no promise ever obliges.

The conclusion must be wrong — a friend who breaks a casual promise plainly does something objectionable — yet each premise seems hard to deny, and I should particularly like to keep (1). Which premise or step should I abandon, why did it appear innocent, and what must each of the standard accounts of promising — the reliance or expectation view, the social-practice view, and the view that promising transfers a measure of authority over one's future conduct — concede in order to reject it?
