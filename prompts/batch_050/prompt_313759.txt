I produce a public-affairs program for a public radio station, and next month we are interviewing a political philosopher whose new book argues that John Stuart Mill's On Liberty, read consistently, settles the drug question in favor of full commercial legalization — not decriminalization, not prescription models, but a taxed open market in currently prohibited substances, with the harm principle doing the decisive work. The book is brisk and self-assured, and I do not want to spend the hour letting him restate it.

I intend to read him one passage from chapter V, "Applications," on air, because I think it cuts against his reading rather than supporting it:

"Again, trade is a social act. Whoever undertakes to sell any description of goods to the public, does what affects the interest of other persons, and of society in general; and thus his conduct, in principle, comes within the jurisdiction of society [...] On the other hand, there are questions relating to interference with trade, which are essentially questions of liberty; such as the Maine Law, already touched upon; the prohibition of the importation of opium into China; the restriction of the sale of poisons; all cases, in short, where the object of the interference is to make it impossible or difficult to obtain a particular commodity. These interferences are objectionable, not as infringements on the liberty of the producer or seller, but on that of the buyer."

Please draft eight to ten interview questions, in the order I should ask them, built around this passage and the actual commitments of his position, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give. The set must include, at a minimum:

1. An opening question that puts the passage to him directly: does his reading of Mill absorb it, or must he treat it as a lapse? Mill locates the protected liberty in the buyer, not the seller, and classes poison-sale restriction and the China opium ban as objectionable only insofar as they burden acquisition. When he answers — as he will — that a legal buyer implies a legal seller, the follow-up should press him on why "trade is a social act" does not then license exactly the supply-side regulation his book condemns.

2. A question on what Mill does with poison sales later in the same chapter: he sketches a regime of labeling, registered transactions, and recorded purposes while rejecting any blanket prescription requirement. Does my guest's market model retain anything like that regime, and if not, what in his interpretation licenses discarding it? Anticipate the reply that Mill was discussing poisons, not recreation, and corner the question of why that difference matters in the relevant respect.

3. A question on Portugal: since 2001, possession is decriminalized, users face administrative dissuasion commissions rather than courts, and sale remains criminal. Given the passage, is that not the genuinely Millian position — and his book an overshoot? The follow-up should ask whether he reads Portuguese outcome data as supporting his thesis or a rival's.

4. A question on the principle's own scope conditions: Mill restricts it to those in the maturity of their faculties. Does chronic addiction compromise the competence the principle presupposes, and what does a free market do about buyers the product itself incapacitates? When he dismisses addiction as a fringe case, the follow-up should ask at what threshold of foreseeable incapacitation the exception would swallow his rule.

5. A question on what legal cannabis commercialization — rising potency, aggressive marketing — implies for his claim that the argument runs on principle rather than outcomes.

6. A closing question asking what evidence or argument he would accept as defeating the book's thesis.

No biographical or process questions. Follow-ups should press the point he will try to slide past, not merely rephrase the question. End the draft with a short note identifying which question in the sequence is most likely to fail against a well-prepared guest — where he has a stronger reply than I have allowed for — and what his answer would have to look like for that question to land anyway. Keep the entire draft under 650 words, not counting the Mill passage.
