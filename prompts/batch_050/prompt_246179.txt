ok so I rewatched Arrival (the 2016 Villeneuve one, with Amy Adams as the linguist) and I want a reading of it through Quine on the indeterminacy of translation, because I think the movie accidentally walks straight into the gavagai problem and then pretends it solved it.

Here's what I mean. The whole plot turns on the heptapods' word that gets rendered as "weapon," and the near-catastrophe is resolved by Louise realizing it should have been translated as something more like "tool" or "gift." So the film's drama literally depends on the idea that "weapon" was a mistranslation and there's a fact of the matter about what the word really means. But if you take Quine seriously, what exactly is that fact supposed to consist in? The humans have no shared context with the heptapods, no bilingual informant, nothing but observed behavior and pointing at things on a screen -- which is basically the radical translation thought experiment, except the rabbits are seven-legged squid aliens. Walk me through where the gavagai-style underdetermination actually bites in the film's translation scenes: when Abbott does something in response to a stimulus, what are the competing translation manuals that fit the behavioral evidence equally well, and does the film ever show evidence that could rule between them?

Then the harder part. The film treats "tool not weapon" as a discovery, i.e. as correcting an error. But on Quine's view it seems like at best it's a different analytical hypothesis -- equally compatible with all possible dispositions to heptapod verbal behavior, maybe more charitable, but not more *true*. So does the movie smuggle in a determinacy of meaning that Quine says doesn't exist? Or is there a charitable reading where the film is actually on Quine's side -- e.g. the resolution works because of pragmatic/charity-based reasons to prefer the peaceful manual, not because anyone found the semantic facts? I'm genuinely unsure which reading the movie supports, so argue for whichever you think the text of the film actually backs.

And then the inversion that makes this fun: the film's actual thesis is more like Sapir-Whorf -- learning the language rewires how Louise experiences time. So the movie is Whorfian about thought but seemingly anti-Quinean about meaning. Is that combination even coherent? Can you hold that a language radically reshapes your cognition while also holding that its words have determinate translations into yours? It seems like the stronger the Whorfianism, the deeper the indeterminacy should run, and the movie wants both at once.

End with: if Quine had been the consultant on the heptapod project instead of a Whorfian field linguist, what would he have told the military when they asked what the aliens' word "really means"? I want the answer to take both the film and the philosophy seriously -- don't just use the movie as a coat rack for the gavagai argument, tell me where the film resists the Quinean reading or where Quine flattens something the film genuinely gets right about translation (like the embodied, gestural stuff with the circular logograms, which is nothing like pointing at rabbits in a field).
