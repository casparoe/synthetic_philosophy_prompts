So in my philosophy of mind reading group we got onto Dennett's idea that the self is a "center of narrative gravity" -- the idea being that just like a physical object has a center of gravity (a useful abstract point, not a little part inside the object), the self is an abstract point that a person's stories about themselves orbit around, not a little homunculus sitting in the head. One guy in the group loves this and uses it to argue that the self is "just a fiction" and so things like long-term guilt or promises made by your past self don't really bind anyone, since there's no persisting thing there, only a narrative device. I pushed back that this is doing way more work than the physics analogy can support -- after all, centers of gravity are perfectly real and predictive, nobody says a planet's orbit is "just a fiction."

Can you audit the analogy properly? Lay out what maps onto what (what plays the role of the object, the gravitational field, the fictionalist vs. realist status of the point, etc.), which features of physical centers of gravity carry over to selves and which don't, and where the analogy quietly imports assumptions -- e.g. about whether abstracta can be causally or morally load-bearing -- that the physics case doesn't settle. Most importantly: does the "it's just a fiction, so nothing binds" conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table? And if the center-of-gravity analogy breaks somewhere, is there a better comparison for what the self is (or isn't)?
