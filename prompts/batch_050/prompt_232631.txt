At a workshop in my department last spring, two philosophers -- a colleague of mine and a visiting speaker -- got into an extended dispute about the nature of emotion, and it ended without either conceding anything. I would like an outside adjudication. Here is the exchange as I reconstruct it.

(1) My colleague defended the judgment view. Emotions, she said, have propositional content, are assessable as appropriate or inappropriate, and can be revised by argument. The best explanation is that fear of the dog essentially involves judging the dog to be dangerous. Feelings are optional concomitants: one can fear an IRS audit for a year while feeling afraid only intermittently, so the feeling cannot be what the emotion consists in.

(2) The visitor replied with recalcitrant emotions. A man who fears flying while sincerely judging it the safest way to travel is not contradicting himself, yet the judgment view says he is -- it attributes to him both the judgment that flying is safe and the judgment that it is dangerous. Better, he argued, to treat emotions as perceptual or quasi-perceptual representations of evaluative properties, like danger or offensiveness: they persist in the face of contrary judgment the way the lines in the Muller-Lyer illusion still look unequal after measurement. He added that infants and animals plainly have emotions without possessing the concepts the relevant judgments would require.

(3) My colleague answered that the perceptual view merely relocates the problem. If perceptual contents are propositional, the contradiction returns at a lower level; if they are not, the view cannot explain why emotions answer to evidence and rationalize action -- an optical illusion does not make it rational to treat the lines as unequal, whereas fear does make avoidance intelligible. And representing something *as* threatening is already concept-laden, she said, so the alleged gain over judgment is illusory.

(4) The visitor responded that the judgment view conflates an emotion with its warrant: a judgment can justify fear without being the fear, just as testimony can justify a visual belief without being the seeing. Moreover, the judgment view's own patch for recalcitrance -- claiming that the phobic traveler oscillates between judgments or holds contradictory ones -- is ad hoc psychology invented to save the theory, while the perceptual model predicts recalcitrance for free.

(5) My colleague's final move: strip away both judgment and evaluation-laden perception, and nothing remains to distinguish perceiving danger from coldly noting it except raw feeling -- which both of them had already agreed is neither necessary nor sufficient for emotion. So the perceptual theorist owes an account of what the "affective" in affective perception is, if not judgment or feeling.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating; state who is more right overall; and identify what, if anything, both parties are missing.
