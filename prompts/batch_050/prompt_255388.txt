I am a novelist by trade, and I have been reading *Gödel, Escher, Bach* in the evenings, as one does when one should be working on a manuscript. Hofstadter's way of pulling a message apart into layers — the inner message that the bearer is meant to convey, the outer message consisting of the conventions by which one decodes it, and the frame message that signals there is a message to be decoded at all — struck me as the first precise description I have encountered of something I wrestle with constantly in fiction. A genre cover is a frame message; the epigraph I chose for my last novel was arguably an outer message that many readers treated as inner; and I once watched an audience take a satirical chapter entirely straight, which suggests the three layers can come unglued from one another. Before I embarrass myself in print by writing an essay on where a novel's meaning actually resides, I want to know what I should be demanding of any theory on the subject. So: act as a philosopher of language and aesthetics who also knows the craft of fiction from the inside, and write me the specification — the desiderata that a theory of the location of literary meaning must meet to count as adequate.

Please think carefully and begin by brainstorming the hard cases before distilling them into criteria; then give me the requirements list, with the motivating case or consideration behind each. The cases I would want covered include, at minimum: narrators whose accounts the text itself quietly undermines; satire that some readers decode correctly and others do not; epigraphs, dedications, and jacket copy that steer interpretation without belonging to the story; translations; authors who declare after publication what the book means; works whose genre cues are invisible to readers from another culture or another century; and the plain fact that rereading changes what a book means to a person without changing a word of it. Mark which distinctions the theory may not collapse — say, intended meaning versus textual meaning versus what a given reader takes away, or a message's presence versus its reception — and note which results from ordinary critical practice it must not contradict. Crucially, flag any pair or subset of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, because that is the part I most want to see, and tell me which single requirement knocks out the most popular contenders. Then, only briefly, score the usual suspects against the list: the view that the author's intention settles meaning, the view that the text alone does, hypothetical intentionalism, and reader-response accounts. Keep the weight on the criteria themselves rather than the scorecard, and say explicitly which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of a particular camp. Finally, tell me whether the three-layer scheme itself survives contact with fiction or needs revising.

Since I will be leaning on your answer in public, I would like an honest sense of its reliability: state how confident you are in the main claims — particularly the verdict on joint satisfiability and on which desideratum does the most excluding — and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of, with a sentence on why. I do not need a reading list or citations; reasoned argument from the cases will do, and where you gesture at a known position, a name in passing is enough.
