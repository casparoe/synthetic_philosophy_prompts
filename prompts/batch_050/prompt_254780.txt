I am a novelist, at present stalled on a book set in the 1890s, and my difficulty is philosophical as much as technical. Last month, at a dinner following a literary festival panel, I listened to two acquaintances argue for the better part of an hour, and I have been turning their exchange over since. One, Eleanor, is a historian who has spent her career in archives; the other, Marcus, is a fellow novelist who writes historical fiction. The dispute ran roughly as follows.

(1) Marcus opened by declaring that the old Rankean ambition — reconstructing the past as it actually happened — is dead, and that historians know it. The archive is not the past; it is what institutions had reasons to preserve. On the line of argument associated with E. H. Carr, historical facts are made rather than found: what counts as a fact of history, rather than a mere fact of the past, is settled by the historian's selection. Every history, therefore, is partly a work of fiction, differing from the novel only in declining to admit it.

(2) Eleanor replied that selection is not fabrication, and that conflating the two is the characteristic sleight of this whole style of argument. A historian who misreads a document has made an error; a novelist who moves a date has made a choice. The historian is answerable to the record in a way the novelist is not, and an unreachable ideal can still govern practice: fidelity to the evidence functions as a regulative ideal, and regulative ideals do not need to be attainable to do their work.

(3) Marcus conceded that historians can get particulars wrong, but said this misses the deeper point, which he associated with Hayden White: even a history whose every particular is verified must arrange those particulars into a narrative, and the past arrives without a narrative form. Emplotment — the decision that these events constitute a tragedy, a comedy, a progress — is invented, not discovered, and the plot is where the meaning lives. It follows, he said, that an invented scene which honestly owns its invention may convey the truth of a period better than a chronicle of verified facts dressed up as a discovered story.

(4) Eleanor answered that this proved too much in two directions at once. First, from the claim that narratives are constructed it does not follow that all constructions are equal: some emplotments are ruled out by the record, and a comic emplotment of a famine would be not merely unconventional but wrong. Second, candour is not truth. The novelist's honesty about invention is a virtue of self-description; it confers no epistemic standing on the thing invented. 'More honest' and 'more true' are different compliments, and Marcus was spending the first as though it were the second.

I have not said which of them I think won, because I genuinely do not know — and my manuscript is waiting on the answer, since I cannot see what I am doing when I invent a scene until I know whether Marcus's claim for it can be made good. Please adjudicate the dispute move by move: rate each of the four moves above on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justify each rating briefly, and then say what, if anything, both parties are missing. Please write your answer in British English.
