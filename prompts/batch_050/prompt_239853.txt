Ok so I've been chewing on an idea for a while and I want a short story out of it. Bear with me while I set it up, since you can't read my mind and the blog posts I'm ripping this off of are kind of deep in the weeds.

The setup: imagine a person, call her whatever, who can answer questions only by delegation. She gets a question, and instead of answering directly she's allowed to spin up temporary copies of herself, hand each copy some sub-question, read their answers, and stitch together a reply. The catch is each copy works under the same rules -- a copy can't just sit and think for a year either, it can only delegate further down. So answering "should I take the job in Berlin" becomes this enormous tree of tiny fast conversations, each individual one kind of shallow, and the hope is that the whole thing in the limit adds up to what she'd think if she could deliberate forever. People on the forums I read argue about whether the tree's output is really *her considered judgment* or just some weird artifact of how you chop the question up -- like, the answer might depend on the decomposition, and there's no decomposition-free fact of the matter.

Write me a short story (1500-2500 words ish) that takes this seriously as fiction. I'd like it told from inside the tree somehow -- maybe from the POV of one copy who starts to suspect that the sub-question it was handed isn't actually the question the root person would want answered, or that some sibling copy upstream is steering the decomposition toward a particular answer. The story should make the reader feel the tension between "the tree's answer is what she thinks, idealized" and "the tree's answer is just what this particular bureaucracy of selves happened to produce." Don't resolve it with a tidy moral; I'd rather the ending stay uncomfortable. Also please no characters named after famous philosophers, that always reads as a wink and it takes me out of it.
