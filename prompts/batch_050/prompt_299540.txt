I'm trying to settle what I actually think about the aggregation of moral claims, and I'd rather do it through cases than through another pass over the literature. Below are my verdicts on a set of rescue and allocation choices. What I'd like from you is: (a) a principle, or at most two candidate principles, that best captures the pattern, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a fresh case without you in the room; (b) an indication of which clause of the principle each case motivates; (c) the principle's verdict on a case I haven't listed; and (d) an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and must therefore overturn. Please answer in British English throughout.

The cases. In each, assume I can do exactly one of the two things, that everyone involved is a stranger to me, and that there are no special obligations in play.

(1) I can save either one person or five people from drowning. Verdict: save the five, and it isn't close.

(2) I can either save one person from death or spare a million people a single day of mild headache. Verdict: save the one. The headaches don't add up to anything that could compete with a life.

(3) A technician is trapped in machinery and will be seriously injured unless I interrupt a live broadcast that a million people are thoroughly enjoying. Verdict: interrupt the broadcast. The viewers' disappointment, however widely distributed, doesn't count for anything here.

(4) I can either save both of one person's legs or save one leg each of two other people. Verdict: I lean towards saving the two people's one leg each — but this is the case I keep going back and forth on, and I can't articulate why.

(5) I can either prevent one person from being paralysed or cure ten people of blindness. Verdict: prevent the paralysis. The stronger individual claim wins however many weaker claims stand against it.

(6) I can save one person with certainty, or attempt a rescue with a fifty-fifty chance of saving five and otherwise saving no one. Verdict: attempt the risky rescue. Expected claims matter, roughly in proportion.

(7) I can either save one identified child now or fund a programme that will, with certainty, save three presently unidentified children over the next decade. Verdict: fund the programme. Numbers don't stop counting merely because the beneficiaries are statistical.

Note the apparent tensions. Cases (2), (3) and (5) seem to reject summing harms across persons; cases (1) and (7) seem to require exactly that; case (6) adds weighting by probability; and case (4) is unstable in my mind in a way I can't diagnose.

So: is there a single principle that delivers all seven verdicts — for instance, something in the contractualist neighbourhood, where each person's strongest individual complaint is compared and numbers enter only to break ties between equal complaints — or is some variant needed? If no principle gets all seven, tell me which verdict must go and why, and say whether the principle that survives is still recognisably contractualist or whether I've smuggled in aggregation through the back door. Then apply your favoured principle to this case I haven't listed: I can either restore one person's sight in both eyes or perform a procedure that will save another person's life but leave them blind — and tell me whether its verdict there rings true. Finally, once the principle is fixed, say what it implies for case (4) and whether it vindicates or overrules my wobble.
