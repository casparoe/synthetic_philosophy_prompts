Write a sestina about Frege's puzzle concerning identity statements. The six end-words of the sestet should include "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus"; the other four are yours to choose, but pick them so that each captures a different way a single thing can be given to us — a time of day, a direction of the sky, a role in a ritual, a place in a story — since the whole weight of the poem should rest on the difference between what a name picks out and how it picks it out. Give the poem a speaker: an astronomer of some older world who has spent a lifetime recording the morning star and the evening star as two distinct lights, with distinct names, distinct tables, distinct prayers, and who has begun to suspect they are one. The sestina's form should do philosophical work: the same six words recur in every stanza, always in altered position, so that recurrence-with-different-presentation is built into the verse itself, and the reader should feel the referent persisting underneath the shifting order before the speaker can say so. Until the envoi, Hesperus and Phosphorus must never appear in the same line; the envoi is where they meet for the first time, and that meeting should land the discovery — why "Hesperus is Hesperus" could never have been news to this speaker, while "Hesperus is Phosphorus" rewrites his life's records. Keep the astronomy pre-telescopic and the epistemology honest: the identity is established by observation and calculation, not revelation. Someone who knows Frege should recognize the puzzle and its standard moral without finding a single claim that would make them wince; someone who does not should still read a good poem about a man learning that two of his gods were one planet.
