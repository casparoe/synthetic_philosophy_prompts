I'm in an AI ethics reading group and last week a friend made an argument that's been bugging me. She said training reinforcement learning agents is morally like dog training: you give treats for good behavior and scoldings for bad, the dog learns to seek one and avoid the other, and so does the RL agent with positive and negative rewards. Her conclusion: since an RL agent in training might rack up millions of episodes full of negative reward, large-scale RL is potentially like running a factory farm of perpetually scolded dogs, and nobody's even counting it. Someone else at the table pushed back hard and said the analogy is doing all the work -- a reward is just a number in a config file, not a scolding, and the comparison flatters a thermostat into a puppy.

Audit the analogy for me. Start with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the treat, the scolding, the dog's anticipation when it hears the treat bag (value function? td error?), the learned habits, the dog itself, and the trainer's intentions. Then the breakdowns -- but sort them into the ones that matter for my friend's conclusion and the ones that are technically real but don't touch it. In particular I want you to get at: the dog comes to training with a body, needs, and a nervous system that was already registering pain before anyone showed up with treats, whereas the agent's entire "wanting" is defined by the reward signal -- does that make the penalty worse (pure aversiveness with no other life) or meaningless (nothing there to be averse to)? Also: when the dog gets scolded, the scolding works because of how it lands in the dog, but the reward works however it lands in the update rule -- is the analogy smuggling in the assumption that the signal is *felt* somewhere? Finally, tell me whether her factory-farm conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the dog picture fails, what a better analogy would be for thinking about negative reward at scale -- or argue that no everyday analogy can carry this and we should just say what we mean directly.
