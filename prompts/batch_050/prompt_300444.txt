I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm trying to describe a case accurately rather than adopt anyone's preferred framing. A hospital uses an algorithm to flag deteriorating patients. One night the system was offline for maintenance, and the on-call engineer decided not to activate the manual fallback — hourly bedside checks — judging it an overreaction. A patient deteriorated and died; the internal review concluded that a 3 a.m. check would very likely have saved her. The complication: the nurse rostered for that round was simultaneously being pulled into an emergency elsewhere, so the check might well have been missed even had the fallback been activated.

The family says the engineer caused her death. The hospital's lawyers say he merely failed to prevent it — which, they argue, is not a kind of causing at all — and add that the outcome would probably have been the same either way. Are these two defenses independent of each other, and does either succeed? If a failure to prevent can be a genuine cause, what distinguishes this engineer from, say, a passerby in the corridor who also did nothing? And if the causal verdict and the moral verdict come apart here, which one belongs in my headline?
