Draft the joint statement that a defender of John Searle's doctrine of original intentionality and a defender of Daniel Dennett's rival account could both sign at the close of a good-faith adversarial collaboration on whether any intentionality is intrinsic to minds.

The background both parties bring to the table: Searle holds that intentionality comes in two grades. Original intentionality is intrinsic to certain biological systems — human and animal brains — and belongs to them independently of how any observer interprets them. Derived intentionality is the aboutness of artifacts: sentences, maps, gauges, and computers mean things only because agents with original intentionality design them or use them to mean things. Dennett's counterexample is the two-bitser: a vending-machine coin acceptor designed and tuned in the United States to admit U.S. quarters and reject physically similar slugs. Suppose the machine is shipped to Panama, where the quarter-balboa — a coin nearly identical to the quarter in size, weight, and composition, and circulating alongside U.S. coinage — is legal tender, and where local operators feed the machine quarter-balboas and treat it as a balboa-acceptor. When a quarter-balboa is inserted and accepted, did the machine's internal state correctly detect a quarter-balboa, or did it misclassify the coin as a U.S. quarter? Dennett argues there is no deeper fact of the matter: the state's content is fixed, if at all, by a design, selection, and use history that underdetermines the answer — and he infers that all content attribution, including the attribution of content to ourselves, is of this character, our own intentionality being conferred by evolution and learning rather than intrinsic, so that the original/derived distinction is at best one of degree. The Searlean replies that the machine has no intentionality of its own whatsoever, that the indeterminacy infects only our loose façon de parler about it, and that the intentionality of its designers and users remains fully determinate and observer-independent.

The deliverable is the document such a collaboration would produce, in four parts:

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of propositions both sides can sign — about the two-bitser case itself, about what a system's physics alone does and does not fix, about whether marks and stop signs possess underived aboutness, about what the Chinese Room argument, if sound, would and would not establish, and about what the dispute is not (neither side denies that brains cause behavior, nor that artifacts are used to mean things). No agreed claim may be vacuous: for each, name the position a third party could hold that would contradict it.

2. Disagreements. A table of the residual disputes, each stated twice — once in wording the Searlean accepts as a fair statement of her own view, once in wording the Dennettian accepts for his — with no formulation either party would reject as a strawman. Include at minimum: whether indeterminacy of content ascription is a deep fact about all intentionality or a superficial feature of artifact-talk; whether natural selection can confer anything properly called derivation; and whether intrinsic, observer-independent aboutness is even a coherent notion.

3. Cruxes and registered predictions. For each disagreement, the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one party, and for each crux a case or argument both sides accept in advance as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down. Candidate tests you should consider — and may adopt, amend, or reject with reasons — include: gradual neuron-by-neuron replacement of a brain with functionally identical silicon; Swampman, the molecule-for-molecule human duplicate formed by sheer accident, with no selection history at all; a lineage of two-bitsers produced by cumulative replication and modification rather than deliberate design; and a community that circulates quarters and quarter-balboas interchangeably, never drawing any distinction between them. For each case, record what each party predicts it shows, and whether they agree on what would make it probative.

4. Verbal versus substantive. A closing section sorting the disagreements into those the parties now agree turn on divergent uses of terms like derived, intrinsic, or interpretation, and those that remain substantive; for each merely verbal dispute, give the two regimented theses that remain once the vocabulary is fixed, and for each substantive one, say why no amount of terminological repair dissolves it.
