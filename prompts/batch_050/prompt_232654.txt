I'm a philosophy grad student and my office-mate Priya is writing her dissertation on semantic paradoxes. Over the past year she's given me her verdicts on basically every tricky sentence I've thrown at her, and I'm convinced she's running one definite theory under the hood, but she won't confirm which until I guess it — there's a bet on this now, actual money, so I want to get it right before our next coffee. Her verdicts so far, as best I remember them:

- The plain liar ("this sentence is false"): pathological, has no truth value, and any assignment you try collapses on you.
- The truth-teller ("this sentence is true"): also pathological. I pushed on this, since nothing inconsistent follows from calling it true or calling it false, and she said that's precisely the problem — nothing in the world pins it down, it just floats free of the facts.
- A Curry sentence ("if this sentence is true, then 2+2=5"): pathological as well, and emphatically not true. Her comment was that the moment you grant it any truth value you can derive whatever you want, and that should tell you it never had one.
- A two-sentence loop (A says "B is true", B says "A is false"): both pathological — no sentence needs to refer to itself for things to break.
- A finite chain that bottoms out (P says "Q is false", Q says "7 is both prime and not prime"): P is just plain true, boring, no paradox, because the chain grounds out in arithmetic.
- Yablo's infinite sequence, where each sentence says that all the later ones are false: every sentence in it is pathological, and she called it the cleanest paradox going since there's no self-reference anywhere in sight.
- A contingent one I made up: "everything Smith will say tomorrow is false", where as a matter of pure coincidence Smith will utter that very sentence and nothing else. She said it depends on the empirical facts — the sentence isn't intrinsically pathological, it just happens to come out ungrounded if that's how the world is.

Here's the new case I want to spring on her: an infinite sequence S1, S2, S3, ... where each sentence says "if at least one later sentence in this sequence is true, then 2+2=5". So it's Yablo's structure but with Curry-style conditionals instead of falsity claims. Predict her verdict on the sequence, and tell me which approach her overall pattern of verdicts best fits — a Kripke-style groundedness/gap view, a revision-theoretic one, a glut view, or something else — being honest about the fact that several of these agree on everything she's ruled on so far.
