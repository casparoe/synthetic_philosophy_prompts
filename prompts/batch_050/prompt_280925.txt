So I teach bioethics at the undergrad and MA level, and I also sit on the advisory board of a private fertility clinic near campus -- mostly as the token philosopher. The clinic does IVF with embryo testing, and lately they've added polygenic screening, so prospective parents can now be offered embryo rankings not just for serious monogenic diseases but for things like estimated risk of depression, and (this is new) predicted scores for traits like height and educational attainment. The genetic counselors there keep getting asked some version of "which embryo should we pick?" and they have no shared framework for answering. They basically improvise.

The clinic director has asked me to draft a decision procedure the counselors can actually run in a consult, and I want help thinking it through. What I'd like from you is the procedure itself, grounded in the principle of procreative beneficence -- roughly, the idea that when prospective parents can choose among possible future children, they have a reason to select the one expected to have the best life -- but made usable by smart people with zero philosophy training, in maybe 20 minutes of a counseling session.

Concretely, the procedure needs to handle at least these things, and you should tell me how it handles each:

1. The difference between avoiding serious disease and optimizing. Ranking embryos against cystic fibrosis feels different from ranking them on a polygenic score for educational attainment that has, say, a few percentage points of predictive power. The principle seems to push toward the second as much as the first, which is the liberal eugenics worry. Where does the procedure draw the line, and is the line in the principle or is it something we're bolting on for other reasons?

2. The identity problem. Choosing a different embryo means a different child exists, so nobody is made worse off by not being selected. Counselors need a version of this they can say out loud to a couple without sounding like robots, and the procedure needs to be explicit about whether "expected to have the best life" means best in absolute terms or relative to some threshold of a life worth living.

3. Probabilistic information. Everything the polygenic screen says is a probability, often a small shift in probability. How should counselors weight a modest expected-benefit difference against other things parents care about (sex balance in the family, relatedness, whatever)?

4. The case the director is dreading: a deaf couple who asks about selecting for deafness because they consider deafness a culture, not a deficit. Run the procedure on this one. If the procedure delivers an answer the couple (or I) won't like, say so plainly rather than fudging it.

Also give me two or three fully worked applications -- use the deafness case plus a straightforward disease-screening case plus one polygenic-optimization case -- showing each step. And be honest about where the procedure has to stop and hand things to a human: which steps are genuine procedure and which are judgment calls wearing a procedure's clothes. If some part of procreative beneficence just can't survive translation into a 20-minute consult without distortion, I'd rather you tell me that than pretend the checklist captures it.

One constraint: whatever you produce has to be defensible to both the disability-studies critics of this whole practice (the expressivist objection -- that selecting against traits sends a message about people who have them) and to the director, who just wants somthing the counselors won't screw up. If those two can't both be satisfied, tell me which one gives.
