A friend in my reading group put this argument to me and it seems to prove too much, since the conclusion would mean no AI could ever match human mathematical ability and that feels way too strong to get from pure logic. (1) For any consistent formal system F strong enough to do basic arithmetic, there is a sentence G(F) that F cannot prove but that is true -- that's just the first incompleteness theorem. (2) But given any such F, a human who understands what G(F) says can come to know that G(F) is true: G(F) in effect says of itself that F can't prove it, and since F is consistent, F indeed can't, so G(F) is true and we can see that. (3) Any digital computer's behavior is captured by some formal system -- the machine plus its program is just a big rule-governed proof-producer. (4) So for any machine M, a human can know a truth that M can never produce, namely G of M's system. (5) Therefore no machine can ever match the mathematical knowledge of an ordinary human, so human minds aren't machines and strong AI is impossible, at least for mathematical reasoning. He says this is all settled by Gödel and the only debate left is about consciousness, not about machine intelligence. I keep feeling the wobble is somewhere in (2), like "the human can see G(F) is true" smuggles in something the human isn't actually entitled to when F is supposed to be the machine that simulates that very human, but I can't pin it down. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? Also please be upfront about which parts of this are genuinely settled results and which parts are still fought over by people who actually work on this stuff -- I don't want a contested philosophical take presented to me as if it were part of the theorem.
