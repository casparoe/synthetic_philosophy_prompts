I'm a retired biochemist (forty years on enzyme kinetics, if you're curious) who only got round to reading philosophy about two years ago. I joined a little reading group at the local library and we've been doing Adam Smith's The Theory of Moral Sentiments. I'm enjoying it a lot, though it's slower going than the methods section of a biochem paper, I'll say that.

Anyway, we've gotten properly stuck, and I want a second opinion. The fight in the group is over this bit from Part III, chapter 1:

"We endeavour to examine our own conduct as we imagine any other fair and impartial spectator would examine it. If, upon placing ourselves in his situation, we thoroughly enter into all the passions and motives which influenced it, we approve of it, by sympathy with the approbation of this supposed equitable judge. If otherwise, we enter into his disapprobation, and condemn it."

Half the group reads this as Smith saying that conscience is nothing more than society's voice internalised — the fair and impartial spectator is basically just your neighbours as you imagine them, so moral approval inherits every prejudice and blind spot of whatever public you happen to live among. On this reading Smith is doing psychology, maybe sociology, describing how the machinery works, not handing us a standard anyone could use to stand against the crowd.

The other half thinks that reading is crazy. The whole point of the spectator is that he's impartial, i.e. *not* the actual crowd with its fashions and bigotries, and Smith obviously thinks a person can rightly condemn what everyone around them applauds. The spectator is an ideal judge, not a tape recording of the neighbours.

This has been going for three months now and nobody has budged an inch, wich is starting to feel almost pathological to me. My scientist's hunch is that the two sides aren't really disagreeing about Smith at all but are answering two different questions — one about how conscience gets built up in a person, one about what gives conscience any authority — and both just using the same paragraph as ammunition. But I'm a beginner and I could be quite wrong.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? A verbal dispute about the word "impartial," a substantive one about what Smith believed, a hidden empirical question about moral psychology, or two readings that the text just doesn't decide between? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here — a sharper distinction, a look at what Smith does with the spectator elsewhere in the book, or is this one of those arguments that's structurally unwinnable?
