I'm prepping an episode of my little philosophy-of-science podcast and my guest is a theoretical physicist turned philosopher who's written a book arguing that mathematical beauty is a genuine guide to truth in fundamental physics -- that symmetry, simplicity, and naturalness aren't just nice to have but are actual evidence a theory is on the right track. Act like a veteran philosophy-of-science interviewer who's read the literature and is a bit skeptical. Think carefully about where this view is weakest before you draft anything; do the brainstorm silently and then give me eight to ten questions.

Cover at least these pressure points, and for each question give me a follow-up keyed to the answer he'll most likely give:

- The LHC came up empty on supersymmetry and all the naturalness-driven predictions people were confident about on aesthetic grounds. Wasn't beauty actively *misleading* there, not just silent? If his answer is that beauty is a fallible heuristic rather than a guarantee, press on whether a heuristic that fails exactly when it matters most still counts as *evidence* rather than just motivation or mood.
- The historical record looks cherry-picked. Everyone cites Dirac, but the aesthetically favored option has failed plenty -- the obsession with perfect circular orbits held astronomy back for centuries, and the ugly ellipse won. If he responds that ellipses turned out to be the deeper kind of beautiful, ask how we can ever distinguish that from just redefining beauty retroactively to fit whatever physics ended up working.
- Renormalizability used to be treated as a non-negotiable aesthetic requirement and then got quietly demoted once effective field theory showed up. If our taste in what's elegant keeps getting rewritten by the physics itself, isn't the arrow of explanation backwards -- physics trains our sense of beauty, not the other way around?
- Beauty pluralism: general relativity people and quantum gravity people seem to have genuinely clashing aesthetic intuitions (smooth geometry vs discreteness, say). If beauty is evidence, whose taste wins and why?
- A deflationary rival hypothesis: maybe beauty just tracks the mathematical structures that are easy for humans to work with or that crop up everywhere, so the correlation with success is explained by our limitations, not by any deep fact about nature.
- End with a question about what observation or experimental result could count *against* the view -- if the answer is nothing, what does that say?

No softball stuff like "what got you interested in physics." Jump straight into the numbered question list, no intro, no preamble, no summarizing at the end.
