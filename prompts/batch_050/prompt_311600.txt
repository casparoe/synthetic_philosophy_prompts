I'm preparing a seminar on the extended mind thesis and want to use Christopher Nolan's Memento as my central case study. Read the film through the lens of Clark and Chalmers's parity principle: Leonard Shelby's tattoos, annotated Polaroids, and case file look like an extreme version of Otto's notebook, so test them against the standard conditions for extended memory, such as constant availability and automatic endorsement. The film seems built to stress those conditions, since entries can be planted by others, and in the film's final revelation Leonard turns out to have falsified his own record, deliberately setting his future self up to kill Teddy. Does this show that his system never counted as part of his mind, or instead that the usual conditions are too weak and that an extended memory needs safeguards against manipulation, including manipulation by one's past self? I'd also like the reading to say where the film resists the philosophy rather than merely illustrating it. Please separate what is broadly agreed in this literature, such as how the parity principle is formulated and the coupling-constitution objection it faces, from what remains genuinely contested, and don't present a contested premise of the reading as settled.
