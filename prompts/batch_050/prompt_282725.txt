I'm applying for an eight-week summer research grant at my university's bioethics center, and the application requires a one-page proposal for a project ending in a paper of roughly 8,000 words. Write the proposal for me.

My half-formed idea is this. Philosophers discuss precommitment in two literatures that barely speak to each other: Ulysses contracts in medical ethics, where a person binds a future self whose judgment they expect to be compromised, and constitutional entrenchment in political theory, where a founding generation binds future majorities it expects to be tempted. I suspect the intrapersonal case is systematically misdescribed when it is treated as a miniature version of the constitutional one. The constitutional case has resources the personal case lacks — amendment procedures, genuinely distinct parties who can renegotiate, the possibility of exit — and the personal case has something the constitutional case lacks: a single valuer whose later preferences, however altered, are still that same person's. The question I want to pursue is which features of a precommitment device are actually doing the justificatory work in each case, and in particular whether the authority of a psychiatric Ulysses contract rests on a claim about the later self's competence (the future self cannot properly weigh things) or on a claim about continuity of values (the future self's considered preferences do not count because they are not really the patient's). If the binding force comes from the competence claim, then a patient whose values genuinely change during illness, rather than merely becoming confused, seems wrongly bound; if it comes from value-continuity, the contract starts to look less like an advance instruction and more like a constitution, complete with the dead-hand worries that comparison invites.

Turn this into a proposal with: a precise statement of the question, framed so that it has a determinate answer; an account of why the two standard framings — honoring the patient's earlier competent values versus deferring to the patient's present wishes whenever some minimal competence threshold is met — leave the question open rather than settling it; the method, meaning what I would actually do across the eight weeks and why conceptual analysis informed by carefully constructed cases rather than, say, a survey of patient attitudes or a straight application of personal-identity theory; the possible conclusions and which considerations would decide between them; what would count as the project failing; and an honest assessment of what could make it too large for the time available. Do not include citations or a bibliography; I will add those myself.
