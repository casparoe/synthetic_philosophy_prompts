I'm on this informal ethics review panel at a community hospital (long story, I got roped in through a friend who does patient advocacy) and we keep getting asked whether patients' consent actually counts in messy situations. We've developed a bit of a track record and I want to pressure-test it. Our past calls: (1) A doctor told a patient he was really enthusiastic about a blood pressure medication when he was privately lukewarm about it; patient agreed to take it — we said valid consent. (2) A man lied to his partner about being free of a transmissible infection and she agreed to unprotected sex — we said her consent was invalid. (3) A wealthy collector offered a destitute man $15,000 for one of his kidneys and the man agreed — we said invalid consent. (4) A manager offered an employee a bonus for covering weekend shifts during a crunch; the employee needed the money for rent and agreed — valid. (5) An executive told his assistant she'd be fired unless she came away with him for a weekend; she agreed — invalid.

Now the new case: an uninsured patient owes the hospital about $40,000, and the hospital offers to forgive the entire debt if she enrolls in a phase II trial of an experimental drug with moderate but honestly disclosed risks. Nothing is hidden from her, nobody threatens to send the debt to collections faster or anything, she just really wants out from under it, so she signs. Did she validly consent?

What I'd like: tell me which way we should rule if we want to stay consistant with ourselves, and which of the earlier cases is the closest analog. I have a feeling our track record is compatible with at least two different principles here — something like "consent fails when someone exploits your desperation or dependence for a lopsided bargain" versus "consent fails when you had no acceptable alternative" — and that they pull apart on this case, since she technically had alternatives (payment plan, bankruptcy) but the hospital is arguably leveraging its position over her. If so, say what each principle would have us decide, what we'd be committing ourselves to by picking either one, and what hypothetical case down the road would finally tell the two principles apart.
