I am the best man at my brother's wedding in six weeks, and I need help writing the toast — roughly five minutes when read aloud, say 650 to 750 words.

The complication is my brother. He spent six years in graduate school defending the thesis that romantic love, properly understood, has no reasons. His argument, which he used to test on me over holiday dinners, goes like this: if you say "I love you because you are kind, brilliant, and funny," then your love is hostage to those qualities. Should you meet someone slightly kinder, slightly funnier, consistency would demand you transfer your affections upward — and nobody thinks love works like that. Worse, should your beloved lose the qualities, your love would lose its license. So, he concluded, reasons are the wrong shape of answer to "why do you love her?" Love is not a judgment that tracks value; it is something more like a decision that creates value in its object. He was fond of pointing out that a parent asked why she loves her child does not produce a list of the child's merits, and that this is not a failure of articulation.

His new wife is, in fact, kind, brilliant, and funny — also stubborn in arguments, catastrophically bad at remembering birthdays (including his), and the only person I have ever seen make him stop lecturing mid-sentence by raising one eyebrow. She has read his dissertation and told me, at the engagement party, that she thinks it is "a lovely theory nobody could survive believing."

So here is the bind the toast has to navigate, and I want the philosophy to do real structural work rather than serve as decoration. A wedding toast is supposed to praise the bride — to say, in effect, here is why she is wonderful and why my brother is lucky. But every straightforward way of doing that walks into his objection: praise her qualities and I have given reasons that would, by his own logic, license trading up the moment someone better-rounded appears. The obvious retreat — say she needs no reasons, that his love is gloriously groundless — is equally bad, because it makes the match sound arbitrary, as if a coin flip could have landed him anyone, and because his wife has publicly rejected the view anyway.

Write the toast so that it threads this. I want it to (1) genuinely praise her, using the specific qualities above rather than generic warmth; (2) acknowledge my brother's argument fairly and get a laugh from it — he will be sitting ten feet away grading me; (3) offer, without naming any philosophers or using jargon a wedding guest would need explained, a resolution the toast actually commits to: some account of how a person can be loved for reasons without being loved replaceably, or else a clear-eyed case that no such account is needed and why that is not the insult it sounds like; and (4) land emotionally for the three-quarters of the room who have never read a word of philosophy, so the resolution has to be carried by stories or images, not by argument alone.

A few craft constraints. Do not mention philosophers by name; the ideas have to survive on their own. Do not use the words "appraisal" or "bestowal." Keep jokes affectionate — the eyebrow line and the birthday line are available material, but she must come out of the toast looking better than anyone. End with a raised glass. And after the toast itself, on a separate final line, state in one sentence the toast's central claim about the relationship between love and reasons, so I can check that the speech and I agree about what it is saying.
