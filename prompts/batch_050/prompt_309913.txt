I teach in a primary school that, like most I know of, has settled on a very characteristic pair of policies. On the one hand, corporal punishment is not merely prohibited but unthinkable; no one on staff would defend striking a child even in principle. On the other hand, the school runs an elaborate positive-incentive economy: stickers and house points for completed work, certificates at assembly for good behavior, a prize at the end of term, gold stars on a wall chart, and letter grades from about age eight. The official line is that we have simply replaced the stick with the carrot, and that this is what progress in education looks like. I recently read Locke's Some Thoughts Concerning Education and was unsettled to find that he rejects both halves of this arrangement. He argues against beating children, but on grounds that have nothing to do with children's rights: beating, he says, produces slavish temperaments and teaches children to love or hate things for the wrong reason, namely the pain attached to them. And he is nearly as dismissive of what he calls the sugar-plum side of the matter — giving children treats and trifles as payment for learning — because it feeds the very appetites an education should be teaching them to master. The only incentives he endorses are reputation: he wants children habituated to care about the esteem of those around them and to feel disgrace keenly, which he takes to be the foundation of virtue.

What I want to understand is what my school's practice commits us to, philosophically, and Locke's critique is my lever into the question. When a classroom bans coercion by pain but then systematically purchases compliance with tokens, prizes, and grades, what view of motivation and of moral formation does that presuppose? Does it assume that the reasons a child acts for don't matter, only that she acts — or that good habits formed for bad reasons will later be held for good ones, or that desire is a fixed input to be managed rather than something education is supposed to shape? Locke clearly holds a stronger view: that the source of a child's motivation is itself a central object of education, so that extrinsic rewards of either sign corrupt even when they work. Is his position internally consistent — he condemns sugar-plums and rods together but embraces esteem and disgrace, which look to me suspiciously like a reward and a punishment wearing social clothes — or is there a principled difference between incentives that operate through the child's standing among others and incentives that operate through pleasure and pain? Finally, taking the modern practice on its own terms: does it strictly presuppose some theory Locke would reject, or is it merely compatible with several theories and underdetermined between them? If you rebuilt an incentive structure around Locke's preference for reputation — no stickers, no grades, but cultivated pride and shame before one's peers — what would concretely have to change in a school like mine, and would the result be recognizable as kinder, or just differently coercive?
