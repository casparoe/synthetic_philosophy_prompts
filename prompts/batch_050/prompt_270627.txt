I am a retired physical chemist who spent forty years working on statistical thermodynamics, and I have only in the last couple of years begun reading philosophy of mind in earnest. I keep running into the position called eliminative materialism: the claim, as I understand it, that beliefs, desires, and the rest of our everyday "folk psychological" vocabulary do not refer to anything real, so that a mature science of the mind will dispense with these categories rather than explain them, much as chemistry dispensed with phlogiston and physics with the ether. Opponents argue instead that beliefs and desires will be preserved in something like the way temperature was preserved -- reduced, not eliminated.

Here is what I would like. Restate this debate, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually command: intertheoretic reduction, bridge principles, approximate reduction and limiting cases, coarse-graining, the difference between a concept that survives in corrected form (temperature as mean kinetic energy) and one that is simply discarded (caloric, vital force), and the criteria scientists in practice used -- rightly or wrongly -- to decide which fate a given concept deserved. In other words, treat "belief" and "desire" as candidate theoretical posits and ask what their trajectory would have to look like, by the standards of my old discipline, to count as reducible versus eliminable, and which trajectory the eliminativists and their critics are each betting on.

Then give me a residue report: which elements of the philosophical dispute translate cleanly into this vocabulary, which translate only with distortion, and which seem to have no counterpart at all. For the last category, I want your judgment on whether the gap shows that my physicochemical vocabulary is missing something essential about minds -- intentionality strikes me as a likely suspect, but I may be wrong -- or whether it instead reveals that part of the philosophical dispute is less substantive than its participants suppose. Please do not quietly import the assumptions of one side and present them as neutral translation; where you must take a stand to make the mapping work, flag it.

Finally, two requests about the epistemic character of your answer. First, indicate how confident you are in the main claims of your translation and your residue report, distinguishing where you are reporting settled features of the debate from where you are exercising your own judgment. Second, single out explicitly the one point in your entire answer that you are least sure of, and briefly explain what would change your mind about it.
