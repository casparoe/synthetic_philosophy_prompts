So I'm grading problem sets for the logic course I TA and a student handed in an argument that I can't find the hole in, and it's been bugging me for a week because the conclusion can't be right. Here's the argument, cleaned up a bit from her version. (1) Take any true disjunction, like "either the butler did it or the gardener did it." From that, you can straight-up infer "if the butler didn't do it, the gardener did." That's just how we reason, detectives do it on TV every week. (2) So whenever ¬A or B is true, the indicative conditional "if A then B" is true. (3) But ¬A or B is just the material conditional A ⊃ B, that's literally its truth table. (4) So indicative conditionals are true exactly when the antecedent is false or the consequent is true -- indicatives are truth-functional. (5) Now test it: "If the moon is made of cheese, my rent is due friday." Antecedent false, so true. "If I flip this coin, it lands heads" and "if I flip this coin, it lands tails" -- one of them is true right now, before I flip, just by luck of the truth table. (6) And it gets worse for the subjunctive case, since "If I had parked in the loading zone, I would have gotten a ticket" and "if I had parked there, nothing would have happened" would both come out true whenever I didn't park there, and those two can't both be true. (7) So either we bite all of this, or... something.

Her conclusion is basically that conditionals are a mess and truth-functionality wins by default, which feels like proving too much -- (5) and (6) are bullets nobody wants to bite. But when I press on each step individually they all look like things I do in my own reasoning. I really want to keep (1), the or-to-if move, because it seems like the most natural inference in the world, so if that's the culprit I want to know what I'm paying for giving it up. Walk me through the options: where's the weakest step, why did it look fine, and what do the standard fixes (Grice's pragmatic story, Lewis/Stalnaker-style accounts, the restrictor view that "if" isn't even a connective) each have to concede to block it? Bonus points if you can tell me which of these fixes saves the detective inference as good reasoning even if it's not strictly valid, since that's the part my students actually care about.
