You are a philosopher of science with working fluency in formal verification, model checking, and system identification. I am a formal-verification engineer with no philosophical training. I am comfortable with trace semantics, observational equivalence, abstraction and refinement, hidden state, test coverage, oracle limitations, assumption–guarantee reasoning, interventions, and statistical identifiability. I am not comfortable with unexplained philosophical jargon. Think carefully, distinguish nearby concepts before translating them, and check that the translation does not quietly turn a philosophical claim into a merely engineering claim.

I want a systematic translation of the underdetermination of theory by evidence into this vocabulary, followed by a report on what does not survive the translation.

Use the following situation as the fixed case study:

A safety-critical controller can be investigated only through executions of its public interface. An execution produces a finite trace containing commanded inputs, reported sensor values, status flags, and actuator commands. The controller may contain hidden state, but investigators cannot inspect that state directly. They can reset the system, choose test inputs, vary timing within specified tolerances, and occasionally induce faults. Every candidate account is packaged as a triple consisting of:

1. a transition model with hidden and observable state;
2. auxiliary assumptions about clocks, sensor delays, resets, fault behavior, and the test environment;
3. a test procedure and oracle that classify traces as successful, failed, or inconclusive.

Two such packages generate exactly the same accepted traces under every test the current laboratory can afford. They explain the observed failures differently: one attributes them to an internal counter, while the other attributes them to interaction between sensor delay and a recovery routine. A third package preserves its original transition model when failures occur by revising its assumptions about the test harness instead.

Please structure the answer as follows.

First, provide a translation dictionary. Render the central underdetermination claims in terms of equivalence classes of model–assumption–test packages, observational equivalence, finite evidence, feasible test budgets, refinement, hidden-state extensions, and identifiability. Cover at least:

- several distinct models being compatible with the same finite body of evidence;
- transient underdetermination that further feasible tests could remove;
- in-principle underdetermination among models with the same observable consequences;
- the Duhem–Quine point that a failed prediction tests a package of theory and auxiliary assumptions rather than an isolated hypothesis;
- the difference between evidential non-uniqueness and the claim that no rational choice among packages is possible;
- appeals to simplicity, explanatory unity, or robustness as possible grounds for preferring one member of an equivalence class.

Be precise about whether the relevant object is a transition model by itself or the whole model–assumption–test package.

Second, analyze the case study through the dictionary. Separate equivalence under the laboratory’s current finite test suite from equivalence under every physically permitted test, and both from stronger equivalence under counterfactual or interventional conditions. Explain what additional evidence might distinguish the first two packages even though their internal states cannot be observed. Then analyze the third package: when is revising the test-harness assumptions a legitimate localization of failure, and when does it become an unfalsifiable immunizing maneuver? Avoid treating the fact that assumptions can always be revised as proof that every revision is epistemically equal.

Third, give the residue report in three explicitly labeled categories:

1. Clean translations: philosophical claims that map naturally onto trace equivalence, identifiability, package-level testing, or related notions.
2. Distorted translations: claims that can be expressed only by narrowing them, strengthening them, or adding assumptions not present in the philosophical debate.
3. Unexpressed residue: claims or questions with no genuine counterpart in the verification vocabulary.

For each distorted or untranslatable item, say whether the residue exposes a limitation of my framework or instead suggests that the original philosophical claim was vague, overly strong, or partly non-empirical. Consider especially empirical adequacy versus truth, the status of hidden structure, the difference between behavioral equivalence and causal organization, theory choice, scientific realism, and the normative force of simplicity.

Fourth, perform a reverse audit. Identify conclusions that the engineering translation makes tempting but that the philosophical underdetermination thesis by itself does not license. For example, consider whether behavioral equivalence makes the distinction between two hidden-state models meaningless, whether finite non-identifiability implies permanent underdetermination, whether equal empirical adequacy makes all theoretical preferences arbitrary, and whether package-level testing abolishes rational failure-localization.

Conclude with a judgment about how much of the underdetermination problem is captured by identifiability and observational equivalence. If something important remains, state whether it is best understood as a problem about truth beyond observable behavior, rational theory choice, the semantics of theoretical claims, or something else. Do not provide citations or a reading list; make the answer self-contained.
