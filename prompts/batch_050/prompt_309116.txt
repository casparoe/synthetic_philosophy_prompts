So last weekend our HOA meeting got heated because there's a proposal to put automatic license plate readers at the two entrances to our neighborhood, plate + timestamp stored for 90 days, accessible to the board and (on request) the police. I said I didn't love it and my neighbor Dave shut me down with "look, if you've got nothing to hide, you've got nothing to fear." Everyone nodded and we moved on and I'm still annoyed about it, partly because I couldn't say on the spot what exactly was wrong with it.

I want you to act as a philosopher who specializes in privacy and surveillance ethics, the kind who's good at taking a dumb-sounding one-liner seriously before dismantling it. Think carefully, and before you commit to anything, brainstorm at least six different precise claims that slogan could be making — then cut it down to the four or five best/most interesting readings and organize your answer in these sections, in this order:

1. The Menu (the bulk of the answer, ~120 words per reading): give each reading a short name, state the precise thesis as a claim that could be true or false (e.g. is it an empirical claim about who surveillance ends up harming, a normative claim about who deserves concealment, a claim that privacy only has instrumental value as a shield for wrongdoing, a claim about whether my unease is even rational?), and for each one give a verdict: platitude, substantive-but-defensible, or just false — with a sentence or two on why. Watch out for readings that quietly slide between "hiding something" and "hiding something wrong," and flag any reading that does that.

2. What the Slogan Needs Here (~150 words): which of these readings would Dave actually need to be true for his one-liner to settle the license plate reader question, specifically? Keep in mind the system records metadata (who comes and goes, when) rather than content, and notice which of your readings treat that distinction as doing real work and which don't.

3. What Dave Probably Meant (~80 words): your best guess at which reading was actually doing the work in his head, and whether he'd still endorse it if stated baldly.

4. My Comeback (~100 words): a short, non-smug thing I can actually say at the next meeting that targets the reading from section 3, not a strawman of it.
