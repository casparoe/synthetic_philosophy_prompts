I am a philosophy professor putting together the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate course on contemporary moral theory, and I need a question on Scanlon's contractualism that I cannot simply write myself this year because several students have seen my old exams circulating.

Act as a professional moral philosopher with long experience examining on contractualist ethics. Before writing anything final, take a moment to brainstorm internally about where students most often go wrong on this material, and let that reflection shape the question you design.

Here is the situation I want the question to probe. Scanlon's contractualism grounds wrongness in what no one could reasonably reject, and the standard objection is that it cannot aggregate: because the test proceeds person by person, it seems to imply we must save one person from death rather than save a million people from headaches, since no single headache-sufferer's complaint outweighs the dying person's complaint. My students all know this objection as a slogan. What almost none of them can do is say precisely which feature of the contractualist test generates the result, distinguish the objection from the structurally similar complaint that contractualism cannot justify rescuing the greater number when everyone stands to lose the same thing, or evaluate Scanlon's own attempts to let the number affected matter without collapsing the view into a form of aggregation that loses the person-by-person structure.

Write one essay question that a strong student could answer in 75 minutes. It must be unanswerable by reciting the reasonable-rejection formula plus the standard objection. In particular, it should force the candidate to do at least the following: explain why the individualist restriction on complaints is what blocks aggregation rather than some other feature of the view; say whether a contractualist can coherently hold that numbers count when the claims at stake are equal in strength while denying that many small claims can outweigh one large one; and take a position, with reasons, on whether the resulting view is an unstable halfway house between strict pairwise comparison and full aggregation or a principled alternative to both.

After the question, provide:

1. A model answer of roughly 700 words, written at the level of the best student you have taught, taking a definite stand rather than surveying options.
2. A marking scheme out of 25 marks, built around five must-hit points. For each point, state the marks it carries and add one sentence describing the specific way candidates typically fumble it -- for example, treating the equal-stakes rescue case and the one-death-versus-many-headaches case as raising the same difficulty, or assuming that any concession to numbers makes the view rule-consequentialist in disguise.
3. One short paragraph telling me what an answer that merely summarises the view without engaging the crux should score, and why.

Finally, and this matters to me: throughout the model answer and marking scheme, clearly separate what is settled ground -- the structure of Scanlon's test, what the standard objection says -- from what is genuinely contested among philosophers who work on contractualism, such as whether the equal-stakes cases can be handled without smuggling in aggregation or whether Scanlon's own response succeeds. Do not present a contested verdict as though it were the consensus of the literature; where the model answer takes a side, have it acknowledge the live opposition.
