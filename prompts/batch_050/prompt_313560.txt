I am writing an essay on Robert Zemeckis's Cast Away (2000) and want a philosophical reading of it. A FedEx engineer stranded alone on an island converts the contents of washed-up packages into tools: ice-skate blades become knives and an instrument for pulling a bad tooth, the netting of a dress becomes a fishing net, videotape is unspooled for rope, and a volleyball becomes Wilson. Read the film through Heidegger's distinction between readiness-to-hand and presence-at-hand and his account of equipment breakdown in Being and Time. The tension I most want explored is this: for Heidegger, a tool is what it is only within a totality of involvements sustained by practices and a social world of others, yet Chuck's island has no workshop, no shared practices, no one else at all. Does the film show equipmentality surviving the collapse of that totality, or does it depict something Heidegger's analysis would have to deny is possible — a world of equipment with no with-world? Work through at least the fire-making sequence as a case of breakdown rendering things conspicuous, Wilson as an object never absorbed into use yet hardly merely present-at-hand, and the unopened package with the angel wings, deliberately withheld from use to serve as a symbol. Then ask whether the return home — lighters that work at a flick, ice on demand, frictionless abundance — vindicates or complicates the claim that breakdown is what reveals the world. Be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the film to an illustration of the text, and say plainly where the film resists the Heideggerian reading and what it adds that the analysis alone does not contain.
