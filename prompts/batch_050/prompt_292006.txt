I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and over the past several years I have watched the same controversy recur with almost mechanical regularity: a prestige biopic or streaming drama about real, mostly dead people is released; historians and surviving relatives object to specific invented scenes; the showrunners respond that they are making drama, not documentary; critics split; the cycle resets with the next release. I have interviewed screenwriters, biographers, and family members of the portrayed, and what strikes me is that nobody's position ever changes and nobody's argument ever lands. The defenders say some version of: fiction has always taken liberties, emotional truth matters more than factual accuracy, and no reasonable viewer confuses the two. The objectors say some version of: these were real people, the invented scenes will become the public record in practice, and there is something indecent about putting words and deeds into a dead person's mouth for entertainment. Both claims seem true, and that is precisely the problem.

Here is my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the debate is stalled because the two sides are not actually disagreeing about a single question. I think there are at least three distinct harms in play — a possible harm to the dead person themselves (reputation, dignity, or whatever posthumous interests one believes in), a harm to the living (descendants, friends, communities whose history is being rewritten), and an epistemic harm to the audience (the way a vivid dramatization quietly becomes what the public 'knows') — and that the defenders and objectors are each tracking a different one. Defenders tend to argue as if the only candidate harm is harm to the dead, which they take to be impossible or negligible; objectors tend to argue as if the epistemic harm to audiences settles everything. If that is right, the two sides could both be correct about their own preferred harm and never touch each other. But I may be flattening real substantive disagreements — about whether the dead can be wronged at all, about what fiction owes to fact, about whether viewers are as gullible as the objectors assume — into a mere case of talking past one another.

So my question is diagnostic, not verdict-seeking: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a disguised metaphysical dispute about posthumous harm, a disguised empirical dispute about how audiences process dramatized history, a dispute about artistic norms and who owns a life story, or several of these at once under one heading? Please engage with what each side actually says rather than with caricatures, test my three-harms hypothesis against the debate as it really plays out, and — given your diagnosis — tell me what would count as progress: a distinction that would sort the cases, an empirical finding that would change one side's mind, a conceptual argument that would close off a position, or a reason to think no progress is available because the disagreement bottoms out in intuitions neither side can argue for.
