So I'm in a 3rd year metaphysics seminar and somehow got voluntold to run one of the weekly discussion hours for our reading group (about 14-16 people, 75 minutes, seminar room with movable chairs and a whiteboard). The week I got assigned is on the identity of indiscernibles and Max Black's two-spheres counterexample. Our prof had us read Leibniz's Monadology, and in sections 8 and 9 Leibniz argues (this is my paraphrase not a quote) that every monad must have some qualities, and that in nature there are never two beings that are perfectly alike -- there is always some internal difference to be found, so two things can never differ only numerically. Basically: if x and y have ALL the same qualities, they're not two things, they're one thing.

The problem: everyone in my group nods along when Leibniz says this, and then nods along when Black says but imagine a universe containing only two exactly similar spheres some distance apart -- and nobody seems to notice they've now endorsed contradictory things?? Like they've read both texts as word clouds and felt no tension at all. I want to run an activity where they actually get stuck, where the tension happens TO them in the room before I name it.

Design me something runnable: rules, setup, any props (nothing expensive, I have index cards, coins, ping pong balls, tape). Ideally something where participants first try to single out / refer to / pick one of two objects and discover their descriptions keep failing to discriminate, and then we escalate the symmetry until we're basically staging Black's universe with actual people standing in it. Points or a small prize mechanic would help them take it seriously but it can't need real money. Also:

- a debrief script that maps specific moments in the activity onto the specific philosophical moves (qualitative vs numerical identity, whether spatial relations like "two feet apart" are themselves qualities the spheres share, whether "the one on the left" smuggles in a reference to us)
- what will predictably go wrong when I run it (I suspect people will cheat the symmetry by using where they're standing -- I kind of want them to, so we can talk about it, but tell me how to handle it)
- be honest about which part of Black's case the activity can't reproduce. Like, his spheres supposedly think -- every thought in one is in the other -- and I don't see how to simulate THAT with undergrads, so tell me what I have to just supply verbally instead

One more thing: at some point I want to bring it back to the Leibniz paraphrase above and force the question of whether his claim is supposed to be a necessary truth or just a contingent fact about our actual world, because I think the activity will push people toward treating it as contingent and I want to use that. Work that in somewhere.
