A colleague of mine in the law school has been leaning on an analogy in our recurring argument about campus speech codes, and I want to stress-test it before our next department lunch turns into round four. His line is that regulating certain kinds of hateful or harassing speech on campus is like regulating secondhand smoke: speech is a byproduct of someone's private activity that drifts into shared space and measurably harms third parties who never consented to the exposure, so the university banning it is not censorship at all but environmental hygiene -- the same category as clean-air rules in a dorm. He says this dissolves the usual Millian objection, because Mill's harm principle itself licenses restricting conduct that harms others, and on his picture hateful speech is just harmful conduct in a cloud. He also claims the analogy defuses the free-speech trump card, since nobody thinks a smoking ban is a blow against free inquiry.

I think the analogy is doing more work than it can bear, but I'm struggling to say exactly where it snaps. The obvious disanalogies seem to be: smoke harms you whether or not you pay attention to it, whereas most alleged speech-harm routes through the audience actually taking up a message; smoke has no propositional content, so banning it restricts no idea, whereas the speech rules he wants are unavoidably content-based; exposure to smoke is involuntary in a way that exposure to an idea arguably isn't, since you can argue back, leave, or counter it; and the dose-response picture (cumulative, invisible, no safe threshold) may fit some speech harms and badly misfit others, like offense. But I'm not sure which of these actually matter for his conclusion, and which are just places where any analogy is imperfect. There's also a wrinkle he throws back at me: Mill himself seemed fine restricting speech in cases like an excited crowd outside a corn-dealer's house, so maybe Mill's own principle is already more like the smoke model than free-speech absolutists admit.

So here's the task. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the smoker, the smoke, the bystander, the room, the ban, the dose. Then go through the disanalogies and sort them into ones that are fatal to the intended use (licensing content-based restriction without it counting as censorship) versus ones that are merely untidy. Tell me whether his conclusion -- campus speech codes are hygiene, not censorship, and so don't even raise the Millian alarm -- survives once the real breaks are on the table, and if it doesn't, propose a better analogy from public health or elsewhere that could carry what he actually needs, or say why no analogy can. End your reply with a last line of the form Verdict: followed by one of survives, survives-in-part, or fails, plus at most ten words of gloss.
