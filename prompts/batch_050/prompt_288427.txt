So I'm on a sort of citizen advisory panel for our city council (it's less fancy than it sounds lol) and we've been asked to look at four election reforms: (1) making voting compulsory with a small fine if you don't, (2) lowering the voting age to 16 for local elections, (3) letting non-citizen residents vote locally after, say, three years of living here, and (4) this one's a wildcard a member threw in -- giving extra votes to people with higher education qualifications.

In prep I've been reading chapter VIII of Mill's Considerations on Representative Government ("Of the Extension of the Suffrage"), and it's messing with my head a bit. Mill's whole line there, as I read it, is that voting isn't really a private right you exercise for yourself the way free speech is -- it's an exercise of power *over other people*, more like holding an office. And from that he concludes stuff that sounds shocking today: everyone should have a voice, sure, but not necessarily an *equal* voice; he'd happily exclude people who can't read or write or do basic arithmetic, and even people living on poor relief, because (paraphrasing hard here) if you can't support yourself you shouldn't get a say in disposing of other people's money. Meanwhile he'd give plural votes to the educated, since the point of the franchise isn't fairness between voters but getting the decision made competently.

Here's what I want from you: take that core principle -- the vote is a trust/power over others, so its distribution should track competence and stake rather than equal entitlement -- and rank all four proposals above by how well they hold up if you take the principle seriously. Give each one a score out of 10 and a few sentences of reasoning. I'm especially interested in the non-obvious moves: does the principle actually back compulsory voting (forcing the disengaged to exercise a "trust" they don't want?), does it cut *for* votes at 16 in some ways and against in others, and does it maybe support non-citizen residents more strongly than equal-rights talk does, since they clearly have a stake?

Then, separately: point to the one spot where you think Mill's principle collapses under its own weight when applied to a modern electorate -- like, where does the competence-tracking logic break down or eat itself? I'd rather have a ranking with a clear flaw identified than a wishy-washy "it depends on your values" answer.
