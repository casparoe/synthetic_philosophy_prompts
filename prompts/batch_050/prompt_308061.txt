John Stuart Mill died in 1873, well over a century before philosophers began to argue in print that the state should license parents. Suppose, counterfactually, that Mill were alive today and asked to take a position on two ideas from contemporary applied ethics. The first is the proposal, associated in the literature above all with Hugh LaFollette, that people should be required to obtain a state license before raising a child: we license drivers, physicians, and adoptive parents on the ground that incompetent performance predictably harms third parties, and grossly incompetent parenting harms children at least as reliably. The second is the idea, associated above all with Joel Feinberg, that children have a right to an open future — a right against having their adult options drastically foreclosed by their upbringing, even where nothing amounting to abuse or neglect has occurred, as when a religious community educates children only enough to keep them within the community.

I would like a careful reconstruction of the position Mill would most plausibly take on each of these proposals, and of where the reconstruction is forced to guess. Please work from the actual texture of On Liberty rather than from its popular reputation. Readers who know Mill only as the author of the harm principle tend to be startled by the essay's final chapter, in which he describes bringing a child into existence without a fair prospect of being able to provide for its body and mind as a moral crime against both the offspring and society, and in which he accepts that the Continental laws of his day forbidding marriage to people who could not show the means of supporting a family did not exceed the legitimate powers of the state. The same chapter insists that the state may compel parents to educate their children while itself staying as far as possible out of the business of providing schooling, and warns that a general state education is a contrivance for molding people to a single model. And the essay's first chapter explicitly excludes children, and anyone whose faculties are not yet mature, from the protection of the liberty principle. An adequate answer has to take all of these materials seriously, not merely the ones convenient to a preferred verdict.

Three things would be especially valuable. First, the reconstruction itself: does the procreation passage commit Mill to something like prior administrative vetting of would-be parents, or only to enforcing an antecedent duty whose violation can be addressed after the fact — and could he accept the licensing analogy with drivers and doctors, given the utilitarian rather than rights-based grounds of his liberty principle? Second, the friction points: for Mill, a child is another person, so a parent's conduct toward a child is never the self-regarding conduct that the harm principle shields; yet his fear of uniformity and of state-manufactured character pulls hard against any right of the child that would be enforced through compulsory, state-supervised schooling. Work out whether his position on education already settles this conflict or merely relocates it. Third, throughout the answer, keep three epistemic strata cleanly separated: what Mill actually wrote, which is textually settled; how the apparently conflicting passages are best reconciled, which is genuinely contested among interpreters of Mill; and what follows for proposals he never encountered, which is irreducibly speculative. Do not present a contested reconciliation, or your own extrapolation, as the consensus reading of the text; where serious scholars of Mill diverge, say so, and indicate what turns on the divergence.
