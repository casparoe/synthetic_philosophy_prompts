So I've been going down a Scanlon rabbit hole lately and it got me thinking about the old torture-vs-dust-specks debates from the LW days. Quick setup for context: Scanlon's contractualism says an act is wrong if it would be ruled out by principles nobody could reasonably reject, and his so-called individualist restriction says you can't pool lots of tiny burdens spread across different people into one big complaint — each person's complaint stands on its own. Which seems to imply that if you can save one person from a broken leg, or save a million people from one mild headache each, the headache crowd just has no standing to complain, and you go fix the leg.

I want you to construct a thought experiment that puts real pressure on this. Specifically: build a case where the individualist restriction delivers a verdict that even a sympathetic contractualist should feel queasy about, but without leaning on absurd sci-fi numbers — no googolplex dust specks, no torture, definately no utility monsters. Make it a choice someone could plausibly face in real life: a hospital administrator allocating a budget, a city official setting a safety standard, something like that. Constraints: the harm to each of the many has to be genuinely small but genuinely nonzero, the choice has to be forced (no clever third option where you fix both), the numbers should stay mundane (dozens to thousands, not astronomical), and the single person with the big complaint has to be symmetric with the many in every morally relevant respect except complaint size.

Give me your answer as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro or conclusion: (1) the scenario itself; (2) the verdict strict contractualism gives and why it gives it; (3) the verdict straight aggregative consequentialism gives and why; (4) which single structural feature of your case is doing the work, and whether removing it collapses the disagreement; (5) the best move a contractualist could make to dodge the queasy verdict, and whether that move actually works or just relocates the problem one step over. American English, please.
