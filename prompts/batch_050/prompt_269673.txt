Eight months ago, in the middle of an otherwise ordinary workday, I underwent what my psychiatrist later identified as an episode of depersonalization: for roughly half an hour there was plainly experience — the hum of the air conditioning, my own hands on the keyboard, a mounting dread — yet none of it felt like mine, and the familiar sense of being someone to whom things happen was simply missing. Trying to understand what happened to me (or what failed to happen to anyone), I have been reading Thomas Metzinger's self-model theory, according to which the self is not an entity but a phenomenal self-model: a representation the brain operates with but cannot recognize as a representation, which is precisely why it presents itself as a thing rather than as a model. On this view, depersonalization, out-of-body experiences, and certain meditative states are windows onto the constructed nature of what we ordinarily mistake for the subject.

Write a dialogue between a defender of Metzinger's position and a phenomenologically minded critic who concedes that every bit of self-content is indeed modeled, yet maintains that experience possesses an irreducible first-personal givenness — a for-me-ness — that no self-model theory explains and that no episode of self-dissolution ever actually removes. The two should be evenly matched: neither a naïf nor a mouthpiece, and each must respond to what the other actually says rather than to a prepared script. Over the course of the exchange, the Metzingerian should press the question of what this givenness could possibly be other than one more layer of representational content, and the critic should press the question of whose model the self-model is supposed to be if there is genuinely no one at home. Each should also be forced, at some point, to say what my episode was an episode of — and for whom it occurred. Conclude with the two either isolating the exact premise on which they disagree or conceding, with reasons, that they cannot.
