I've been chewing on Taurek's question -- should the numbers count? -- for years, and I keep finding that my case-by-case verdicts don't obviously cohere. I'd like you to take my verdicts below and tell me what principle, if any, lies behind them, or tell me which verdict I have to abandon.

Case 1: I can steer a rescue boat to one of two rocks. Five strangers are stranded on one, one stranger on the other; everyone not reached will die. My verdict: save the five, straightforwardly, and flipping a coin to give the lone person a 50% chance would be monstrous.

Case 2: Same setup, but the lone person is my spouse. My verdict: saving my spouse is permissible, maybe even obligatory, even though the five still die.

Case 3: One dose of a drug remains. Giving it all to patient A certainly saves A's life. Splitting it five ways gives each of B, C, D, E, F a 20% chance of survival and leaves A to die. My verdict: give the whole dose to A. Five 20% chances don't seem to add up to one life.

Case 4: A runaway trolley will kill one person on the main track; I can divert it onto a siding where it will kill five. My verdict: diverting is wrong, and not merely because of some doing-versus-allowing distinction -- the numbers simply aren't a reason here in the way they are in Case 1, and I can't articulate why the two cases differ.

Case 5: I can either save one person's life or cure a thousand people of a persistent but mild headache. My verdict: save the life, and the number of headaches is simply irrelevant -- there is no number large enough.

Case 6: Equal numbers on both rocks, all strangers, and I genuinely cannot reach both. My verdict: now flipping a coin is exactly right, and just arbitrarily picking one side would be a (small) wrong against the other.

So: I want the numbers to count in Case 1, to be defeasible by partiality in Case 2, to not aggregate probabilistically in Case 3, to stop counting in Case 4, to not aggregate across different magnitudes of harm in Case 5, and to license a fair lottery only in Case 6. Is there a single principle about what we owe each individual that delivers all six verdicts, or at least five of them? If so, state it precisely, tell me which clause each case motivates, and tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: a weighted lottery in Case 1, where the lone stranger gets a 1/6 chance of being rescued instead of none. If no such principle exists, tell me which of my verdicts is most likely the mistaken one and argue for discarding it rather than multiplying clauses.
