So I've been chewing on HCH — Christiano's setup where a human answers a hard question by consulting copies of itself, which consult copies of themselves, on down the tree — and the factored cognition hypothesis underneath it, i.e. the claim that basically all cognitive labor can be chopped into pieces small enough that short-lived agents, each handling one tiny piece and passing results upward, can collectively match what a much smarter continuous reasoner would do. I'm interested in whether this is even a coherent idealization of deliberation.

Objections I already know about, so dont bother repeating these or close variants:

1. Holistic cognition: some thinking (mathematical taste, sensing whether a proof idea is promising, humor, design sense) doesn't seem to factor into independent subtasks at all.
2. Context-transfer overhead: the nodes only get short messages, so the tacit background a real deliberator carries can't be handed down cheaply — either you write a giant manual or the sub-agents work half-blind.
3. Error compounding: mistakes at lower nodes flow upward and the root can't necessarily detect them, and the deeper the tree the worse it gets.
4. The decomposition bottleneck: breaking the problem into sub-questions is often itself the hard cognitive step, so the scheme presupposes at the root the very insight it's supposed to produce.
5. Distillation alignment: in practice you'd swap the human copies for learned models (iterated amplification), and those can be subtly wrong, Goodharted, or adversarial in ways the overseer can't check.

Give me one more objection that is NOT on this list — not a rewording, special case, or combination of the five. Briefly explain how it differs from each of them. Then be honest about whether it's actually any good: does it threaten factored cognition as an idealization of deliberation (the theoretical claim), or only HCH as a practical scheme, or neither? If it turns out to be weak, say so and say why.
