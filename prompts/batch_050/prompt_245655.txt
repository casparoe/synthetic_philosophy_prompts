Last night a close friend and I ended up arguing about AI safety until nearly two in the morning, and although I held my ground at the time, her parting remark — that my position is three separate views stapled together rather than one coherent view — has been nagging at me. I would like an honest audit of my commitments before I see her again.

Some stipulative context, so we are talking about the same thing. By "control" I mean the family of safety measures designed to work even if the deployed model is actively misaligned: restricting which high-stakes actions it can take, having its outputs checked by a weaker but trusted model, subjecting a random sample of its consequential behavior to intensive auditing, and adversarially red-teaming the whole arrangement. My friend's position is that anything short of genuine alignment is security theater, because a sufficiently capable schemer will route around any protocol devised by its inferiors. I think she is wrong, but I want to know whether I can consistently think so.

Here is what I find myself committed to:

1. For the next several generations of frontier models, we cannot rule out scheming, so the safety case for any deployment must not assume the model is aligned; it must survive the assumption that the model is adversarial.

2. A control protocol of the kind described above is nonetheless sufficient, at current capability levels, to justify deploying an untrusted model internally, even granting (1).

3. The main reason to accept the residual risk in (2) is that untrusted models should be put to work on alignment and safety research itself, where their labor buys down future risk faster than it adds present risk.

4. Labs therefore need not slow capability progress in the near term; control, supplemented by model-assisted safety research, is an adequate substitute for solved alignment at the current frontier.

5. On this basis, I would approve letting next year's flagship model conduct largely autonomous machine-learning research inside a lab under such a protocol, and I would not approve giving the same model open internet access under any protocol.

My friend insists that (2) and (4) contradict (1) on their face, and I suspect her objection trades on conflating trusting a model's motives with relying on its outputs — but I cannot fully articulate the distinction, and I am less sure than I was at midnight that the rest of the set is innocent.

So: which of these commitments genuinely stand in tension, and which only appear to? Where the tension is genuine, order the problems by how much damage they do to the position overall, and say for each what would have to be surrendered to resolve it. Where the conflict is merely apparent, show why it dissolves. Finally, if consistency requires revision, which single commitment can be weakened at the smallest cost to the whole, and do my verdicts in (5) survive that revision?
