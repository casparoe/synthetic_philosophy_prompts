You are both a metaphysician specializing in personal and artifact identity and a professional satirist. Write a deadpan mock report, approximately 700–900 words, issued by the Office of Maritime Continuity after inspecting a museum’s celebrated Ship of Theseus. The ship has been repaired one component at a time until none of its original wood remains; meanwhile, the removed pieces have been carefully preserved and assembled by a rival institution into a second ship. Both museums claim to possess the authentic vessel. The report should take the form of an absurdly meticulous bureaucratic finding—complete with inspection criteria, inventory procedures, stakeholder testimony, and an official recommendation—rather than an ordinary essay or narrative.

The satire should target our habit of treating identity as if it were an administrable property: something that can be preserved through paperwork, labels, chains of custody, certificates, and majority votes. Let the humor arise from genuine philosophical pressure points, including spatiotemporal continuity, continuity of function, material composition, historical provenance, gradual versus sudden replacement, vagueness, and the possibility of branching. Include at least one invented expert who argues that the continuously repaired ship is authentic, one who favors the reconstructed original materials, and one administrator who proposes a procedurally tidy but philosophically disastrous compromise. Do not turn the piece into a lecture; the officials should sincerely believe that enough forms, measurements, and committee hearings can settle the matter.

Remain internally consistent and avoid simply announcing that identity is conventional or mysterious. The reader should be able to infer which real intuitions each bureaucratic criterion expresses, notice where those intuitions conflict, and feel why the branching case resists an easy verdict. End with an official decision that is superficially decisive but actually exposes the inadequacy of the office’s framework. No bibliography, citations, or philosophical name-dropping: the piece should be intelligible to a general reader while rewarding someone familiar with debates over the Ship of Theseus.
