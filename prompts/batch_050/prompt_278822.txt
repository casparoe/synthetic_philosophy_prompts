A colleague and I have formulated a causal-exclusion argument that appears to prove far more than the familiar claim that mental states are causally inert:

1. If an effect has a sufficient physical cause at a time, then no distinct property instantiated at that time causes the effect, except in cases of genuine causal overdetermination.
2. Every bodily movement has a sufficient microphysical cause.
3. A desire or intention is not identical with any one microphysical condition, since the same mental state can be realized by different neural and microphysical states.
4. Ordinary action is not a case of genuine overdetermination: the movement would not have two independent sufficient causes in the way a death can be caused by two simultaneously fatal wounds.
5. Therefore, desires and intentions do not cause bodily movements.
6. But premises parallel to 1–4 hold for chemical, biological, geological, economic, and other multiply realizable properties.
7. Therefore, no special-science property ever causes a physical event; only microphysical conditions do.

We find the conclusion incredible, but replacing premise 3 with type identity seems to abandon multiple realizability, while rejecting premise 1 seems to abandon the physical causal closure that motivated the argument. Identify the premise or inference that should be rejected, explain why it initially seemed plausible, and compare the costs of at least three responses, such as property or trope identity, a suitable notion of causal sufficiency, counterfactual or interventionist accounts of causation, and treating higher-level causes as distinct from but realized by lower-level causes. Please do not merely name positions; show which specific step each response blocks and what it must concede.
