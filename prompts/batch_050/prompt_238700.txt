I am a social scientist by training and have, over the years, become a committed methodological individualist. The anchor for my commitment is a passage from Max Weber's Economy and Society (1922), in the first chapter, where he sets out how sociology should handle talk about collective entities. After noting that we routinely speak of social collectivities — states, associations, business corporations, foundations — as if they were individual persons, Weber writes:

"in sociological work these collectivities must be treated as solely the resultants and modes of organization of the particular acts of individual persons, since these alone can be treated as agents in a course of subjectively understandable action"

I have always read this as more than a stylistic guideline. On my reading, Weber is saying that any adequate explanation of a social phenomenon must bottom out in the actions of individuals, and that the privilege of the individual level is earned by the fact that only individuals have motives we can interpret from the inside. Macro-level claims that cannot, even in principle, be cashed out in terms of what individuals were doing and why are at best placeholders and at worst mystifications.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure: what would count against it, taken as seriously as I take the position itself.

Please address at least the following, and rank what you find from decisive to merely awkward:

1. Empirical and formal results. What kinds of findings would genuinely threaten the view? I have in mind candidates such as macro-level regularities that appear robust across wild variation in the individuals composing the system, or simulation results in which the aggregate pattern is insensitive to nearly everything about the agents. Does the mere existence of such patterns refute Weber's precept, or only its usefulness? What is the difference between showing that individual-level mechanisms exist (which individualists say is enough) and showing that reference to them is required for understanding (which seems to be Weber's actual claim)?

2. The constitution of individuals. The strongest objection I encounter is that individuals' motives, categories, and options are themselves formed by the collectivities they belong to, so that explanation in terms of individual action merely pushes the holism back a step. Does this objection touch Weber's position as stated, or does it only touch a stronger ontological version he never held? Would Weber have to concede anything here, and if so, what?

3. The interpretive warrant. Weber's reason for privileging individuals is that their action is subjectively understandable in a way a state's "action" is not. What would have to be true about interpretation, or about collective agents, for that warrant to fail? If it turned out that our understanding of individual motives is itself a culturally loaded projection — no more direct than our understanding of institutions — does the doctrine collapse, or survive in a weakened form?

4. Unfalsifiability. When challenged, defenders of the view tend to retreat to the claim that mechanisms at the individual level must exist in principle, even if we never spell them out. That retreat seems to make the position risk nothing. Is that a fair criticism, and is it fatal? In the same passage's vicinity Weber insists that an individualistic method implies nothing about an individualistic system of values, which suggests he wanted the doctrine insulated from political dispute — but a doctrine insulated from everything may be insulated from evidence too. Tell me honestly whether the position I hold has been qualified into something that no finding could dislodge, and if so, whether that matters for a methodological precept as opposed to an empirical theory.

Finally: if the position does fail, tell me what the strongest version of the rival looks like — not a straw holism that believes in group minds, but the minimal departure from Weber's precept that a finding or argument would actually license.
