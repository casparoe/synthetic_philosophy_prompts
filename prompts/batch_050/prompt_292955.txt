Question:
(a) Explain the difference between decision under risk and decision under ignorance.
(b) State the maximin rule and the minimax regret rule precisely, and illustrate each with a small payoff table in which the two rules recommend different acts.
(c) Explain why Rawls's original position is often associated with maximin reasoning, while Harsanyi's defense of average utilitarianism treats the parties' situation as one of risk.
(d) Which treatment of the parties' epistemic situation is more defensible, and why?

Student's answer:

(a) Under risk, the decision-maker can assign probabilities to the possible states of the world; under ignorance, she knows which states are possible but has no basis for assigning probabilities to them. Expected utility theory applies straightforwardly only under risk.

(b) Maximin says: rank each act by its worst possible outcome and choose the act whose worst outcome is best. Minimax regret says: for each act and each state, compute the regret — the difference between the payoff the act yields in that state and the best payoff any available act yields in that state — then choose the act whose maximum regret is smallest. Consider two acts over two states: in state s1, A gives 4 and B gives 6; in state s2, A gives 2 and B gives 1. Maximin selects A (worst case 2 rather than 1). The best available payoff in s1 is 6 and in s2 is 2, so A's regrets are 2 and 0 (maximum 2) and B's are 0 and 1 (maximum 1); minimax regret therefore selects B. A further merit of minimax regret is menu-independence: since regret is measured state by state, adding a third act to the choice set cannot reverse the ranking of two acts already present.

(c) Behind the veil of ignorance the parties know nothing of their place in society, so they cannot assign probabilities to occupying any particular position. Rawls's position is that maximin is the rational rule for choice under complete ignorance generally, and the difference principle is simply that general rule applied to the basic structure. Harsanyi instead holds that rationality requires the parties to assign equal probability to being each person in society; this converts the choice into one under risk, and maximizing expected utility then yields average utilitarianism. The Rawls–Harsanyi dispute therefore reduces to an empirical disagreement about which probability assignment the parties would in fact make.

(d) Rawls's treatment is more defensible. The parties are choosing institutions that will shape their entire life prospects, with no opportunity to choose again if they end up at the bottom; gambling on expected utility, as Harsanyi requires, is out of place when the stakes are that high and the choice is irreversible.
