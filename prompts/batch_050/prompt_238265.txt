I am writing a paper on what, if anything, adult children owe their parents, organized around Jane English's friendship model — the view that grown children owe their parents not repayment of a debt for past sacrifices but roughly what friends owe one another, grounded in an ongoing relationship of mutual care, so that where no such relationship exists or survives, little is owed. I want to pressure-test five candidate grounds of filial obligation against that model: first, gratitude for sacrifices made during one's upbringing; second, the duties of an ongoing friendship, as English proposes; third, a duty generated by the parent's current vulnerability together with the child's unique position to relieve it; fourth, an implicit reciprocity norm created by accepting substantial parental support into adulthood, such as university tuition; fifth, a bare role-based duty attaching to the parent-child relationship as such, independent of its history or quality. To make the comparison sharp, evaluate each ground against three test cases I have constructed to isolate different variables: the dutiful but cold parent, who met every material obligation without affection and with whom the adult child now has no relationship; the parent with advanced dementia who no longer recognizes the child, so that the relationship survives, if at all, only in memory; and the parent who abandoned the family and reappears, destitute, after thirty years of silence. Please rank the five grounds from most to least defensible as the primary basis of filial duty, judging each by how many of the three test cases it handles without either licensing no obligations where there intuitively are some or fabricating obligations where there intuitively are none, and explain in a few sentences each why it lands where it does, including where English's own view ends up and whether any of the cases breaks it. Conclude by identifying which single ground a pluralist could most plausibly retain in only a secondary or backup role, and which ground does the most work that the friendship model cannot. Write the answer in continuous prose, as in a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
