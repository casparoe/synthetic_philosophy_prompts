For my school's literature magazine, I'd like a short story of 800–1,200 words that dramatizes the idea that a self is not a thing inside us but something like a center of gravity: an abstract point fixed by the stories told about a person rather than by anything they privately are. Set the story in a town where, by long custom, every resident has a publicly kept account of their character and deeds, updated by their neighbors and read aloud each birthday, and where this account — not memory or intention — is treated as who someone really is. The protagonist, recovering from an illness that erased their memories, finds that the town's account of them describes someone they cannot recognize, and must choose between growing into the written self, fighting to rewrite it, or trying to live with no settled story at all. Complicate this with the figure of the account-keeper, who writes everyone else's story but whose own account has always been left blank. The philosophical tension should emerge from what the characters do and decide, not from any speech about the nature of the self. Begin the response with the story itself, with no preface or commentary.
