I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on contemporary moral theory (14 students, one 80-minute session), and we're spending a week on moral particularism versus generalism. The reading has done its job at the level of argument: the students can reconstruct the claim that reasons are holistic -- that a consideration which counts in favor of an action in one context can count against it, or be no reason at all, in another -- and they can state the generalist's reply, that reasons have a default polarity that context merely modifies or defeats. What they cannot do is feel why anyone would need holism in the first place. To them it looks like an overreaction to ordinary context-sensitivity, and I want to break that confidence before I let them see it stated.

Design a structured in-class activity whose central mechanic forces students to discover holism in their own judgments before the term is introduced. Concretely, I want something where each student first commits -- in writing, on the record -- to the polarity of a small set of morally relevant considerations (e.g., that an act would give someone pleasure, that it was promised, that it would cause pain, that it was the person's own choice), and then works through a sequence of carefully ordered cases in which those very considerations keep changing their moral valence, so that by the end the students' own earlier commitments are unusable as a scoring key. The cases should be sequenced so the flip is felt, not announced: each new case should initially look like one where their committed polarity applies straightforwardly, and only on inspection turn out to be one where it doesn't. Specify the cases or the case-generation rules in enough detail that I could run this tomorrow, including how you order them and why that order matters, what students write down and when, and how you prevent the obvious cheat of retroactively editing the earlier commitments.

Then give me the debrief: how to map specific moments in the activity onto the holism-versus-atomism dispute, including where the generalist's "default polarity plus defeating conditions" reply actually regains traction (I do not want the activity to function as propaganda for particularism), and how to use the students' written record to show that the dispute is about the structure of reasons and not about whether context matters, which both sides already grant. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first two times I run it -- I have in mind things like students treating the exercise as a test of consistency and therefore digging in, or constructing ad hoc finer-grained descriptions of the considerations to rescue fixed polarity -- and how to handle each failure mode without lecturing. Finally, be honest about which feature of the view the activity cannot reproduce: my suspicion is that a card-sort or case-sequence exercise can establish that valence flips across contexts, but not the stronger particularist claim that there is no context-independent generalization at all, and I want your candid assessment of whether that gap is closable in a classroom or must simply be supplied in lecture.

Begin your response with the core mechanic of the activity in a single sentence, with no preamble or restating of the task, then give the reasoning and details.
