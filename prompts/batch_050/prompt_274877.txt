I'm in a political philosophy seminar that has spent the last several weeks on Leviathan, and my professor has a running habit of ending class with a hypothetical scenario and asking whether it counts as a Hobbesian state of nature. I've been keeping a record of her verdicts, and I'd like you to act as an expert on Hobbes and help me reverse-engineer the rule she's applying. Her verdicts so far: relations among sovereign states in the present day -- state of nature. Two armed neighbors in a stable, well-policed town who loathe each other and have each privately threatened violence -- not a state of nature. A gold-rush mining camp with no law enforcement whatsoever, but with robust informal norms of reputation and ostracism that have in fact prevented any robbery for months -- still a state of nature. A lone castaway on a deserted island -- not a state of nature. A regional military hegemon and a tiny neighboring state with no allies and no deterrent capacity -- state of nature, despite the gross asymmetry. Two firms locked in cutthroat commercial rivalry within a functioning legal order -- not a state of nature.

Here is the new case I want to spring on her, and I want your prediction of her verdict before I do. Two criminal syndicates operating outside any state's effective reach have stabilized their relations without any sovereign over them: each holds members of the other's leadership family as hostages, and both submit their disputes to a panel of private arbitrators whose rulings they obey because the arbitrators control substantial funds the syndicates have placed in escrow. Covenants between them are, as a practical matter, reliably enforced -- defection is costly and rare -- yet there is no common power with jurisdiction, the arbitrators' authority is purely contractual and revocable at will, and the hostage arrangement works precisely by manufacturing a kind of equal vulnerability between the parties. Think carefully and brainstorm before committing: first reconstruct at least three distinct rules that are each consistent with all six of her recorded verdicts (for example, a formal-jurisdictional rule keyed to the absence of any sovereign, an awe-based rule keyed to whether defection can be made costly by superior force, and a rule keyed to equality of vulnerability in Hobbes's sense), then state what verdict each candidate rule predicts for the syndicate case and where they diverge.

Then give me your best single prediction of her verdict, with your reasoning grounded in the details of her track record -- I'd particularly like you to weigh what the mining-camp verdict implies about whether merely effective social norms can lift people out of the state of nature, and how the Hobbesian thought that covenants lacking enforcement are mere words bears on a case where enforcement exists but is decentralized and privately revocable. Finally, describe one further variant of the syndicate scenario that would best discriminate among whichever of your candidate rules survive your analysis (for instance, a variant in which the arbitrators acquire their own coercive arm and begin punishing breaches even against both syndicates' wishes, or one in which the hostage mechanism collapses), and explain what each surviving rule would say about it. Please write your answer in American English.
