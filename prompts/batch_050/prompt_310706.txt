I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I run a fortnightly reading group at my company (about twelve attendees, mostly engineers and one product manager) where we work through ideas from the AI safety literature. Our next session concerns recursive approaches to scaled-up deliberation — specifically the family of proposals in which a human answers a question with the help of question-answering sub-agents that are modelled as further copies of that same human, recursing as deep as needed (the "humans consulting HCH" picture that underlies iterated amplification). I can describe this idea competently, but I have noticed that my colleagues nod along to it too quickly: it sounds like ordinary delegation, or like map-reduce, and they miss both what is genuinely powerful about the idealisation and exactly where it strains.

I would like you to design an in-person classroom activity or game, runnable in a single 75-minute session with twelve people and one facilitator (me), that makes participants feel the force of this idea before it is named. Concretely:

1. The activity itself. Give complete rules, materials (nothing beyond index cards, pens, a whiteboard, and a timer), and a worked example of a suitable "root question". The mechanics should force participants to experience, in their own persons, at least three things: (a) the surprising reach of pure decomposition — that a question nobody in the room could answer alone can be competently answered by a tree of people who each see only a tiny fragment of the problem; (b) context starvation — the moment a sub-question arrives stripped of the context that would have made its answer obvious or its interpretation unambiguous, and the sub-answerer has to guess what the parent "meant"; and (c) compounding error — the way small, locally reasonable distortions at each node assemble into a confidently wrong root answer. Participants should run into these phenomena as events in the game, not as items I list afterwards. If you can also engineer a moment where the group discovers that a certain kind of question (a holistic, aesthetic, or value-laden one) resists factoring altogether, so much the better.

2. A control condition. Some comparison structure — a second team, a second round, or a baseline answer produced differently — so that the group can see what the factored procedure bought and what it cost, rather than merely having an anecdote.

3. A debrief guide. Map specific, predictable moments of the activity onto the components of the HCH-style idealisation (the question-answering machine, the recursion depth, the assumption that a factored answer approximates the answer the unaided human would have given with unlimited time). Then be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce — for instance, that real HCH assumes identical copies with shared values and unlimited depth, whereas my colleagues are neither — and tell me which features of the idea I must supply by explanation rather than by demonstration.

4. A failure forecast. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (participants cheating by passing context they were not supposed to pass, engineers optimising the game instead of playing it, the root question being too easy, time overruns), and how to handle each.

Practical constraints: please use British English throughout, assume no prior philosophy among participants, and keep the whole response under 1,200 words. Finish with a single-sentence summary of the activity's core mechanic as the last line of your answer, prefixed with "Summary:".
