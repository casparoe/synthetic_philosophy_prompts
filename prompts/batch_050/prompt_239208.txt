A colleague recently pressed an argument on me against pragmatic encroachment — the view that what is at stake for a subject can make a difference to whether she knows — and although the conclusion is absurd, I cannot locate the flaw. The argument runs as follows. (1) If pragmatic encroachment is true, then how much justification one needs in order to know that p depends on what turns, practically, on whether p is true. (2) What turns on p is not limited to the immediate decision: acting on a fallibly believed p can affect indefinitely many later choices, one's own and others'. (3) For any fallibly believed p that one might act on, there is some nonzero chance that an enormous amount later depends on having gotten p right — a missed train that becomes a missed transplant, a wrongly dated check that cascades into a default. (4) So for any fallible belief, the stakes of acting on it are, in expectation, arbitrarily high. (5) But when the stakes are high enough, fallible justification does not suffice for knowledge; that is precisely what the encroacher's bank cases are supposed to show. (6) Therefore fallible knowledge is impossible: knowledge requires justification that excludes every chance of error. (7) Almost nothing we believe meets that standard, so we know almost nothing. (8) Since we plainly do know things, pragmatic encroachment is false. My colleague adds that the same chain could be run in reverse, starting from encroachment and ending in skepticism, which is why he thinks (1) has to go. I would rather keep encroachment — I find the bank cases compelling — and my suspicion falls on (4), but I cannot articulate why it fails. Tell me which step should be rejected, why it looked plausible, what rejecting it costs, and whether the same diagnosis lets the encroacher keep ordinary low-stakes knowledge while blocking the slide from (4) to (6). Please answer in continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
