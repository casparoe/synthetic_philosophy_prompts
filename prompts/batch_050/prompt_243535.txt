I am a novelist at work on a book narrated in the first person by a man who suffers a severe stroke and spends some weeks in a state his doctors describe as minimally conscious, before recovering enough to speak. To render his inner life credibly, I have spent months interviewing people who have emerged from comas and similar conditions, and I have read their memoirs attentively. I have run into a problem I cannot think my way out of, and I would like your advice.

The accounts disagree with one another, and some disagree with themselves. One man tells me that during those weeks he heard everything and was screaming inside, though his neurologists assure me the relevant brain activity was largely absent, and though his own family reports that he responded to nothing. A woman says she experienced nothing at all, yet flinches, in the hospital footage her husband kept, whenever her daughter cries. My editor takes the simple line that these people are the sole authorities on their own experience and that I must simply transcribe what they tell me. But I am being asked to write what it was like, and what it was like seems to be precisely what is in dispute -- between the reporter and the evidence, and sometimes between the reporter and himself.

So my questions are these. Whom should I trust when the only witness to an experience may be an unreliable one, and on what grounds? If I treat a survivor's account of his own mind as a claim to be weighed against other evidence rather than as testimony to be accepted, am I committing some further wrong against him, as one or two of my subjects have pointedly suggested? And does the answer change when my aim is not clinical truth but a novel, where I must in the end simply choose what to put on the page?
