My roommate hit me with this last night and its been bugging me all day, because the conclusion is insane but I cant find the bad step.

(1) To make a rational decision between options, you weigh up what each option is likely to lead to — expected value, computed with your own current probabilities.
(2) So rational deliberation requires having probabilities, right now, for what happens conditional on each of your options.
(3) Your probabilities about your own future behavior come from the same place as your probabilities about anyone else's: track record. And my track record is embarrassingly predictable — same coffee order for six years, always cave when my sister pushes, never once picked the hard option when the easy one was on the table.
(4) So for most choices I deliberate about, an honest version of me already assigns a very high probability, before deliberating, to one specific option being the one I'll end up taking.
(5) But if I already assign ~0.95 to "I'll take the easy option," in what sense am I choosing? Deliberation where you're basically certain of the outcome in advance is theater — the verdict was in before the arguments were heard.
(6) So either I set aside my self-knowledge whenever I deliberate (which seems irrational — why would knowing less about myself make me more rational?) or I admit that my "deliberations" are mostly after-the-fact rationalizations of a decision that was never really open.
(7) And since this gets worse the better you know yourself, the limiting case is: a perfectly self-aware agent couldnt deliberate at all. Total self-knowledge is incompatible with genuine choice.

Step (7) has to be wrong — people who know themselves well obviously still make real decisions — but every individual step looks... fine? Which one do I give up? Ideally I'd like to keep (2) and (5), since they seem close to the definitions of decision-making and deliberation respectively, but I'll sacrifice whatever needs sacrificing. I'd also like to know what rejecting the bad step costs me in the everyday case, not just in the spooky "perfect self-knowledge" limit.
