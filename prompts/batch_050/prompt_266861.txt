I'm a PhD student working on the aesthetics of music (broadly — my dissertation is on musical expression, tho lately everything keeps getting hijacked by AI debates). Last week a friend in my cohort who works on generative models played me some tracks from one of those text-prompt music generators, and I had the predictable reaction of "this is unsettlingly competent," but then I started wondering what Schopenhauer would say, and I genuinely can't settle it. I've been rereading the music sections of The World as Will and Representation (Vol. 1, §52), and he seems to give with one hand and take with the other. On the one hand, his whole point is that music is unlike the other arts because it isn't a copy of the Platonic Ideas, i.e. not a representation of anything in the phenomenal world at all — it's a direct objectification of the will itself, which is why it hits us so much harder than painting or sculpture. On the other hand, the way he talks about composition makes the composer sound almost like a medium: the genius expresses the inner nature of the will in a language his own intellect doesn't fully grasp, like someone speaking truths in a trance they couldn't explain awake.

So here's my question. Suppose Schopenhauer were shown modern AI music systems: models trained on huge corpora of recorded music that produce genuinely affecting new pieces on demand, no human composer involved, no will (in his technical sense) anywhere in the causal story. What would his considered view actually be? I can see the argument that he'd dismiss it as worse than the imitative program music he already sneered at — mere mimicry of the outer shell of music, since there's no suffering, striving will behind it, and so whatever it expresses is borrowed without being lived. But I can also see the argument that his theory is oddly permissive here: if music was never a copy of the phenomenal world to begin with, and its value for the listener is the temporary silencing of the will, the momentary release from striving, then maybe the causal source is irrelevant and an AI-generated adagio that genuinely stills someone's will does exactly what music is for. Which reading do you find more faithful to his system, and why? I'd especially like you to (a) locate where in his account of music the answer is actually constrained versus where the system just underdetermines the question, (b) say something about whether his claims about genius and the composer's non-conceptual grasp of the will survive the possibility that the "trance speaker" is a machine, and (c) resist the boring answer that he'd just hate it because he was a grump. Assume he gets a patient explanation of how the technology works, not a caricature.
