Ok so I TA an undergrad ethics of war class and the students got into it over nuclear deterrence and I realized I don't actually know what I think, my gut verdicts seem all over the place. Here they are:

1. The US threatening massive retaliation if the Soviets invade West Germany: seems fine, or at least fine-ish. It arguably kept the peace for decades.
2. Actually carrying out that retaliation after deterrence fails -- cities are already lost, millions of deaths avenge nothing and protect nothing: monstrous. No question.
3. A doomsday device that launches automatically, no human in the loop, so the threat is 100% credible: feels deeply wrong, maybe worse than the human threat, even though it's the same threat with the bluff removed.
4. Brinkmanship -- deliberately escalating a crisis where neither side fully controls events and things could spiral by accident (think a naval blockade where an itchy trigger finger could start it): feels more acceptable than the doomsday device, even though both work by making disaster possible.
5. Pure bluffing -- threatening retaliation with a firm secret intention never to follow through: feels totally fine morally, just strategically risky.
6. Threatening nuclear first use in response to a purely conventional attack on your own homeland: feels... probably acceptable? Not comfortable with it but not clearly wrong either.
7. Deliberately making the threat *about* civilians -- announcing we target your cities, hold your population hostage -- feels worse than threatening the same destruction framed as counterforce, even if the deterrent effect and the actual targeting plan are identical.

The pair that keeps bugging me is 1 and 2 together: if carrying it out would be monstrous, how can the threat be fine, unless it's secretly a bluff (case 5), and doesn't it being a bluff undermine 1? And 3 vs 4 also seems unstable -- the doomsday device is *more* honest than brinkmanship.

So: is there one principle (or two) that captures all or most of these verdicts? State it precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it saves and which I'd have to give up, and say what it implies about a case I haven't decided: a leader who publicly gives launch authority to a subordinate known to be erratic and vengeful, specifically so the threat is credible, while privately hoping the subordinate never has to decide.
