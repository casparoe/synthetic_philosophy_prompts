Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been reading in economics, and I keep running into the so-called ergodicity economics literature associated with Ole Peters and his collaborators. The central observation strikes me as entirely natural: for a multiplicative process, such as wealth evolving through repeated proportional gains and losses, the average across an ensemble of parallel trajectories need not coincide with the average along a single trajectory over time, and the two can point in opposite directions. A gamble offering a 50% gain or a 40% loss on each play has a positive expectation value (+5% per round) but a negative time-average growth rate (1.5 × 0.6 = 0.9, so a typical player loses roughly 5% of wealth per round in the long run). What I cannot yet tell is how much of the surrounding dispute is substance and how much is terminology: defenders of orthodox expected utility theory reply that Bernoulli's logarithmic utility already encodes precisely this point, while others accept the mathematics but doubt that real economic lives are multiplicative enough for it to matter.

For a reading group of fellow retired scientists, I would like a decision tree that takes a reader from the starting commitment above to a named position in this dispute. Please build it as follows.

Root node: "I accept that for repeated multiplicative gambles, the ensemble average and the time average can diverge, and the 1.5/0.6 example demonstrates this."

Branch nodes should be diagnostic questions, each phrased as a concrete case or claim that a reader could answer without knowing in advance where it leads — no more than eight of them. Among them, please include:

- A node on the retirement case I actually care about: two drawdown strategies with the same ensemble-average return, one with volatile annual returns and one smooth; does the difference in sequence-of-returns risk for a single retiree settle the choice between them, or does it remain a matter of taste?
- A node on whether risk aversion is a fact about psychology (tastes, reference points, loss aversion) or a theorem about dynamics — that is, whether logarithmic utility is derived from the process itself or assumed as a preference.
- A node on the word itself: in physics, ergodicity has a precise measure-theoretic meaning, whereas this literature uses the term more loosely, roughly as "the ensemble average is a poor guide to the individual trajectory." Does that looseness do any argumentative work, or is it harmless rebranding?
- A node on whether actual wealth dynamics are multiplicative enough: additive wages and pensions, consumption floors, taxation, finite lifetimes.

The leaves — at least five — must include: the Peters-style position that expected value is simply the wrong criterion for individual decisions; the orthodox reply that this is Bernoulli rediscovered and that expected utility theory already handles it; the view that the mathematics is correct but empirically idle given non-multiplicative complications; the view that time-average optimization smuggles in its own arbitrariness (why maximize the growth rate of log wealth rather than of some other observable?); and a policy-first position that takes the divergence as grounds for social insurance and risk-pooling regardless of how the foundational quarrel settles. For each leaf, give one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Finally, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot really separate. I suspect the Peters position and "expected utility theory with logarithmic utility" may be indistinguishable in the advice they give individuals; say whether your tree can force them apart. And note any logically available combination of answers that no one seems to occupy, with a brief diagnosis of whether that cell is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
