I am a novelist drafting a book set in a historically working-class neighborhood where a developer has proposed a large market-rate apartment complex on a long-vacant lot, and I need the central argument scene. You are a professional dialogue writer with expertise in urban economics and political philosophy. Before drafting, briefly brainstorm the two or three strongest points available to each side; then write the dialogue itself.

The exchange is between two characters, both intelligent and arguing in good faith: MARA, a tenant organizer raised in the neighborhood, who favors new public and social housing but is leading the campaign against this market-rate project, and DANE, a housing researcher who contends that blocking market-rate construction in high-demand neighborhoods accelerates the very displacement she fears. Neither may be a strawman: Mara is no nostalgic preservationist quietly protecting property values, and Dane must not wave away attachment to place as mere sentimentality. Each must answer the other's actual arguments rather than talk past them, and the dialogue must genuinely engage at least these questions: whether a neighborhood's character and social networks are the sort of thing anyone can hold a right to; whether the harm of displacement is exhausted by the rent differential or is something distinct; and who counts as a rightful claimant to the city — current residents, those already pushed out, or those who would like to move in. Let each character concede at least one real point to the other without either capitulating. Roughly 600–900 words. As the very last line, state in a single sentence, beginning "Crux:", the one point of disagreement on which their dispute ultimately turns.
