I am a novelist drafting a historical novel in which a European traveler repeats atrocity stories about a New World people he has never met. I want the chapter to turn on a genuine conceptual puzzle rather than a generic moral. In Book I, Chapter XXX, “Of Cannibals,” Michel de Montaigne writes: “By which it appears how cautious men ought to be of taking things upon trust from vulgar opinion, and that we are to judge by the eye of reason, and not from common report.” Please explain how this injunction can coexist with the rest of the essay, where Montaigne’s own account depends on an intermediary informant and his criticism of prejudice often seems to reverse, rather than suspend, the hierarchy between “civilized” Europeans and “barbarous” peoples. Is the “eye of reason” an impartial standpoint, merely a way of exposing customs that masquerade as reason, or something else? Please focus on the interpretive issue, not on historical background or bibliography.
