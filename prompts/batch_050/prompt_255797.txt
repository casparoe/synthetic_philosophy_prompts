For the past six years I have co-organized a small annual rationality workshop -- the usual alumni of that corner of the internet: people who came up through the Sequences, forecasting tournaments, and the literature on cognitive bias, plus a rotating handful of graduate students in philosophy and statistics we invite as guest instructors. Every year I give the opening remarks on the first evening. This year I have a specific problem to address, and I want the remarks built around it. Over the past year, a cluster of our more committed alumni has begun invoking the dogmatism paradox -- the epistemological puzzle associated with Kripke, the one where knowing that p seems to license you to regard any future evidence against p as misleading in advance -- as a kind of slogan for dismissing criticism. One alumnus, for instance, recently told me he declined to read a lengthy rebuttal of a paper he had publicly endorsed because, having already satisfied himself of the conclusion, he knew in advance the rebuttal had to be misleading somewhere, so reading it could only waste his time or, worse, trick him. Others have adopted the same posture with less candor. This is, I am fairly confident, a corruption of the paradox rather than an application of it -- but it is a *sophisticated* corruption, and I cannot counter it by simply telling people to "keep an open mind," because the workshop's whole curriculum rests on the idea that it is sometimes right to hold a belief with great confidence, to act on it decisively, and to stop re-litigating settled questions. The paradox does real structural work here: whatever I say about conviction, updating, and evidence has to thread between the two failure modes without sliding into either.

Please write the opening remarks. The practical details: roughly eight to ten minutes when read aloud; an audience split three ways between alumni who know the paradox by name and will notice any philosophical sloppiness, newcomers who have never heard of it and need it made vivid without jargon, and our guest academics, who will catch me if I misstate the puzzle or pretend it has an uncontroversial resolution. The speech must do at least the following: present the paradox clearly enough that a newcomer feels its pull (it has to seem genuinely compelling, not like a straw man the alumni foolishly fell for); diagnose where the misuse goes wrong in a way that does not simply collapse into "always stay uncertain," which would undercut the rest of the workshop; and give the audience something they can actually *do* -- some usable distinction or habit of thought that separates the legitimate settling of a question from the illegitimate pre-emptive dismissal of evidence. If the honest philosophical verdict is that the paradox is still open and every response to it carries a cost, the speech should say so plainly rather than gesture at a fake resolution; the audience respects that more than tidiness.

Approach this as both a professional epistemologist who has worked on the dogmatism paradox and an experienced writer of speeches for mixed expert/lay audiences. Think carefully about the underlying philosophy before you draft anything. First, briefly sketch -- for me, not as part of the speech -- two or three candidate resolutions of the paradox that could anchor the remarks (for example, lines that distinguish between regarding evidence as misleading and regarding it as irrelevant, or between first-order confidence and the treatment of higher-order evidence about one's own reliability), note the standard objections to each, and say which one you think can survive being simplified into speech-language. Then write the speech itself. Afterward, go back over your own draft adversarially and flag any sentence that a careful philosopher in the third row would object to, with your one-sentence defense of keeping it or your suggested fix.
