A research institute has offered to fund a five-year adversarial collaboration between two camps who have been talking past each other in print: a cliodynamicist who works in the tradition of structural-demographic theory -- history as a quantitative science, agrarian states cycling through integrative and disintegrative phases of roughly two to three centuries, political instability driven by state fiscal strain, mass immiseration, and above all elite overproduction, with the 2010 forecast of elevated instability in the United States around 2020 held up as a proof of concept -- and a skeptical historian who holds that large-scale historical prediction fails on principle: the samples are too small and non-independent, the data are reconstructed rather than measured, exogenous shocks and contingent choices swamp structure, and any model flexible enough to fit a few dozen secular cycles can fit almost anything, so apparent successes are survivorship and hindsight. Before the money is released, the two must file a joint statement with the funder. Write that statement.

It should contain the following, in this order:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. These must be things both sides can actually sign, not anodyne filler: include claims about what the historical record does and does not license (e.g., whether demographic-fiscal patterns in premodern states are real regularities and what their existence does not yet establish), about the difference between retrodiction and prediction, about what would count as a track record, and about whether the 2020-era instability in the United States bears on structural-demographic theory at all.

2. A disagreements table with three columns: the issue, the cliodynamicist's position worded so the cliodynamicist would sign it, and the historian's position worded so the historian would sign it. Cover at least: whether structural variables can be measured without smuggling in the outcome they are meant to predict (how does one quantify elite overproduction or immiseration in a way that is not just instability wearing a costume); the degrees-of-freedom problem for a theory with tunable parameters fit to a few dozen cases; whether exogenous shocks are noise the theory should absorb or a counterexample to the whole enterprise; and whether the individual contingency the historian prizes is invisible to the model or genuinely causal.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a test, dataset, or argument that both parties agree in advance would bear on it, and write down each side's prediction before the test is run. At least one crux must take the form of a concrete preregistered forecast the collaboration will actually run -- say, country-level political instability across twenty wealthy democracies to 2035 -- and you must settle the details adversaries would fight over: the agreed proxies for each structural variable and who may propose them, the scoring rule, the baseline the theory must beat (a naive extrapolation? a panel of unaided experts?), and the stopping rule, i.e., what result would count as the theory failing rather than as grounds for recalibration.

4. A short closing section in which the statement names any disagreement the parties concluded was verbal rather than substantive, explains why, and identifies the single most likely way the collaboration ends without either side updating -- and what both agree to do to prevent that.

Throughout, honor two ground rules the parties negotiated in advance: no retrofitting (post hoc parameter adjustments after a miss count against the theory, not as refinement of it) and no lucky-hit credit (one qualitative prediction coming true is not evidence unless it was ex ante improbable on the rival view). The statement should read as a document both principals could put their names to: no strawmen, no victory laps, and every residual disagreement made exact.
