Next month I'm presenting at a social ontology workshop a paper arguing that "generations" can be real kinds. My thesis: a generation is a genuine social kind when its members are bound together by shared exposure, during a developmental critical window (roughly adolescence to early adulthood), to the same formative events and institutional regimes — I analyze this as a homeostatic property cluster rather than an essence — while cohorts carved out by arbitrary birth-year cutoffs are mere bookkeeping. On my view, some currently used labels may pick out real kinds while others do not, and the standard fifteen-to-twenty-year brackets fail because they track publishing convenience rather than causal structure. The audience: about ten philosophers of social science, three demographers who work on age-period-cohort identification problems, and two sociologists who regard generations as media inventions. Prepare my Q&A sheet as a table with these columns: (1) Rank — ordered by how much damage the objection would do to the thesis if left unanswered; (2) Objection — phrased the way that audience member would actually put it, in their vocabulary; (3) Likely source — philosopher, demographer, or sociologist; (4) Target — which component of my view it hits (the critical-window claim, the property-cluster analysis, the cutoff critique, or the real-kind conclusion); (5) Reply — what I can say in about two minutes; (6) Weak point — where that reply is softest. After the table, add two short notes: what I should concede up front to buy credibility with the demographers, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks, along with my best available answer to it.
