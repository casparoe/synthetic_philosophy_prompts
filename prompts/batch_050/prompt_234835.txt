I'm applying for a small internal grant at my university (about $8,000, nine months, no research assistance) to fund a philosophical project on the Ellsberg paradox, and I need a two-page proposal. Here is my half-formed idea. The standard discussion treats ambiguity aversion as one thing: a preference parameter, however modeled, that makes people choose the known-odds urn over the unknown-odds urn even when symmetry considerations give them no reason to think either color is more likely in the unknown urn. My suspicion is that this lumps together at least two distinct phenomena. First, aversion to missing information where the gap is plainly relevant to the choice, regardless of why the information is missing. Second, something closer to distrust: in the classic setup, the unknown urn is handed to you by an experimenter who knows its composition and chose not to tell you, and it is not crazy to treat the concealment itself as evidence about the bet you're being offered. If the second component is doing real work in the canonical cases, then models that bake ambiguity aversion in as a pure taste are misdescribing at least some of the data, and the normative question (is ambiguity aversion irrational?) may have different answers for the two components.

Turn this into a proper proposal. It should state the research question precisely (including what would count as showing that the two components are genuinely distinct rather than two descriptions of one disposition); explain why the existing responses to Ellsberg — both the formal models and the straightforward "it's just irrationality" verdict — leave this question open rather than answering it; describe the method, which is conceptual rather than experimental: constructing and analyzing variations on the urn scenario that pull the two components apart (e.g., cases where the missing information is missing through chance rather than through anyone's choice, and cases where concealment is present but manifestly irrelevant), and examining what norms of practical rationality apply to each; say what a deliverable looks like at nine months and what would count as the project failing — for instance, if the variations turn out not to generate diverging intuitions under analysis, or if the distinction collapses into a point already standard in the literature.

Format the proposal as exactly five numbered sections, each one or two short paragraphs, corresponding to: the question, why it is open, the method and why this method rather than the obvious alternatives, the deliverable, and the failure conditions.
