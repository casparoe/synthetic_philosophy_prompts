I am a retired evolutionary biologist -- forty years in systematics and population genetics, now filling my evenings with philosophy, which I find both stimulating and maddeningly unquantified. I have lately been reading about the sociology of generations: the claim that labels like Baby Boomer, Millennial, and Gen Z pick out something real, versus the demographers' complaint that these are arbitrary binning of a continuous variable, versus intermediate views (I gather associated with Karl Mannheim) on which a generation is constituted by shared formative exposure to historical events rather than by birth-year boundaries.

I want this debate restated in the vocabulary I actually think in, as systematically as you can manage. My working toolkit includes: the major species concepts (biological, phylogenetic, morphological); the clade-versus-grade distinction; chronospecies and the problem of drawing vertical cuts through an anagenetic lineage; ring species and other cases where a continuum resists partitioning; phenetic clustering versus genealogical individuation; homeostatic property cluster accounts of natural kinds; and the general question of when a taxon name denotes a real unit of nature versus a convenience for filing specimens. I am fluent in all of this -- do not simplify it.

Please take the main positions in the generations debate one at a time -- the nominalist cohort view, the strong realist view, and the shared-formative-experience view -- and restate each as precisely as possible in taxonomic terms. In particular, I want to know: Is the demographers' cohort essentially a morphospecies erected on a continuous cline? Is the Mannheim-style generation better understood as a grade -- a cluster held together by a common selective environment -- than as a clade, which would seem to require some analogue of reproductive isolation? Does the chronospecies problem, where paleontologists draw lines through an unbroken lineage for practical reasons while admitting the lines are conventional, capture what is going on with birth-year cutoffs, or is there a disanalogy I am missing?

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the sociological debate translate cleanly into systematics vocabulary, which survive only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all? For the parts that do not translate, I want your judgment on whether the fault lies with my framework -- biology being about gene flow and selection, things that have no social analogue -- or with the original debate, which may be trading on intuitions about kinds that dissolve once you state them precisely. I have a standing suspicion that much of what sociologists argue about under the heading of generations is our old species-concept problem in disguise, and I would like that suspicion either vindicated or shown to be superficial.

One more constraint: do not paper over the asymmetry that bothers me most. Species (on some views) are individuals -- spatiotemporally bounded particulars -- whereas generational labels look like predicates of people. If the translation founders on that, say so plainly rather than forcing it.
