I recently took up a new post as director of sustainability at a mid-sized university (roughly 12,000 students), after our board passed a resolution committing the institution to "meaningful" reductions in its contribution to climate change and industrial animal agriculture within five years. The resolution is deliberately vague, and turning it into purchasing policy is my job: what the dining halls buy, how we treat staff air travel, what we do with the endowment. Nearly every proposal I bring forward meets the same objection — from the CFO, from faculty, sometimes from my own inner monologue — that our choices are a rounding error. One cafeteria going meatless does not save a single animal; one professor staying home does not cancel a flight; selling our sliver of an index fund does not raise anyone's cost of capital. I have read enough of the philosophical literature on collective harm and the inefficacy objection to know this is genuinely contested — cases like Parfit's harmless torturers, arguments about threshold effects in consumer markets, the distinction between expected marginal and average impact — but I cannot run a procurement committee on journal articles, and I have about an hour per proposal and publicly available information only.

Write me a decision procedure I can apply, one purchasing category at a time, to determine when an individual institutional choice of ours makes enough of an expected difference to justify its cost, and when the inefficacy objection actually wins and the money is better spent elsewhere. Specifically, I want:

1. The procedure itself as an ordered sequence of questions I can put to any proposal, with a statement of what counts as an adequate answer at each step, and the points where the procedure terminates — either with "the expected marginal impact is too small to justify this" or with "this is a value judgment the committee must take over, not a fact the procedure can supply."

2. An account of which distinctions from the literature must survive the translation into a checklist and which can be safely dropped. In particular, tell me how the procedure should treat: goods whose supply responds smoothly to demand versus goods governed by thresholds (slaughter schedules, flight schedules, production runs); the probability that my purchase is the one that trips a threshold, and how to reason about it when I cannot get that probability; and downstream effects that operate through other people (signaling, norm-setting, market-shaping) — including how much weight the procedure is permitted to give these without becoming unfalsifiable.

3. Honesty about the procedure's failure modes: the cases where the philosophy genuinely forks and the procedure must pick a side (say which side it picks and on what grounds), and the cases where a proposal will pass every step and still be pointless, or fail a step and still be worth doing.

4. A worked application to three proposals currently on my desk: (a) switching dining-hall chicken to a certified higher-welfare supplier at a 40% cost increase; (b) a policy of no university-funded flights for journeys under six hours by train; (c) divesting the endowment from broad fossil-fuel indices. For each, show every step of the procedure being applied, including the numbers you assume where I would have to supply real ones. At least one of the three should come out with an answer I will not enjoy delivering to its advocates — do not sand it down.

Finally, give me a short paragraph I can paste into my committee's terms of reference explaining what this procedure can and cannot establish, so that when it says no to a popular proposal, or yes to an expensive one, I have something to point to other than my own judgment.
