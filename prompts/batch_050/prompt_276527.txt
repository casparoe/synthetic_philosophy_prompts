A legislative commission in a mid-sized democracy is redrafting the national marriage statute and the framework for sex education in state schools, and it has seated two advisors who disagree fundamentally. The first is a Russellian in the tradition of Russell's Marriage and Morals (1929): sexual morality is to be judged by its consequences for human happiness and above all for children, not by inherited taboo; children should receive frank and early sexual knowledge rather than be protected by concealment; marriage is an institution whose chief public purpose is the rearing of children; divorce should be far easier to obtain than the law historically allowed — available by mutual consent at least where the marriage is childless, and never withheld merely because one spouse has been unfaithful; jealousy is in large part a convention trained into people, and ethics should not be built to appease it; contraception and non-marital companionship among the childless young are morally unobjectionable. The second is a traditional institutionalist: marriage is a practice constituted by norms of exclusivity and permanence whose authority is not exhausted by any tally of outcomes; children's interests depend on reliable commitment norms, which permissive exit rules erode gradually and in ways that short-run welfare measures systematically fail to detect; jealousy and the desire for exclusive attachment are deep features of human attachment that institutions must accommodate rather than re-educate; and the costs of weakened norms fall disproportionately on later generations who never consented to the reform. The commission's working draft proposes a two-track statute — an optional high-commitment track with restrictive exit conditions and a default track dissolvable by mutual consent — together with a mandated school curriculum. Draft the joint statement the two advisors could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Requirements: (1) a numbered list of at least six agreed claims, substantive rather than vacuous, with at least one claim that is uncomfortable for the Russellian and at least one that is uncomfortable for the institutionalist; (2) each residual disagreement stated in a single sentence phrased so that the side whose view it describes would accept the wording, plus a note identifying any disagreement that turned out to be verbal and what it was really about; (3) four or five cruxes — sub-questions whose resolution would change at least one side's recommendation on the two-track statute or the curriculum — presented in a table with exactly these columns: Crux (the sub-question in one line); Russellian position; Institutionalist position; Agreed test or evidence (a natural experiment, historical case, or body of data both sides accept as bearing on the crux — not a case rigged to decide it); Russellian prediction (a concrete, checkable forecast recorded in advance); Institutionalist prediction (likewise); Concession owed (the specific revision each side would owe its recommendation if its prediction fails — not an expression of surprise). Close with one paragraph naming the crux whose settlement would do the most to move the two camps toward a common statute, or explaining why no single crux would.
