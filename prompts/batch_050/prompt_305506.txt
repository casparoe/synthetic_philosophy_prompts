So I'm auditing this mixed seminar (half lit students, half philosophy students) and I volunteered to lead one session, and in a moment of weakness I pitched: Kafka's Metamorphosis read through Descartes on animal minds. Now I have to actually pull it off and I'm realizing my idea is better as a one-liner than as an hour of discussion, so help me build the reading. Quick recap of the Descartes bit for context: he held that non-human animals are essentially very intricate machines, and his famous test was linguistic — if a creature could use language flexibly, to express genuine thought rather than parroting sounds or emitting hardwired cries, that would prove a mind; animals never pass, ergo no mind, and (on the harsher reading) arguably no real suffering either.

Here's why I think the story is made for this. Gregor wakes up as a giant vermin (the exact German word is apparently contested, but whatever) with a completely human inner life, and crucially, in the opening scene he can still talk — he gives a whole apologetic speech to the chief clerk from behind the door, and it's fully intelligible to him but comes out the other side as animal noise. From then on the family treats him as a beast, and the story itself seems to drift toward agreeing with them: by the end it's genuinely ambiguous whether his inner life has decayed into something insect-like or whether he dies still loving them, thinking about Grete's violin. So is the family running Descartes' test, and is the story showing that test failing catastrophically — a mind that fails the behavioral criterion from outside while passing it from inside? Or is it doing something weirder: suggesting that losing the ability to be understood gradually destroys the mind itself, in which case Descartes turns out half-right in a way he'd hate?

I want a reading that's fair in both directions. Don't just use the story as a stick to beat Descartes with — his point about the flexibility of reason versus fixed behavioral routines isn't stupid, and the story's Gregor arguably becomes more routinized and appetite-driven as it goes on, which should trouble the easy anti-Cartesian reading. And don't flatten the story into an allegory of disability or capitalism, though feel free to say where those readings intersect this one. The thing I keep circling: Descartes' problem is epistemic, it's about whether we can ever know, but Kafka puts us inside Gregor's skull from page one — we know, and it doesn't help Gregor at all. Is that a refutation of Descartes, a diagnosis of why everyone becomes a practical Cartesian skeptic anyway, or a third thing? And if you can work in the apple wound, the charwoman's total lack of horror, or why it's specifically Grete who gets to pronounce that this creature is no longer Gregor, even better.
