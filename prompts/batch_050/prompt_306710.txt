Two friends of mine build warehouse robots for a living and got into a long argument over something I thought was basicly trivial. (1) Their robot sometimes faces two shelf slots that are exactly equally good by every metric they program in -- same distance, same cost, everything. Their code just picks one at random, and Anna said this shows Buridan's ass was never a real problem: any rational agent can break ties arbitrarily, so choice without preference is easy, and the starving donkey is just a badly designed agent. (2) Ben said that misses the whole point. The random tiebreak only works because the robot has a higher-order preference not to freeze, and once you add that, the options aren't really equal anymore -- the ass paradox is defined on the assumption that no such higher-order reason exists, so "just pick randomly" doesn't solve the paradox, it assumes it away. (3) Anna said fine, but then the paradox is trivial too: it describes an agent so thin it couldn't exist, since any actual agent that has to act does have that higher-order preference. A paradox about an impossible agent proves nothing about real rationality. (4) Ben said no, it proves something about us: the fact that we can just flip a coin and not stall shows our choices aren't fully determined by reasons, and the leftover slack is either genuine freedom or genuine randomness, and the paradox forces you to say which. (5) Anna came back with: the coin flip isn't even the robot's preference, it's the designer's, so the slack is borrowed too. Ben said that just pushes it back a level -- what does the designer do when two tiebreak mechanisms are themselves equally good? Anna said eventually you bottom out in arbitrary hardware noise and that's fine, and Ben said "so rationality rests on noise, great," and they were both too annoyed to keep going.
