I am preparing a seminar unit on the justification of constitutional monarchy, and before we read any of the standard arguments I want the standard itself. Write the desiderata that any adequate justification of constitutional monarchy within a modern parliamentary democracy must meet, taking the United Kingdom, the Scandinavian monarchies, and Japan as the reference cases.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp (monarchist, republican, liberal, traditionalist). The list should cover at least the following ground:

- Which features of the institution the justification must explain rather than merely excuse — above all hereditary succession, since a defense that works equally well for an appointed ceremonial president has not yet defended monarchy.
- Which hard cases it must have something to say about: a hung parliament in which the choice of prime minister is not automatic; a ministry requesting a dissolution against the wishes of the legislature; the hypothetical refusal of royal assent; and genuine constitutional breakdown. Spain in 1981, where the king is widely credited with helping defeat an attempted coup, is the case defenders cite; the Australian dismissal of 1975 is the case critics cite, and the justification must say whether a viceroy's exercise of reserve powers counts as evidence about the monarchy itself or only about its deputies.
- Which distinctions it must not collapse: between the personal qualities of the present occupant and the institution; between formal legal powers on paper and discretion actually exercised; between the monarch as symbol of national continuity and the monarch as a political actor.
- What the justification must be consistent with empirically: that the Scandinavian monarchies have been progressively stripped of formal political functions — Sweden's mid-1970s constitutional reform being the limiting case — without obvious damage to their democracies, and that Japan's postwar constitution assigns the emperor a purely symbolic role with no governing authority. A justification that proves too much, such that these countries count as defective democracies, has thereby refuted itself.

Then the part I care most about: identify any pair or larger set of these desiderata that you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and show the conflict concretely rather than by gesture. I have in mind the tension between requiring the monarch to be strictly politically neutral and requiring the Crown to serve as a constitutional safeguard in a crisis — a safeguard seems to demand precisely the discretionary political judgment that neutrality forbids — but tell me whether that tension is genuine or resolvable (for instance, by restricting the safeguard to cases where the constitution itself dictates the answer), and whether other joints in the list are similarly stressed.

Finally, score briefly — a short paragraph each, no more — the three main families of justification against the list: the functional argument associated with Bagehot's The English Constitution, on which the monarchy carries the dignified and ceremonial work of the state so that electoral politics can carry its efficient work; the traditionalist argument that a hereditary institution embodies continuity and commands a loyalty no elected office could; and the republican challenge treated as a generator of desiderata, that is, as a statement of what a democrat is entitled to demand of any head of state. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scoring is secondary, and where a desideratum is contested, say whose desideratum it is.
