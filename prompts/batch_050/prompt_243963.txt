Bit of an odd one, but: write me a sonnet about generics — those bare-plural claims like ducks lay eggs and mosquitoes carry malaria that somehow stay true even though most of the kind lacks the property (drakes lay nothing, and the overwhelming majority of mozzies carry nothing worse than an itch). The constraint: I want the volta at line nine to be an exception that refutes nothing — say a drake strutting through the octet's eggs — so the form itself shows how a generic absorbs its counterexamples, whereas somewhere the poem should quietly register that ducks are female would NOT survive the same drake, i.e. exceptions don't all behave alike. Bonus if the closing couplet gestures at why a rare but nasty property (malaria) gets to characterise a kind while a common but boring one might not — the striking stuff seems to outweigh sheer headcounts. Someone who knows the generics literature should recognise the distinctions doing the work, not just the examples; someone who doesn't should still get a decent poem out of it.
