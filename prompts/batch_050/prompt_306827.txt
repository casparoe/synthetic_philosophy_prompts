Write a deadpan theater review, in the voice of a jaded veteran drama critic for a respectable newspaper, of a long-running production called The Cartesian Theater. The premise of the show, which the critic must describe with a straight face, is that everything the audience sees, hears, and feels is first gathered together and projected onto a single small stage inside the head, where a solitary inner spectator sits in the best seat and watches it; nothing counts as experienced until he has seen it. About 550 words.

The comedy must come from the real difficulties with this picture of consciousness, treated as theatrical flaws rather than stated as philosophy. Among the things the review should work in, without ever lapsing into a lecture: the production's awkward reliance on the fact that its lone spectator apparently needs a still smaller theater and a still smaller spectator inside his own head, and so on down, casting calls for which have been ongoing for centuries; the management's insistence that every performance begins at one precise instant, even though nobody -- not the lighting crew, not the ushers, not the audience -- can ever say when the lights actually came up, and critics armed with stopwatches keep getting contradictory answers depending on where they stand; and the show's curious backstage policy that incoming material must queue in a single line and be finished before it is presented, despite rumors that it is being revised right up to and after the moment of performance.

Across the street, a rival production called Multiple Drafts has been running for decades. It has no stage, no screen, no single seat, no final cut, and no discernible showtime -- just innumerable overlapping versions of the material being continuously revised in different corners of the building, with no master copy and nobody whose job is to watch. Our critic has seen it several times and grudgingly concedes it may be the more coherent production, but cannot shake the feeling that it is not, strictly speaking, a show at all, since there is no one there to see it. The review should end with the critic admitting that, whatever its structural absurdities, The Cartesian Theater remains the show he instinctively feels he is attending every waking moment -- thereby landing, without ever winking, on the real source of the idea's grip: its seductiveness, not its coherence. The critic should review the show, not its originators; no real philosopher need be named, though the titles of the two productions are fair game.
