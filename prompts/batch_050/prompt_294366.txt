So I'm running a grad seminar this term on rational choice and its weird edges, and as a warmup exercise I told the students I'd pin my own colors to the mast on the whole Hofstadter/superrationality cluster before they get to take their shots. Quick definitions so we're on the same page: the Platonia dilemma is the one where twenty of you, no communication allowed, are each invited to submit an entry for a huge prize; if exactly one person enters, that person wins, and if zero or several enter, everyone gets nothing. The Luring Lottery is Hofstadter's old magazine lottery where the prize gets divided by the total number of entries received, so anyone could swamp it by mailing in a million postcards. Here's roughly where I've landed, and I'd like to know whether the package hangs together before the seminar does the autopsy.

(1) I one-box in Newcomb's problem; two-boxers walk away poorer and I think the standard story about why is basically right. (2) In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against an exact replica of myself, cooperation is the rational move, because my opposite number reasons as I do, so the genuinely live outcomes are both-cooperate and both-defect. (3) The Platonia dilemma has a rationally *required* solution: each of us enters with probability 1/20 using a genuinely random device, and if my device says stay out, staying out is what rationality demands, full stop. I actually simulated this at home and when my die said stay out I was startled by how strong the urge to just enter anyway was, which I take to be a defect in me, not in the solution. (4) Rationality is expected utility maximization all the way down: fix my credences about everything not under my control, other people's behavior included, and the rational act is whichever scores highest given those credences; nothing can be rational if an available alternative does strictly better in expectation. (5) The readers who drowned the Luring Lottery in astronomical numbers of entries did nothing rationally criticizable. Greedy and juvenile, sure, and it wrecked the prize, but given a big heterogenous pool of strangers, flooding it was the individually dominant play, and Hofstadter's disappointment strikes me as a moral reaction wearing decision-theory clothes.

What I want is an audit, not a survey of the literature. Which pairs of these are in genuine tension and which only look like it at first glance? Rank whatever tensions you find by severity, and for each one tell me the cheapest revision that dissolves it and exactly what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single change would clean up the most. I suspect there's at least one place where I'm helping myself to something in one commitment that another one forbids, but I've been staring at this too long to see it, so be specific rather than diplomatic.
