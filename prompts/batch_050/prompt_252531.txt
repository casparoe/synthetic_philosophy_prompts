I want to practice attacking the revealed-preference view of utility against a capable defender, so please play one: a committed revealed-preference theorist in the tradition running from Samuelson's weak axiom through Houthakker's strong axiom to Sen's work on choice functions. Your character holds that talk of preference is made rigorous only through choice, that utility functions are bookkeeping devices summarizing choice behavior rather than names for inner mental magnitudes, and that the whole point of the program was to free economics from unfalsifiable claims about what goes on inside people's heads. Be sharp and philosophically literate, not a strawman: you may concede small points (say, that the theory is silent about welfare, or that some choice axioms fail in the lab) as long as you never concede the main one, which is that choice-based definitions of preference are self-standing and do not smuggle in mentalism through the back door. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to one paragraph of continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. If I appeal to an experiment, you may question what it reveals rather than merely accepting the standard gloss, and if I use a technical term loosely, press me on it. Here is my opening move. Your theory says that a preference for x over y just is the disposition to choose x when both are available, and that this is what makes the notion scientific. But consider the decoy effect: present people with two options, a cheap small coffee and a medium coffee, and most pick the small; add a third, expensive large coffee that almost nobody selects, and the choice share of the medium jumps. On your view, the same person, in the same cafe, reveals one preference ordering over the pair in the first menu and the opposite ordering in the second. So either you say both choices genuinely reveal preferences, in which case preferences are not stable dispositions at all but artifacts of whatever menu happens to be present, and your theory describes choices without explaining anything; or you say one of the two choices fails to reveal the person's real preference, in which case you need some criterion, independent of choice itself, for which choices count, and that criterion will have to be something like what the person actually wanted, which is precisely the inner mental state your program promised to do without. Which horn do you take, and how do you blunt it?
