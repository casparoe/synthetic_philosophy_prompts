After a long and rather tense argument with a friend last night, I’m trying to make sense of the move that ended it. We were disagreeing about whether it is morally defensible to keep buying cheap clothes from companies with credible reports of abusive labor practices. I said that even granting the impersonal harm, my strong aversion to wearing something whose production depended on misery still seemed morally relevant to what I should do with my own money; my friend replied, “That’s not a reason, that’s just a bias,” and added that my disgust probably tracks class socialization, cleanliness norms, and a desire not to feel implicated rather than any fact about workers. I don’t think explanations of where an attitude came from automatically disqualify it, but I also can’t deny that some moral feelings are better treated as noise than as evidence. When someone says “that’s just a bias” in ethics, what are the distinct precise claims they might be making—about genesis, reliability, justification, partiality, motivationally distorted salience, or failure to generalize across persons—and which of those claims are platitudes, which are substantive but defensible, and which are simply false or question-begging? Please lay the readings out as a menu rather than choosing one for me, and for each reading say whether it would actually license my friend’s conversational move of stopping rather than answering my first-order argument.

To keep the disambiguation useful, please apply it to three invented diagnostic labels I brought up after the argument had already gone off the rails, but without treating them as established terminology: a Counterfactual Socialization Test, which asks whether I would still endorse the attitude had I been raised with different markers of status and purity; an Asymmetry-of-Affect Test, which asks whether the feeling intensifies only when the victims are socially distant or narratively inconvenient; and a Reflective Stability Test, which asks whether the attitude survives after I state the strongest case against it in terms my opponents would accept. I want to know whether passing or failing these tests distinguishes a moral preference that merely happens to be mine from a bias that distorts judgment, or whether it instead reveals that the preference/bias boundary is itself doing normative work and cannot be drawn neutrally. Finally, address the harder version of the dispute: if every moral sentiment has some causal genealogy, what non-circular standards remain for saying that one genealogy debunks while another merely explains, and does the slogan “that’s just a bias” ever give more than a defeasible prompt to re-examine, as opposed to a reason to disregard the attitude altogether?
