You are a professional philosopher with expertise in normative ethics and twentieth-century political philosophy. I would like you to referee an argument. Please think carefully rather than reaching for the nearest textbook verdict: before answering, sketch for each premise the strongest consideration in its favour, and only then decide where, if anywhere, the argument goes wrong. Number your diagnoses so I can refer back to them, and write your answer in British English.

Some context. In Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974), Robert Nozick treats persons as bearers of rights that function as side-constraints — claims that may not be overridden even for the sake of minimising rights-violations overall. In a much-discussed aside, he asks what morality permits in our treatment of animals and floats the idea that our dealings with animals might be governed by something closer to a utilitarian calculus of suffering, while our dealings with persons are governed by strict Kantian constraints. A friend of mine, who admires Nozick, has constructed the following argument to show that this hybrid is unstable, and that Nozick's framework is pushed towards a conclusion nobody should accept:

(1) Rights-as-side-constraints cannot be grounded in species membership itself, since species membership is a biological accident and not a morally relevant property; whatever grounds the constraints must be some property that matters in its own right, such as rational agency or having a life of one's own.

(2) Any such property that is possessed by all the human beings we insist on protecting with side-constraints — including infants and the severely cognitively impaired — is also possessed by at least some non-human animals.

(3) Therefore at least some non-human animals are protected by genuine side-constraints, and not merely by a utilitarian requirement to count their suffering.

(4) If deer are protected by side-constraints, then a wolf that kills a deer violates the deer's rights: rights hold against the world, not only against responsible agents, and what violates a right is the deed, not the moral status of the doer.

(5) Where a rights-violation can be prevented at reasonable cost by those in a position to prevent it, justice requires that it be prevented; side-constraints generate correlative duties of protection, and Nozick's own minimal state exists precisely to discharge such duties.

(6) We are in a position to prevent predation on an enormous scale — culling predators, contraception, habitat management — at costs that are large but not obviously beyond what we already spend on things of far less moral weight.

(7) Therefore justice requires a programme of mass intervention against predation in the wild.

(8) No such programme is required; the conclusion is absurd.

My friend concludes, by modus tollens, that (3) is false: the move from (1) and (2) to (3) must fail, animals receive utilitarian consideration only, and Nozick's hybrid is vindicated. I am not persuaded the repair is that cheap, but I cannot locate the misstep myself. Please do the following. First, identify exactly which step or steps you reject, and for each rejected step explain both why it initially looked plausible and what rejecting it costs. Second, address three questions I keep circling: is (4) correct that rights hold against the world rather than only against agents, or does it quietly convert an agent-relative constraint into an agent-neutral requirement? Does (5) actually follow from a side-constraint view, or does it smuggle in a positive duty of beneficence that such views are designed to exclude? And if (2) stands, does Nozick's hybrid position collapse outright, or can something recognisably Nozickian survive? Third, order your diagnoses by how much of the original argument each preserves, so that I can see which repair is cheapest and what it abandons. If, on your analysis, the argument is valid and the real question is which premise to surrender, say so plainly and tell me which premise you would surrender and why.
