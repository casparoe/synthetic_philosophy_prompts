I sit on the ritual committee of an independent, traditionally observant Jewish congregation, and we are revising our prayer book. The contentious item is the morning recitation of the korbanot — the passages describing the daily and festival sacrifices in the Temple — together with the standing petitions that God restore the Temple service speedily in our days. A motion before the committee would cut the sacrificial passages entirely and replace the restoration petitions with language of gratitude that prayer has taken their place. The motion's sponsor argues from Maimonides: in the Guide of the Perplexed, Maimonides explains the sacrificial cult as a divine concession to a people habituated to pagan worship, a pedagogy that redirected rather than endorsed the ancient world's instinct to serve its gods through offerings, since commanding the sudden abandonment of all such worship would have been impossible for that generation. On this reading, petitioning for the restoration of sacrifice asks God to reinstate an accommodation we have long since outgrown. The motion's opponents reply that this same Maimonides codified the laws of sacrifice in full as binding law in the Mishneh Torah, ruled that the messianic king will rebuild the Temple, and himself fixed the petition for the restoration of the Temple service within the daily liturgy. They add the critique associated with Nachmanides: if sacrifice were only a weaning device, it is hard to explain why the patriarchs and Noah offered sacrifices before idolatry had spread, or why the Torah describes them as pleasing to God.

Draft the committee's decision memo recommending one of three outcomes: retain the passages and petitions unchanged, retain the passages while modifying the petitions, or adopt the motion as proposed. The memo should (a) state a clear bottom line the committee can actually vote on; (b) reconstruct, rather than merely name, the Maimonidean argument and assess whether a historical explanation of a commandment's origin can bear on present practice at all, given that Maimonides himself held that identifying the reasons for the commandments does not license their suspension; (c) take seriously the internal-tension objection — whether one may invoke the Guide against liturgy that the Mishneh Torah itself mandates — and say what would follow from reading Maimonides' two works either as consistent or as in real conflict; and (d) close with a paragraph giving the losing side the strongest version of its case, in terms its members could endorse.
