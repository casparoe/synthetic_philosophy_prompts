So I'm a novelist, mid-draft on a book set in the 1840s, and at dinner last week two friends of mine got into a fight about it that I keep turning over. Marta's a historian, Dev writes novels too. I'd love a move-by-move verdict, not just who "won." Also, please answer in American English.

My book, for context, centers a real minor figure who did one genuinely heroic thing, and I compress and polish a lot around it.

Marta's opening shot: the draft is basically Nietzsche's monumental history in costume — the past as a shelf of great exemplars meant to fire people up now — and that's the most dangerous mode for fiction specifically, because it smooths out the ugly and the merely ordinary, so readers end up loving a past that never existed and can't tell where the history stopped and the myth started.

Dev said she's assuming the novel owes history anything. Novels aren't in the history business; but if you're going to map them onto Nietzsche's three, fiction is naturally critical history — every narrator judges the past from the present whether they admit it or not, so better to drag the past before the tribunal openly than smuggle in nostalgia.

Marta said critical history in a novel curdles into presentism — characters sentenced for failing to be us — and Nietzsche's worry about the critical mode (it uproots people, leaves them unable to live with their own inheritance) hits fiction twice as hard, because a novel has to make its people livable-from-the-inside or they're cardboard.

Dev shot back that this leaves her defending the antiquarian mode, which is obviously the worst fit for a novel — museum-glass preservation, reverence, no conflict, the book as antiques roadshow.

Marta's last move, which kind of suprised me: Nietzsche never ranked the three modes, he dosed them — a healthy life needs all three in proportion — so asking which mode a novel *should* be is malformed. The real question is which mode the surrounding culture already has too much of, and since she'd say we're drowning in critical history, the monumental impulse in my draft might actually be the corrective — provided I do it honestly, whatever that means.

Who's right about what, at each step? Does Marta's final move quietly take back her first one? And what, if anything, are both of them missing — does any of this actually settle what I should do with the book?
