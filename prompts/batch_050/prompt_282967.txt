I'm putting together a seminar unit on the definition of death and I want to pressure-test something with you. Here's the practice as I understand it. Organ procurement in the US and a bunch of other places runs on the dead donor rule: vital organs may only be taken from donors who are already dead. Two routes to get there. Route one: brain death -- catastrophic brain injury, whole-brain criteria met, heart still beating on a ventilator, organs procured from a body that's warm and pink. Route two, donation after circulatory death: a patient with a devastating prognosis (but not brain dead) has life support withdrawn in line with their wishes, the heart stops, the team waits a no-touch period of somewhere between 2 and 5 minutes, declares death, and procures.

Now the wrinkles that keep bugging me. First: after 2-5 minutes of arrest, circulation is not *irreversible* -- in many cases you could restart it with CPR or ECMO; it just *won't* be restarted, partly because there's a DNR in place. People in this literature mark the difference by saying the operative standard is permanence (won't come back) rather than irreversibility (can't come back). Second wrinkle, and newer: some procurement programs now do normothermic regional perfusion -- after death has been declared by circulatory criteria, they restart circulation *inside the donor's body* on a bypass machine to keep the organs in good shape, but clamp off the vessels to the brain so it never gets blood again.

So: what does this whole apparatus presuppose about what death is? Does the no-touch period make any sense except as a proxy for waiting until the brain is gone -- and if so, is the circulatory criterion doing any real work of its own? Does the permanence reading presuppose that death is partly constituted by our intentions about what we'll do with the body, which is a weird thing for a biological fact to depend on? And does normothermic regional perfusion give the game away -- that the operative criterion was the brain all along, so the practice unofficially runs on a brain-centered view while officially citing a cardiopulmonary one? Also: do the two routes presuppose one unified concept of death, or does the system quietly operate with two different ones depending on which organs are needed?

Reconstruct the implicit theory of death the practice runs on. Separate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, tell me where its parts presuppose incompatible things (if they do), and say what concretely would have to change if we swapped in your preferred rival definition. And flag where the practice is just underdetermined, i.e. where several theories of death would license it exactly as it stands.
