I'm on the steering committee of a small interdisciplinary institute that funds work at the boundary of philosophy and behavioral economics, and this year we've earmarked funding for roughly four person-years of work on weakness of will -- specifically on the long-standing disconnect between how philosophers theorize akrasia (acting against one's better judgment, or against one's all-things-considered intention) and how economists and psychologists model self-control failures through present-biased, hyperbolic discounting of future rewards. The two literatures mostly ignore each other: philosophers tend to treat discounting experiments as psychology beside the conceptual point, while economists tend to treat the philosophical puzzles (can one act freely and intentionally against one's better judgment, and if so what explains the irrationality of doing so) as semantic housekeeping. I suspect some of the open questions on each side are actually the same question in different vocabularies, and some genuinely aren't.

Acting as a philosopher who is also fluent in behavioral economics, please give me a defended prioritization of the open questions at this intersection, framed as advice to our committee on where the four person-years should go. Consider, at minimum: whether hyperbolic discounting should be understood as a pattern of preferences (in which case acting on it may not be akratic at all, just intertemporal preference change) or as a mechanism of failure; whether the philosophical phenomenon of clear-eyed akrasia -- doing what one judges worse while fully appreciating that it is worse -- even occurs, or whether apparent cases are better redescribed as preference reversal, judgment shift, or rationalization; whether commitment devices (deadlines, deposits, Ulysses contracts) presuppose a picture of the self as divided across time, and what that picture implies for responsibility and blame; and whether the normative question of why one ought to act on one's better judgment has any answer that doesn't collapse into the triviality that one ought to do what one has most reason to do. You are welcome to add questions I've omitted or to argue that one of mine is ill-posed.

For each candidate question, I want three things: why it is upstream of other work in either literature (or both), how tractable it is with the tools philosophers and behavioral economists actually have rather than the tools they wish they had, and what finding or argument in the next few years would move it up or down your ranking. Then give the ordering you'd actually recommend and defend it against the obvious alternative orderings -- in particular, defend it against the objection that we should just fund whatever is most neglected, since neglectedness and importance often come apart. Please think through the dependencies between the questions carefully before settling the ranking, and begin your reply by stating your recommended ranking in a single sentence, with the defense following. Write your response as continuous prose, the way one would write a memo or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. And please do not cite papers or name specific studies beyond what's needed to identify a position -- I care about the structure of the problem space, not a bibliography.
