I am a third-year undergraduate taking a philosophy course on degrees of belief and rational choice. We have just discussed Dutch book arguments, but our seminar treated them as a clever arithmetic result rather than as something with real rational force. I want to run a 75-minute activity for about 24 students that makes them experience why incoherent confidence can be costly before anyone mentions probabilism or Dutch books. Design a game in which students act as market makers for propositions about concealed cards, urns, or a similarly manageable source of uncertainty: they privately state the prices at which they would buy and sell different bets, and an automated or facilitator-controlled counterparty can accept selected combinations of those offers. A later phase should introduce new information and conditional or sequential bets so that the distinction between coherence at a time and consistency across time can emerge naturally.

Please provide a genuinely runnable design, including roles, rules, payoff scheme, materials, timeline, worksheets, and concrete numerical examples that require little more than arithmetic. There should be no real-money stakes; use points, a small snack prize, or another incentive that makes decisions matter without encouraging reckless gambling. The mechanism must let the counterparty construct a guaranteed profit from a student’s own commitments without the facilitator merely declaring the prices irrational. At the same time, build in safeguards against the game becoming a lesson in spotting deliberate trickery too early: students should initially make what seem like reasonable local pricing decisions and only later see how those decisions compose. The name Dutch book and the standard probability axioms should not appear until the debrief.

The debrief should map specific moments from the game onto synchronic coherence, additivity, conditionalization or diachronic consistency, and the difference between a sure loss and ordinary bad luck. It should also distinguish a Dutch book from a money pump, explain the assumptions needed to connect degrees of belief with acceptable betting prices, and identify where that operational interpretation is philosophically contestable. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run—students misunderstanding buy versus sell prices, losing track of liabilities, overpricing emotionally salient outcomes, or coordinating to defeat the counterparty—and how the facilitator should respond. Finally, be candid about which feature of the philosophical argument the game cannot reproduce and must be supplied through discussion rather than play.
