You are a professional speechwriter with a doctorate in philosophy and a longstanding interest in the philosophy of love. Think carefully about the brief below before drafting anything. I'd like you to begin by privately brainstorming two or three possible structures for the toast, discard the weaker ones, and then show me a short rationale for the winner before giving me the speech itself.

Here is the occasion. Next month I'm giving a toast at the wedding of two friends who met as PhD students in the same philosophy department, and who famously disagree about the reasons of love — the debate between appraisal and bestowal views. In rough outline: appraisal views hold that love is a response to the beloved's valuable qualities (their wit, their kindness, their beauty), so that those qualities are among the reasons for loving; bestowal views hold that love is not a response to antecedent value but rather confers it — the beloved matters to the lover because they are loved, not the other way around. Each view carries a familiar cost. Appraisal seems to imply that one ought to "trade up" when someone with better qualities comes along, and that loving a person is really loving a replaceable bundle of features. Bestowal seems to make love arbitrary: if nothing about the beloved grounds the love, why this person rather than any other? (The room will know the standard names — Frankfurt is associated with the bestowal side, Velleman with something closer to appraisal, and Kolodny with the view that love is grounded in relationships rather than in either qualities or bare bestowal — but you shouldn't need to invoke them; the philosophers present will supply the footnotes themselves.)

The couple: she wrote her dissertation defending a bestowal view; he wrote his defending an appraisal view. Their first date was an argument about whether "I love you because you're kind" is a compliment or a category error. Their long-running joke is that each claims the other's dissertation commits them to not really loving anyone. Both halves of the family have been hearing versions of this argument at dinners for six years without fully understanding it.

The audience is about half philosophers, several of whom have published on this exact debate and will catch every error, and half relatives who have never heard the word "bestowal" used as a noun. The toast must land for both at once.

Constraints:

- Five minutes, so roughly 700 words.
- The toast must not take a side or declare a winner — and it must also not reach for the lazy resolution of saying they're both right in their own way. The disagreement should remain standing at the end, but transfigured somehow into something about this particular marriage.
- The appraisal/bestowal tension must do structural work: the shape of the speech should arise from it, not merely mention it in a passing joke.
- It must not imply anything either of them would consider false about their love — don't suggest she would trade up, don't suggest his love is ungrounded, and don't attribute to either of them a view they've argued against.
- Avoid standard wedding-toast machinery: no soulmates, no two-halves-completing-each-other (the picture of love as completion is one both of them have argued against in print, and the philosophers will laugh for the wrong reasons), no "may your love grow stronger every day."

Deliverable: first, 150–250 words explaining the structure you chose, which lines are pitched at which half of the room, and how you handled the constraint of leaving the debate unresolved without papering over it; then the toast itself.
