You are a professional epistemologist with particular expertise in the theory of rational belief revision and higher-order evidence. You have received the following email from a junior colleague, a psychologist who works on reasoning biases. Write a careful reply. Begin by brainstorming, for your own use, the main philosophical issues the email raises; then compose the reply itself, in which you should engage each of the colleague's concrete questions rather than merely gesturing at them.

Dear Professor,

I've been reading about the dogmatism paradox in epistemology, and it seems to me that the argument there establishes something practically important. As I understand it, the reasoning goes like this: if you know that p, then p is true, so any evidence you might later encounter that points against p must be misleading evidence — after all, it points away from the truth. But if you already know, in advance, that such evidence is misleading, then when it arrives you are rationally entitled to disregard it. So knowledge comes with a kind of built-in entitlement to dismiss future counterevidence. The paradox thus vindicates a principled form of intellectual stubbornness: once you know something, closing your mind to objections is not a failing but what rationality demands.

This strikes me as genuinely useful for my own research habits, and I want to operationalize it. Three questions. First, at what point does one's confidence in a theory cross the threshold into knowledge, so that the entitlement to dismiss counterevidence kicks in? Is there some credence level — 0.95, say — or is it rather a matter of the theory's track record of correct predictions? Second, once I'm past that threshold, should I simply stop reading critical responses to my work, or is the entitlement only to discount them, in which case I should still read them but assign them near-zero weight? Third, does the entitlement transfer via testimony? If I know that my framework is correct, can my students and collaborators rationally dismiss counterevidence to it on my say-so, even before they've examined the evidence themselves?

I realize the conclusion is called a "paradox," but as far as I can tell the argument for it is sound, and I take it philosophers call it a paradox only because its practical upshot offends against certain polite academic norms about open-mindedness — norms which, I suspect, the argument shows we should abandon.

Yours, etc.
