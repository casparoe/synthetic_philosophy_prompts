I got voluntold to run a 30-minute slot at our engineering book club next month (we read one philosophy-ish thing a quarter, nobody has any formal background, half the room is there for the free pizza). I picked Chesterton's fence, partly because I genuinely think it matters and partly out of annoyance: the version everyone in tech already "knows" is the Twitter version, which is basically "don't delete code you don't understand," and I'm convinced that version is almost wrong. It makes the whole thing sound like a local debugging heuristic when the actual claim is something stranger and stronger about why institutions and norms can encode reasons nobody currently remembers, and it also makes it sound like the fence argument says never remove fences, which it definately doesn't. So I want to teach the real thing to a room of skeptical senior engineers who will push back hard.

Don't explain Chesterton's fence to me -- plan how I should explain it. I want the sequence: what has to come first and what each later step depends on (e.g., do I need them to feel the difference between "we don't know the reason" and "there is no reason" before the fence example will land at all?). Tell me whether to use a code example anyway and handle it carefully, or go with something non-technical (a weird tradition, an odd law, an architectural oddity), and say what the example must include and must lack to carry the idea.
