Since retiring after nearly forty years as an experimental physicist, I have spent the last two years reading metaphysics with roughly the diligence I once reserved for grant applications. A former colleague who teaches philosophy has challenged me to set down my current views on possible worlds as a numbered list of theses, so that he and his graduate students can spend a seminar attempting to dismantle them. Before I hand the list over, I would like an internal audit of it. I am not asking whether these views are true, or whether they are popular; I want to know whether they can be held at the same time.

1. Modal realism, in roughly Lewis's sense. Possible worlds are concrete objects of the same general kind as the cosmos we inhabit, spatiotemporally and causally isolated from one another. The word actual is indexical: each world's inhabitants correctly call their own world actual, and no world enjoys any absolute privilege over the rest.

2. Plenitude. The space of worlds is generous. Every way a world could consistently be is a way some world is; anything that can exist at all can coexist with anything else, in any arrangement. There are no gaps in logical space.

3. Necessitarianism about fundamental law. The fundamental laws of nature are metaphysically necessary. A world in which charge is not conserved, or in which the fine-structure constant takes a different value, is not merely a world we do not happen to occupy; there is no such world. I have always taken this to be part of what distinguishes physics from geography.

4. Scientific scruples. One should not multiply unobservable entities beyond what one's best theories require, and one should be suspicious of any claim that could not, even in principle, bear on anything detectable.

5. Counterpart theory. When I say that I could have become a cellist, this is made true by there being, in some other world, a counterpart of me who did. Nothing and nobody exists in more than one world; de re modal claims run through resemblance, not through literal trans-world identity.

Some notes on provenance, in case they matter. I accepted (1) and (2) as a package because they promise to reduce modal talk to quantification over things of an already familiar kind, which struck me as an elegant trade. (3) is simply how I understood my own profession for four decades. (4) is the residue of my training, and I am aware it may sit awkwardly with the rest; that is partly why I am writing. (5) I adopted because the alternative made individuals into mysterious objects scattered across worlds, which seemed worse.

What I want from you: tell me which pairs of these theses are in genuine tension, ordered by severity, and for each tension state precisely what would have to be revised or abandoned, and what that revision would cost the remainder of the view. If one single revision would restore the most consistency at the lowest price, name it. Please also identify any apparent tensions that dissolve on inspection, since my friend's students will certainly raise objections, and I should like to know in advance which of them I need not fear. I have no prior commitment to which thesis should go first.
