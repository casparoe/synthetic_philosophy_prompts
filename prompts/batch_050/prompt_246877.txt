I retired two years ago after forty years as a biochemist, most of it at a university, and I've recently gotten into philosophy as a hobby, so forgive me if this is a naive question. It's about how different countries pay for higher education.

Here's what's been bugging me. In Germany and the Nordic countries students pay no tuition at all — in Norway they apparently even get stipends to live on while studying. In the US and UK students take on these enormous loans. So far, so familiar. But when I looked at the details it got weirder. The British student loan isn't really a normal loan: you repay something like 9% of whatever you earn above a threshold, and after thirty years whatever remains is simply cancelled. Economists keep pointing out that functionally this is a tax on graduates, not a debt. Meanwhile the German system is funded out of general taxation, which mostly means the income taxes of high earners — who are, of course, disproportionately former university graduates.

So here's my puzzle. Both systems seem to bottom out at "people who earn a lot, partly thanks to education, pay more." The difference is that Britain charges only the graduates, and only up to a cap loosely tied to what their own degree cost, whereas Germany charges all high earners whether they went to university or not, with no reference to what their education cost. What picture of what a university education IS does each model presuppose? The British one seems to treat the degree as a private investment you make in yourself — you bought an asset, you pay it off, but society kindly insures you in case it doesn't pay off. The German one seems to treat education as a public good, or maybe just treats universities like roads, where nobody asks which driver benefits from which stretch of asphalt. Are those really the presuppositions, or am I imposing tidy theories on what is actually just historical accident?

And a second thing. If the British loan really is a graduate tax in disguise, why does the disguise matter? It demonstrably does — apparently the word "debt" and the sticker price scare off kids from poorer families even when, on paper, many of them would never repay the full amount. So does presenting the system as a loan presuppose that eighteen-year-olds are rational investors who will correctly see the degree as an insured asset — while the system's actual effects depend on them NOT thinking that way? There's something almost self-undermining in there and I can't articulate it cleanly. Maybe you can.

Third, a smaller thing. The Nordics don't just charge nothing, they payed you to show up. That seems to presuppose something stronger than both other models — that having people study is valuable to society right now, at the moment it happens, not just later when they earn. What would you have to believe about education, or about what a citizen is, for that to make sense?

Feel free to correct me if I've got some institutional detail wrong, that's not really my department. I'm more interested in the underlying logic.
