A friend and I have been arguing about whether you can learn what's right or wrong simply by being told. She has an argument that seems to show moral testimony never gives you real knowledge, but I think it proves too much, and neither of us can find the weak step. Here it is. (1) A belief counts as knowledge only if the believer could, at least in principle, explain why the belief is true, rather than merely repeat it. (2) Someone who accepts a moral claim purely on another person's say-so cannot explain why it is true; the most they can offer is that a trustworthy person said so. (3) Therefore no purely testimonial moral belief is knowledge. (4) But premises (1) and (2) are not specific to morality: someone who accepts a historical date, a geographical fact, or a scientific result purely on testimony is in exactly the same position of being unable to explain why it is true. (5) So testimony never transmits knowledge anywhere, which is absurd — nearly everything I know came through testimony. (6) Therefore something in the argument is wrong. My friend wants to keep (3) — she thinks there really is something second-rate about taking your moral beliefs on trust — and she suspects the flaw is in (1) or in the generalization at (4). I suspect the flaw is elsewhere and that she will have to give up (3). Where does the argument actually go wrong, if it does? If the problem is (1), explain why it seemed plausible and what weaker requirement on knowledge could replace it. If the problem is (4), explain what could make moral understanding genuinely different from ordinary factual understanding, and whether that difference is strong enough to bear the weight.
