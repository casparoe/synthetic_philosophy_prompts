You are an ancient historian who moonlights as a writer of scholarly satire. Think carefully about the assignment before drafting, and begin by briefly listing, for your own use, six candidate criticisms the referee below might raise; then build the report around the strongest five.

The set-up. A respectable journal, the Mediterranean Antiquity Quarterly, has received an unsolicited manuscript: Herodotus's Histories, submitted as a new work. Write the referee report, in the voice of a reviewer formed by the severe tradition that descends from Thucydides: evidence should rest on the author's own autopsy or on the interrogation of eyewitnesses; chronology should be exact; informants should not be cited as authorities for their own hearsay; the marvellous should be excised rather than retailed. The report should be a perfectly deadpan professional document of 550 to 600 words, with no winking at the reader and no acknowledgement that the manuscript is two and a half millennia old; the comedy must arise from the genuine application of source-critical standards, not from anachronistic gags alone. The target is a style of historical discourse, and the ironies should remain legible to a reader who has never opened Herodotus.

The report must turn on the following passage — Book IV (Melpomene), §42 of The History of Herodotus, in G. C. Macaulay's translation of 1890 — in which the author describes the circumnavigation of Libya by Phoenicians sent out by the Egyptian king Necos:

"I wonder then at those who have parted off and divided the world into Libya, Asia, and Europe, since the difference between these is not small; for in length Europe extends along by both, while in breadth it is clear to me that it is beyond comparison larger; for Libya furnishes proofs about itself that it is surrounded by sea, except so much of it as borders upon Asia; and this fact was shown by Necos king of the Egyptians first of all those about whom we have knowledge. He when he had ceased digging the channel which goes through from the Nile to the Arabian gulf, sent Phenicians with ships, bidding them sail and come back through the Pillars of Heracles to the Northern Sea and so to Egypt. The Phenicians therefore set forth from the Erythraian Sea and sailed through the Southern Sea; and when autumn came, they would put to shore and sow the land, wherever in Libya they might happen to be as they sailed, and then they waited for the harvest: and having reaped the corn they would sail on, so that after two years had elapsed, in the third year they turned through the Pillars of Heracles and arrived again in Egypt. And they reported a thing which I cannot believe, but another man may, namely that in sailing round Libya they had the sun on their right hand."

At least one section of the report must wrestle with that final sentence. The referee cannot decide whether an author who publishes testimony he himself disbelieves is displaying scrupulous candour or merely laundering rumour through a disclaimer, and the report should let the reader feel the awkward fact that the one detail the author refuses to endorse — the sun on the right hand — is precisely the detail many modern commentators treat as the strongest evidence that the voyage took place. The referee is not permitted to resolve this tension, but must take it seriously as a methodological problem.

The remaining criticisms should track genuine historiographical issues rather than invented vices: the author's habit of giving two or more competing versions of an event and declining to adjudicate; his reliance on interested informants, such as Egyptian priests with a civilisational pedigree to defend; his inclusion of gold-digging ants, griffins and other marvels under a flag of agnosticism; the vagueness of his chronology; and his claims to autopsy in places he may only describe. Where the referee's severity inadvertently exposes a real strength of the manuscript — the refusal to suppress distrusted evidence, the careful separation of what was seen from what was merely heard — let that emerge only as a grudging concession, or as something the referee fails to notice.

After the report, append two items. First, a table with one row per criticism and four columns — Referee criticism | Genuine historiographical issue it encodes | Best available defence of the author | Referee's recommendation (accept / minor revisions / major revisions / reject). Secondly, a short out-of-character note of five or six sentences in which you state how confident you are in the historiographical picture the satire presupposes, and single out the one point on which you are least sure — for instance, where the report leans on a contested reading of Herodotean or Thucydidean practice, or flattens a live scholarly disagreement for comic effect.

Write in British English throughout.
