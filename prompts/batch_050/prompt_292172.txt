I'm in a philosophy of biology seminar, and for the unit on biological individuality the instructor has asked each of us to write down our "organism profile" -- where we think the boundaries of organisms lie in the hard cases -- so that the group can try to break it. I've been going around in circles on this for a week, because every time I defend one case with a principle, that principle seems to give me a verdict I don't want somewhere else. Here is where I've landed:

(1) A honeybee colony is literally a single organism -- a superorganism, not a metaphorical one -- because its members are as functionally integrated and as mutually dependent as the cells of a body, and the colony behaves like a coherent, goal-directed whole. (2) A human being plus their gut microbiome is not a single organism: the microbes are passengers or at most partners, and my boundaries stop at my own cells. (3) A Portuguese man o' war, despite being a colony of specialized zooids, is clearly one organism and not a society of organisms. (4) A single coral head is one organism, but a coral reef -- thousands of corals plus sponges, algae, and fish in dense ecological interaction -- is not, no matter how integrated the reef's nutrient cycling becomes. (5) To be an organism is, at bottom, to be a unit that selection can act on as a whole: organisms are Darwinian individuals, things that reproduce with enough fidelity for lineages to evolve.

My sense is that the trouble is hiding in how (1), (2), and (3) relate, but I can't pin it down. Please audit the set: tell me which commitments are in genuine tension and how severe each tension is, which pairs only look like they conflict but actually dissolve on inspection, and what the cheapest single revision would be -- i.e., which one commitment, modified or dropped, restores the most coherence with the least collateral damage to the others. If it turns out I'm quietly running two different criteria of organismhood and switching between them case by case, name them and show me where the switch happens.
