I am a novelist serving as an outside marker for an undergraduate philosophy of mind seminar. Grade the following response out of 10, giving point-by-point comments that identify its sound distinctions, conceptual imprecisions, unsupported inferences, and any misattributions. Philosophical accuracy should matter more than prose style.

Question:

In my novel, a municipal clinic employs an intake machine that sorts patients by symptoms and prints DANGER for some cases. Its designers chose its classifications, but the machine now operates without their supervision and often detects patterns that no individual programmer anticipated. A philosopher in the story claims that the machine’s states have only derived intentionality, whereas a nurse’s judgment that a patient is in danger has original intentionality. Explain how Dennett’s two-bitser can be used to challenge this Searlean distinction. Your answer should distinguish derived content from mere causal reliability, original intentionality from consciousness, and syntactic symbol manipulation from semantic understanding. It should also state what Searle’s Chinese Room argument is intended to establish and assess whether evolution can serve as a source of content in the relevant sense.

Student’s answer:

Searle holds that mental states possess intrinsic or original intentionality, while inscriptions and artifacts possess only intentionality conferred by intentional agents. The two-bitser seems to support the distinction: its sorting state means quarter because its designers and users treat it as a coin detector. If its designers disappeared, the machine might continue to sort coins, but its states would retain their content only because of that prior design history.

Dennett’s point is not simply that machines can be reliable. Human intentional capacities are themselves the products of a design process, namely natural selection. Thus, if an artifact’s content is derived because a design history explains its function, human content is derived too; and if human content remains original, design history alone cannot settle whether content is merely derived. On Dennett’s view, original intentionality is not a special intrinsic ingredient. Content attribution depends instead on functional organization, history, and interpretive success.

The Chinese Room is intended to show that implementing a formal symbol-manipulation program is not by itself sufficient for understanding, not to prove that no machine can understand. Searle can therefore allow that a brain is a machine while denying that correct output alone supplies semantics. Additional causal embedding, learning history, and interaction with an environment might be relevant.

There remains an ambiguity in the term original. If it means non-derived, evolution cannot literally lend content because it is not an intentional agent, so the analogy with the two-bitser may fail. If it means independent of any external standpoint, Dennett would reject that requirement. But if original intentionality requires consciousness, then even a nurse’s merely dispositional belief while asleep would not count, so Searle must connect it to occurrent conscious states.

The clinic machine has derived intentionality because every representational state must be interpreted by a person on each occasion of use. Consequently, Searle’s distinction survives Dennett’s objection, although the Chinese Room does not establish that all computational understanding is impossible.
