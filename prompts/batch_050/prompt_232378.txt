I'm in a reading group on the philosophy of time, and this week we're on Gödel's 1949 papers -- his cosmological solution to Einstein's field equations (a rotating, non-expanding universe permeated with closed timelike curves) and the short companion essay where he draws the famous conclusion that the lapse of time is "ideal" rather than real. I find the standard reconstruction of his argument baffling, and I want to pressure it in a short paper.

As I understand it, Gödel's inference from the rotating universe to the ideality of time only works if the rotating model is taken to describe, or at least approximate, our actual universe -- since the conclusion is about time as such, not about time in some hypothetical cosmos. But ours is expanding, and as far as I know there is no observational evidence for the kind of global rotation his model requires, and Gödel himself reportedly checked astronomical data for signs of rotation and came up empty. So the argument seems to collapse into: in a universe we don't live in, under conditions that don't obtain, time wouldn't be real. Why should that tell us anything about time here? It looks to me like philosophers have been far too generous in treating this as a serious argument for the ideality of time rather than a curiosity about an exotic spacetime that happens to satisfy the field equations. And if the charitable response is that Gödel was really just showing time travel to be physically possible, that seems even worse: possible time travel is a premise most people in the philosophy of time debate would contest, not a result.

Please explain where, if anywhere, my reconstruction goes wrong. In particular, I'd like to know whether Gödel's argument actually requires his universe to resemble ours, what work the notion of the *laws* being shared between his model and ours is doing, and what the strongest version of the worry I'm raising would be if it's aimed at the right target. Please write your answer in American English.
