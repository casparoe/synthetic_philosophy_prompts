I'm a philosophy graduate student, and next month I've been asked to lead one ninety-minute session in a second-year undergraduate course on philosophy of mind. The students have just spent two weeks on the hard problem of consciousness: they broadly understand why a purely physical description of a brain seems to leave something unexplained, though their grip on this is fragile and mostly slogan-level. My instructor wants the session to introduce panpsychism, but she was emphatic that the session must end on its central difficulty, the combination problem, because she thinks that difficulty is the intellectually honest reason to take panpsychism seriously rather than the embarrassment it is usually presented as. Her framing, which I want to preserve, is that the combination problem is structurally a mirror of the very problem panpsychism was invented to solve — and that students who grasp this have understood something deep, while students who leave thinking panpsychism is the view that electrons have opinions have understood nothing.

Design the explanatory arc of the session. Tell me the order in which the components have to be introduced and what depends on what: in particular, I suspect the students need a reasonably clear picture of why anyone would be a panpsychist in the first place (what it is about physics' grip on the world that leaves the intrinsic nature of matter open) before the combination problem can even be felt as a problem rather than heard as a trivial gotcha, but I'm open to being corrected on the sequencing. Identify the single carrying example the session should be built around — something that makes vivid the difference between ordinary aggregation, where wholes really are just sums of parts arranged a certain way (a pile of sand, a glass of water), and whatever it would take for many small subjects to constitute one unified subject like the student sitting in the seminar room. Say what the example must contain and, just as importantly, what it must not smuggle in, since most illustrations I can think of either beg the question by helping themselves to a perceiver of the whole or reduce the problem to mere addition of experience-stuff.

Then give me the misconception map: the predictable errors these students will bring or form, in roughly the order they will surface during the session, and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact. I anticipate at least three: that panpsychism attributes human-like mentality to fundamental particles; that the combination problem is just the question of how tiny amounts of experience add up to a large amount (a problem of quantity rather than of subjecthood); and that neuroscience's binding problem is the same difficulty, so whatever solves one solves the other. Finally, tell me what check at the end of the session would show the mirror-image point has actually landed — some question or task whose successful handling requires the students to see the structural parallel rather than recite it — and tell me what I should deliberately leave out at this level (I'm tempted by cosmopsychism, by the idea that experiences rather than subjects are fundamental, and by appeals to quantum entanglement) while signaling honestly that these exist as live responses rather than pretending the problem is either solved or hopeless.
