I'm a PhD student in political theory and I'm stuck on a chapter about secession, so help me think through a case. I keep going back and forth between the two big families of theories: remedial-right views (a group has a right to unilateral secession only as a remedy for serious injustice -- annexation, persistent discrimination, breached autonomy agreements, constitutional channels genuinely blocked) and primary-right views (any territorially concentrated group that democratically opts for independence has a presumptive right to it, no special grievance needed). Take the Catalan bid of 2017 as the case. The facts I think matter, tell me if I'm wrong on any: a pro-independence majority in the Catalan parliament after the 2015 election, which they explicitly framed as a plebiscite on independence; the October 1 referendum ruled illegal by Spain's Constitutional Court before it happened; roughly 90% yes on about 43% turnout, with some polling stations disrupted by police; Puigdemont's October 10 declaration of independence, suspended in the same speech; Madrid invoking Article 155, dissolving the parliament, and calling snap elections; several leaders ending up jailed or in exile. Also two background facts: Catalonia is a net fiscal contributor to Spain, and the Spanish constitution's amendment rules mean the region's status can only be changed with statewide approval, so a statewide majority can in principle veto forever.

Do two things. First, the disciplined mapping: for each theory, what were the morally relevant options of the Catalan government and of Madrid in the fall of 2017, what did each theory say they should have done at the moment of the suspended declaration, and which features of the episode each theory explains well versus misdescribes or goes silent on. Second, the part I actually care about: I've been playing with a distinction between reparable and structural grievances. A reparable grievance is one a good-faith central government could fix without the state dissolving itself -- fiscal arrangements, language policy, devolved powers. A structural one is baked into the constitutional order itself, like an amendment rule that lets a permanent statewide majority block the region's preferred status by the very means the theory counts as legitimate. My hunch is that remedial-right theories look strong on Catalonia until you notice the structural grievance is real, at which point they collapse into either "wait forever" or primary-right in disguise -- but maybe that collapse is too fast. Use the distinction to say which theory actually handles 2017 better, including whether the 43% turnout is a defeater for primary-right views or just morally irrelevant to them.

I don't want a survey of positions, I've read those. Commit: give me your verdict on which theory gives the better assessment of the October bid and of Madrid's response, run the analysis back the other way (what does the episode reveal as each theory's weak point), and defend your verdict against the strongest obvious objection to it.
