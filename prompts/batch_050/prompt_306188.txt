I teach a second-year undergraduate philosophy of perception module, and I am writing this summer's exam. Every year I put something on Molyneux's problem, and every year I get the same disappointing essays: students retell the letter to Locke, say that Locke and Berkeley answered 'no' for empiricist reasons, mention that modern cataract-removal cases seem to vindicate them, and stop. Almost nobody engages with what would actually have to be true for the answer to go one way or the other. I want a question that makes that evasion impossible.

The angle I have in mind is this. Suppose a newly sighted adult, blind from birth, is shown a cube and a sphere and immediately names them correctly. Students tend to think this settles the matter in favour of a 'yes'. I want them to confront why it might not: perhaps the subject has inferred rather than seen (she knows, as a piece of common knowledge, which object looks which way); perhaps the question was about whether vision and touch share a single shape concept, not about whether one can draw inferences across the modalities; perhaps Leibniz's amendment to the original question already conceded the interesting ground. Conversely, if the subject fails, does that show the modalities never share spatial content, or only that her vision is not yet functioning as normal vision does? I want a question that forces students to state precisely which claim each experimental outcome would and would not establish, and to distinguish at least two different propositions the original question has been taken to ask.

Please write: (1) one essay question on this material, answerable in 45 minutes, pitched so that a student who has merely memorised Locke's and Berkeley's positions cannot score above a bare pass; (2) a model answer of roughly 600 words; (3) a marking scheme out of 20, built around five must-hit points with marks attached, where the scheme names the specific moves that separate a first-class answer from an upper-second, and notes in one line per point the characteristic way weaker candidates fumble it. The question, model answer, and scheme should all be in British English.
