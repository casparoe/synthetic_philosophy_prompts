I teach an introductory aesthetics course at a small college, and we are about to spend two weeks on aesthetic testimony: the question of whether you can properly form an aesthetic judgment of a work -- that a film is moving, that a melody is tense, that a poem is clumsy -- on the basis of someone else's say-so, or whether genuine aesthetic judgment requires firsthand acquaintance with the work itself. My students reliably say the right things about this debate while plainly not feeling why anyone has ever cared about it. I want one 75-minute session, with roughly 18 students, built around an activity or game that makes them feel the asymmetry in their own experience before we ever name it.

Design the activity. The broad shape I have in mind is that some judgments in the room should be made on the basis of other people's descriptions or reports, and some on the basis of direct encounter, and the difference between those two positions should become uncomfortable for the people in the first one -- but I am open to a better structure if you have one. The rules, materials, and any stakes should be specified concretely enough that I can run it the first time without improvising: what the objects of judgment are (keeping in mind a classroom with a projector, speakers, and no budget), who does what at each stage and in what order, how long each stage lasts, and what kind of small stakes or scoring, if any, would sharpen the experience without real money or grades. The crucial constraint is that the point has to emerge from what participants do and feel during the activity, not from anything I say during it. I do not want to explain the acquaintance-first intuition up front or tip off the testimony-side participants that they are supposed to feel undermined; ideally the moment of recognition -- for instance, discovering that a judgment you hold confidently and may even have gotten right still feels somehow unearned or unusable when someone presses you on it, or that you keep cheating by trying to see the thing yourself -- should land on them in the middle of play.

Then give me a debrief plan: the questions I should ask, in order, to get students to articulate what happened to them; how to map specific moments of the activity onto the actual positions in the debate (the claim that aesthetic testimony is simply illegitimate versus the subtler claim that it can transmit knowledge but not judgment, versus the optimist's denial that there is any real asymmetry at all); and how to connect what they felt to the analogies and disanalogies with moral and historical testimony, since students always raise those. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this -- students gaming the scoring, the testimony side quietly peeking, the whole thing collapsing into a memory test rather than a judgment test, or whatever else you foresee -- and how to handle each failure mode. Finally, be honest about which feature of the philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in discussion: for example, whether what participants feel is anything more than the difference between seeing and being told, or whether it really tracks the distinctively aesthetic norm the literature is about.

I would like your answer written as continuous prose, the way one would write a long email to a colleague -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Walk me through the design, the rationale, the debrief, the failure modes, and the limitations in that order, but in ordinary paragraphs.
