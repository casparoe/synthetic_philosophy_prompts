So I cover physics and philosophy of science for a general-audience magazine, and I keep getting pitched pieces on the many-worlds interpretation, and every time I end up stuck on the same thing: where do the probabilities come from? Here's the situation as I understand it, correct me where I've got it wrong. In Everettian quantum mechanics there's no collapse, everything that can happen does happen in some branch, the wavefunction evolves deterministically, end of story. But then what could it possibly mean to say that the Born rule gives the right probabilities? If every outcome occurs, saying one outcome is "more likely" seems to have no content. And yet Everettians claim they can recover the Born rule, and their critics claim they're cheating.

The positions as I understand them: Deutsch and Wallace say you can derive the Born rule from decision theory -- a rational agent in an Everett universe, constrained by rationality axioms and the structure of quantum mechanics, has to bet according to the squared amplitudes, and that's all probability ever was anyway. Zurek has his envariance argument, where symmetries of entangled states supposedly force the Born weights. Carroll and Sebens go the self-locating uncertainty route: after a measurement but before you look, you're uncertain which branch you're on, and a principle they call the Epistemic Separability Principle makes you distribute credence by branch measure. On the other side you've got people like Kent and Albert saying the whole thing is confused -- either there's no coherent notion of probability at all in a branching universe (the "incoherence problem"), or even if there is, nothing in the physics privileges the Born measure over any other measure, like one that counts branches (the "quantitative problem"). The Everettians reply that branch-counting is a non-starter because branches aren't discrete countable objects, and the critics reply that this just shows the Everettians are helping themselves to a measure they haven't earned.

My working hypothesis, which I'm floating for a piece: both sides are actually winning, but at different games. The Everettians have solved the practical question -- how should a rational agent in a branching universe bet -- and the critics have won the metaphysical point that no amount of decision theory turns squared amplitudes into genuine chances that drop out of the physics itself. So the debate keeps "stalling" because neither side will say which question is the real one, and maybe that's because answering it would require settling what probability is in general, which is its own hundred-year swamp. But maybe I'm wrong and there's a real formal disagreement buried in there -- e.g. whether the decision-theoretic axioms smuggle in the Born measure, or whether the epistemic principles Sebens and Caroll invoke are question-begging.

What I want is a diagnosis of why this debate has gone in circles for decades, not another explainer of the interpretations. Is this a substantive disagreement about physics, a verbal one about the word "probability," a clash of bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for, or are the two camps just answering different questions under one heading? Engage with what each side actually argues, not a caricature. And then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis -- a theorem, a sharper distinction, a concession one side could afford to make -- or make the case that no progress is possible and the whole problem is ill-posed. I'd rather get a sharp diagnosis I have to push back on than a balanced survey that leaves me where I started.
