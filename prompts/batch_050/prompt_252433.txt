Since retiring from my chemistry professorship I have joined a reading group of fellow retired scientists — physicists, engineers, one geologist — and we have lately been working through philosophy of mind. At our next meeting it falls to me to introduce the question of whether an ant colony could have a mind: the idea, familiar from Hofstadter's figure of the conscious ant colony Aunt Hillary, that mental properties might arise from the organization of many simple interacting parts rather than from the stuff the parts are made of, so that neurons need not be the only permissible substrate.

My audience's difficulty will not be with emergence in general — these are people who happily accept that temperature is nothing over and above molecular motion — but with the step from collective behavior to collective mentality. They will grant that a colony forages, allocates labor, and regulates its nest as a unit, and they will still balk at saying the colony thinks or experiences anything. I want a plan for the explanation itself, not the explanation: the conceptual sequence that gets them from emergence they already accept to the genuinely contentious claim, and the design of that sequence matters to me more than eloquence in delivery.
