A colleague of mine in metaphysics has, over several years of conversation, given me a strikingly stable set of verdicts about grounding — about which facts obtain in virtue of which others. I have logged the following:

1. The existence of the singleton set {Socrates} is grounded in Socrates's existence, and the grounding runs strictly from member to set, never the reverse.
2. The disjunctive fact that snow is white or grass is pink is grounded in snow's being white; the false disjunct, he says, contributes nothing.
3. The conjunctive fact that snow is white and grass is green is grounded in its two conjuncts taken together; he explicitly denies that either conjunct alone grounds it.
4. The rose's being red is grounded in its being scarlet; asked whether its being scarlet might instead be grounded in its being red, he called the suggestion explanatorily backwards.
5. The truth of the proposition that snow is white is grounded in snow's being white, not in any fact about propositions, sentences, or minds.
6. The fact that there are infinitely many primes is, in his view, flatly ungrounded: purely mathematical necessities, he holds, obtain in virtue of nothing at all.

Last week a new case came up in our reading group and I realized I could not predict his answer: the disjunctive fact that Socrates exists or two plus two equals four. Since Socrates's existence suffices for the disjunction, does this fact have a ground — and is Socrates's existence that ground — or is it instead ungrounded on the grounds that it is a necessary truth?

Please do the following, treating his logged verdicts as your only evidence about his views. First, predict his verdict on the new case and identify which single logged verdict is its closest analog. Second, formulate at least two general rules about grounding, each consistent with all six logged verdicts, that deliver opposite verdicts on the new case; for each rule, state what it predicts and why someone might find it independently attractive as a principle about grounding. Third, say which rule you judge to fit the overall pattern of his verdicts better, and why. Fourth, describe one further case — not the new case itself and not a trivial re-dressing of it — whose verdict would most sharply discriminate between your two rules, and explain what each rule would say about it.

Lead with your predicted verdict in the first sentence and then give the reasoning; no preamble, and do not restate the setup.
