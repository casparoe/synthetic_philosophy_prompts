I am preparing a sixty-minute seminar for museum conservators and registrars on the philosophical problem of material constitution. They know a great deal about restoration, fabrication, and object records, but have no background in metaphysics. By the end, I want them to understand why a statue and the clay composing it can seem both to be one object and to have different histories and possible fates, and to see that constitution is at least prima facie distinct from identity, composition, and mere similarity.

Plan how I should teach this rather than presenting the full seminar itself. Specify the dependency structure: which distinctions must be secured first, when the statue-and-clay case should be introduced, and why examples involving replacement of parts should come only after the basic problem is intelligible. Design one central museum case to carry the session—perhaps a modeled clay work that is later crushed while the clay is retained, or a better case of your own—and state what the case must contain, what it must avoid, and how it should support discussion of sameness without presupposing the coincidence theory.

Map the predictable misunderstandings in the order they are likely to arise: that the statue is just the clay’s shape, that constitution simply means being made of the same parts, that two coincident objects would have to be physically interpenetrating duplicates, that differing survival conditions settle nonidentity without argument, and that the whole issue is merely about how we choose to use the word statue. For each, distinguish a genuine conceptual error from an intuition that supports a respectable rival position, and indicate exactly where in the teaching sequence it should be addressed. Include a brief comparison of the coincidence, phase-sortal, and four-dimensionalist responses, but only to the extent needed for this audience.

Finish with one exercise or cataloguing question that would reveal whether participants have grasped the problem rather than merely adopted a preferred solution, and identify what technical material—such as counterpart theory, relative identity, or the grounding problem—I should explicitly leave for another occasion.
