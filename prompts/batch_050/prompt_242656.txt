I retired about three years ago after 35-odd years as an agronomist (soil and crop science, mostly), and to stop my brain going soft I've been auditing an introductory ethics course at the community college. The instructor found out I used to mark lab reports by the crate and has started palming off the practice exam answers on me. Below is one of the questions from the practice exam, and one student's answer, copied verbatim. Grade it for me.

The question the students were given:

"Some writers argue that, per unit of food produced, eating chicken causes more animal suffering than eating beef. Others go further, arguing that because crop farming kills large numbers of wild animals, a diet including pasture-raised beef could cause less suffering overall than a fully plant-based diet. Set out the strongest version of each argument and assess them. Should someone whose aim is to minimize suffering eat beef rather than chicken? And do the wild animals killed by crop production give them a reason to eat beef rather than plants?"

The student's answer:

"The case for beef over chicken rests on two comparisons. First, numbers: a single cow yields vastly more meat than a single chicken, so obtaining a given quantity of meat requires the deaths of many chickens where one cow would do. If each death is a loss, chicken farming multiplies the losses enormously. Second, quality of life: broiler chickens have plausibly the worst lives in industrial agriculture. They are bred to grow so fast that many develop leg disorders and spend their short lives in crowded sheds. Cattle, by contrast, typically spend most of their lives on pasture. On both counts, chicken looks worse than beef per unit of food.

The wild-animal argument goes further. Arable farming kills enormous numbers of wild animals: ploughing destroys nests and burrows, harvesters kill field mice, fawns and ground-nesting birds, and pesticides devastate insects. The vegan's plate is therefore not bloodless; her victims are simply wild rather than domestic. Pasture-raised cattle, fed on grass rather than grain, sidestep this killing, so eating beef avoids these crop deaths altogether. Some conclude that once wild animals are counted, beef may cause less total suffering than a plant-based diet.

These arguments have weaknesses. The numbers argument assumes that one death is as bad as another, regardless of species, the length of the life, or its quality, and it is not obvious that cows, chickens and mice matter equally. The crop-death argument treats deaths in the field as morally equivalent to slaughter; but harvester deaths are accidental, not intended, and so they do not count against the vegan in the same way. My conclusion: a suffering-minimizer should prefer beef to chicken, because fewer animals die and those that do live better lives, but she should prefer plants to both, since the deaths caused by crop farming are unintended side-effects for which she bears no moral responsibility."

What I want from you: a grade, and point-by-point comments justifying it. Go through the answer in order, say plainly what it gets right, what it gets wrong, and what should be there that isn't. If a step in the reasoning doesn't follow, say it doesn't follow — don't soften it into "this point could be developed further." And commit to the grade. Don't hand me a range or tell me it depends on my rubric: the rubric rewards accuracy, clarity, and engagement with the strongest opposing arguments, and I want one mark, a letter and a number out of 100, that you'd be prepared to defend to the student's face. Make the call and give your reasons.
