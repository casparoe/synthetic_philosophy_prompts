I teach early modern political philosophy, and I am currently preparing the materials for a graduate seminar session devoted to Chapter V of John Locke's Second Treatise of Government. The famous paragraphs — self-ownership, the mixing of labour, the "enough, and as good" proviso — I can handle, and my students reliably find them provocative on their own. What I keep stumbling over is a passage a few sentences later, in §28, which is quoted far less often and which strikes me as quietly doing something strange. Here it is, in full:

"We see in commons, which remain so by compact, that it is the taking any part of what is common, and removing it out of the state nature leaves it in, which begins the property; without which the common is of no use. And the taking of this or that part, does not depend on the express consent of all the commoners. Thus the grass my horse has bit; the turfs my servant has cut; and the ore I have digged in any place, where I have a right to them in common with others, become my property, without the assignation or consent of any body. The labour that was mine, removing them out of that common state they were in, hath fixed my property in them."

What I would like help understanding is the last sentence — "the labour that was mine" — read against the cases Locke has just listed. In the ore case, the first-person phrasing is unproblematic: I dug, so the labour is mine in the straightforward sense of §27, where labour is the unquestionable property of the labourer because each person has a property in his own person. But in the turf case, the cutting was done by my servant, and in the grass case, the "removal" was effected by my horse, which does not seem capable of labouring in the morally loaded sense at all. Yet Locke slides all three under the single formula "the labour that was mine," as though nothing of consequence had happened. So my questions are these. First, what exactly has to be true about the relation between me and my servant for his cutting to count as my labour — does the passage presuppose that labour, or at least its appropriating effects, is alienable by wage or by contract of service, and if so, does that sit in tension with §27's grounding of labour-ownership in self-ownership, which sounds like precisely the sort of thing one cannot transfer? Second, is the horse case merely a rhetorical flourish, or does it show that what grounds acquisition is not really labour as such but something like the extension of one's agency through instruments one owns — and would that reading quietly collapse the labour theory into a prior theory of ownership (of servants' time, of horses) rather than a foundation for it? Third, if the appropriating work can be delegated in this way, does the "enough, and as good" proviso constrain the servant's act, the master's accumulation, or both — and does Locke give us the resources to say?

I am not asking for a survey of the secondary literature — I can assemble that myself. What I want is a careful, text-sensitive reconstruction of what is going on in this paragraph: whether "the labour that was mine" is a harmless ellipsis, a revealing slip, or a window into a more considered view of Locke's about delegated labour, and what each of those options commits him to elsewhere in the chapter. If you think the puzzle dissolves on a better reading of §27's self-ownership premise, or that it instead exposes a genuine instability between the labour-mixing account and the realities of hired work, please show your reasoning from the text rather than from general impressions of Locke.
