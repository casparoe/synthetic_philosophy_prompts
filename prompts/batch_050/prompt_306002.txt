A friend of mine who's way too into decision theory hit me with this argument last week and it's been bugging me. It starts from the Pasadena game: a fair coin gets flipped until it lands heads, and if the first heads comes on flip n, you win (-1)^(n+1) * 2^n / n dollars. So flip 1 pays $2, flip 2 loses you $2, flip 3 pays $8/3, flip 4 loses $4, and so on. The probability-weighted terms work out to 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which only converges conditionally, so by the Riemann rearrangment theorem you can shuffle the terms to make it sum to literally anything. Hence no well-defined expected value. Fine. But here's where he takes it:

(1) The rational way to compare two gambles is by their values, and a gamble's value is its expected value when it has one.
(2) If a gamble has no expected value, there's no number that measures how good it is, and without that number there's no rational basis for ranking it against anything else -- not against a sure thing, and not against another expectation-less gamble. Call this: undefined expectation means no rational comparison.
(3) The Pasadena game has no expected value (the series is only conditionally convergent).
(4) Now define Pasadena-plus: exact same coin, exact same flips, but every single outcome pays $1 more than the corresponding Pasadena outcome.
(5) Pasadena-plus also has no expected value, since its series is just the same conditionally convergent mess shifted around.
(6) By (2), then, there is no rational basis for preferring Pasadena-plus over plain Pasadena. Turning down the free upgrade is a rationally permissible choice.
(7) But Pasadena-plus pays strictly more than Pasadena in every possible outcome of the coin flips, no exceptions.
(8) So it can be rational to turn down an option that pays you strictly more money no matter what happens.

He says this shows statewise dominance reasoning is overstated and philosophers lean on it too casually. I'm pretty sure (8) is nuts -- if you can prefer strictly less money in every outcome, the word "rational" has stopped meaning anything. But I also can't just assert (6) is wrong, because his case for (2) honestly sounds like textbook expected utility theory to me. If I had to keep things, I'd most want to keep dominance (the principle behind rejecting (8)) and the claim that Pasadena really has no expectation -- those feel non-negotiable.

So: which step do I actually reject, why does that step look so plausible given how decision theory is usually taught, and what does rejecting it cost me? If the fix means rational comparison between gambles is only a partial ordering, tell me what concretely breaks when I give up completeness.
