The question set was:

Noether's theorem and explanation by symmetry

(a) State Noether's first theorem, making clear what its antecedent conditions are and precisely what it delivers.

(b) It is often said that Noether's theorem explains the conservation laws of physics by tracing them back to symmetries. Assess this claim. Your answer should address:

(i) what, if anything, a theorem of pure mathematics can explain about contingent physical fact;

(ii) the direction of the explanatory relation between symmetry principles and conservation laws: does a symmetry explain the conservation, could the conservation instead be evidence for the symmetry, or is 'explanation' the wrong relation between them altogether;

(iii) whether the connection between symmetry and conservation holds in the same way for global symmetries and for local (gauge) symmetries, illustrating your answer with the conservation of electric charge.

The student's answer:

(a) Noether's first theorem says that to every continuous symmetry of a system's action — every transformation of the dynamical variables, built up from infinitesimal ones, that leaves the action invariant — there corresponds a conserved current: a quantity whose divergence vanishes whenever the equations of motion are satisfied, and hence a charge that remains constant in time along physically possible trajectories. The standard examples are spatial translation invariance giving conservation of momentum, time-translation invariance giving conservation of energy, and rotational invariance giving conservation of angular momentum. Conversely, every conservation law of a theory derives from such a symmetry, so the theorem sets up a one-to-one correspondence between the symmetries of a theory and its conserved quantities.

(b)(i) The theorem is a piece of mathematics, so on its own it can explain nothing about the actual world; what it establishes is conditional: if the laws governing a system possess a given symmetry, then a corresponding quantity is conserved. Whatever explanatory force the symmetry-to-conservation link has must therefore come from the contingent premise that our actual laws exhibit the symmetries in question. What the theorem adds is that, granted the premise, the conservation is no accident but follows from the structure of the dynamics.

(ii) On the direction of explanation, the natural reading is that the symmetry explains the conservation and not the reverse. One way of making this precise is to treat symmetry principles as meta-laws — laws about the first-order laws rather than about particular happenings — which constrain what the laws of motion can be and thereby explain them in something like the way that laws explain particular facts. The reverse direction, conservation explaining symmetry, seems to me to get things backwards: the conserved quantity is a bookkeeping device, whereas the symmetry is a structural feature of the dynamics. A different response would deny that a mathematical result can explain anything physical at all, treating the theorem as merely displaying a correlation within the formalism; but that seems to understate how much physical information the antecedent of the conditional carries.

(iii) The connection holds for gauge symmetries just as it does for global ones, and electric charge is the standard illustration. Electrodynamics is invariant under local redefinitions of the phase of the electron field, compensated by gauge transformations of the electromagnetic potential; applying Noether's theorem to this gauge symmetry yields the conserved electric current, and so gauge invariance explains why charge is conserved. Global and gauge symmetries differ in other respects — gauge symmetry is often said to be a mere redundancy of description — but both generate conserved quantities in the same way, so the explanation of charge conservation by symmetry goes through.
