So I'm a retired chemist (40 years in materials science, now mostly reading philosophy books on my porch and annoying my wife with questions) and I've been chewing on something about prostitution law. Bear with me.

From what I gather, different countries have landed on very different setups. Sweden and the Nordic countries criminalize buying sex but not selling it -- the seller is treated as a victim and offered exit programs, the buyer gets fined or worse. Germany and the Netherlands went the other way: legal, regulated, licensed brothels, sex workers pay taxes and get labor protections like any other job. New Zealand decriminalized the whole thing outright. Same transaction, three legal systems, three completely different treatments.

What bugs me is the Nordic model specifically, because it seems to want to have it both ways. If selling sex is something a person can never really consent to -- because of poverty, coercion, whatever -- then the seller is a victim and the buyer is doing something to her, and you'd expect the buyer to be treated as committing something like sexual assault, not paying a fine. But if the seller is a genuine agent making a choice, however constrained, then what exactly is the buyer's crime? We don't criminalize the customers of sweatshops, or the people who hire desperate day laborers at terrible wages. We regulate the conditions, if anything.

So here's my question. What view of consent and agency does the Nordic model presuppose? Does it only make sense if you think economic desperation undermines consent in sex but not in coal mining? Or is there a coherent version where sex is just different, and if so, different in virtue of what? And is the asymmetry (punish him, protect her) actually one theory, or is it a political compromise wearing a theory's clothes -- i.e., could you keep the exact same laws while believing several incompatible things about what's going on? Finally: what would the law have to look like if you took the Germany-style view seriously instead, and does anything in actual practice (the exit programs, the fines, the way cases get prosecuted) tip off which theory the lawmakers really held?

I'd like a proper reconstruction, not just a summary of the debate I could find on Wikipedia. At the very end, give me a last line in this exact form: \answer{...} where the ... is a short phrase naming the implicit theory you think the Nordic model most plausibly presupposes (or "no coherent theory" if that's where you land).
