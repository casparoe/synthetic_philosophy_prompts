I TA'd a political theory course last semester and a student cornered me with this argument after the Hobbes lecture, and I gave a bad answer on the spot and it's been bugging me ever since. Pretend you're a political philosopher who specializes in early modern thought but who also takes contemporary constitutionalism seriously, and think carefully before answering -- maybe brainstorm the candidate weak points first before settling on one.

Here's the argument, cleaned up from the whiteboard:

(1) Cooperation between self-interested people is only stable when there's a common power that can credibly punish defectors. Otherwise whoever cooperates first gets exploited, everyone can see this in advance, and so rationally nobody cooperates. That's just the state of nature logic.

(2) But a power can only play that role if nothing can override or punish it. If something can override it, then that something is the real power, and the whole question -- what keeps that thing in line? -- just starts over.

(3) So the regress only stops at a single, final, unaccountable authority. Divided powers, judicial review, constitutional limits -- those don't end the war of all against all, they just move it inside the government.

(4) And Hobbes himself says the state of nature doesn't require actual fighting, just the standing absence of a common power -- "warre" as a disposition, not constant battle. So a society can be in the state of nature while looking perfectly calm.

(5) Modern constitutional democracies have no single unaccountable authority. That's the whole point of them.

(6) Therefore modern constitutional democracies are in the state of nature.

(7) But they're obviously not -- they're measurably more peaceful and cooperative than most of the actual absolute sovereignties in history.

(8) So something in (1)-(5) has to go.

Which step do you reject, and why does it look fine at first glance? Don't just give me the textbook summary of Leviathan, I know that part. For what it's worth I'm most reluctant to drop (1), since the cooperation-under-anarchy problem seems basically right, and I refuse to accept (6) as anything but a reductio. If the fix requires saying Hobbes himself wouldnt have endorsed some of these premises, that's fine, tell me. Also tell me what rejecting your chosen step costs -- i.e., what true-seeming thing I can no longer say, and whether Hobbes's own political conclusions (he wanted absolute sovereignty, after all) survive the repair. If you think the argument is actually sound and I should just bite (6) with some story about why it feels absurd, you can argue that too, but you'd better have a good story.
