Write a dialogue between two philosophers, Ada and Bruno, who agree about the facts of the following case but disagree about how to analyse it.

The case: A chemical plant has two independent safety systems monitoring a reactor. System A detects rising pressure and vents it; had System A failed, System B would have detected the rise a fraction of a second later and vented it instead. System A works as designed, the pressure is vented, and no explosion occurs. However, an inquiry later establishes that the pressure would have risen at all only because a junior engineer, Priya, overrode a warning light; and that, had she not done so, the reactor would have run normally. Meanwhile, a valve that had been stuck for months happens to be irrelevant, because the venting did not require it.

Ada defends a counterfactual-dependence theory of causation in the tradition of Lewis, including the claim that where there is no direct dependence, causation may still be secured by a chain of stepwise dependences or by patterns of counterfactual influence. Bruno defends an interventionist account in the tradition of Woodward, on which causal claims are to be understood in terms of what would happen under hypothetical interventions, and he is suspicious of any appeal to comparisons between merely possible worlds.

In the course of the dialogue, they should each take a view on: (i) whether System A's venting caused the absence of an explosion, given that System B stood ready as a backup; (ii) whether System B's doing nothing is itself a cause of anything, and more generally whether omissions and absences can be causes; and (iii) whether the stuck valve's irrelevance shows that causal structure outruns actual counterfactual dependence. Bruno should press Ada on how her similarity ordering of possible worlds is fixed without smuggling in causal judgements, and Ada should press Bruno on whether his notion of a possible intervention is covertly anthropocentric or, if it is made fully abstract, whether it collapses back into counterfactual talk.

Both characters should be equally competent: neither may caricature the other's position, and each must respond to the other's actual arguments rather than recite a prepared case. The dialogue should run to roughly 900–1,200 words, should end with the two of them identifying (but not necessarily resolving) the precise point at which their disagreement bottoms out, and should be written in a natural register — spoken philosophy, not two encyclopaedia entries in alternation.
