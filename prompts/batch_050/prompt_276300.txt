Analyze the United Kingdom’s 2016 referendum on leaving the European Union as a case study in the democratic boundary problem: when a decision changes the boundaries and terms of political membership, who is entitled to participate in making it?

For purposes of the analysis, assume the following features of the episode. The franchise covered most adult British, Irish, and qualifying Commonwealth citizens resident in the United Kingdom, as well as eligible voters in Gibraltar and some British citizens who had lived abroad for no more than fifteen years. It generally excluded sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds, most other EU citizens lawfully resident in the United Kingdom, and British citizens who had lived abroad for more than fifteen years. Yet withdrawal would alter residence and family rights, labor-market access, trade relations, diplomatic obligations, and legal protections for many people inside and outside the United Kingdom, including future residents and future generations.

Evaluate the referendum through three rival answers to the boundary problem: the all-affected-interests principle, under which anyone whose important interests are affected by a decision should have some say over it; the all-subjected-to-coercion principle, under which those bound by a polity’s coercive laws should participate in authorizing them; and a shared-membership or social-bonds principle, under which the demos consists of people connected by continuing institutions, reciprocal obligations, and a common political fate. Define each principle carefully rather than treating its name as self-explanatory.

Map the major groups onto each principle: ordinary UK voters, resident EU citizens, resident Irish and Commonwealth citizens, British emigrants, Gibraltar residents, sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds, citizens of other EU states, immigrants with strong prospective claims to residence, and future generations. In doing so, distinguish having any voice from having an equal vote, and distinguish inclusion in this particular referendum from permanent inclusion in the British demos. Pay special attention to the circularity at the heart of the case: the existing electorate was using rules it had inherited to decide who would remain subject to a substantially reconfigured political relationship.

Then assess whether any feasible franchise could have been democratically self-authorizing, or whether some degree of circularity is unavoidable. Consider at least three institutional alternatives: extending the vote to some excluded groups, requiring a special majority or some other constitutional threshold, and separating the initial decision to negotiate withdrawal from later ratification of the concrete terms. Explain what each alternative would solve and what new boundary or legitimacy problem it would create.

Finally, let the episode test the theories rather than merely illustrating them. Which principle best explains the most troubling exclusions, and which becomes implausibly expansive, indeterminate, or circular when applied consistently? Does the referendum show that the all-affected-interests principle is indispensable but unworkable, that coercion is a better criterion than affected interests, or that democratic legitimacy must sometimes rest on an imperfect preexisting demos? Conclude with a precise judgment about whether the franchise was a defect that merely weakened the referendum’s legitimacy or one that undermined its authority to settle the question, without turning the answer into an assessment of whether Brexit itself was substantively wise.
