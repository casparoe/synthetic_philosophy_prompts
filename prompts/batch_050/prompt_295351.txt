My father was recently diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment, and it has made our family think hard about trust in one's faculties. He insists his memory is still good, and when my sister presses him on why he's so sure, his answer is always some version of: because I remember all the times I remembered correctly. Something about that felt off to me, but I couldn't say exactly what. Coincidentally, a general-audience philosophy podcast I listen to covered this territory last week, and a guest (an epistemologist, I believe) summarized the relevant debate in roughly these terms:

"The bootstrapping problem — also called the problem of easy knowledge, following Stewart Cohen — shows that no one can ever be justified in believing that a basic source of belief, like perception, memory, or testimony, is reliable. The reason is that any check you run has to employ the very source under examination: you verify your eyesight's track record by remembering what you saw, and you verify your memory by consulting what you seem to remember. All such track-record arguments are therefore viciously circular, on a par with trusting a crystal ball on the grounds that the crystal ball says it is accurate. Cohen and others use this to refute reliabilism, the view that beliefs are justified simply in virtue of coming from a reliable process: since the reliabilist is committed to counting crystal-ball reasoning as good reasoning, the view collapses, and justification requires independent, non-circular grounds for trusting each source. The honest upshot is a modest skepticism — strictly speaking, no one knows their faculties are reliable, so the justification of our ordinary beliefs is at best derivative."

I found this persuasive in the moment, but on reflection a few things niggle: I thought bootstrapping and "easy knowledge" were at least somewhat distinct topics, and the leap from "this kind of self-verification looks fishy" to "no one is ever justified in trusting their faculties" seems like a big one. Could you audit the summary for me? Which parts would a working epistemologist endorse as accurate, which are overstatements or conflations of claims the literature keeps apart, and which (if any) are simply misrepresentations of what Cohen-style arguments purport to show? Most importantly for my situation: once the summary is corrected, does anything in the corrected picture bear on whether my father's confidence in his own memory — resting as it does on remembered successes — is rational, irrational, or simply a different kind of question than the epistemologists are asking?
