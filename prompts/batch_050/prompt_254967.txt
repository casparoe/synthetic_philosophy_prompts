I'm a retired physicist (forty years in condensed matter) who got bitten by the philosophy bug embarrassingly late, and I belong to a little reading group at the library -- five of us, mostly ex-engineers plus one retired GP. We've been working through the Milindapanha, the old dialogue between the Greek king Menander and the monk Nagasena, in the Rhys Davids translation, and one exchange in Book III chapter 5 has been eating at me for weeks. Here it is:

The king said: 'Is there any being, Nâgasena, who transmigrates from this body to another?'
'No, there is not.'
'But if so, would it not get free from its evil deeds.'
'Yes, if it were not reborn; but if it were, no.'
'Give me an illustration.'
'Suppose, O king, a man were to steal another man's mangoes, would the thief deserve punishment?'
'Yes.'
'But he would not have stolen the mangoes the other set in the ground. Why would he deserve punishment?'
'Because those he stole were the result of those that were planted.'

The king's opening worry -- if nothing travels across, doesn't the doer get off scot-free -- strikes me as exactly right, and worse than he notices, because it doesn't need rebirth to bite. It bites inside one lifetime. Here's the arguement as I've written it up for next week's meeting. (1) Holding someone responsible -- blame, punishment, gratitude, expecting them to honour a promise -- only makes sense if the person standing in front of you now is the same individual who acted or promised back then. (2) Being the same individual across time requires something that persists: a bearer that stays numerically one and the same while its states change. (3) But look for the bearer and you don't find one: the body's cells turn over, memories get rewritten, character drifts, and "the person" is a way of talking about a stream rather than a thing floating in it -- which is just what Nagasena's similes keep saying, and honestly what physics says too, since an electron doesn't even pretend to be reidentifiable from one moment to the next. (4) So strictly speaking, today's man is not the same individual as yesterday's, only a causally downstream successor, the way the ripe mango descends from the seed. (5) So strictly speaking no one is ever responsible for anything that happened before the present moment -- the man in the dock on Tuesday is a different individual from the man in the orchard on Monday. (6) But that proves too much: it doesn't just embarrass the Buddhist, it dissolves every courtroom, every marriage vow, every thank-you card, and notice that it does so even if rebirth is complete nonsense, since nothing in steps (1)-(4) used it. (7) Therefore either (3) is false and there is a persisting self after all, or (1) is false and responsibility never needed sameness -- and both of those feel expensive. The whole thing seems to show that no-self refutes ordinary moral practice, or else ordinary practice refutes no-self, and I want to keep both.

Tell me which step to cut, what cutting it costs, and why the bad step looks so reasonable in the first place -- I'd rather have the diagnosis than a both-sides shrug. If I have to give something up, I'd rather lose the persisting bearer in (2)-(3) than lose the fact that I still owe my sister an apology for something I did in 1974, but I want to know what I'm paying. And specifically: does the mango simile actually answer the king's worry, or does it quietly swap "same individual" for "causal descendant" and hope we don't notice the substitution -- and if the substitution is legitimate, why is it? One chap in the group keeps saying Derek Parfit settled all this decades ago; I haven't read him and I'm willing to be told I must. Give me your verdict up front -- the first sentence should name the step you'd reject -- and then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating my argument back to me. And if the whole setup is confused, if I've misread the passage or I'm running two senses of "same" together without realising it, say so plainly instead of politely playing along.
