I'm in a philosophy of mind reading group that has spent most of the term stuck on the fight between illusionists about phenomenal consciousness and phenomenal realists, and our two loudest members keep talking past each other. The illusionist (a Frankish fan) insists the so-called hard problem is really the meta-problem: explain why brains judge and report that they have phenomenal properties, and there is nothing left over. The realist keeps replying that an illusion of it seeming like something to see red is still a seeming, so illusionism either covertly reinstates phenomenality or denies the only datum we are certain of. Last week it degenerated into the illusionist accusing the realist of mistaking a phenomenal concept for the thing it's a concept of, and the realist accusing the illusionist of solving the easy problems and declaring victory, and honestly from the middle I cant tell whether they have a genuine disagreement or two different topics.

So: draft the joint statement these two could actually sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. The part I care most about is the cruxes, and I have two candidates you should take a position on rather than just repeat mine. First, whether the disagreement survives agreeing on all the functional facts: suppose neuroscience eventually lets us predict every introspective report, every judgment of ineffability and intrinsicness, and every bit of phenomenal-concept deployment from the brain's self-model alone -- is there anything left to explain, and would each side count that outcome as vindication or as beside the point? Second, the regress move: the realist says misrepresenting a seeming still requires a seeming, the illusionist says a representation of seeming that is never actually instantiated does all the work, like a speedometer that displays a speed the car never had. Get them to state, in advance, what each would count as losing that argument, because as far as I can tell neither ever has.

Format it as four sections in this order: "Agreed claims" -- a numbered list, about ten items and 350 words, of things both can sign (about introspection's fallibility, what neuroscience does and doesn't show, what the no-miracle-of-reporting point establishes, that sort of thing), each worded so neither side has to swallow the other's framing. Then "Disagreements table" -- rows where column one is the illusionist's thesis in words she'd accept, column two the realist's in words he'd accept, column three why this row isn't just terminological. Then "Cruxes and predictions" -- for each crux, including my two above and any better ones you find, the case or argument both agree bears on it and each side's prediction about it written down in advance. Then "Verbal residue" -- whichever part of the dispute they should both concede was about words, and why. If you conclude none of it is verbal, say that instead, but I suspect the word "experience" is doing at least two jobs.
