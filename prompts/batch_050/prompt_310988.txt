I have no formal background in political theory, but I have been thinking about the different responses of Finland, Sweden, and Switzerland to Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine. Finland joined NATO while retaining conscription and a large reserve. Sweden also joined NATO after having reintroduced selective conscription. Switzerland remained outside the alliance, retaining armed neutrality and its militia system.

Analyze this episode through the republican idea that freedom means not merely being left alone for now, but not living under another power’s capacity to interfere arbitrarily. Please keep the following distinctions separate: legal neutrality, military self-reliance, and actual strategic independence.

First, map the episode in the theory’s terms. For each country, identify the relevant external and internal powers, the kinds of vulnerability involved, and the options realistically available. Does armed neutrality reduce domination by avoiding entangling alliances, or does it merely leave a state privately responsible for deterring a much stronger neighbor? Conversely, does NATO membership reduce vulnerability to Russia only by creating a new dependence on the United States and other allies? Explain when dependence counts as domination and when it is instead a reciprocal, limited, or freely revisable relationship.

Second, examine conscription. A citizen army might express republican freedom because the population shares the burden of defending its own political order rather than depending on a professional warrior class or foreign protectors. But compulsory service is itself a form of state coercion, and in these systems the burden often falls more heavily on young men. Which consideration should carry more weight, and does it matter whether conscription is universal, selective, or organized through a militia? Consider both the distribution of sacrifice and the possibility that citizen service gives the public more control over decisions about war.

Third, say where the republican analysis explains the episode well and where it leaves important facts out. In particular, discuss whether it adequately captures geography, credible deterrence, uncertainty about future intentions, alliance bargaining, public opinion, and the difference between a threat that is presently unlikely and one that cannot be blocked if it materializes. If the theory cannot rank the three countries’ choices without additional assumptions, say so rather than forcing a verdict.

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: what does this episode reveal about the theory? Does it show that non-domination is too indeterminate to guide small-state security policy, or does it expose problems in treating formal neutrality and formal alliance membership as sufficient evidence of independence? End with the strongest republican case for each of the three approaches—NATO with conscription, armed neutrality with a militia, and reliance mainly on a professional force—and identify what further consideration would have to be true for one case to defeat the others.
