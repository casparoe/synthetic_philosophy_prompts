I've been in a political philosophy reading group that just spent two sessions on patriotism -- MacIntyre's defense of it as a constitutive virtue, Kateb's attack on it as an abdication of moral judgment, and the usual cosmopolitan replies -- and I came away unsure where I actually stand. So I tried the method of going by my gut on cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, underlies the pattern. Here are my verdicts. Cheering for your national team at the World Cup, anthem, flag-painted face and all: perfectly innocent. Paying somewhat more for domestically made shoes in order to support workers in your own country rather than cheaper foreign labor: fine, maybe even mildly admirable. But a hiring manager at a private firm who passes over an equally qualified immigrant applicant in favor of a fellow citizen, saying we should look after our own: not fine, and I can't easily say why it's worse than the shoes. A soldier who refuses an order to burn a village, explaining that he loves his country too much to let it do this: admirable, and I want to call it genuinely patriotic because of the refusal, not in spite of it. A civil servant in the 1940s who passes military secrets to his country's enemies because his government has become monstrous: admirable; a civil servant who does exactly the same for money: despicable; so here my verdict seems to track the cause entirely and the loyalty not at all. A voter who supports subsidies that will foreseeably ruin poor farmers abroad because they help farmers here: not fine, though I notice this may just be the shoe purchase done by ballot. Refusing to look at credible evidence that your country committed atrocities because you don't want to think ill of it: the paradigm vice, whatever it is. Feeling real grief, decades on, at a memorial for your country's war dead, including conscripts who died in a war you consider unjust: fine, perhaps even good. An immigrant who grows deeply attached to her adopted country while feeling nothing much for the country of her birth: unobjectionable, which suggests to me that whatever legitimate patriotism is, it can't be about blood or birthplace.

What I want is a principle -- or, if no single one will do it, two or three candidates -- stated precisely enough that I can see which of my cases motivates each of its clauses. In particular, I'd like it to either reconcile the shoe purchase with the hiring decision and the subsidy vote, or tell me honestly that one of those verdicts has to go, and which one, and why. I'd also like to know what the principle implies about a case I keep circling without settling: a citizen who quietly declines to stand for the national anthem at a public event as a protest against a current government policy -- not out of hatred for the country, she'd say, but because standing would feel like endorsing what is being done in its name. Sometimes I think this is the shoe-burning soldier's case in miniature, and sometimes I think it's gratuitous, since the anthem belongs to the country and not to the government. If the best principle you can find condemns her, or vindicates her, I'd rather hear that than a principle so flexible it says nothing. And if the upshot is that my gut verdicts don't cohere at all -- that patriotism is just several unrelated dispositions wearing one name -- I'd like that argued rather than assumed.
