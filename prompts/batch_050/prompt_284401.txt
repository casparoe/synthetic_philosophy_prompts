The prompt the students got was:

"Explain the fitting-attitude (or 'buck-passing') analysis of value, according to which for something to be good or valuable just is for it to be fitting to favor it, or for there to be reason to favor it. Then state the 'wrong kind of reasons' problem for this analysis, using a concrete example. Finally, consider the reply that restricts the analysis to object-given as opposed to state-given reasons, and assess whether that reply fully rescues the analysis."

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"The buck-passing or fitting-attitude analysis says that value is not a further property things have on top of their natural features; instead, to call something valuable is to say that we have reason to respond to it in certain ways -- to admire it, desire it, pursue it, and so on. On this view, facts about value reduce to facts about reasons, so normativity bottoms out in reasons rather than in goodness.

The wrong kind of reasons problem, due to Parfit's demon case in Reasons and Persons, goes like this. Suppose a demon credibly threatens to torture me unless I admire him. Then I have a strong reason to admire him, since admiring him is the only way to avoid torture. But he is obviously not admirable. So the analysis says he is admirable (there's reason to admire him) when he isn't -- the analysis counts the wrong kind of reasons.

The standard fix, from the object-given / state-given distinction, is that the demon's threat gives me only a state-given reason: a reason grounded in facts about the state of admiring (that it would save me from torture), not in facts about the object (the demon's actual character). Object-given reasons, by contrast, flow from the features of the thing itself. Since you can't just decide to admire someone at will anyway, the threat isn't really a reason to admire at all -- it's a reason to make yourself admire, or to want to admire, which is a different thing. The analysis, restricted to object-given reasons, therefore survives.

I think the fix works. Once we see that the demon case only ever generates state-given reasons, the counterexample dissolves, and so buck-passing is vindicated in full generality: being valuable is having object-given reason to be favored, and no case in the literature escapes this treatment."
