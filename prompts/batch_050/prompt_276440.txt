Disjunctivists about perception hold that a veridical perception of, say, a ripe tomato and a subjectively indistinguishable hallucination of a ripe tomato are mental states of fundamentally different kinds: the veridical case essentially involves the tomato itself as a constituent of the experience, while the hallucination does not, and there is no highest common factor — no single experiential state present in both cases that fully accounts for what each experience is like.

Here is my difficulty, and I would like to know whether it is fatal to the view. If the two states really differ in their fundamental nature, then surely that difference must be manifest to the subject undergoing them, since an experience's nature just is what it is like to have it. But by hypothesis the hallucination is introspectively indistinguishable from the veridical perception: nothing in the hallucinatory experience announces that the tomato is absent. So the disjunctivist seems forced to claim that two states can be of entirely different metaphysical kinds while sharing exactly the same phenomenal character — which strikes me as unintelligible, because phenomenal character is precisely the dimension along which experiences are individuated. Alternatively, if the disjunctivist instead concedes that the phenomenal characters are identical, then there plainly is a common factor after all, namely the shared phenomenal character, and the view collapses back into the intentionalist or sense-datum accounts it was designed to replace.

So my question is this: doesn't the bare possibility of indiscriminable hallucination refute disjunctivism outright, by showing either that it posits differences with no experiential reality or that it smuggles the common factor back in? If disjunctivists have a coherent response, what exactly is it, and does it explain why hallucinations are indiscriminable from perceptions rather than merely asserting that they are? Please answer in American English.
