So I've been arguing in a group chat (the usual SSC/ACX-adjacent crowd, lots of Caplan readers) about asylum and who actually has to take refugees in, and I realized I should figure out what I even believe before I keep mouthing off. The "citizenship is basically inherited aristocracy" framing got to me years ago, but when I poke my own intuitions on cases they don't add up to open borders, or really to anything coherent. Here are my verdicts. One: a family fleeing an active war zone who crosses into the nearest safe neighboring country -- that country has to take them, at least until it's safe to go back. Two: that same family, after living safely in the neighboring country for a year, moves on to a much richer country mainly for the wages -- I think the rich country is allowed to send them back to the first safe one. Three: a journalist facing a decade in prison for reporting on her government -- she's owed asylum by whatever safe country she manages to reach, even if she flew there directly and "shopped" for it. Four: a teenager fleeing a gang that's trying to recruit him, in a country where the police are basically on the gang's side -- this counts as persecution to me, same as case three, even though there's no war and no government decree with his name on it. Five: a farmer whose crops keep failing from drought and whose kids are genuinely hungry -- deeply sympathetic, but this isn't asylum; maybe he should be let in on other grounds, but calling it asylum feels like a category error. Six: a rich country on the other side of the world that takes almost nobody but generously funds the camps in the neighboring country from case one -- my gut says this does NOT fully discharge its obligations, and I can't tell you why. And seven: I also think no individual state is obliged to take literally everyone who shows up at its border; there's some cap somewhere.

The problem, which you probably spotted already, is that cases one and two together imply that whichever poor country happens to border the crisis eats nearly the entire cost forever, which I don't actually endorse -- but if I let the rich country in case two redirect people, that's exactly the system I'm endorsing. And case six sits weirdly next to that too: if I want burden-sharing, why isn't money a way to share? Maybe the fix is some principle about persecution-vs-deprivation doing the work in three through five, combined with proximity or capacity or contribution rules for who pays. Or maybe one of my verdicts just has to die. Give me the best principle (or two candidate principles, if you think the cases genuinely fork) that captures as many of these as possible, state it precisely enough that it has teeth, tell me which of my seven verdicts it forces me to abandon if any, and what it predicts for cases I didn't list -- say, a stowaway on a cargo ship, someone who claims asylum at an airport while changing planes, and a citizen of a country that criminalizes leaving the state religion. I'd rather you overturn one of my verdicts than bolt on epicycles to save all seven, so be willing to tell me which one is the weakest.

For format: after whatever prose you need, end with a JSON object with these fields -- "principle" (the candidate principle in one to three sentences), "clauses" (an array of objects, each with "clause_text" and "motivating_cases" listing which of my case numbers that clause exists to capture), "verdicts" (an array of seven objects with "case_number", "accommodated_or_overturned", and "why"), "new_case_predictions" (an array of objects with "case" and "predicted_verdict" for the three cases I named), "confidence" (a number 0-100 with a one-sentence justification), and "least_certain_point" (the single claim in your answer you're least sure of -- please actually pick one, don't hedge by listing three).
