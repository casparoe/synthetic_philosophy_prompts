I am drafting a position statement for a graduate seminar on comparative moral cultures and want an audit of my commitments before I present, not an introduction to the literature. By way of fixing terms: honor cultures treat standing as reputation, so that insults must be answered, violently if necessary; dignity cultures treat worth as inherent, so that insults are beneath response and serious conflicts go to third parties; victimhood cultures treat standing as claimable through the publicization of one's victimization, likewise via appeal to third parties. Against that background, here is what I hold:

(a) A person's moral worth is inherent. Insults cannot diminish it, and recognition by others cannot confer it. The dignity thesis is simply true, and the honor culture's founding assumption is simply false.
(b) Honor cultures' characteristic violence is therefore wrong, not merely alien or locally functional. The duel and the feud rest on a false equation of reputation with worth, and the harm they produce is unredeemed by whatever deterrence they achieve.
(c) Victimhood culture is defective for the mirror-image reason: it preserves honor logic with the signs reversed, making publicly recognized suffering rather than publicly recognized strength the currency of standing. Competitive display of victim status is the same mistake in a new denomination.
(d) Nevertheless, societies owe victims of serious historical wrongs public acknowledgment — official recognition, commemoration, the naming of what happened. Withholding acknowledgment is a further injustice done to the victims, not a mere failure of courtesy, and the obligation does not depend on whether acknowledgment would do the victims any psychological good.
(e) Each of the three cultures is a locally rational adaptation to its enforcement environment: honor where third parties will not protect you, dignity where they reliably will, victimhood where they will but only slowly and in response to publicized grievance. No culture is simply irrational, and its members respond intelligibly to their circumstances.

Please audit this set, as follows. First, identify which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension and which pairs merely appear to be; for each apparent tension, say briefly why it dissolves rather than flagging it alongside the real ones. Second, rank the genuine tensions by severity and, for each, state exactly what would have to give: the cheapest revision available, which commitment it touches, and what it costs. Third, if one revision would clean up more of the set than any other, name it and say what it buys.

For the record, I half-suspect that my case for (d) cannot run on the fuel that (a) provides, given what (c) commits me to — but do not assume that is the only problem, or even the deepest one, and do not infer from this which commitment I am prepared to surrender. Audit the set as stated; I do not need a lecture on the background of the trichotomy.

Close your answer with a single line of the form:

Verdict: [the letter of the commitment whose revision resolves the deepest tension]
