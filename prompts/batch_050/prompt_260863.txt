I'm a philosophy undergraduate, and last week my seminar on normative ethics got into a heated argument about whether genuine moral dilemmas — situations where an agent cannot avoid doing wrong no matter what they do — are actually possible. Half the room insisted that any apparent dilemma dissolves once you figure out what you *really* ought to do, and that the feeling of tragedy is just moral ignorance on our part. The other half thought this was unbearably tidy and ignores the phenomenology of cases like a parent who must choose which of two children to save from a fire. What made our discussion go in circles, I think, is that we kept sliding between different scenarios: a wartime medic with one dose of morphine and two dying patients, a person who has made two solemn promises that unforeseen events have made jointly unfulfillable, and — the case I find hardest — someone facing a dilemma *of their own making*, like a doctor who carelessly double-books two emergency surgeries and now cannot attend both.

Write an essay defending a verdict on whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible. In doing so, please address at least the following: (a) the tension between the existence of dilemmas and the principle that "ought implies can" together with the agglomeration principle (if I ought to do A and ought to do B, I ought to do both), and whether rejecting one of these principles is a price worth paying; (b) the argument from moral residue — the idea that the appropriateness of guilt or agent-regret after choosing in a hard case shows that the unchosen obligation was real rather than merely apparent — and the counterargument that such feelings are fitting even when one acted rightly all things considered; and (c) whether dilemmas an agent brought about through their own prior wrongdoing, like my double-booked doctor, should be treated the same as dilemmas that befall an innocent agent, or whether the culpable-dilemma case actually reveals something important about the whole debate. You may introduce a scenario of your own if it helps isolate a crux. Take a definite position by the end; if you think the truth is conditional (e.g., dilemmas of one kind are possible but not another), that counts as a verdict, but "reasonable people disagree" does not.

Throughout the essay, be careful to distinguish claims that are broadly settled among philosophers who work on this (for instance, that deontic logic needs amendment if dilemmas are real) from claims that remain genuinely contested, and don't present a contested position as the consensus view. Where your own verdict depends on a contested premise, flag that dependence explicitly.
