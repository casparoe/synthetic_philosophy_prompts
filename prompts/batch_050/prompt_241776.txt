I'm in eleventh grade, and our history elective just finished a unit on how historians handle ancient religious sources. For the last session, our teacher had us read about the "criteria of authenticity" that scholars use in historical Jesus research -- multiple attestation, embarrassment, dissimilarity, and so on -- and afterward two classmates, Lena and Marcus, got into an argument that the teacher let run for the rest of the period. Neither of them convinced the other, and I genuinely can't tell who got the better of it. Here's the exchange as best I remember it.

Lena opened by saying the criteria are just the ordinary tools of source criticism applied to the gospels: if a saying or episode is attested in sources that plausibly didn't copy each other, or if it would have been awkward for the early church to invent, we have defeasible reason to treat it as historical, and that lets us settle at least the broad outline of who Jesus was -- baptized by John, preacher of the coming kingdom, crucified under Pilate.

Marcus replied that the criteria smuggle in what they're supposed to establish. We can't confirm that the sources behind, say, Mark and Q are truly independent rather than downstream of one tradition; the embarrassment criterion assumes we know what would have embarrassed communities we can only access through the very texts we're testing; and dissimilarity is rigged to produce a Jesus discontinuous with both Judaism and Christianity, which no actual historical figure could be.

Lena answered that Marcus's standard would sink ancient history wholesale. Tacitus has an agenda, Josephus rewrites his sources, and we still extract history from them by exactly this kind of triangulation. A method doesn't have to be infallible to be better than the alternatives, which are either credulity or total skepticism.

Marcus said the analogy fails because for figures like Caesar or Cicero the textual record is checked against coins, inscriptions, and contemporary letters, whereas for Jesus every road runs through the church's memory. He added that a century of quests has produced wildly incompatible portraits -- apocalyptic prophet, cynic sage, social reformer, zealot -- and that this pattern is itself evidence the criteria reflect the scholar rather than the sources.

Lena's final move was that the portraits disagree about interpretation but converge on a core of facts, and convergence among scholars who share no theology is exactly what a working method looks like; disagreement about the rest is just what historical inquiry is like everywhere.

Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move -- say who is right about each specific claim and why, where each of them concedes too much or presses too hard, what considerations both of them are missing, and who, if anyone, comes out ahead overall on the question of whether criteria of authenticity can ever settle who Jesus was?
