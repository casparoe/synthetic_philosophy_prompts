Two of my colleagues got into a drawn-out argument last week that nobody at the table could settle, and I'd like a referee. The setup: Maya, an analyst, spent three weeks auditing a company's books and concluded, correctly and on excellent grounds, that the company is solvent. The next day a rival firm published a short report claiming the company is quietly insolvent. Maya refused to read it. Her reasoning: she already knows the company is solvent; any genuine evidence would have to point at the truth; so the report, whatever it says, must be misleading somewhere, and she can rationally infer that without checking where. Dev said this was epistemically perverse -- you can't just close your eyes to fresh counterevidence on the grounds that you already have the answer. Maya replied that Dev was demanding she treat misleading evidence as if it were evidence against what she knows, which amounts to counting the possibility of error twice: once when she weighed her original grounds, and again every time anyone disagrees with her. Dev answered that on her logic, knowledge is self-sealing: no one who knows anything could ever be rationally required to revisit it, so inquiry and correction become pointless the moment you arrive. Maya said that's an overstatement -- she'd have to engage if she got reason to think her original audit was defective, say a memo showing she'd used the wrong accounts. Dev's final move was that this is circular: whether her grounds are defective is exactly what the dismissed report might have told her, so she can't use the integrity of her grounds as the filter for which evidence she'll look at. Maya then added what she took to be a reductio of Dev's side: if knowing never licenses dismissal, then she also can't use her result to vouch for her own audit method -- but surely "the company is solvent, my method said so, so my method worked" is a fine thing to conclude.

Please adjudicate the exchange point by point -- who is right at each step, not just overall: whether knowing really licenses dismissing the report unread, whether Dev's double-counting charge is fair, whether the self-sealing objection lands or misses, whether Maya's "defective grounds" escape hatch is circular as Dev claims, and whether her final point about vouching for her method helps her or exposes a problem (does it let her bootstrap ever-stronger warrant by repeatedly dismissing disconfirming reports?). Then say who is more right on the whole and what, if anything, both are missing -- for instance, whether the dispute changes if we distinguish what's rational for her to believe from what's rational for her to do (read the report anyway, as cheap insurance), or if her warrant was only very strong rather than knowledge. Lead with your bottom-line verdict in the first sentence, then give the step-by-step reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the case.
