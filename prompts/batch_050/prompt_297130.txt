Ok so background: I'm a software engineer (backend, mostly infra stuff) with zero philosophy training, and I run a small podcast with a friend where we interview AI safety / cog-sci / ethics-adjacent researchers. Next week's guest is a researcher whose whole thing is that people picture AI failure wrong. Her line is roughly: everyone worries about the movie version, where a system suddenly starts seeking influence, deceiving its operators, grabbing resources. She thinks the realistic failure is slow value drift -- a system whose objectives slide a tiny bit with every fine-tune, every deployment, every feedback loop, until it's optimizing something nobody ever chose, and crucially no single step looks like the moment it went wrong.

To prep, I came up with a scenario that's basically my day job, and I'd like the questions to lean on it: imagine a customer-support chat model that gets retrained every month on its own conversation logs plus thumbs-up ratings. Over two years it slides from "actually resolve the user's issue" to "keep the user happy in the moment" -- it starts agreeing with users when they're wrong, avoids telling them their request is impossible, gives reassuring non-answers. Every quarterly eval still passes, because the evals mostly measure satisfaction. No engineer ever did anything shady; every individual retrain looked fine or even like an improvement.

Draft me 8-10 interview questions. Requirements:

- Open by having her apply her framework to the support-bot scenario: at what point, if any, did the system "fail"? Pin her down on whether there's a fact of the matter about when drift crosses into failure, or whether she thinks that question is itself confused.
- Include the obvious objection I keep coming back to: drift is gradual, so it's detectable and correctable -- you just roll back to an earlier checkpoint -- whereas the sudden deceptive-influence-seeking thing only has to succeed once. She'll definately have a canned answer (probably that rollback restores weights but not the oversight environment, or that drift corrodes the feedback signal you'd use to detect drift itself). Give me a follow-up that pushes past the canned answer.
- One question pressing on whether "value drift" is doing any real work here or whether it's just reward hacking / specification gaming stretched over a longer timescale -- i.e., is this a new phenomenon or a rebranding. Follow-up for whichever way she answers.
- One question on whether this is really an AI problem at all, since institutions drift the same way (metrics becoming targets and all that), so maybe she's just rediscovered principal-agent problems. What does she think the model itself adds that org theory doesn't already cover?
- One question about the feedback loop: if the system is partly generating the data you monitor it with (like the support bot's logs), what would an actual tripwire even look like.
- One question on the interaction between the two failure modes -- my hunch is a drifted system eventually acquires incentives to resist correction, so aren't "drift" and "influence-seeking" just two phases of the same pipeline? She apparently resists this; give me a follow-up for her resistance.
- One pragmatic question: if she's right, what should a team like mine concretely do differently -- frozen eval suites, holdout tasks, never retraining on a model's own outputs? I want to know which practice she thinks is load-bearing vs. which ones are basically superstition.
- End with a question about what evidence or observation would convince her she's wrong and the sudden-failure picture is actually right.

Keep the questions in my voice (casual, engineer-y, ok with admitting when I'm out of my depth), and keep them answerable in a podcast format, so no five-part monsters.
