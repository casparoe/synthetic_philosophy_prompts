I am a novelist drafting a scene set in a nearly automated port city. A labor organizer argues that robots are like an inexhaustible workforce of immigrant laborers: they arrive from elsewhere, work continuously, accept no wage, never strike, and therefore inevitably take jobs and drive down the price of human labor. A Marxian dockworker objects that this analogy mistakes the worker’s real antagonist. On her view, a machine under capitalism is not another worker competing in the labor market but dead or objectified labor owned by capital: it transfers value rather than creating new value, lets firms produce more with less labor time, intensifies the work of those who remain, and swells the reserve army whose existence disciplines the employed. She concludes that automation is threatening because of the social relations governing ownership and the distribution of productivity gains, not because machines are inherently rival persons.

Audit the analogy and its Marxian correction. Give an explicit correspondence table covering the machine, immigrant worker, employer, wage, strike, consumption, reproduction, and job displacement; then separate the disanalogies that matter from those that do not. In particular, address whether a machine’s needing engineers, maintenance, energy, and prior labor weakens or strengthens the comparison; whether AI systems that can modify themselves complicate the dead-labor characterization; and whether the analogy quietly imports the assumption that labor-saving technology must appear as competition for scarce jobs rather than as a reduction in necessary toil. Finally, assess which conclusion survives: the organizer’s near-inevitability thesis, the dockworker’s claim that the problem lies in capitalist ownership and class power, or some qualified combination. Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence, then provide the reasoning directly, without preamble or restating the question.
