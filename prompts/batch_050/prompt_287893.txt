I serve on my country's Council for National Well-Being, a standing advisory body attached to the finance ministry, and I have been asked to write the opinion that will anchor our next plenary vote. The question before us is whether the government should adopt, as an official policy target, rising into the top tier of the annual world happiness rankings within a decade. The prime minister's office favors the proposal — not least, I suspect, because announcing it costs nothing — but the council is genuinely split, and several members think the whole idea rests on a confusion.

Some background the memo has to reckon with. The rankings are built primarily on a single survey item, the so-called ladder question: respondents imagine a ladder whose top is the best possible life for them and whose bottom is the worst, and say where their own life stands on a scale from 0 to 10; the report's analysts then relate national averages to income, health, social support, freedom, generosity, and perceived corruption. The proponents on the council argue that whatever its imperfections, the ladder is the best-validated single measure of how people's lives are going, that its explanatory factors are exactly the things good policy should be promoting anyway, and that a public target disciplines a bureaucracy that otherwise drifts. The skeptics press three objections: that a reflective life evaluation is not happiness in the everyday sense — a country could top the ladder while its people report less frequent joy and more worry than people elsewhere, and the rankings may in effect be grading contentment, optimism, or a willingness to answer pollsters as one is expected to; that comparing 0-to-10 self-ratings across cultures is shakier than it looks, given translation of phrases like best possible life, shifting reference groups, and differing norms about self-presentation; and that turning the ranking into a target invites Goodhart-style gaming, with policy aimed at the survey response rather than the life behind it.

Please draft the council's decision memo, roughly 900 words, recommending a definite course of action — adopt the target, reject it, or adopt some modified version (for instance, targeting the underlying factors rather than the rank itself) — with a concrete bottom line the ministry could act on, including what we would measure and what we would not promise. The memo should engage the strongest argument on the losing side seriously rather than waving it away, and it should keep separate two criticisms the skeptics in our meetings have tended to run together: that the rankings measure the wrong thing, and that pursuing a ranking is the wrong way to obtain even the right thing. Finally, close with a short section stating what would change the recommendation — which empirical finding or conceptual argument, if it materialized, would flip the verdict — and identify where the memo's own reasoning is most likely to fail.
