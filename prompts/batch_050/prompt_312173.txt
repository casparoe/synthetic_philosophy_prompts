I am applying for a twelve-month dissertation fellowship and need a two-page research proposal. The project concerns Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities, and I want you to turn a half-formed idea of mine into a proper proposal.

Here is the idea. Standard Frankfurt-style cases describe an agent who acts entirely on her own while a dormant backup mechanism stands ready to force the same action had she shown any sign of choosing otherwise. Defenders of the principle of alternative possibilities typically reply by hunting for "flickers of freedom" — some prior sign or alternative the agent could still have suppressed — and much of the literature has become a technical arms race over whether a sufficiently robust case can be constructed that eliminates every flicker. My conjecture is that both sides are fixated on the wrong variable. These cases are always described from the outside: the agent never knows the device exists. But responsibility is something agents avow, disavow, and deliberate under, and what an agent takes her options to be shapes her practical reasoning. I want to develop what I tentatively call first-person Frankfurt cases: variants in which the agent knows, or has strong evidence, that she could not have done otherwise, and then to ask whether believed unavoidability excuses, partially excuses, or merely changes the kind of responsibility at issue — even when every actual-sequence fact is held fixed. If our assessments shift with the agent's perspective on her alternatives, then the metaphysical question of whether alternatives exist may be downstream of an epistemic condition on responsibility that neither side of the standard debate has made central.

The proposal should include: (1) a precise statement of the research question, including how the first-person variants differ from merely adding knowledge to an existing case; (2) an explanation of why the two standard responses — actual-sequence accounts of responsibility and flicker-of-freedom defenses of the alternative-possibilities principle — each leave this question open rather than answering it; (3) the method, including what I would actually do month by month across the twelve months, and why a purely philosophical analysis rather than, say, an experimental survey of lay intuitions; (4) a careful treatment of what the project would have to show about different kinds of responsibility (e.g., being answerable for an act versus deserving blame for it) so that the argument does not trade on an undifferentiated notion; and (5) what would count as the project succeeding and what would count as it failing — for instance, if the first-person variants turn out to collapse into a familiar dilemma about manipulation and responsibility, I want the proposal to concede that in advance and explain what would still be learned.

Keep the scope realistic for one year of work by a single researcher. Do not include a bibliography or citations to particular authors or papers; refer to positions and arguments by description, and I will supply the references myself.
