I spent thirty-odd years as a marine biologist identifying specimens for surveys before I retired, and since then I've been going along to a philosophy reading group at the local library. Last month we got onto the so-called species problem, which is slightly embarassing for me, because it turns out philosophers have been arguing for ages about what a species even is, and I signed off thousands of identifications in my career without ever being able to say.

In Chapter II of On the Origin of Species (1859), Darwin writes:

"No one definition has as yet satisfied all naturalists; yet every naturalist knows vaguely what he means when he speaks of a species. Generally the term includes the unknown element of a distinct act of creation. The term 'variety' is almost equally difficult to define; but here community of descent is almost universally implied, though it can rarely be proved."

Write me an essay that takes this passage seriously. The core question: does "species" pick out something real in nature, or is it, as Darwin half-suggests here, a label we stick on clusters of similar organisms for convenience? Please run through the main modern answers -- the biological species concept, phylogenetic and ecological concepts, pluralism, and the more radical view that we should scrap the species category altogether -- and test each one against the passage. In particular: does any modern concept actually escape his jab that the term smuggles in an element nobody can observe, or have we just swapped "creation" for something equally elusive? And what does his point about varieties imply for how sharply we should expect the line to be drawn?

One thing though: I don't want a survey that ends with "it's complicated". Reach a verdict -- say which position you'd defend, or what the defensible position would have to look like, and why the alternatives fall short. British English throughout please, and somewhere around 1200-1500 words.
