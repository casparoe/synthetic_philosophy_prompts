I'm a software engineer, not a biologist or philosopher, but I've been going down a rabbit hole about what counts as a single organism, and I've been trying to pin down my own intuitions by running through cases the way I'd test a piece of code against edge cases. Here are my verdicts, as best I can articulate them. Pando, the clonal aspen grove in Utah where thousands of trunks share one root system and genome: that feels like one organism, full stop, even though it looks like a forest. A honeybee hive: not an organism; the bees are organisms and the hive is something else, maybe a very tight-knit community, and I hold this even though I know worker bees can't reproduce on their own. The Portuguese man o' war, which is apparently a colony of genetically identical, specialized zooids that can't survive independently: that feels like one organism again, which I realize sits uncomfortably next to my bee verdict. Lichen, a fungus and an alga living as an integrated unit: two organisms in a partnership, not one. A human being plus their gut microbiome, without which the human would sicken: one organism plus hangers-on, not a single composite "holobiont" organism, though I concede this verdict is doing a lot of work I can't yet justify. And the cellular slime mold Dictyostelium, where independent amoebas stream together into a slug that crawls around and produces a fruiting body in which some cells sacrifice themselves as a stalk: while aggregated, it is one organism; while dispersed, many.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts or tells me honestly which ones have to go. I'm aware that biologists and philosophers have proposed criteria involving things like reproductive bottlenecks, policing of cheaters, functional integration, and immunological boundaries, but I don't know the literature well enough to know whether any of those, alone or combined, actually reproduces my pattern. Notice that my man o' war verdict and my lichen verdict are both about multi-partner integrated systems and I reach opposite conclusions, and my hive verdict seems to conflict with whichever one I use to save the man o' war. So please: state the principle or principles precisely; say which clause of each principle is motivated by which case; flag which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and whether I should treat that as a defect of the principle or of my intuition; and then apply the winner to two cases I haven't considered -- a coral reef and a mule -- and tell me what it says. Please write your answer in American English, and feel free to tell me my verdicts are a mess if that's the truth.
