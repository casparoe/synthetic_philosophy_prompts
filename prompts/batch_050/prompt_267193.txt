Democratic theory has spent decades arguing about what to do with career politicians, and as far as I can tell the three camps -- term-limit advocates, sortition advocates, and defenders of professionalized legislatures -- have barely engaged one another despite agreeing on most of the facts: incumbency advantages are large, party organizations shape agendas, and long tenure correlates with both expertise and capture. Everyone seems to nod at the same evidence and then retreat to their original position. My hypothesis is that the dispute is stalled because each side is answering a different question: term-limit reformers ask how to discipline elected representatives, sortition advocates ask whether elections are the right selection mechanism at all, and defenders ask what competence a legislature needs to function -- so that empirical findings about any one of these leave the other two untouched. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a single underlying disagreement here -- about what political skill is, about whether legitimacy rests on consent or on descriptive representation, or about what accountability requires -- that all three positions are implicitly staking out? Give me a diagnosis with reasons, drawing on what each camp actually claims, and say what would count as progress: a natural experiment (state-level term limits, allotted citizen assemblies), a formal result about selection mechanisms, or a conceptual distinction that would force the camps onto common ground. If you think no such progress is available, say why the impasse is structural rather than merely stubborn.
