I'm teaching a philosophy of math seminar next week and want a dialogue I can hand out as a reading. Write it in American English, and make it about the four-colour theorem: is it actually proved, given that no human can check the whole case analysis by hand?

Set it up between two equally sharp people. One holds the "verificationist" line: a proof is whatever establishes the truth by valid inference from accepted axioms, the computer's case-checking is just reliable testimony like a long manual calculation, and demanding that proofs be humanly surveyable is psychologism about mathematics. The other defends the "understanding" line (roughly Tymoczko's worry): proofs aren't merely certificates of truth, they're supposed to give mathematicians understanding, so the four-colour result occupies a new epistemic category, something between a proof and an empirically supported conjecture, and the discipline quietly changed its standards to accommodate it.

Some requirements:

- Real back-and-forth. Each speaker has to answer the other's actual last point, not restate their opening. Let the verificationist press hard on where the line gets drawn (no human has surveyed every step of, say, a long group theory classification either) and let the other side press back on the difference between a long but surveyable-in-principle proof and one whose structure nobody can hold in view.
- Bring in the Flyspeck project on the Kepler conjecture and the fact that formal proof assistants now exist, and let them fight over whether formal verification dissolves the worry or just relocates it (who checks the checker? who checks the encoding of the statement?).
- Neither character is a strawman, and don't have one of them cave at the end. End with each of them stating what would change their mind, i.e. what kind of development or argument would make them concede, and have them agree on at least one specific place where the disagreement is empirically resolvable rather than purely philosophical.

Keep it around 600-900 words. After the dialogue, add a short paragraph for me noting where each character's position is most vulnerable to objection, so I can use it to kick off discussion.
