I am an eleventh-grade student in an advanced economics and philosophy elective, and next Thursday I have to present a proposal for how our student council should divide a $1,200 grant between the robotics club and the theater club. I want to defend the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution rather than the more familiar Nash bargaining solution. In my example, the disagreement allocation is $400 for robotics and $200 for theater, while the largest individually feasible allocations are $900 and $500. Kalai–Smorodinsky gives each club the same proportion of its possible gain above the disagreement point, producing $775 for robotics and $425 for theater; maximizing the Nash product would instead produce $700 and $500. My thesis is not that Kalai–Smorodinsky measures objective moral desert, but that it is the most defensible public procedure for a school budget because it makes equal proportional progress from a baseline and responds better than the Nash solution when one group’s feasible opportunities expand.

My audience will include an AP economics teacher who prefers Nash’s axiomatic approach and is likely to raise independence of irrelevant alternatives, two mathematically inclined students who will focus on normalization and the somewhat artificial ideal point, the student-council treasurer, who will care about clubs strategically exaggerating their needs or aspirations, and one ethics-focused student who thinks ambition should not entitle a club to more money than need. Prepare me specifically for that audience, not for professional bargaining theorists. Give me the objections in approximately the order they are likely to arise, phrased naturally by the person most likely to raise each one, and rank them by how much damage they could do. For each, provide a reply I could deliver in sixty to ninety seconds, state what I should concede immediately, identify the weakest part of the reply, and explain whether I should answer with the numerical example or with a general principle.

Please take a definite position rather than presenting every option neutrally. Tell me whether my thesis survives as stated or whether I should narrow it to the more modest claim that Kalai–Smorodinsky is a useful procedural compromise under publicly verified baselines and maximums. If narrowing is necessary, formulate the strongest revised thesis I should actually defend. Also identify the single reply that must succeed for the proposal to remain plausible, and the objection I should hope nobody asks because it exposes the most serious weakness in my position.
