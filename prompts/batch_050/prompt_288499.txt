Here's something that bugs me about how the law treats untruthfulness, and I'd like you to do some philosophy on it.

Under US perjury doctrine, a witness who gives a literally true but deliberately misleading answer generally hasn't commited perjury. In the classic case (Bronston v. United States, 1973), a witness asked under oath whether he'd ever had a Swiss bank account answered "The company had an account there for about six months, in Zürich" -- literally true, totally evasive, and meant to hide that he personally had had one for years. The Supreme Court said: no perjury, it's the examiner's job to pin the witness down with sharper questions. Meanwhile, over in advertising law it's the opposite: a company can be sanctioned for claims that are literally true but likely to mislead a reasonable consumer, like ads that imply a health benefit without ever stating one. So misleading gets you punished in one building and excused in the other, while outright lying is punished in both.

What does that split presuppose? Does perjury law only make sense if there's a morally deep difference between lying and misleading, or is the real difference institutional -- about what the adversarial system owes defendants, or about who bears the burden of conversational cooperativeness in a courtroom vs. a marketplace? Reconstruct the most charitable implicit theory that makes both doctrines consistent, tell me whether it actually works or one of them has to give, and then: if you're persuaded (as some philosophers are) that intentionally misleading is morally on par with lying, which doctrine should change, and what would the reformed version look like in practice?
