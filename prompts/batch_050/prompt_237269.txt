I have taught personal identity long enough to distrust my own intuitions, which is why I want them systematized. Below are my verdicts on the standard menagerie of teletransportation-style cases, and I would like to know what principle of survival, if any, delivers them. Verdict first, then the case.

In the ordinary teletransporter, where my body is scanned cell by cell, destroyed, and a perfect replica assembled on Mars with all my memories, character, and intentions, I judge that I survive; I would step into the machine for a conference. In the branch-line variant, where the improved scanner leaves the original intact on Earth and merely builds the replica on Mars, I judge that I do not go to Mars: the replica is a newly made person who falsely remembers being me, and I am the one stuck on Earth. In fission, where my two cerebral hemispheres are transplanted into two bodies and both recipients wake up fully psychologically continuous with me, I judge that I cannot be identical to both, that I am not obviously identical to either, and yet that this outcome is not remotely as bad as ordinary death. In gradual replacement, where my neurons are swapped one at a time for silicon functional duplicates over the course of a year, I judge that I survive the whole process. In the reassembly case, where my body is frozen, taken apart atom by atom, and put back together from the very same atoms a decade later, I also judge that I survive. And in the imperfect-copy case, where the machine malfunctions and produces a replica sharing only about seventy percent of my memories and dispositions, I judge that the result is not me but someone somewhat like me, and that this is close to being death.

Two of these verdicts already worry me. The first and second cases seem to differ only in what happens to the original body, and it is strange that whether I am the person on Mars should be settled by an event on Earth that the Martian person never detects. And my confidence about gradual replacement and reassembly rests on physical continuity, which the teletransporter case cheerfully does without. So: is there a single principle of survival that captures all six verdicts, stated precisely enough that I can see what each clause is doing? If the best you can do is a principle with a no-competitors rider to handle branch-line and fission, say whether that rider is principled or merely decorative. If no acceptable principle saves all six, tell me which verdict I should surrender and why that one is the cheapest to lose. Then give me the principle's verdicts on two cases I have not listed: a teleporter that destroys the original only after the replica has lived on Mars for a month, and a machine whose copies vary continuously in fidelity from perfect down to random noise, so that I can see whether your principle forces a sharp cutoff in an apparently continuous series. Finally, if you conclude that my pattern of verdicts is best explained by the thought that what I actually care about in survival is not identity at all but some relation that admits of degree and of branching, say whether that dissolves my original question or simply declines to answer it.
