Before I commit to any theory of rational belief or acceptance for a paper I am drafting, I want the specification pinned down first, because the literature seems to argue past itself: one side builds everything around the lottery case, the other around the preface, and each side declares victory on its home turf while conceding the other's. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of when it is rational to outright believe a proposition (as opposed to merely assigning it a high probability) must satisfy. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it and say whether you take it to be a genuine constraint on any theory or a partisan demand of one camp.

At minimum, address the following, but do not limit yourself to them:

- The theory's verdict on the lottery case: for any ticket in a fair lottery I rationally believe it will lose, yet I do not rationally believe that every ticket will lose. Any theory must either reproduce this asymmetry or explain convincingly why one of the two judgments is mistaken.
- The theory's verdict on the preface case: an author rationally believes each claim in her book while rationally believing that the book contains at least one error. Is this a rationally inconsistent set of beliefs, and if not, why not?
- Closure: does rational outright belief have to be closed under known logical consequence, and if a theory denies this, what does it cost -- can I rationally believe p, rationally believe q, and rationally withhold belief from their conjunction?
- The relationship between outright belief and credence: must the theory be reducible to a threshold on degrees of belief, and if so, which of the paradoxes does the threshold view mishandle?
- The connection to ordinary knowledge claims and to action: we routinely say we know where we parked, that the shop is open, that the flight leaves at six, on evidence that is less than certain. Must a theory vindicate these, or is revising ordinary practice an acceptable cost?

Where two desiderata appear to be jointly unsatisfiable -- which I suspect is the real lesson of these paradoxes -- say so explicitly and identify which requirement a theory must give up to escape, since that triage is the part I most need. Then, briefly and only at the end, indicate how the main families of responses (consistency-based accounts, probabilistic threshold accounts, and accounts that treat outright belief as context-sensitive or stakes-sensitive) fare against the list, including which single desideratum does the most work in eliminating candidates.
