So this has been nagging at me for weeks. I'm 31, I fly a fair amount for work, I eat meat, I upgrade my phone every couple of years, and a few months ago I got into a late-night argument with a friend who does climate policy about whether people like me are *guilty* of anything, or whether that word just doesn't apply. She said something like, our generation will be judged by our grandkids the way Germans were judged after the war, and I pushed back hard, but honestly I couldn't articulate why the comparison felt both wrong and a little too close for comfort. She then told me to look at Karl Jaspers, who wrote a short book right after WWII (The Question of German Guilt, from lectures in 1946) where he splits guilt into four kinds: criminal guilt for people who actually broke laws, political guilt that falls on citizens of a state for what their state does, moral guilt that's between you and your conscience, and metaphysical guilt for failing human solidarity even when you did nothing illegal. He also insisted, as I understand it, that nobody is guilty for crimes committed before they were born or by people they happen to share a passport with -- collective liability for a state isn't the same as personal guilt.

Here's my actual question. Jaspers died in 1969, well before climate change was a public issue, so he never applied this scheme to a slow, collective, everyone-contributes-a-little catastrophe where the worst harm lands on people who don't exist yet. If he were alive today, how do you think he'd sort the climate case into his four categories? Be concrete: does the fossil fuel executive who funded disinformation campaigns land in the criminal category or does the slowness and legal grayness break that? Are ordinary voters in high-emitting democracies politically guilty the way he thought Germans were, even though no one ever voted *for* warming? Where does someone like me fall -- moral guilt, metaphysical guilt, or nothing at all? And the part I really can't settle: can a nation, or a generation, or "humanity" be guilty of a process nobody individually intended, and does that guilt survive the people who make it up? If my grandkids blame *me* personally in 2070, would Jaspers say they're making a category mistake, or would he say they're getting something right that his own generation got wrong about the Germans?

I don't want a balanced survey of what different philosophers might say -- I want you to commit. Tell me which parts of Jaspers's scheme you think map cleanly onto the climate case, which parts strain or break, and where he'd have to actually revise his view rather than just apply it. If you think my friend's grandkids-comparison is sloppy, say so and say why; if you think it's uncomfortably apt, say that instead. Please write the answer in American English.
