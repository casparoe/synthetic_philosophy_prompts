I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I am applying for a six-month independent research grant. Please turn the following half-formed idea into a polished two-page research proposal.

My starting point is the familiar scale argument concerning insects: even if the probability that insects are sentient is low, human practices affect such enormous numbers of them that the expected moral stakes could be very large. This is often represented as a one-shot calculation: number of animals multiplied by probability of sentience multiplied by some estimate of welfare impact. I suspect that this framing misses an important temporal feature of actual decisions. A society does not merely choose once under uncertainty; it chooses whether to build institutions, supply chains, and technologies that may become difficult to reverse, while also deciding how much effort to devote to learning whether insects can suffer. For example, rapidly expanding insect farming might create economic interests that make later evidence less likely to change practice, whereas a pause, smaller-scale deployment, or welfare-sensitive design could preserve the ability to respond to future discoveries. I provisionally call this problem epistemic irreversibility: a decision can matter both because of its direct effects and because of how it changes our future capacity and willingness to learn.

The proposal should have a precise title and should state a tractable version of the central question: under what conditions, if any, does preserving the ability to learn about insect sentience justify stronger precautions than a one-shot expected-value calculation would recommend? Keep the question philosophical and decision-theoretic rather than turning it into an empirical review of insect neuroscience.

Please include:

1. The problem and why it matters, explained without assuming familiarity with academic philosophy.
2. Why the obvious positions do not settle it. Address at least three responses: maximizing expected moral value; applying a broad precautionary principle; and discounting very small probabilities of sentience. Explain what each gets right and where it leaves the temporal or institutional problem unresolved.
3. The key distinctions the project would need to make. These should include the difference between a numerical probability and the reliability of the evidence behind it, between uncertainty about sentience and uncertainty about welfare intensity, and between an outcome being physically irreversible and an institution making reversal politically unlikely.
4. A clear research method. I am comfortable with simple formal models, so the project may construct a minimal sequential-decision model, but the mathematics should be only as complicated as the philosophical point requires. Explain what cases or thought experiments would be used to stress-test the model, and say why this approach is preferable either to merely criticizing existing intuitions or to attempting a broad survey of all animal-ethics positions.
5. A realistic six-month work plan, broken down by month.
6. The possible conclusions. These should include at least one conclusion that vindicates my hunch, one that shows ordinary expected-value reasoning already handles it, and one intermediate conclusion identifying limited conditions under which epistemic irreversibility changes the recommendation.
7. What the final deliverables would be: ideally an 8,000-word paper plus a short decision framework that a policy-maker or technologist could use without accepting any single comprehensive moral theory.
8. What would count as the project failing, including the possibility that the concept of epistemic irreversibility collapses into ordinary option value, becomes too vague to guide decisions, or depends on empirical claims that cannot be established within the project’s scope.

Do not include a bibliography, literature review, or invented citations; I will identify and add relevant sources separately. You may describe positions and arguments generically. Also, please be direct if my framing contains a confused premise or if the proposed task cannot be done well as stated: I would rather have the proposal challenge the idea than politely build on a conceptual mistake.
