I am writing a novel whose central character, a semi-retired epistemologist, has a tiresome habit of lecturing her dinner guests. In a pivotal scene she explains to a young journalist why his profession's casual relationship with accuracy is worse than he imagines, and I want her monologue to be philosophically sound enough that a knowledgeable reader will not be pulled out of the book. To prepare her lines, my research assistant — who minored in philosophy some years ago and is otherwise reliable — gave me the following summary of the debate about the norms of assertion, and I would like to know how much of it can safely go into her mouth.

His summary runs as follows. Contemporary epistemologists hold that assertion is governed by a single constitutive rule, much as a game like chess is governed by its rules: you must assert only what you know. On this view, associated mainly with Timothy Williamson, asserting something false is not merely a factual error but a breach of the very rule that defines assertion, so that a liar and an honest but mistaken speaker are guilty of the same offense. Because knowledge requires truth, the knowledge rule automatically vindicates the older and simpler idea that truth alone is the norm of assertion. The main rival view — that you may properly assert whatever you sincerely believe — was abandoned once lottery-style cases showed that even fully justified beliefs can be improper to assert. It follows that whenever anyone asserts something, they represent themselves as knowing it, which is why the comeback 'How do you know that?' is always in order.

Here is what I need from you. First, a sentence-by-sentence audit of the summary: separate what is accurate from what is a defensible simplification a specialist would let pass in conversation, what is contested — that is, one side of a live dispute presented as settled — and what is simply wrong or a non sequitur. Second, tell me what a defender of the knowledge norm would insist on adding or qualifying, and what defenders of the rival norms (a truth norm, a justified-belief norm, a sincerity norm) would object to most strongly, so that if my character has a clever interlocutor at the table I can write his reply. Third, and most important for my purposes: the scene currently ends with my character claiming that because the journalist knew his source was unreliable, his published assertion was not merely false but a violation of the rules that constitute speech itself, and she compares it to moving a rook diagonally. Once the summary is corrected, does that closing flourish still stand, or does it lean on one of the errors? Please be candid about how much philosophical weight a line of dialogue can bear.
