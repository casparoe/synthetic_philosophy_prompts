My housemate and I have been arguing about Kelly betting for weeks and he's finally landed me in a chain of reasoning I cant find the exit from. (1) You only get to live one sequence of bets, so what should matter to you is the time-average growth rate of your own bankroll, not an average across parallel possible worlds, which is what plain expected value computes. (2) For repeated multiplicative bets, the time-average growth rate is maximised by maximising the expected logarithm of wealth on each round, which is just the Kelly fraction. (3) So Kelly betting is rationally required of everyone, whatever their tastes or goals happen to be. (4) But (3) proves far too much: it makes the widow who refuses to stake half her pension on a genuinely favourable coin flip guilty of an arithmetic error rather than holder of a different preference, and it demotes every attitude to risk except exactly-logarithmic from a taste into a mistake. (5) Rationality tells you how to pursue your ends, it doesn't fix what your ends or tastes must be, so (3) is false. (6) Therefore one of (1) and (2) has to go. He insists (2) is pure maths and hence untouchable, which would sink (1) and, as he cheerfully points out, most of the ergodicity critique of expected value along with it. I'd rather salvage the intuition behind (1) than accept (3), if forced to choose. Which step actually fails, why does it look so solid, and what does rejecting it cost me?
