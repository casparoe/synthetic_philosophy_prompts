So I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a feature about whether chatbots "believe" anything. Every draft I read from my colleagues just name-drops Dennett's intentional stance and moves on, and I want to actually pressure-test the argument instead of gesturing at it. Here's where I've gotten.

Dennett's line in Real Patterns, as I understand it, is roughly that a pattern is real when some description that appeals to it is genuinely indispensable for compressing and predicting the data -- you lose real predictive power if you refuse to talk in its terms. And the three stances (physical, design, intentional) are supposed to be different vocabularies you adopt depending on which patterns pay rent.

So the specific claim I want your credence on: attributing beliefs and desires to current large language models picks out a real pattern in Dennett's sense, i.e. belief-talk is close to indispensable for predicting what these systems do, and not merely a habit we could always in principle replace with design-stance talk about architecture and training, or with brute statistical talk about token distributions.

Before you answer, chew on the complication that's been bugging me, because I havent seen it addressed anywhere. Even if belief-talk compresses LLM behavior beautifully, these models are trained on human text that is already saturated with belief-desire descriptions. So maybe the pattern we recover is a pattern in *descriptions of minds*, inherited from the corpus, rather than a pattern produced by anything doing the believing. Analogy: a novel about a chess grandmaster is wonderfully compressed by the description "a grandmaster picked these moves," but the novel doesn't play chess. The compressibility is borrowed from the source material. Call this the echo objection.
