A friend who took one psychology course summarized the situationist critique of virtue ethics for me like this: 'Virtue ethics holds that morality is fundamentally about possessing stable character traits like courage and honesty, and that right action flows from them. But social psychology has empirically refuted this picture. Milgram's obedience experiments and Zimbardo's prison study show that ordinary, decent people can be made to harm or abuse others purely by features of the situation -- an authority's instructions, an assigned role -- so behavior is driven by situations, not character. That means there are no robust character traits, and a normative theory built on the idea of cultivating them is built on sand. The only reply virtue ethicists have is to say that genuine virtue is rare, which concedes that their theory describes almost no one and is useless for the rest of us.' I suspect this is mostly right but partly unfair, though I can't say where. Which parts would a careful proponent of the critique (say, in the spirit of Harman or Doris) endorse, which parts conflate two claims the literature keeps apart -- for instance, the claim that broad, cross-situationally consistent traits are empirically unsupported versus the claim that traits don't exist at all -- and which parts present a standard objection as if it were a decisive refutation? Does the 'built on sand' verdict, or the 'useless for the rest of us' verdict, follow from the parts that are accurate? In answering, please separate what is settled -- about the experiments, about what virtue ethics actually claims, and about how the debate has developed, including personality psychology's response and the replication-era reassessment of the classic studies -- from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers and psychologists, and don't present a contested position as the consensus.
