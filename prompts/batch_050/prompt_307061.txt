I recently took over teaching the ethics survey at my university and, while preparing the unit on deception, I found myself defending a position I hadn't expected to hold: that there is a genuine and morally weighty difference between lying and merely misleading. My students pushed back hard, so I went back to the classical source of the view, Aquinas's treatment of lying in the Summa Theologiae (Second Part of the Second Part, Question 110, Article 1). There he writes: "However, the essential notion of a lie is taken from formal falsehood, from the fact namely, that a person intends to say what is false; wherefore also the word 'mendacium' [lie] is derived from its being in opposition to the 'mind.'" And a few lines later: "That a person intends to cause another to have a false opinion, by deceiving him, does not belong to the species of lying, but to perfection thereof." What I take from this is that the core wrong of lying is not the production of a false belief in the hearer at all — that is merely the lie's characteristic effect — but the speaker's asserting against his own mind, a kind of misuse of assertion itself. On this basis I've come to hold that deliberately misleading someone with words one takes to be literally true (through implicature, strategic omission, or the carefully ambiguous answers the old casuists discussed under the heading of mental reservation) is a categorically lesser wrong than outright lying, and is sometimes permissible where lying never is, even when the hearer ends up with exactly the same false belief in both cases.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure: what would count against it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Please don't simply restate the intuition that "misleading is just as bad" — I know that intuition exists; I want to know which arguments, cases, or results should actually move me, and why. Some candidates I can already see, and I'd like your assessment of how damaging each really is: the objection that a hearer wronged by deceptive true speech typically cannot tell which wrong was done to her, so that any asymmetry between the two must rest on the agent's inner orientation rather than on anything the victim could detect — and whether that is a problem for the position or just a consequence of taking norms of assertion seriously; the case of so-called bald-faced lies, where someone asserts what they believe false with no expectation of deceiving anyone, which seems to test whether the Thomistic account of lying's wrongness can survive without any deception at all; and the worry from philosophy of language that once you take a theory of implicature seriously, the speaker who misleads through implicature is genuinely committing himself to the false content in a way that collapses the tidy distinction between what is asserted and what is merely conveyed. I'd also like your honest verdict on whether the position has been insulated into near-unfalsifiability: by defining lying as assertion against the mind, every hard case of deceptive-but-true speech gets sorted out of the category by construction, so no amount of wrongful deception by true words can ever touch the thesis — and whether that sort of definitional protection is a vice for a view of this kind or simply what having a principled account looks like.

Finally, two requests about how the answer should be organized. First, for each defeater, tell me whether a committed defender of the Thomistic asymmetry — a casuist of the old school or a modern philosopher of language sympathetic to assertion-based accounts — would concede it as genuine pressure or dismiss it as missing the point, and on what grounds; I want to know which of my view's risks are ones its own defenders acknowledge, not just which ones hostile critics press. Second, tell me what this position stands or falls with: if the moral difference between lying and misleading turns out to depend on a general theory of assertion, or on some account of what hearers are entitled to expect from speakers, then say so explicitly and tell me which developments in those neighboring areas would demote my view from defensible to untenable. I'm going to have to defend this in front of my students next term, and I'd rather find out now which part of the position is load-bearing and which part is scaffolding.
