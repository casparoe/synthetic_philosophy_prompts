I'm a graduate student in philosophy TAing a seminar on rational choice and distributive justice, and this week we covered axiomatic bargaining theory. One of my students found a set of unofficial course notes online and brought them to office hours, asking whether he could rely on them for the final paper he's writing on how two co-authors should divide credit (and royalties) for a joint book. The notes contain the following summary of the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution, and I want to check it carefully before I tell him anything, because some of it matches what I remember but a few sentences set off alarm bells I can't quite localize:

"The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution works like this. First, find each player's ideal point: the most that player could possibly get from the feasible set of agreements. Then choose the agreement where each player receives the same share of the total surplus, measured as a fraction of their ideal point -- so it is essentially just the familiar egalitarian principle of equal shares, applied to bargaining. It is the main rival of the Nash bargaining solution, which instead picks the agreement maximizing the product of the players' utility gains. The two solutions rest on nearly the same axiomatic foundations: both satisfy Pareto efficiency, symmetry, invariance to affine rescalings of utility, and independence of irrelevant alternatives, and Kalai–Smorodinsky additionally satisfies a monotonicity axiom -- if the feasible set grows in a player's favor, that player should not end up worse off -- which the Nash solution violates. So on the axiomatic merits, Kalai–Smorodinsky is strictly better justified than Nash. One caveat: because the solution divides the whole feasible pie without reference to what happens if negotiations break down, it can be insensitive to the players' outside options."

How fair is this summary? Please go through it claim by claim and separate what a bargaining theorist would accept as written, what is a common simplification that a charitable reader could let pass, and what is actually wrong or conflates two things the theory keeps apart. I am particularly suspicious of the sentence about the axiom list and of the final caveat, but I would like your independent read rather than leading you to my guesses. Where the summary is wrong, please state the correct claim precisely enough that I can explain it to a smart undergraduate without a problem set. Finally, the notes go on to conclude that because Kalai–Smorodinsky is "strictly better justified," it is the solution my student should recommend his co-author adopt for the royalty split.
