A colleague who took a political philosophy course years ago gave me this summary of Derek Parfit's nonidentity problem, which she said was the reason she stopped worrying about climate ethics:

'Parfit's nonidentity problem shows that large-scale policy choices change which people come to exist, because different policies produce different patterns of who meets whom and conceives children when. So when we choose a polluting energy policy over a clean one, the people who later suffer from the degraded climate cannot claim to have been harmed by our choice: had we chosen differently, they would never have existed at all, and someone is only harmed by an act if that act made them worse off than they would otherwise have been. Parfit drew the conclusion that, strictly speaking, we have no obligations of justice to future generations, since we cannot wrong people our choices bring into existence -- duties regarding the distant future are at most duties of beneficence. He took the only consistent escape from this conclusion to be total utilitarianism, and regarded any view that resists it, including so-called person-affecting views, as confused.'

Something about this sits badly with me, but I can't locate the problem. If the argument is sound, it seems to let present generations off the hook in a way that can't be right, yet the underlying logic -- you can't be harmed by something without which you wouldn't exist -- has some pull. Could you audit this summary for me? Specifically: which parts accurately state the nonidentity problem as philosophers understand it, which parts misrepresent Parfit's own conclusions about what the problem shows, and where does the summary slide from the claim that future people cannot claim to have been made worse off to the much stronger claim that our choice does nothing morally wrong? I'd also like to know whether the premise that harm requires being made worse off than one would otherwise have been is as uncontroversial as the summary assumes, and whether rejecting that premise is the only way -- or even the standard way -- philosophers have tried to resist the conclusion. Finally, if the summary is corrected, does anything like my colleague's policy conclusion survive, or does the nonidentity problem leave ordinary intuitions about our duties concerning climate change essentially intact?
