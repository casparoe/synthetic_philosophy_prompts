I'm a novelist, mid-career, and lately I've been preoccupied with a feature of my own profession that I find philosophically puzzling. Almost everything that gives literary life its structure seems to be addressed to people who do not yet exist. We speak of books being tested by time; we treat posthumous fame as the only unambiguous kind; estates spend real money administering the work of authors long dead, and scholars spend whole careers in archives preserving papers whose authors never managed to preserve themselves. Copyright in my country outlasts the author by seventy years. My late teacher left instructions that her unfinished final novel be destroyed; her executors published it instead, and the literary press praised them for it, on the grounds that posterity's claim outweighed hers. Yet at the same time the law refuses the dead almost all standing: you generally cannot libel them, their control over their work expires, and posterity itself — the audience all this labor supposedly serves — is nobody in particular.

I recently encountered a thought experiment associated with the philosopher Samuel Scheffler, sometimes called the afterlife conjecture. Imagine that you would live out your natural life in full, but that shortly after your death — say, thirty days later — an asteroid would extinguish humanity. Scheffler's suggestion is that for most of us, a great deal of what makes our projects matter would drain away under this condition, and that our valuing things therefore presupposes, far more than we ever notice, that humanity will continue after we are gone. I recognize myself in this more than I'd care to admit: the thought of my novels being read in the year 2200 moves me in a way I cannot cash out in terms of anything that will ever happen to me. And yet I notice two wrinkles. I never bother to imagine readers in the year 12,000, so it isn't an open-ended future I'm counting on. And a guarantee of exactly two more centuries of human readers would feel very different to me from an open-ended one — and different again from the promise of perfect digital storage that outlasts all readers, which moves me almost not at all.

So here is my question. What does the institution of posthumous literary reputation — the archives, the estates, the canons, the decades-long tail of copyright, the norms about honoring or overriding dead authors' wishes — actually presuppose? Does it presuppose that human beings will exist and read indefinitely, or only that they will exist for some bounded stretch, and if the latter, how long a stretch does the practice need? Does it presuppose that the value of my work depends constitutively on future people, as Scheffler-style reasoning suggests, or could the whole edifice be reconstructed in terms that make no reference to the far future at all — as a wager on near-term reputation, a present-tense satisfaction, or a kind of self-continuation that requires only my current belief in future readers rather than the readers themselves? Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely sits well with, and tell me which rival accounts it quietly rules out. Finally: if we came to know the horizon — if it were certain that humanity ends two hundred years from now — what, concretely, would have to change about how we publish, preserve, and honor books, and what could stay exactly as it is?
