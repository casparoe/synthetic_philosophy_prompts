Act as a political philosopher who specializes in egalitarian theories of distributive justice, and take your time with this -- I'd rather you think carefully and sketch some initial brainstorming before settling on the final answer than rush it.

Background: I'm drafting a paper that tries to defend a luck egalitarian view -- the rough idea that it's unjust when people are worse off than others through no fault or choice of their own, but not (or at least less) unjust when the gap traces to choices they could reasonably have avoided. Before I commit to any particular version, I want the full specification first, because the literature keeps attacking straw versions and I don't want to build on sand.

So: write the desiderata that any adequate theory of luck-sensitive egalitarian justice must meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Please make sure the list at least engages with:

- the harshness problem (e.g. the uninsured driver who negligently crashes and now faces ruinous medical bills -- does the theory really say he gets nothing?)
- the "expensive tastes" case (someone who cultivated a taste for champagne and pre-seasoned lacross sticks vs someone whose expensive needs are congenital)
- disabilities people are born with vs ones that result from their own risky conduct
- the worry that applying the theory in practice requires intrusive state inquiries into how responsible each person is for their situation -- imagine a government running "responsibility audits" before granting any assistance
- the complaint that the theory rests on demeaning or pitying judgments about the badly-off
- whether compensating bad brute luck requires leveling down the better-off even when that helps nobody

For each desideratum, say why it's a genuine requirement and not just a prejudice of one camp. Then: which of these are non-negotiable and which are matters of taste? Are any pairs of desiderata jointly unsatisfiable -- I have a suspicion that full responsibility-sensitivity and avoiding harshness can't both be had, and if the list itself turns out to be inconsistent I'd rather know now. Also tell me which single desideratum does the most work in knocking out rival theories.

Very briefly at the end, you can note how the main rivals (relational egalitarianism, prioritarianism, sufficientarianism) do against the list, but keep that short -- the criteria and their motivating cases are the actual deliverable, the scorecard is just a bonus.
