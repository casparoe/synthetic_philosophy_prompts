After a recent disagreement about whether I was obliged to honor a commitment that had become far costlier than I anticipated, I began reading Kant seriously, and I am now trying to determine what his universalizability test actually delivers by checking it against my settled judgments about cases. My verdicts: borrowing money on a promise to repay that I do not intend to keep is clearly wrong; never developing any of one's abilities is wrong even though it harms no one identifiable; breaking a casual promise to meet a friend merely because a better invitation arrived is wrong; lying to a visitor who asks whether my guest is home, intending to harm him, is permissible, perhaps even required; quietly taking benefits from cooperative schemes (voting systems, cost-sharing arrangements) while never contributing is wrong; and giving nothing at all to charity while comfortably off feels wrong but markedly less so than the promise cases. My problem is the pair I cannot reconcile: universalizing the lying promise seems to fail the test in exactly the same way as universalizing the protective lie, yet I want the first condemned and the second permitted. Please tell me which version of the test — the contradiction-in-conception reading, the contradiction-in-the-will reading, or some principled combination — best captures these verdicts, stating the principle precisely and identifying which cases motivate each part of it. If no version saves all six verdicts, say plainly which one must be abandoned and why it is the best candidate for abandonment. Finally, apply your preferred principle to the case I remain genuinely undecided about: assuring my landlord that I intend to renew my lease, to secure a small concession, while privately planning to leave.
