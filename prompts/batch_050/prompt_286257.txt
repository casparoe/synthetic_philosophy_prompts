I write literary fiction for a living, and I recently stumbled into a late-night argument with a physicist friend about what philosophers call the underdetermination of theory by evidence -- the claim, as I understand it, that a body of evidence never forces a single theory on us, because many incompatible theories can accommodate the same observations, especially once you're allowed to adjust the surrounding assumptions. My first reaction was that this is simply my day job: give me a fixed ending and a set of established facts about the characters, and I can write you half a dozen genuinely different novels that all reach that ending without contradicting anything on the page. My friend insisted the scientific problem is different in kind, not just in stakes, and I couldn't tell whether he was right or whether he was flinching from how much his field resembles mine.

So here is what I'd like. Restate the underdetermination thesis -- both the modest version (any failed prediction can be absorbed by revising an auxiliary assumption rather than the theory itself, since no hypothesis is tested alone) and the radical version (there exist wholesale rival theories, empirically equivalent forever, between which no evidence could ever adjudicate) -- in the vocabulary of fiction writing, which is the framework where I'm fluent: plot constraints, continuity, retroactive fixes to earlier chapters, canon, genre conventions, the reader's demand for coherence, the editorial process. Use whatever precision that vocabulary permits. For instance, is a failed experimental prediction like a plot hole that a reviewer has flagged -- and is the Duhemian freedom to blame an auxiliary assumption just my freedom to rewrite chapter three instead of chapter nine? Is a "crucial experiment" like a scene a reader has been promised must come, or does the analogy break exactly there?

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which elements of the philosophical debate translate cleanly into novelist's terms, which only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all? My suspicion is that the untranslatable residue is the world itself: my manuscripts answer to nothing but coherence and the reader's patience, whereas theories answer to something that pushes back, and I want to know whether that difference dissolves the philosophical problem or merely relocates it. If I'm wrong and there's a deeper disanalogy I've missed -- or, conversely, if the translation reveals that some part of the philosophers' worry was an artifact of their vocabulary all along -- I'd rather be told plainly. I'm fluent in fiction, not philosophy, so please don't assume the standard names and citations; explain any technical terms you need as you go.
