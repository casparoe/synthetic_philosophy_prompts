I teach a first-year philosophy of mind course and I'm screening Inside Out (the Pixar one) in the week we do Dennett. Read the film through his critique of the Cartesian theater and the multiple drafts model. On its face the movie is exhibit A for the prosecution: there's a literal headquarters, a console, a screen showing what Riley sees, and five little people inside her skull who watch it and fight over the controls. Joy is a homonculus if anything is. So here's what I want the reading to actually work through:

- The regress. The emotions themselves have inner lives -- Joy remembers things, misjudges Sadness, has a breakdown in the memory dump, learns something. The film never asks whats inside Joy's head (a smaller console? even smaller emotions?). And when it briefly cuts to the control rooms inside Mom, Dad, the dog, the cat, it seems to universalize the regress and play it for laughs. Does the film know it's a regress? Is the joke a confession or a dodge?
- Where does the film quietly look more like multiple drafts than the theater? I'm thinking of the memory system: the orbs get replayed on a screen, but Sadness touching an orb permanently changes it -- a happy memory becomes a sad one. That's not a recording screened for an audience, that's reconstructive, revisable memory where every retrieval rewrites the trace. Does the film endorse a drafts-ish picture of memory while staying Cartesian about everything else? Are there other spots that resist the theater reading -- the dump, the Train of Thought, Dream Productions literally shooting a movie each night to be screened in HQ (a theater inside the theater)?
- The global workspace complication. Baars' global workspace theory openly uses a theater metaphor but without a single homunculus viewer -- the point is broadcasting to many unconscious consumers. The console screen is watched by all the emotions at once and their joint struggle at the controls produces behavior. So is the film homuncular, or is it a global workspace with the workers gathered around the broadcast? Is it better evidence for Baars than for the naive picture Dennett attacks? This is the part I most want help with because I genuinely can't decide.
- Who is Riley? The film splits its protagonist: Riley the girl, who skates and lies and gets on the bus, versus the emotions who seem to be doing the deciding. The running-away sequence, where the console greys out and none of the emotions can operate it, depicts something like depression as a hardware failure rather than as any character's act. Is that the film's most philosophically honest moment about persons, or is it where the whole machinery breaks down?
- And the meta question: maybe you just can't tell a story about a mind without giving it a protagonist and a place where it all comes together. If so, does the film's failure to escape the Cartesian theater explain why the intuition is so sticky -- that it's a narrative necessity, not just a philosophical error? What would a film that faithfully dramatized multiple drafts even look like, and would anyone watch it?

Be faithful in both directions: don't use the movie as a punching bag for Dennett, and don't let its emotional truth excuse the metaphysics. If the film gets something right that the papers miss, or the reverse, point to the specific scenes that prove it. And skip the bibliography, my reading list is sorted -- I want the actual reading.
