I cover science for a general-interest magazine, and I've been invited to run a ninety-minute seminar for a dozen early-career science writers on how to handle evolutionary explanations skeptically. I want to build the session around Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique of adaptationism, since most attendees will have already filed at least one piece that opens with a plausible-sounding story about why some human trait was selected for. Plan the session rather than writing it for me: what has to be in place before the critique can land (I'm assuming the adaptationist programme needs to be presented in its strongest form first, but tell me if that's wrong), the one example to carry the whole session — the San Marco spandrels themselves, or something strictly biological like the human chin — and what that example must show and must not pretend to show. Then map the misconceptions these writers will bring, in roughly the order they'll surface: that the critique denies natural selection does much, that a spandrel is just an unimportant trait, that adaptationist claims are unfalsifiable and can therefore be waved away — and where in the sequence I should head each off. The failure mode I most fear is overcorrection: attendees leaving with "that's a just-so story" as a thought-terminating move rather than a demand for a different kind of evidence, so say where the plan should inoculate against that. Finally, give me one closing exercise with a fresh case that would show they can apply the critique rather than recite it, and note what I should deliberately leave out at this level.
