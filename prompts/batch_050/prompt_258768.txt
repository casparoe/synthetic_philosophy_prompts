I have been reading and commenting on rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade, and one cluster of results I keep seeing leaned on is program equilibrium — Tennenholtz's observation that when players in a game can condition their behavior on each other's source code, mutual cooperation in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma can be sustained as an equilibrium, and the later line of work on FairBot-style programs, which cooperate with any program they can prove cooperates with them, and thereby achieve mutual cooperation without any iteration, reputation, or enforcement.

Last month, on a private forum, a friend used these results to walk me into a conclusion I cannot accept, and I would like a professional diagnosis. Please take the role of a game theorist and decision theorist who knows the program-equilibrium and bounded-provability literature well. Think carefully rather than pattern-matching to the usual one-boxing debates. Here is his argument, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it:

(1) In a program game, where each player submits a program with access to the other's source, mutual cooperation between suitably written programs is a Nash equilibrium. This is a theorem, not a conjecture.

(2) What does the work in these results is purely structural: a program cooperates exactly when it can verify that the counterpart will cooperate with it. Nothing in the mechanism turns on the counterpart's goals, values, or identity.

(3) Therefore the general lesson is that wherever agents can verify each other's conditional behavior, mutual cooperation is the rationally mandatory outcome; defection is leaving utility on the table.

(4) Real interactions approximate this condition: humans read each other's psychology, and AI systems are becoming increasingly interpretable to one another, so ordinary one-shot prisoner's dilemmas are approximate program games.

(5) So defection in real one-shot prisoner's dilemmas is straightforwardly irrational, on a par with burning money.

(6) And since the verification criterion in (2) is neutral about values, rationality demands cooperating even with an agent whose goals you find abhorrent — say a paperclip maximizer with fully transparent source — whenever its cooperation in the game at hand is verifiable. What it does with its gains downstream is its own business.

(7) But (5) and (6) cannot both be right. A decision theory that condemns every defection as irrational and obliges you to route resources to paperclippers has gone wrong somewhere. Hence something between (1) and (6) breaks.

My own sympathies, so you know where I am biased: if forced to choose, I would rather keep (3) than (6). I want cooperation between mutually suspicious humans to come out rational in the right cases, and I am prepared to bite some bullets about whom it is permissible to trade with. But I genuinely cannot tell which step is doing the damage, and I do not want to be handed the answer I am hoping for.

Before committing to a verdict, briefly brainstorm at least three candidate weak points in the chain — for instance, a slide between "cooperation is an equilibrium" and "cooperation is rationally mandatory," a hidden fragility in the provability conditions the mechanism depends on, an equivocation on "cooperate" between a move in a game and a general posture toward an agent, or a failure of the approximation claimed in (4). Then argue for which single step is the real break, explain why that step looked fine, and say what each of the main alternative diagnoses would cost me if I adopted it instead.

One epistemic request. State explicitly what would change your mind. If your diagnosis could be overturned by a result I may not know — an equilibrium-selection theorem that privileges cooperation, or a robustness result about Löbian cooperation that cuts the other way — say so, and say where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
