I recently joined an advisory panel that evaluates proposals for deliberate, high-power transmissions from Earth toward nearby exoplanetary systems — what is often called active SETI or METI. Our first case has just landed: a well-funded consortium wants to beam a repeated, content-rich message toward a temperate exoplanet a few dozen light-years away, with a transmission schedule lasting years. I have to take a position before our next session, and I keep being pulled back to the dark forest hypothesis.

Please describe the most important implications of that hypothesis for how our panel should assess this proposal. By the hypothesis I mean the line of reasoning that runs roughly as follows: survival is every civilization's overriding goal; the time lags imposed by interstellar distance make it impossible to verify another civilization's intentions; any civilization's capabilities can surge explosively within the interval a signal takes to cross the gap; and a rational actor facing that combination of ignorance and risk may conclude that the safest response to discovering another civilization is to destroy it before it becomes a threat — in which case announcing one's location to the cosmos is a potentially fatal act.

I am not asking for a summary of where this idea appears in fiction. I want the implications worked out for decisions we actually have to make, specifically:

First, which of the hypothesis's load-bearing assumptions matter most for our case — for instance, that attack is cheap relative to defense, that a signal reveals the sender's location precisely enough to act on, that peaceful intent cannot be credibly communicated across a one-way channel, and that at least one aggressive civilization exists within earshot — and how the force of each assumption changes with the proposal's parameters: transmission power, target distance, repetition over years versus a single burst, and how much information the message contains about us.

Second, what the hypothesis implies about the distinctions the panel will be pressed on: listening versus transmitting, a single narrow-beam message versus an omnidirectional beacon, and our proposed transmission versus the radio and radar leakage Earth has been emitting for over a century — including whether the hypothesis, taken seriously, makes our deliberation moot because the announcement already happened.

Third, how the panel should weigh a very-low-probability, extinction-level downside against benefits that are real but modest and mostly scientific — and whether ordinary expected-value reasoning is even the right instrument here, given that the probability estimates are guesses rather than grounded frequencies, and given that the risk is borne by people who never consented to the transmission.

Finally, please identify where the hypothesis's logic breaks down or proves too much: the conditions under which it would counsel against astronomy, space exploration, or any detectable activity at all, and what follows for a panel whose recommendations must be ones member states could plausibly adopt. Where an implication depends on a factual assumption nobody can currently check, say so explicitly rather than papering over it.
