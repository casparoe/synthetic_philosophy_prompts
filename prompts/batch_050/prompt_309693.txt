I'm putting together the final for my upper-level formal epistemology course and I need one essay question on objective Bayesianism and the maximum entropy principle -- specifically on whether there can be such a thing as *the* rational prior. The trouble with past years' questions is that students could get full marks by reciting the standard pitch: entropy measures uncertainty, maxent picks the least committal distribution given your constraints, Jaynes showed it works for statistical mechanics, done. I want a question that forces them to confront the real crux, which is that maxent's answer depends on how you set up the sample space -- so a flat prior over a cube's side length and a flat prior over its volume contradict each other, and "be maximally uncertain" doesn't tell you which one to use. The question should push them to evaluate the invariance/transformation-group response (choose the prior invariant under the symmetries of the problem, e.g. log-uniform for scale parameters) and to take a stand on whether that genuinely rescues rational priors or just relocates the arbitrariness to the choice of which symmetries "count."

To seed the question, include this concrete setup inside it: a factory produces cubes whose side lengths are known only to be somewhere between 1cm and 3cm, and the maxent prior over side length gives different answers than the maxent prior over volume -- students should have to say whether one of these descriptions is *rationally privileged*, and if so by what. Students will have 75 minutes. I've told them all term that "ignorance is not a distribution," so the question should partly test whether they noticed the tension between that slogan and the objective Bayesian program.

Please give me: (1) the question itself, phrased the way it would appear on the exam paper; (2) a model answer of about 600 words written at the level of your best student; and (3) a marking scheme built around five must-hit points with marks totalling 20, where for each point you add one line on how candidates typically get it wrong (e.g., treating the Bertrand-style paradoxes as a merely technical nuisance about reparameterization rather than a challenge to the whole idea of a uniquely rational prior, or assuming the transformation-group fix is uncontroversially available in every problem). The scheme should make clear what seperates an answer that understands why the cube factory case is a *problem for a theory of rational belief* from one that just narrates it.
