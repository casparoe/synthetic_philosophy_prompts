The assignment prompt was: "A rescue worker can save either one person stranded on one rock or five people stranded on another, but cannot reach both groups in time. Set out Taurek's argument in 'Should the Numbers Count?' for the conclusion that it is permissible to save the one, including what he says about giving each person an equal chance. Then raise what you take to be the strongest objection to his position and assess whether he has the resources to answer it. (About 500 words.)"

Here's the student's answer:

"Taurek considers a case where you can save either one person or five strangers, but not both. The standard view says you must save the five because five deaths are a worse outcome than one. Taurek rejects this. His central move is to deny that we can weigh harms across persons at all: each of the five suffers only their own death, and no single person undergoes a loss five times as great as the one person's loss. Since harms can't be compared or summed across individuals in this way, the claim that the five deaths are 'worse' is meaningless, and there is no aggregation-based reason to save the greater number.

Taurek therefore concludes that the right thing to do is flip a coin, giving each of the six people an equal chance of being saved, and that it would be wrong to simply save the five outright without doing so. He also notes that he would save someone he cares about over five strangers, and thinks no one could reasonably blame him for this, which shows that the numbers have no moral force even from an impartial standpoint. Since each person's loss is only their own loss, it also follows that a bystander has no special obligation to save anyone at all in such cases; the morality of rescue is a separate matter.

The strongest objection, I think, is the hypothetical-choice objection: if any of the six people had to choose a rescue policy in advance, not knowing which rock they'd end up on, they'd choose the policy that maximizes their chance of survival, i.e. always save the greater number. So the aggregationist policy is the one everyone would rationally consent to. On Taurek's behalf, one might reply that this objection assumes what it is trying to prove, namely that the right standpoint is an impersonal one in which people are interchangeable. But this reply seems weak, because the hypothetical-choice reasoning is individualist through and through -- it starts from each person's own prospects, not from an impersonal sum. So I conclude that Taurek's position, while initially appealing in its respect for each person, probably cannot survive this objection."
