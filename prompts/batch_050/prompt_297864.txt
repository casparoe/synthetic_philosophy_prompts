On Kant's account, animals lack reason and so cannot be owed duties directly; our duties regarding animals are at bottom duties concerning humanity, since cruelty to animals deadens the sympathetic dispositions we need for treating persons well. From that account I've assembled the following argument, and I can't accept where it ends up:

1. An act toward an animal can be wrong only indirectly — in virtue of its effects on the agent's morally serviceable character or on other persons.
2. So if an act toward an animal has no such effects — if it neither weakens the agent's sympathy nor harms any person — it is not wrong at all.
3. A sufficiently self-disciplined agent can compartmentalize: someone can be unfailingly gentle and honest with humans while relishing cruelty to animals, so that the cruelty costs them nothing in human-directed sympathy.
4. Cruelty performed unobserved, by such an agent, therefore produces none of the effects that are supposed to make cruelty wrong.
5. Hence such cruelty is morally permissible.

Each step looks licensed by the indirect-duty picture, yet (5) is absurd. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? If I must give something up, I'd rather weaken (3) than (1) — but I want the costs itemized. In particular: does blocking the argument require quietly reintroducing direct duties to animals, and if so, can Kant's framework afford that without conceding that non-rational beings can make claims on us?
