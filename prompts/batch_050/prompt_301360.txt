My mother died this past spring, and in sorting her belongings I have spent a great deal of time with her photographs. In doing so I have noticed a pattern in my own responses that I cannot classify, and I would like to know whether it is a recognized phenomenon with an established name.

The pattern, concretely. Looking at a photograph of my mother, I say and feel, without any strain, "there she is," not "there is a picture of her." Yet when an artist friend, as a kindness, made a meticulous pencil drawing from one of these photographs — near-indistinguishable from it at arm's length — I could not say "there she is" of the drawing, and it gave me nothing of the quiet sense of contact the photograph gives me. Second, the photographs let me look at someone who no longer exists; this feels like a kind of seeing extended across time, and not at all like remembering her. Third, and this is what makes me distrust easy answers: I was shown, as a joke, an AI-generated "photograph" of my mother, and the instant I learned what it was, the sense of contact vanished, although nothing visible in the image had changed. So whatever the phenomenon is, it seems to track the history of the image rather than its appearance.

Is there an established concept for this — for the way photographs, and apparently only photographs and similarly tethered images, seem to put us in perceptual touch with their subjects? I have a vague impression that philosophers of art have disputed whether we literally see things through photographs, but I do not know the state of that debate or its vocabulary. I am not looking for a bare label. I want to know what the phenomenon is taken to be, what it is not — how it differs from ordinary depiction, from memory, and from whatever a mirror, a telescope, or a live video feed provides — and where its edges lie: photographs of the dead, doctored and generated images, film footage, photocopies of photographs. If nothing established fits the whole pattern, say so plainly, and coin a term with a definition and the boundary cases.
