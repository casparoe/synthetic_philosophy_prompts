Ok so some background: I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, but I volunteer on the data safety monitoring side of an open-source clinical trials registry project (long story) and I keep running into this concept called clinical equipoise. As I understand it, the idea going back to Freedman's 1987 paper is that a randomized trial is only ethical when there's genuine uncertainty in the expert medical community about which arm is better — not uncertainty in each individual doctor's head, but disagreement among experts, so no patient is knowingly being given something the profession already considers inferior.

Here's what's bugging me. I went down a rabbit hole and it seems like bioethicists have been fighting about whether equipoise is the right standard for like four decades, and as far as I can tell nothing has moved. One camp says equipoise is essential and it's what separates a trial from just experimenting on people. Another camp (I think Miller and Brody are the big names here?) says the whole concept is confused because a trial isn't therapy, so the doctor-patient obligations the whole idea rests on don't apply, and you should justify trials some other way — social value, informed consent, whatever. Meanwhile actual IRBs and guidelines apparently still require equipoise, so the practitioners seem to have ignored the philosophers entirely, or maybe the philosophers ignored them back. Everyone seems to know all the arguments already and just keeps restating them.

So my question: act as a professional philosopher who also understands how research ethics oversight actually works in practice. Think carefully about this before answering, and start by brainstorming the possible diagnoses before commiting to one. Why has this debate stalled? Specifically: is this a substantive moral disagreement, or are the two sides just answering different questions under one label — like one side asking "what does a doctor owe this patient" and the other asking "when is it ok to run this experiment on people"? Is there a hidden factual disagreement doing the work, e.g. about whether trial participation is actually on average worse for patients? Or is it a verbal dispute about what "equipoise" even picks out? My own hypothesis, which might be totally off: the two camps disagree about whether a trial should be evaluated as an extension of clinical care or as something more like an engineering study with human subjects, and equipoise is just where that deeper disagreement happens to surface. Pressure-test that.

Whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would actually count as progress: a distinction nobody's drawn yet, some empirical finding, regulators just changing the question, or is this the kind of thing where no progress should be expected and the field should just route around it? Reasons, not a survey of the literature please.
