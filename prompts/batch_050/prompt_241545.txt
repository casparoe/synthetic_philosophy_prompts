So Aristotle's biology says, roughly, that the father supplies the form and the mother the matter -- the semen "sets" the menstrual fluid the way a craftsman shapes wood, and that's how a new animal gets going. Fine. But now imagine he gets dropped into a modern fertility clinic: eggs and sperm from anonymous donors frozen in tanks, mixed and matched, embryos created in a dish and implanted in a third woman who gestates them. Here's what I want you to work out. Given his actual commitments -- hylomorphism, the form/matter split in reproduction, his whole teleological picture -- what would Aristotle say about the puzzle of whether *you* could have come from a different sperm and egg? Specifically: does his view commit him to an asymmetry, where swapping in a different sperm gets you a numerically different person but swapping the egg just gives the same person different raw material (or the other way around)? Or is he actually pushed toward thinking both gametes are essential, like Kripke-style origin essentialism says? Then the harder part: modern genetics tells us both gametes contribute half the DNA, so there's no neat form/matter division at the biological level. Would that discovery force Aristotle to give up the asymmetry, or could he absorb it -- say by relocating the form/matter distinction somewhere other than the gametes? Reconstruct the position he'd argue for himself, in his own terms, not a textbook summary, and flag the one spot where what he'd have to say clashes most sharply with what contemporary metaphysicians actually beleive.
