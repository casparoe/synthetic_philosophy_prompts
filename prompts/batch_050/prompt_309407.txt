I'm trying to get my political philosophy of privacy into a defensible shape before a workshop, and I keep going round in circles. I don't need a country report here -- treat German-style data protection, American-style open government, and Chinese-style social credit as stylised regulatory instincts rather than precise descriptions of actual law.

My commitments are roughly these:

1. Privacy is about control over the contexts in which information travels, not about secrecy. Even a technically public fact can be used in a privacy-invading way if it is removed from its original context or made much easier to aggregate.

2. Citizens do not owe the state a general duty to make themselves legible. Each demand to collect, retain, or link personal information needs its own proportionality justification.

3. A healthy democracy needs broad open-records rules: court proceedings, land ownership, political donations, corporate beneficial ownership, professional sanctions, and public officials’ financial interests should be searchable by anyone, without the requester having to show a special interest.

4. People should have a right to practical reinvention. After some suitable period, old minor convictions, spent bankruptcies, and certain youthful misconduct should become genuinely hard to find, even when reports about them are true and the original records were lawfully public.

5. Purpose limitation should be strict. Data collected for healthcare, taxation, schooling, or welfare should not be reused for policing, immigration enforcement, welfare sanctions, or commercial risk assessment without fresh legislation, a specific justification, and notice to the people affected.

6. The state should nevertheless run joined-up risk analysis across tax, welfare, medical, licensing, and border records, proactively identifying people who might commit benefit fraud, become violent, or pose an epidemic risk before anything has happened.

7. I object to a general social-credit score because it rolls conduct from many different spheres into one durable reputation and makes citizenship itself conditional. But I accept driving-licence points, professional bans, sex-offender registration, and narrowly drawn no-fly lists.

8. Transparency should be asymmetric: institutions exercising public power should be highly visible, while private citizens should mostly be able to remain opaque.

Do these commitments hang together? Please audit them rather than just summarising the trade-offs. Identify the pairs or clusters that genuinely conflict, rank the conflicts by severity, and distinguish outright inconsistency from a difficult policy trade-off or a merely apparent tension. For each conflict, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost. Also tell me which single change would restore the most coherence overall. Don't resolve everything by saying that competing values need balancing, and please answer in British English.
