The question I set was: "The argument from divine hiddenness is sometimes presented as a version of the problem of evil and sometimes as something distinct. Reconstruct Schellenberg's version of the argument, explain in what respect it differs from a standard evidential argument from evil, and evaluate one objection to it." Here's the student's answer, which I'd like you to grade:

"Schellenberg's argument runs roughly as follows. If a perfectly loving God existed, then God would ensure that every person has overwhelming, unmistakable evidence of his existence, since love requires being present to the beloved and a God who hides himself is not present. But there are people who do not believe in God -- the evidence for theism is at best ambiguous and disputed. Therefore no perfectly loving God exists. This differs from the evidential problem of evil in an important way: the argument from evil asks us to infer God's nonexistence from the distribution of suffering, which is an inductive inference about what God would probably permit, whereas the hiddenness argument turns on something more direct -- the sheer fact of nonbelief itself, which is not a matter of probabilities. Pascal recognized this too, regarding God's hiddenness as a scandal that theism has to explain away. The best objection is skeptical theism: since we cannot know God's reasons, we cannot know that a loving God wouldn't have good reason to remain hidden, and so the argument fails just as the evidential argument from evil fails. This shows that hiddenness is really just a special case of the problem of evil after all."
