I co-teach an undergrad seminar on the philosophy of probability (the students have done one stats course, no computability theory, no measure theory). Next week I get one 80-minute session on the question "can a finite sequence be random?" and I want to plan it properly instead of winging it like last year.

Plan the explanation rather than giving me the explanation itself. Concretely:

- The order the ideas have to come in, and why that order. My instinct is to open with the fact that any specific 500-flip sequence from a fair coin has exactly the same probability as 500 heads in a row, so probability-of-the-outcome can't be what makes one string "random" and another not. But I suspect that move only lands if something else is already in place first. Tell me what has to be established before the puzzle even makes sense to them, and what depends on what downstream.

- The carrying example. I want to build the whole session around three strings the students can literally stare at: one from actual coin flips I did at home, one alternating HTHTHT..., and one that's secretly the binary digits of pi (or something like it) — a string that passes every eyeball test but is generated by a short rule. Tell me what role each string plays (is the pi string a trap, a resolution, or a third thing?), what each one must have and must lack for the plan to work, and whether three strings is even the right number or I should cut one.

- The misconception map, in the order the students will actually form these during the session, with the point in the sequence where each should get defused (and note where it's better to let one get said out loud first). The ones I can predict: "random just means no pattern" (which runs into the fact that every finite string is describable somehow, and that "no short description" can't be made absolute — there's a Berry-paradox-flavored trap in there); "if a genuinely random process produced it then it's a random string, even the all-heads one"; "Kolmogorov complexity settles it once and for all" (it doesn't — machine-relative, uncomputable, only defined up to a constant); "randomness is just about what we can't predict," i.e. confusing the epistemic reading with something in the string itself. Add the ones I'm not predicting.

- The verdict I should steer them toward. My current landing spot is: calling a finite string random only makes sense relative to a class of tests or compressors, while anything like absolute randomness belongs to processes, not products. Tell me if that's the right landing spot for this audience or if it's a cop-out.

- One check for understanding I can run in the last ten minutes whose answers will show they got the dependency structure and not just the vocab, plus what to openly refuse to cover at this level (Martin-Löf randomness for infinite sequences? Chaitin's omega? cryptographic pseudorandomness?) and how to say "not today" without making the session feel like a trailer for a different course.

Whiteboard only, no slides, and I can do arithmetic on the board but shouldn't write a single line of code.
