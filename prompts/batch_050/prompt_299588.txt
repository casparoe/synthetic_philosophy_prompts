I'm in a small reading group (mostly grad students, some industry people) working through agent foundations / AI safety material, and last week two members, Kat and Marcus, got into a long argument about the Löbian obstacle that the rest of us couldn't settle. I'm going to reconstruct it as best I can and I'd like a verdict, because both of them seemed to be making sense at every step.

Kat's opening move: Löb's theorem says, roughly, that if a sufficiently strong formal system can prove "if P is provable in this system, then P," then it can already just prove P — so no system can certify its own soundness from the inside. Apply that to a self-improving AI: for the agent to safely build a successor that reasons the same way it does, it would need to prove "whatever my successor proves is true," which is exactly the soundness claim Löb forbids. So any self-improving agent either freezes in place or takes an unverified leap of faith in its successor. Deductive guarantees for alignment are mathematically off the table, period. Marcus pushed back: the theorem only blocks verification from within the *same* proof system. Nothing stops a stronger parent from verifying a successor that reasons in a strictly weaker logic (the tiling-agents idea), so proof-based self-modification is possible, you just can't have an infinitely ascending chain of ever-stronger self-verified agents. And the very first agent can be checked from the outside by us, using whatever meta-level math we already trust. Kat's reply: the descending-chain fix is well-founded, meaning it bottoms out — after finitely many generations you hit an agent too weak to verify anything interesting, so you haven't enabled self-improvement, you've scheduled its end. And "we verify the seed ourselves" just relocates her leap one level up: our confidence in the meta-level math is itself an unproven soundness assumption. Marcus's countermove: all of this only bites if you demand *deductive* justification. Actual agents, humans included, trust their future selves on inductive grounds — track record, testing, sandboxing, interpretability checks. Löb is a theorem about provability predicates; it says nothing whatsoever about rational probabilistic confidence, so it constrains one particular design philosophy (proof-carrying self-modification), not AI safety in general. Kat's last word, which is were things stalled: inductive assurance fails exactly at the moment that matters — when the successor is smarter than anything you've tested, off-distribution, where past reliability stops being evidence. So her dilemma survives: get guarantees and cap improvement, or keep improving and lose the guarantee.
