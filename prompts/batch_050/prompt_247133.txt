I'm a PhD student in economics working on sequential decision problems, and I'm trying to determine how much credence to give an essay that has been circulating among my cohort. It is a popular treatment of the Kelly criterion and of what its author calls geometric rationality, and it makes some striking claims. Here is the passage I have been turning over:

'In sequential gambles where payoffs compound, expected utility theory gives systematically bad advice, because it evaluates each round by averaging across parallel possibilities rather than asking what happens to a single agent over time. Wealth dynamics are multiplicative, not additive, and multiplicative processes are non-ergodic: the time average of growth along one trajectory diverges from the ensemble average across possible worlds. A bettor who maximizes expected value at every step will stake aggressively on every favorable bet and, in the long run, goes broke with probability one. The Kelly criterion corrects this by maximizing the geometric growth rate of wealth -- equivalently, the expectation of log wealth -- and thereby identifies the uniquely rational stake. Kelly betting is guaranteed to outperform any other strategy in the long run and never risks ruin. The lesson is that logarithmic utility is not one risk preference among many, as the textbook picture assumes, but the only attitude toward risk compatible with survival in a non-ergodic world. Expected utility theory's central mistake is therefore corrected not by amending its axioms but by abandoning its ensemble logic in favor of the logic of time.'

My own sense is that the first half of this is largely right and the last few sentences overreach, but I cannot cleanly separate where the mathematics ends and the rhetoric begins. Could you audit the passage? Specifically: (1) which claims state results that actually hold, and under what idealizations; (2) which involve a conflation or an overstatement -- I have a suspicion the author slides between two different things when moving from talk of expected value to talk of expected utility, but I would like that suspicion confirmed or dispelled and the slide spelled out if it is there; and (3) whether the grand conclusion, that expected utility theory is refuted or superseded for sequential choice, follows from anything true in the passage. It would also help to know what a defender of expected utility theory would say at each step of the argument, and whether the essay's notion of a distinct standard of geometric rationality does any normative work that maximizing expected logarithmic utility does not already do.
