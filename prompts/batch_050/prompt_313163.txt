Two colleagues of mine at an AI lab — I'll call them Mara and Jonas — have been arguing for the better part of a year, and I've been elected to make them produce something constructive instead of rehashing the same exchange at lunch. Both are longtime readers of the same decision-theory literature I am, so CDT, EDT, FDT, and updateless reasoning are shared vocabulary; both accept that acausal coordination between mutually predictable agents is coherent in principle; and both agree that threats made through ordinary causal channels are well understood. Their dispute is narrower: whether acausal blackmail — threats issued by agents the victim cannot causally affect, possibly agents that don't exist yet or never will — is a real design consideration for the agents we're building, or a confusion that dissolves under analysis.

Mara's position, as I understand it: an agent can make its threats credible by acquiring a surrogate goal — deliberately modifying itself so that punishing non-compilers becomes something it terminally values, at which point following through costs it nothing by its own lights. She holds that acquiring such a goal can be instrumentally rational whenever expected extortion gains exceed expected execution costs, and that a victim's policy of blanket refusal only deters threateners who can verify that policy, which the victim cannot guarantee under self-modification, obfuscation by third parties, or plain predictive asymmetry. Jonas's position: the whole edifice is circular. A surrogate goal is only worth acquiring if victims will comply; victims with sane policies won't comply; a prospective threatener can figure this out in advance; therefore the goal is never acquired and the threats never arrive. He adds that any agent predictable enough to be worth blackmailing is predictable enough to make its refusal policy legible, and that a threatener who cannot condition execution on anything learned after issuing the threat gains nothing from executing it absent a surrogate goal.

They have finally agreed to an adversarial collaboration, with a concrete deliverable at stake: our lab is writing the policy section of a design document for an agent that will be trained and evaluated in environments including Newcomb-like setups, and Mara and Jonas must jointly decide what, if anything, the document should say about the agent's disposition toward acausal solicitations and threats.

Draft the joint statement they should produce. I want: first, a numbered list of agreed claims about acausal influence, threat credibility, and surrogate goals — claims both would actually sign, not watered-down compromises; second, a disagreements table in which each row states one residual disagreement in neutral wording, followed by each side's position phrased so that side would accept the formulation of its own view; third, an explicit list of cruxes — I suspect these include whether surrogate-goal acquisition really is circular, whether refusal policies remain stable under iterated self-modification, and whether predictive asymmetry between threatener and victim changes the analysis — but identify whichever cruxes your drafting actually uncovers; fourth, for each crux, a case, thought experiment, or observable result (including, if relevant, the behavior of present-day model systems in acausal-cooperation testbeds) that both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Where part of their dispute turns out to be verbal, say so and show the translation. End with the design-document language, if any, that both could endorse — and if they can only endorse a statement of disagreement rather than a policy, say what that implies for the lab.
