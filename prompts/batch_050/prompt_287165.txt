Last winter I got into a long argument with my sister that I haven't been able to put down since. She had spent three weekends helping a former coworker — someone she doesn't even particularly like — move into a cheaper apartment and untangle a benefits claim, and when I asked why she bothered, she said it was "above and beyond" and left it at that. I have no background in philosophy; I work in warehouse logistics and I've been reading about these things on my own in the evenings. But the phrase stuck with me, and I've since learned there's an established term for this, supererogation, and that people disagree about what it takes for an act to qualify. As far as I can tell, my own thinking keeps sliding between two different ways of drawing the line, and I'd like your help building a test case that separates them. The first way, which I'll call the tally view, says an act goes beyond duty when you were permitted not to do it and doing it made things morally better than whatever duty required — the emphasis is on the outcome, on there being more good in the world than the minimum demanded. The second way, which I'll call the credit view, says an act goes beyond duty when you were permitted not to do it and doing it earns you moral credit: it reflects well enough on you that praise is fitting, while skipping it would have earned you no blame — the emphasis is on what the act says about you, not just on the results.

Here is what I want: construct one scenario, drawn from ordinary life, in which these two views deliver opposite verdicts — the act counts as above and beyond on one view but not on the other. Some constraints, because I plan to use the case in an online discussion group where examples get picked apart mercilessly. First, keep it mundane: a workplace, a family, a neighborhood — no trolleys, no burning buildings, no rescues at risk of death, no enormous charitable donations, no saints, no organ donors. Second, the scenario must fit in a single short paragraph. Third, do not stipulate the verdicts: don't simply write that the act "was beneficial but not praiseworthy" and leave it at that. Give me the concrete details — what the agent wanted, what they did, what it cost them, and what else they could permissibly have done — from which the two verdicts actually follow. Fourth, the agent's motive has to be pinned down clearly enough that a reader can't just substitute a kinder one and dissolve the case.

Then demonstrate that the construction works. State the act, what duty required of the agent, and why skipping the act would not have been wrong. Give each view's verdict, and identify exactly which feature of the scenario makes the two views come apart. Tell me what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed — I want to know whether the case collapses back into agreement and, if so, in which direction. Finally, give me the strongest objection a defender of whichever view sits less comfortably with the case could raise against it, along with your honest assessment of whether that objection succeeds. If it turns out the case can be resisted so easily that it shows nothing, I would rather you tell me that plainly than pretend the construction is airtight.
