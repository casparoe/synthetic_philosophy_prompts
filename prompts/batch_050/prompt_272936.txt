For the past year I've been part of a working group at a mid-size social media company looking at political polarization on the platform, and our internal debate has calcified in a way I find philosophically interesting, not just practically frustrating. One camp holds that our users are in filter bubbles: they simply never encounter opposing views, so the fix is to inject more cross-cutting content into feeds. The other camp argues that the problem isn't lack of exposure but active distrust — users see the other side plenty, but encounter it in a way that confirms their contempt, so more exposure either does nothing or backfires. This maps loosely onto a distinction I've seen in the philosophy literature between epistemic bubbles (the other side is absent) and echo chambers (the other side is present but pre-discredited), and the same split seems to run through the academic research: survey-based work tends to find that most people actually do encounter a fair amount of cross-cutting content, while at least one well-known field experiment that paid people to follow opposing accounts found their polarization increased. Both camps in my group cite all of this; nobody has changed their mind in a year; and the policy proposals on the table are near-opposites.

My working hypothesis is that the debate looks empirical but isn't, or isn't only: the two sides are measuring exposure differently (what appears in a feed versus what is actually attended to versus what is taken seriously), and underneath that is a deeper disagreement about what the relevant harm even is — holding false beliefs, or holding true beliefs about the other side's worst members for reasons that are still epistemically corrupt. If that's right, the empirical studies each side cites may all be true and yet irrelevant to the actual dispute, which would explain why a year of evidence-sharing has moved no one. But I may be flattered by this diagnosis because it lets me feel above the fray of my own group's argument.

So: diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicating it. Is the exposure-versus-distrust dispute a substantive empirical question that better data could settle, a verbal dispute hiding behind words like exposure and polarization, two research programs answering different questions under one heading, or a bedrock difference about what epistemic health consists in that neither side can argue the other out of? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually says and measures, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a particular kind of study, a reframing of the question — or argue honestly that no progress should be expected and why. If you think my framing is off — for instance, if the two camps are not as symmetric as I've presented them, or if the stalled-debate premise itself rests on a misreading of the evidence — say so plainly rather than working within a confused setup.
