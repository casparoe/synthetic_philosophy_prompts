I'm an assistant principal at a middle school (grades 6-8) and I handle most of the discipline referrals, maybe 8-10 a week. About a third of our families are recent immigrants from a bunch of different countries, and I keep running into the same problem: teachers send kids to me for things like talking back to a teacher, a girl taking off a headscarf in class, a kid refusing to say the pledge, a boy refusing to shake a female teacher's hand, kids sharing food off each other's plates at lunch when that's against our hygiene rule, etc. Some of these the parents think are a huge deal and some of the ones WE think are a huge deal the parents think are nothing. Meanwhile actual stuff like one kid hitting another or stealing gets mixed into the same pile because the referral form just says "disrespect."

I've read a little bit about the psychology literature on how people everywhere distinguish moral violations (harm, fairness) from conventional ones (the rules of the school, how you greet people, what you wear), and also about how the line between those gets drawn in very different places in different cultures -- stuff that Americans file under "social convention" other communities file under "morality" or even "purity/divinity." I don't want a lecture on this, I want a procedure.

Write me a decision procedure I can run in under ten minutes per case, sitting at my desk with the referral slip, that sorts incidents into (a) genuine moral violations where the school should discipline regardless of what anyone's culture says, (b) conventional violations where the school can enforce its rules but should be honest that that's what it's doing, and (c) cases where the moral/conventional boundary itself is contested between the family and the school, which need a different handling than discipline. For each sorting step give me the actual question I should ask or the test I should apply (e.g., something like the "would this still be wrong if the school had no rule against it" test -- but tell me where that test breaks down, since I suspect it does for some of these).

The procedure needs to handle these hard cases explicitly: transgressions with no victim that a community still treats as moral; cases where the kid's family thinks the SCHOOL is the one doing something immoral; and cases where two kids from different backgrounds did the same act but it means something different. It also has to survive review by our district equity office and a parent meeting, so it can't just be "the principal vibes on it." Note where in the procedure judgment calls remain that I can't outsource to the checklist.

Show the procedure working on three cases: the headscarf one, a kid hitting another kid, and one you make up where the procedure spits out an answer I probably won't like (i.e., where it says the school should back off enforcing one of its own rules, or enforce against a family's wishes). And be straight with me about what a sorting procedure like this can't settle -- especially whether the moral/conventional distinction itself being culturally variable means I'm just smuggling in one culture's answer about which stuff is non-negotiable, and what I should say at the parent meeting when someone accuses me of exactly that.
