So I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about why fictional characters feel so weirdly real to us (the loose peg is all the AI-generated fiction floating around, but the piece is really about the philosophy of it). My editor wants a concrete test case instead of hand-waving, so I want to pressure-test one here before it goes anywhere near print.

Build me a scenario: one ordinary setting (a book club, a pub quiz, a parent reading to a kid, a tourist trail, whatever works) in which people naturally say four or five sentences about a single fictional character. Engineer it so the case seperates the three standard views about what names of fictional characters do:

1. the pretense or make-believe view -- the name doesn't refer to anything at all, and talking about the character is a kind of authorized pretending;
2. the Meinongian view -- the name refers to a genuine object, just one that happens not to exist;
3. the creationist or abstract-artifact view -- the author literally brought a new abstract object into being, so the name refers and in a sense the character exists.

The sentences in the scenario should include at least: one that's plainly true in the story; one that connects the character to the real world (fame, tourism, influence on real people, adaptations, merchandise); one flat denial that the character exists; and one comparing the character to a character from a different story. Constraints: invent the character or use a household name, keep the scenario itself under 200 words, and make every sentence something an actual person would actually say -- nobody in my scenario is allowed to talk like a philosopher. And don't smuggle the verdicts in: the sentences have to be natural utterances whose truth values fall out of the theories, not sentences that mention the theories.

Then show the work. For each sentence, give the verdict each of the three views delivers (true, false, neither, true-only-according-to-the-story, whatever the view really says) with a sentence or two of why. Identify which sentence does the most separating work -- where the three views fan out the furthest -- and what the case loses if I cut that sentence for space. Somewhere in there I also want at least one point where two views agree on the verdict but disagree about why, because that's the subtlest thing to get across to readers, so flag where that happens. And for whichever view comes out of my case looking worst, give me the strongest pushback its defenders would offer.

Last thing, the epistemic hygiene: as far as I can tell none of these three is the settled orthodoxy, so don't write it up as if one of them were. Tell me plainly which parts of this are genuinely contested among philosophers who work on fiction -- including whether the problem sentences I've asked for even have agreed-on truth values -- and which parts are common ground everyone in the debate accepts.
