A colleague and I — both software engineers with no medical or philosophical training — keep disagreeing about what death actually is, so we've started trading cases. Her verdicts so far:

- A patient whose entire brain, brainstem included, has been destroyed by trauma, but whose heart keeps beating on a ventilator: dead.
- A patient in a persistent vegetative state — no sign of awareness, but breathing unassisted, with sleep-wake cycles: alive.
- A drowned child pulled from icy water with no pulse for forty minutes, then rewarmed and fully recovered: alive the whole time.
- An organ donor whose heart stopped after withdrawal of life support and who was declared dead after five minutes without a pulse, after which organs were procured: genuinely dead at the moment of declaration.

Here is the new case. An infant's heart stops; the infant is declared dead after only seventy-five seconds without circulation; the heart is then removed and transplanted into a second infant, in whom it starts beating again. (This is a real protocol, not a thought experiment.) Was the first infant dead at the moment of declaration?

Predict her verdict. More important to me: work out which rule she is implicitly running. As far as I can tell, her verdicts are consistent with at least two principles — death as the irreversible cessation of circulation or brain function, versus death as the cessation that will not be reversed in this body, whether or not it could be reversed in another. If more than one principle fits her record, say what each predicts for the infant case, give your best guess at which one she actually holds and why, and describe the further case I could put to her that would decide between them.
