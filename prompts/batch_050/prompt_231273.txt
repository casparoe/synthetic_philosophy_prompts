For a political theory workshop, draft the joint statement that could emerge from an adversarial collaboration between:

1. a Weilian party-abolitionist, who thinks political parties pressure members to accept package deals, subordinate individual judgment to collective victory, and turn every question into a question of what helps the team; on this view, political coordination should instead happen through temporary associations organized around a specific problem, which people can join without endorsing everything else the association says; and

2. a democratic party-realist, who thinks mass politics needs durable parties to aggregate preferences, recruit candidates, coordinate legislation, discipline representatives, and give voters something recognizable to reward or punish; on this view, abolishing parties would probaly just move power to donors, media owners, personal followings, and informal cliques.

Have them assess this fictional reform: party labels are removed from ballots, party primaries and legislative whips are banned, and organizations may receive political funding only if they are issue-specific, publicly disclose their membership and spending, and dissolve after three years. These associations may publish analyses and policy proposals, but may not run candidates, endorse a whole slate, or require members to support a common platform. Candidates campaign as individuals and may belong to several such associations.

The statement should contain:

- a numbered section of claims both people can genuinely accept, including claims about the psychological pressures of party membership, the practical functions parties perform, and the difference between formal abolition and merely driving partisan organization underground;
- a table of the remaining disagreements, each worded neutrally enough that both sides would accept it as a fair description;
- the main cruxes, such as whether parties necessarily tend toward the suppression of independent judgment or only contingently do so, whether accountability requires collective brands, whether package-deal politics is a bug or a useful form of compromise, and whether temporary associations could coordinate across several issues without simply becoming parties under another name;
- for each crux, one concrete empirical case, institutional experiment, or historical comparison both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance;
- one disagreement the collaborators conclude was mostly verbal, especially about what counts as a "party" as opposed to a faction, campaign organization, caucus, or durable association;
- and a final joint diagnosis of whether the fictional reform abolishes parties in Weil's sense or merely redesigns them.

Don't make either side a strawman. The Weilian shouldn't deny that coordination is necessary, and the party-realist shouldn't pretend that party discipline never corrupts judgment. End with the smallest amendment to the reform that each side would offer, and say whether the other side could live with it.
