So I've been reading about the Benacerraf problem (the "is 2 an element of 4?" thing) and I'm trying to work out what I actually think by going through cases and noting my gut verdicts. Here they are:

1. The 2 that answers "how many legs does a dog have" and the 2 from arithmetic class: same number. Obviously.
2. 0.999... and 1: same number, two notations. I'm confident about this one.
3. In the von Neumann construction, 2 = {∅, {∅}} so 2 ∈ 4 literally. In Zermelo's construction it isn't. My verdict: neither is a fact about the actual numbers. Saying "2 is an element of 4" is like saying the king in chess is made of wood -- true of my set, not of the king. It's a representational artefact, not maths.
4. The natural number 2 and the real number 2.0: same number. When analysts write 2 they don't mean some different object from what my kid counts with.
5. The rational 2 (the pair <2,1> or the equivalence class or whatever) and the natural 2: same number, same reason.
6. But "2" in arithmetic mod 5, where 2+3 = 0? That's NOT the same as the natural number 2. It's a different thing that happens to wear the same name.
7. An alien civilisation that has never heard of sets but does arithmetic -- their two is our two. Same number, because the structure is the same.
8. My verdict on the slogan version of structuralism: 2 just IS a position in the natural number structure, the way a wicket-keeper is a position in a cricket side. Positions in structures, that's what numbers are. I find this pretty attractive.

The problem is I'm fairly sure 4, 6 and 8 don't all fit together. If 2 is just a position in the natural number structure, then the real number 2.0, which lives in a completely different structure (no successor function, dense, complete), should be a different object -- but my verdict 4 says same. And yet mod-5 2 (verdict 6) I want to call different, even though the naturals map onto {0,...,4} with addition preserved in some sense... so why does one embedding count as "same number" and the other not?

So: is there a principle about numerical identity across structures that captures as many of my verdicts as possible? I'd like it stated precisely -- something like "x and y are the same number iff there's an isomorphism between their home structures preserving such-and-such", with each clause motivated by one of my cases. If no clean principle saves all eight, tell me which verdict you'd sacrifice and why it's the cheapest to give up. And whatever principle you land on, tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: is the ordinal 2 (second, third, ...) the same as the cardinal 2 (a pair, a duo)? I genuinely don't know what I think about that one yet.
