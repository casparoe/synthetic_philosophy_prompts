There's a podcast I listen to that posts written summaries of each episode, and their one on Spinoza's Ethics says this:

"Spinoza was a necessitarian: he held that everything that happens, happens of necessity, and that nothing could ever have gone differently. God produces the world the way a calculator produces answers, with no choice in the matter, and we produce our actions the same way. So on his view nothing is genuinely free: what we call freedom is just our ignorance of the causes pushing us around, and once you see that, the only sensible response is resignation. There's no point striving for anything, since whatever happens was always going to happen anyway. Late in the book he does introduce an 'intellectual love of God', which is his name for the calm you feel once you stop fighting the inevitable. And since we are all modes of God, this love is really God loving himself — which Spinoza says means God loves each of us personally and wants what's best for us."

Now, before I read that write-up I'd actually looked at a bit of the Ethics itself (in the old White translation), Part 1, Definition 7, where Spinoza says:

"That thing is called free, which exists solely by the necessity of its own nature, and of which the action is determined by itself alone. On the other hand, that thing is necessary, or rather constrained, which is determined by something external to itself to a fixed and definite method of existence or action."

That definition doesn't sound much like "nothing is genuinely free", which is what made me suspicious of the summary in the first place. Though I'm well aware I might be the one misreading him.

So: how fair is the podcast's summary? Go through it claim by claim and tell me which bits a careful reader of Spinoza would accept, which are distortions, and which are just wrong. In particular — does the definition above undercut the summary's slide from "everything happens of necessity" to "nothing is free, so resignation is the only sensible response"? And is the summary right about what the intellectual love of God actually is, and about God loving us personally? My gut says the summary gets the necessitarianism roughly right but then balls up the consequences, but I'd like that checked rather than assumed.
