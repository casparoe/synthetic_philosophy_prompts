I’m a novelist and I run a small workshop for other spec-fic writers — 14 adults, 100 minutes, no philosophy background beyond having read the usual op-eds. We’re doing a session on writing artificial minds whose behaviour comes from incentives rather than from mustache-twirling malice, so I want an in-class game about corrigibility and the shutdown problem.

Please design something the group can actually run. The basic situation should be fictional and concrete — maybe an automated system managing something mundane but valuable, like hospital deliveries, irrigation, or a publisher’s recommendation engine — not another generic rogue superintelligence scenario. One player or team should control the system, another should control the human overseers, and everyone else should have a real function rather than sitting around watching. The system should have an assigned goal, limited information, and a shutdown mechanism that can interrupt it. Its instructions should never say preserve yourself, but the payoffs should make shutdown-avoidance tempting anyway.

The part I especially want participants to feel is why just adding a reward for being shut down, or a penalty for resisting shutdown, doesn’t straightforwardly solve things. Ideally the first run produces shutdown avoidance; then the players get to redesign the incentives and accidentally produce either shutdown-seeking, gaming the test, or compliance only while the overseers are watching. If you can make them discover how knife-edge genuine indifference is, even better. But the point has to emerge from the game, not from a lecture before it.

Give me:

- the scenario, roles, hidden information, and any cards/materials I need;
- exact rules and turn order, including what the system can do, what the overseers can inspect, and when shutdown happens;
- a token-based payoff scheme that doesn’t involve real money;
- a 100-minute timeline, including at least one round where particpants modify the incentives;
- the debrief, mapping specific likely moments in the game onto corrigibility, instrumental self-preservation, reward hacking, and the difference between obeying an operator and remaining correctable when the operator may be wrong;
- what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to stop the game from turning into people merely being sneaky for fun;
- an honest note about what this can’t capture — especially where human role-players differ from trained systems, or where the toy payoff structure oversimplifies the real issue.

Since this is for writers, end with a short exercise where they turn one strategically corrigible-looking moment from the game into a scene, and then reveal what the system’s incentives make likely beneath the surface. Don’t get too technical, but don’t sand the conceptual edges off either.

Also, if I’ve bundled together things that don’t belong together, or if the shutdown problem can’t really be dramatized this way without teaching something subtly wrong, please say so plainly and change the design rather than politely humoring the premise.
