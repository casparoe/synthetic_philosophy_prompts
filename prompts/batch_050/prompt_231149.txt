I work at an interdisciplinary research institute that vets papers and grant proposals from two very different groups: animal cognition people and ML interpretability people. Both camps keep using the word "represents" -- the rat hippocampus represents location, the transformer represents syntactic number, the bee dance represents the direction of nectar -- and referees keep fighting about whether these claims are literal or just dressed-up correlation talk. My job, two or three times a month, is to write a one-page verdict on each: does the system genuinely represent X or merely reliably respond to X. I get an afternoon per case at most, my PI signs off, and we catch grief from both sides -- the biologists think we're stingy, the AI people think we're soft.

I want you to take teleosemantics seriously as the backbone of a decision procedure I can actually run -- the Millikan/Dretske family of ideas where content gets fixed by what a state was selected, by evolution or learning, to indicate. Write it as an ordered set of tests: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each (what evidence I'd actually look for in a paper -- lesion studies, ethograms, training details, whatever), and where the procedure stops with a crisp "no." Our lab shorthand for the no-cases is "telltale states": states that carry information but don't represent. I want the procedure to deliver that verdict cleanly, not mushily. Also mark where it has to hand off to a judgment call because the theory itself is unsettled -- e.g., whether gradient descent counts as a selection process the way learning and natural selection do. Flag that one explicitly instead of pretending the checklist settles it.

Hard parts it must handle: the misrepresentation requirement (if the mechanism can't be wrong it doesn't represent anything, but real mechanisms are noisy, so say how much misfiring is enough), the distality problem (the frog case: flies, small dark moving things, or retinal patterns? give me a tiebreaker rule, not just the observation that there's a problem), and systems with no history at all -- a Swampman-style case like a randomly initialized network that happens to decode something. Tell me whether the procedure shoud bite that bullet.

Then run it on three cases:

1. The classic frog tongue-snap circuit.
2. A linear probe that finds a direction in a trained vision model's activations predicting "dog" with high accuracy -- the interpretability team wants to write "the model represents dogs." I suspect the procedure gives an answer someone here won't like; say whose ox gets gored.
3. Magnetotactic bacteria -- the internal magnetosome chain that aligns them with field lines, which steers them toward oxygen-poor water in the northern hemisphere and would steer them wrong if you shipped them south. This is the real test of your distality rule: oxygen gradient or magnetic north, pick one and show the rule doing the work.

End with a short section on what the procedure can't do -- where content attribution stays a judgment call no matter how good the checklist -- and what I should tell referees who want a purely mechanical test.
