I'm trying to figure out what principle, if any, underlies my judgments in cases where an action changes which people will exist. Here are my verdicts, and I'd like to know what they add up to.

First, the cases I feel confident about. A woman is taking a medication that, for one more month, would cause any child she conceives to be born with a mild but permanent joint condition; the condition cannot be avoided for a child conceived this month, and a child conceived next month would be entirely healthy. She conceives now anyway. I think she acts wrongly, and I don't think the resulting child has a complaint against her, since waiting would have meant a different child existing instead. Second: a society faces a choice between a conservation policy and a depletion policy; both produce lives well worth living, but under depletion average welfare is somewhat lower three centuries from now, and the people who will exist then differ entirely between the two policies because of how the choice reshapes lives and pairings over generations. I think the society ought to choose conservation, though with less confidence than in the medication case. Third: I am inclined to think that I can coherently be glad that certain past injustices occurred -- say, an emigration forced on my great-grandparents -- given that I would not otherwise exist, and that this gladness involves no moral failing on my part.

Now the pair I can't reconcile. Consider a couple for whom the only way to have a genetically related child is to conceive during a window in which the child will have the same joint condition as in the medication case; waiting is not an option, because there is no later healthy child to be had. They conceive. My gut says this is permissible, maybe even clearly fine, provided the child's life will be worth living. But the medication mother and this couple both knowingly bring about a child with a preventable-in-some-sense condition, and the only difference seems to be that the mother had the alternative of a different, healthier child existing in place of this one. I'm not sure that difference should carry the weight my gut puts on it, because in neither case does the actual child end up worse off than that child could have been.

My tentative principle is this: an action that affects who will exist is wrong only if it makes some actual person worse off than that very person could have been. But this principle apparently acquits the medication mother, which I'm reluctant to accept, and it's silent on why conservation is obligatory. So please test the principle against my five verdicts and repair it. I want one or two candidate principles, each stated precisely, with the clause of each principle motivated by a specific case above; an honest statement of which of my verdicts each candidate cannot save, and which candidate you think survives best overall; and each candidate's verdict on two cases I haven't considered: a couple who conceive knowing their child will have a serious condition that leaves life barely worth living, when that child is their only possible child, and a society that must choose between two energy policies where the difference in future welfare is tiny but certain and identity-affecting, while the cost of the better policy is a large burden on identifiable people alive today.
