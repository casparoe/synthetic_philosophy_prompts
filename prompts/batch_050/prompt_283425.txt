Hey, I write a science-and-ideas column for a general audience magazine, and I'm working on a piece about why "what a thing *would* do" is way sneakier than it sounds — the old philosophy chestnut about dispositons, finks, and masks. For a sidebar I want fresh examples instead of the usual fragile-vase and live-wire material, so I cooked up some candidate cases with my editor. I need a philosophy-savvy second pair of eyes on them.

For each case below, give me a score from 1 to 10 for how cleanly it illustrates a MASK — the thing keeps its disposition, but something interferes so the stimulus never produces the manifestation — as opposed to a FINK, where the disposition itself gets yanked away the moment the stimulus shows up. 10 = textbook mask, 1 = textbook fink, anything in the middle = genuinely murky or maybe neither. One or two sentences of reasoning per case, and please flag it if you think I've misdescribed one.

1. The safety drawbridge. A drawbridge is rigged so it would always lower when the button is pressed — except the engineers installed a sensor that cuts power to the motor the instant the button gets pressed. (Yes, this is basically the classic thought experiment, it's my control case.)

2. The stoic comedian. A comedian has a robust disposition to laugh at genuinely funny jokes. But she's drilled herself so that whenever a joke lands, a split second before the laugh starts she recalls something mortifying and the laugh dies in her throat. She still laughs in her sleep at dreamt jokes.

3. The chameleon pill. A pill is water-soluble, full stop. The manufacturer coats it in a shell that only breaks down at body temperature, so dropped into a cold glass of water it just sits there at the bottom. Nothing internal to the pill changes when it hits the water.

4. The jumpy bomb. A bomb is disposed to detonate when its casing cracks. But the casing is a memory metal that, upon any impact hard enough to crack it, instantly reseals — and the same impact triggers a catalyst that converts the explosive inside into an inert compound.

5. The shy pianist. A pianist is disposed to play beautifully whenever she sits down at a piano. But the moment anyone is listening, stage fright stiffens her hands; alone, she plays gorgeously. Is "being listened to" part of the stimulus, or interference with it? I've gone back and forth on this one all week.

6. The fainting goats, fixed. Certain goats have a mutation where their muscles lock when startled, so they keel over instead of running. Say a breeder develops a variant where the very same startle triggers a counter-reflex that relaxes the muscles, so these goats sprint away normally. The trigger is still "being startled" — the counter-reflex fires *because* of the startle. Is the locking disposition masked in the new goats, or did the lineage fink itself?

Then, zooming out: which two cases on this list are hardest to tell apart, and does the fink/mask distinction actually survive contact with cases like these? My working worry is that it collapses into "it depends how you carve up the stimulus conditions," and if that's right I'd rather know before filing the piece than after.
