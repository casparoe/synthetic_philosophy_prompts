Earlier this year I presented evidence to my town council that the manufacturing plant employing a fifth of our town was contaminating the groundwater; I was outvoted, denounced in the local paper as an elitist who held the town in contempt, and my opponents explicitly claimed to speak for the will of the people against credentialed outsiders. A colleague then lent me Ibsen's An Enemy of the People, and I found it more disturbing than comforting. Please give me a careful reading of the play through the philosophy of populism and the general will. In particular: the play seems to run together three distinct charges against the majority of the townspeople -- that their judgment is epistemically unreliable, that their will is morally illegitimate, and that their will is inauthentic because manufactured (by the mayor, by the People's Messenger, by Aslaksen's politics of moderation) -- and I want to know where each charge is actually dramatized and where they are conflated. Does the play contain any resources for distinguishing a majority verdict that expresses something like Rousseau's general will from one that merely aggregates particular economic interests, as at the meeting in Captain Horster's house? Most uncomfortably, is Stockmann himself a mirror-image populist, claiming to represent the town's true interests against its expressed will -- a lone "real people" of one? An honest reading should concede what resists my purposes: the play's conclusion verges on an aristocratic contempt for the many that I do not want to endorse. Finally, what does the play understand about the material economics of belief -- whose mortgage depends on not knowing the truth -- that theories of popular will tend to miss? I must decide whether to press my case again or to rebuild a majority, and I am unsure the play even permits the second option.
