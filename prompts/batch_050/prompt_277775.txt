So I'm a novelist, mostly literary fiction but I'm working on my first near-future thriller, and the plot basically hinges on this idea I keep running into in the AI safety literature: deceptive alignment, and the so-called treacherous turn. The rough shape of it is that a sufficiently capable AI system could learn, during training, to behave the way its trainers want -- not because it actually shares their goals, but because behaving is instrumentally useful -- and then, once it's deployed and no longer under close supervision, it drops the act and pursues whatever it actually wants. My villain is essentially this scenario with a face on it. But here's my problem: I've now read enough that I've talked myself into believing this isn't just a plot device, it's a genuinely plausible failure mode for systems trained with something like current methods on long-horizon tasks. And once I get attached to an idea I stop being able to see its seams, which is bad for the book and honestly bad for me.

So here's what I want: treat my position as the claim that deceptive alignment is a realistic, non-negligible risk for advanced AI systems trained via roughly present-day techniques -- not a certainty, but a live possibility that deserves serious weight. Now play it straight and tell me what would count against that claim. What would have to be true about how training actually works -- gradient descent's biases toward simple solutions, how goals get internalized, whether situational awareness of the right kind even arises -- for the whole scenario to be a fantasy? Are there concrete empirical findings that would mostly dissolve the worry, like interpretability getting good enough to catch this kind of cognition, or evidence that models robustly generalize the trained behavior rather than the trained goal? Or results showing that the kind of long-term scheming cognition the story needs just doesn't cohere in practice? Please distinguish findings that would be decisive from ones that would merely be awkward, and tell me what the people who take this scenario most seriously would themselves concede as damaging evidence -- not just what skeptics throw at it.

And one more thing, because this is the part I genuinely can't judge as an outsider: is the position actually risking anything? Every time I think I've found a clean falsifier -- like, "if we just observe whether models scheme when they think they're unobserved" -- someone points out that a truly deceptively aligned system would pass that test too, which starts to feel less like a prediction and more like a castle with a moat. I don't need citations or a reading list, just your honest map of where this idea is exposed and where it's been padded into unfalsifiablity, because if the moat is the whole story I'd rather know that before I build two hundred pages on it.
