I'm in Grade 11, and this term our school's philosophy club has been working through a unit on the philosophy of mind. Last week we got completely stuck on self-deception. Our teacher set up the puzzle like this: if I deceive you, I have to know the truth while keeping it from you — but if I try to deceive myself, I seem to need to both know and not know the very same fact at the same time, which looks impossible. We talked about the idea of the mind being divided into compartments, about how people maybe just gather evidence in a biased way until they conveniently end up believing what they wanted all along, and about whether you can deceive yourself on purpose without noticing your own intention. Half the club thinks self-deception is an everyday occurrence and the other half thinks the concept is incoherent, and honestly I can see both sides.

For our end-of-term showcase, instead of a normal presentation, I'd like to perform a satirical piece, and I need it written. Please write a self-help guide called something like "How to Lie to Yourself and Win: A Life Coach's Manual," in the relentlessly upbeat voice of an invented motivational guru, around 600-700 words. The comedy should come from the actual conceptual problem: the guru must give practical, step-by-step advice for deceiving yourself while carefully ensuring that no client could ever knowingly follow it. Include tips on curating your "information diet" so inconvenient evidence never reaches you, a chapter on keeping your two hands from knowing what the other is doing, and testimonials from satisfied clients who are not aware that they are clients. The guru should also handle the obvious objection — that deliberately following the guide would ruin the deception — with total confidence and an answer that somehow makes things worse.

The piece should stay deadpan the entire time: no winking, no admitting it's satire. Ideally, someone familiar with the debate between intentional and deflationary accounts of self-deception should recognize exactly which positions are being skewered and find the satire fair, while a classmate who has never read any philosophy should still find it funny as a parody of self-help culture. End with a legal disclaimer that quietly denies that the guide, or the problem it solves, exists at all.
