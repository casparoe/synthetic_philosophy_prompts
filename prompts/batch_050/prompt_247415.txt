I run a monthly philosophy night at my public library — wine, folding chairs, mostly retirees plus a couple of teachers, nobody with any philosophy training. Next month I'm on the hook for about 45 minutes on semantic externalism, i.e. Putnam and the Twin Earth thought experiment, the idea that what our words mean isn't fully fixed by what's going on inside our heads. Don't explain the view itself to me, I understand it fine. Plan how I should explain it.

What I want from you:

1. The order of ideas. My hunch is the standard picture has to go up first, and that it has to go up as two seperate claims — roughly, that grasping a meaning is a state of the individual speaker, and that this state settles what the word picks out — because the argument only bites if they accept both. Tell me whether that's the right dependency structure, and what has to be in place before the Twin Earth case is even intelligible rather than just sounding like science fiction trivia.

2. The carrying example. Should I build the whole thing on Twin Earth itself, or on something closer to home (elm vs. beech, jadeite vs. nephrite, fool's gold)? Say what the one example needs to have and what it must NOT have, and why whichever one you pick beats the alternatives for this audience.

3. The misconceptions, in roughly the order my audience will form them, and exactly where in the sequence to head each one off. My guesses: externalism means the environment literally reaches into your skull and changes your thoughts; it's really just the boring point that dictionaries and experts settle usage; they'll balk at the twins meaning different things by the same word back in 1750 before anyone could tell the two liquids apart; and they'll mix it up with indexicals like "I" and "here." Correct my list if it's wrong or incomplete.

4. One question I can put to the room at the end that shows whether they actually got it, as opposed to just having enjoyed the spaceship story.

5. What I should openly refuse to get into at this level — narrow content, two-dimensional semantics, the arthritis-type cases, whatever you think — and the one-sentence way to punt on each without sounding like I'm dodging.

Since this is a plan rather than a proof, also flag where it's weakest: which of your choices you're least confident in, and what would change your mind — e.g., what about my audience or the time limit would make you reorder the pieces or swap out the main example. American English, and keep it casual.
