In his 1912 address "Intuitionism and Formalism," Brouwer argues that mathematics is a free creation of the human mind, grounded in a basal intuition of two-oneness, and that its exactness resides in mental construction rather than in symbols on paper — with the consequence that classical logic outruns justification where it asserts what no construction can exhibit. I hold that he was essentially right: mathematical truth just is constructibility in principle, and classical mathematics is secure only where it can be read constructively. What would count against this position? Please map its exposure: which formal results (e.g., in metamathematics, reverse mathematics, or realizability), which developments in mathematical practice (e.g., non-constructive existence proofs that became indispensable, computer-checked classical proofs), and which conceptual arguments (e.g., about intersubjectivity of proof, or the apparent inevitability of excluded middle in finite combinatorics) would genuinely threaten it — distinguishing what a committed intuitionist would herself concede as a defeater from what she would dismiss as question-begging, and noting where the position has been so qualified (choice sequences, the creative subject, meaning-theoretic defenses) that it may no longer risk anything. Give your answer as a table with columns: candidate defeater | kind (formal result / development in practice / conceptual argument) | what would have to be true for it to tell against the view | decisiveness (fatal / serious / merely awkward) | conceded or dismissed by the committed intuitionist, and why. End with one row labeled verdict, assessing overall whether the position is exposed, well insulated, or insulated only at the cost of unfalsifiability.
