I'm TAing a survey course on early modern political philosophy, and I'm working through a stack of midterm exams this weekend. I'd like a second opinion on one answer before I finalize the grade, partly to calibrate myself against the rest of the pile. The exam gave students the following passage from Chapter 17 of Spinoza's Theological-Political Treatise (in Robert Willis's 1862 English translation):

"No one, for example, could ever so completely transfer his power, and consequently his rights, to another as to cease himself from feeling as a man; nor was there ever any sovereign power in the world that could dispose absolutely, and at its will and pleasure, of everything belonging to the state and the people."

The question, worth 20 points, read: "Explain what Spinoza is claiming in this passage and what work the claim does in his political theory. In particular: if individuals can never wholly transfer their power, and hence their right, to a sovereign, in what sense does sovereign right remain 'absolute' for Spinoza? And how does this passage bear on his reasons for preferring democracy to monarchy and aristocracy?"

Here is the student's answer, in full:

"Spinoza here is qualifying the account of sovereignty from the preceding chapter, where he argued that individuals transfer their natural right to the sovereign through a covenant, much as in Hobbes. His point is that such a transfer can never be complete. Since a person's right is coextensive with their power, and no one can alienate their power entirely — one cannot stop oneself from feeling, desiring, and judging as an individual — no subject is ever wholly at the sovereign's disposal. Obedience itself, not any motive of fear or hope behind it, is what constitutes the sovereign's right to command.

Sovereign right remains absolute in this sense: within the commonwealth there is no higher legal authority, and since justice exists only where a common power defines and enforces it, whatever the sovereign commands is just by definition, so no law can be criticized as unjust. And because right is nothing but power, whatever the state has the power to do, it does rightly; the more fully a state embodies this collective power, the more its decisions will track reason, which is why the multitude's decisions in a democracy are generally the most reasonable ones available.

The passage also grounds Spinoza's preference for democracy. Because all political legitimacy flows from the consent of the governed, democracy, in which each citizen consents to the rule of the whole, is the only fully legitimate form of state; and because it asks no one to surrender rights they cannot in fact surrender, it is the most natural form, closest to the condition of nature. It is also the most stable, since the multitude is less prone to fits of passion than a single ruler."
