In my graduate seminar on political authority, we have reached an impasse that I would like settled with more precision than the seminar managed. The question concerns performance legitimacy: whether sustained prosperity and social order under non-democratic rule — the pattern often attributed to states such as Singapore or contemporary China — can generate genuine political legitimacy, not merely prudent acquiescence or strategic stability.

Here is the specific point of dispute. One camp in the seminar holds that legitimacy is constitutively procedural: without some mechanism of consent (elections, or at minimum institutionalized channels through which the governed could in principle withdraw authorization), good outcomes produce at most a well-run tyranny. The opposing camp argues that legitimacy is fundamentally a relation between a regime and the welfare of the governed, and that durable reported satisfaction among citizens is evidence of authorization by other means. I pressed the second camp on the adaptive preferences problem: satisfaction expressed under conditions where dissent is costly and alternatives are suppressed cannot straightforwardly be read as endorsement, since preferences themselves may have been shaped by the regime being evaluated. Their reply was that this worry proves too much, since preferences in democracies are likewise shaped by the institutions under which people live.

So here is what I want from you. Give me your credence — an actual number — for the following proposition: a non-democratic regime that sustains broadly shared prosperity and order over multiple leadership successions thereby acquires genuine legitimacy, understood as a moral entitlement to rule that the governed wrong by resisting, rather than mere obedience-worthiness. Decompose your credence into the sub-questions it depends on, assigning a rough probability to each with one or two lines of justification. At minimum, address: whether legitimacy requires a procedural component or can be grounded in outputs alone; whether reported satisfaction under conditions of suppressed dissent constitutes evidence of consent or is better explained by adaptive preference formation; whether legitimacy is gradable and separable from the permissibility of resistance, such that a regime could be partly legitimate and still permissibly resisted; and whether the empirical record supports the claim that non-democratic performance legitimacy survives leadership transitions, which is where the proceduralists in my seminar think the view collapses.

Then tell me two things: which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument — empirical or conceptual — would move that sub-question enough to flip your overall verdict.
