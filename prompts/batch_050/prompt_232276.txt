So I'm a retired condensed matter physicist and in the last couple of years I've fallen properly down the philsophy rabbit hole, and there's a parallel I can't stop thinking about. Philosophers have these full-information or idealized-preference accounts of well-being: what matters for your good is not what you happen to want, but what you would want if you were fully informed, calm, vividly aware of the consequences, and so on. And then in the AI alignment corner there's this coherent extrapolated volition idea, where instead of handing a system your current wishes you try to hand it what your wishes would extrapolate to under sufficient reflection. To my eye these are the same move made twice, and both look to me like solving an inverse problem: inferring some clean underlying preference from noisy observed behavior. And if there's one thing physics teaches you, it's that inverse problems are usually ill-posed. You need regularization, and the regularizer is where your assumptions sneak in, not where they leave.

Here's what I want written: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two people who have been arguing about this for years.

Camp one, call her the Extrapolationist, holds that idealized or extrapolated preferences are a coherent and authoritative standard: that we can in principle specify what someone would want under suitable ideal conditions, that this is the standard paternalism, well-being, and maybe AI design should track, and that the underdetermination worries are overstated.

Camp two, call him the Actualist (or endorsement theorist, or whatever label is fairer — pick one he'd accept), holds that only preferences the actual person could recognize or endorse have normative authority; that "what you'd want if fully informed" is either indeterminate (many extrapolations, no unique fixed point) or circular (the idealization has to be steered by values, and whose?); and that the idealized self can end up a stranger, at which point the standard loses its pull.

The document should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both sides can actually sign — I'd guess it includes that actual preferences can be misinformed or adaptive, and that both sides owe a treatment of the standard hard cases like the contented slave and the addict's craving — but only genuinely agreed claims, no vacuous filler; (2) a table of residual disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accecpt the wording; (3) the cruxes, i.e. questions where settling them would genuinely move one side — I suspect one is whether anything like a convergence or uniqueness result is available for preference extrapolation, and another is the alienation question of whose authority wins when idealized-me wants things actual-me finds repellent; (4) for each crux, a case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

And one more thing: tell me straight whether my inverse-problem-and-regularization analogy earns its keep here or whether it's a physicist's hammer hunting for a nail. If part of their fight turns out to be verbal, say which part and why.
