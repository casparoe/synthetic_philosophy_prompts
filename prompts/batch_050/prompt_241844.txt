Last night my friend and I got into a long argument about supererogation — about which good deeds are genuinely optional and which ones morality flat-out requires of us — and we ended it by agreeing to take a fresh case to her today and see whose prediction of her verdict comes true. I'd like your help reconstructing her implicit standard so I can anticipate what she'll say.

Here are the verdicts she defended, at length and with confidence:

1. Donating a kidney to a stranger who will die without it: admirable, but strictly optional; nobody is obligated to do this.
2. Leaving a customary tip at a restaurant: genuinely obligatory, not optional at all, despite the trivial sums involved.
3. Jumping into a partially frozen lake to save a drowning child when no one else is around: obligatory — she insisted you'd be a moral monster not to, even though the rescuer faces a real risk of death.
4. Giving a tenth of one's income year after year to effective charities: supererogatory, praiseworthy but not required.
5. A soldier diving on a grenade to save nearby comrades: supererogatory, a heroism that cannot be demanded.
6. Keeping a promise that has become unexpectedly ruinous to keep: obligatory; she wouldn't budge on this.

Here's the case we're taking to her: a hospital identifies a woman as the only known compatible bone-marrow donor for a specific child with leukemia. The donation procedure is safe, involves no meaningful risk of death, but requires some discomfort, a brief hospitalization, and a week or two of recovery. She is asked whether the woman is morally required to donate, or whether declining is morally permissible even if regrettable.

I'm less interested in the verdict itself than in the reasoning. Which general principles are consistent with her six earlier verdicts? I can see at least two: one that turns on the difference between risking one's life and certainly sacrificing something (a life, a body part), which seems to fit verdicts 1, 3, and 5; and another that turns on being uniquely positioned to rescue an identifiable victim, which fits 3 but seems to stumble on 5. Do these two principles really survive all six cases, and what do they each predict about the bone-marrow donor? Which prediction do you think best captures her actual pattern of judgment, and why? Finally, what further hypothetical would most cleanly distinguish the rival principles you've attributed to her, in case I want to press her on it after she answers?
