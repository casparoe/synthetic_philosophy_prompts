So I'm a software engineer (distributed systems, some formal methods on the side, zero formal philosophy training) and I've fallen down this rabbit hole about self-modifying systems and the Löbian obstacle, because I'm tinkering with a side project: an agent that proposes changes to its own code but only deploys a change once it's machine-checked a proof that the changed version still satisfies the safety spec. I've been drafting a doc laying out what I actually believe about this, and before I inflict it on my coworkers I want someone to check whether my positions even fit together. Here's where I'm at:

1. If a system is going to rewrite itself, it has to verify its successors -- "I only deploy what I've proven safe" is the whole ballgame, otherwise you get drift across generations and the original guarantees mean nothing.
2. I accept the standard result here, at least as I understand it: no consistent theory strong enough to do arithmetic can prove its own soundness schema (Löb's theorem, roughly that if PA proves "whatever PA proves is true" as a schema then it's already inconsistent-adjacent, plus Gödel II saying it can't even prove its own consistency). So naive self-trust is mathematically out, not just hard.
3. But I think there's a pretty clean patch: let generation n+1's reasoning system be generation n's system plus an extra axiom stating that generation n's system is sound. Then the parent can prove what it needs about the child, the child does the same for the grandchild, etc. The regress doesn't bother me -- every link in the chain is locally fine, and in practice you only ever build finitely many generations.
4. Honestly, part of me thinks the whole obstacle is a bit artificial, because human mathematicians trust Peano arithmetic constantly without proving it sound and nobody calls them irrational. Rational trust and formal provability are just different things, and once you see that, the theorem stops looking like a practical blocker.
5. Löb's theorem and Gödel's second incompleteness theorem are basically the same wall viewed from two angles, so anything true of one should carry over to the other.

So: do these five actually hang together? Which pairs are in real tension vs. which only look like they conflict until you look closer? I'd like the genuine conflicts ranked by severity, not just a flat list -- and for each one, what's the cheapest revision that restores consistency and what does it cost me (e.g. does the side project's deployment rule survive, or am I giving up the thing I cared about). Please don't just say "drop 2" because the theorem is famous -- I'm pretty sure 2 is the one thing here that's actually load-bearing. Also flag anywhere I've stated a real result slightly wrong in a way that matters for the argument, since my logic background is definately shakier than my engineering background.
