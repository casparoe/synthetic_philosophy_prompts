A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about finks and masks, and the argument ended in a way that left me less interested in who was right than in what kind of fight we were actually having. The setup, for context: the old conditional analysis says a glass is fragile just in case it would break if struck. C. B. Martin's finks and Mark Johnston's masks are the standard counters: a fragile glass wired to a device that would strip its fragility the instant it was struck would not break, yet is still fragile; and a glass packed in styrofoam would not break when struck, yet is still fragile. David Lewis tried to patch the analysis by restricting attention to intrinsic properties retained through the causal process; dispositionalists in the spirit of Alexander Bird instead take dispositions as primitive or essential properties and treat the conditional as at best a symptom. My friend insists the fink and mask cases refute the conditional analysis outright and that dispositions are irreducible. I insisted the cases only show that disposition talk is context-sensitive, and that "fink" and "mask" are labels for whatever interferes with the normal manifestation, so the whole debate collapses into stipulation about what counts as normal conditions.

What struck me afterward is that neither of us gave an inch, and as far as I can tell the literature hasn't either: the same two or three cases have been recycled for thirty years, each side redescribing them in its own vocabulary. So my question is not which side is correct but what the disagreement actually consists in. Candidates I can see: (a) a substantive metaphysical dispute about whether dispositional properties reduce to categorical ones, in which case finks and masks are supposed to function as genuine counterexamples; (b) a merely semantic dispute about the truth conditions of disposition ascriptions, in which case "would break if struck" versus "would break if struck, barring masks and finks" is either a harmless ceteris paribus clause or a covertly circular one, and the fight is about the word "fragile" rather than about glass; (c) two different projects being conducted under one heading, one side doing the semantics of ascriptions and the other doing the ontology of powers, so that the apparent impasse is cross-purposes rather than disagreement; or (d) a disguised methodological dispute about whether intuitions about gerrymandered hypothetical cases can bear the weight both sides put on them.

Please diagnose the debate using what the positions actually say, tell me which of these it is (or what I have missed), and say what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a formal result about whether the ceteris paribus clause can be spelled out non-circularly, a distinction the field is missing, or the admission that the question is ill-posed. If your answer is that no resolution should be expected, I want that argued rather than assumed. One request: engage specifically with the fact that the mask case, unlike the fink case, involves no change to the object's intrinsic properties, because I suspect that asymmetry is where the real structure of the disagreement is hiding, and my friend thinks it's a red herring.
