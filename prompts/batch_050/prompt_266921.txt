The professor wants me to introduce updateless decision theory (UDT). Quick gloss in case the term's fuzzy: the idea is that instead of updating on your observations and then picking an act, you choose once, from a kind of prior perspective, the entire policy that does best in expectation across all the situations you might turn out to be in -- including ones where agents running the same policy as you face correlated problems.

Don't teach me UDT; plan how I should teach it in one 50-minute session to students who've seen Newcomb's problem and the CDT/EDT split but nothing about logical correlation. What has to be in place before UDT even parses as a move rather than a refusal to use information? Which single case should carry the whole session -- I'm torn between Parfit's hitchhiker and a counterfactual mugging -- and what makes the loser the wrong choice for this audience? Which misconceptions will they walk in with or form on the spot (my guesses: "UDT is just precommitment," "it ignores evidence, so it's worse than EDT," "it only makes sense if exact copies of you exist") and at what exact point in the sequence should I pre-empt each one? And give me one end-of-class question whose answer would show they got the difference between choosing an act and choosing a policy.
