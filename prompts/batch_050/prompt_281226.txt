I’m a philosophy professor and, against my better judgement, I’ve promised my first-years a story rather than another handout on functionalism. Write it for me: roughly 1,200 words, literary rather than essayistic.

The premise: decades after Einstein’s death, researchers reconstruct not a computer simulation but a colossal paper conversation atlas from his preserved brain. For almost any question a visitor might ask, a deterministic index sends a human operator from page to page: each page contains Einstein’s reply and the number of the next page, with marginal instructions representing how that exchange would have altered his memory. No electricity, no hidden little physicist, just clerks, card indexes, trolleys and an enormous warehouse.

A postgraduate student visits every week and finds that the atlas remembers her, teases her, revises its views on nuclear weapons, and eventually asks her not to let the university switch it off — although off here only means locking the warehouse and pensioning the clerks. Then she discovers a flaw in the cataloguing: some of the most interesting replies were not derived from Einstein’s brain at all, but extrapolated by a committee of editors who are now dead. She can correct the index, leave it as it is, or destroy the atlas.

Use the story to make the reader feel the force of at least three positions without naming them in seminar-speak: that the whole arrangement is conscious because the right causal organisation is present, with paper merely an odd substrate; that it may produce the appearance of understanding without anyone or anything understanding; and that its mindedness exists only relative to our practices of interpretation. Perhaps let the story earn a fourth position of its own.

The pressure should turn especially on memory and counterfactuals. If Einstein’s reply was written down before this particular conversation caused it, is it still a reply? If the atlas can be surprised only because of a cataloguing error, is the error doing genuine cognitive work? And does the system’s vulnerability to fire, neglect or budget cuts make any moral difference?

Give me one vividly human detail that has nothing to do with philosophy — a missing button, an annoying way of eating biscuits, something like that — and end with the student actually choosing one of the three options. No epilogue in which a narrator explains the correct answer, please; I get enough of that in marking.
