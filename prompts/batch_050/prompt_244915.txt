Two coworkers of mine keep a running joint estimate of whether our company will do layoffs this quarter, and it turned into an actual argument last week. Background: they both had layoffs at about 30%. Then Mara caught a blurry glimpse of the CFO's screen over his shoulder and thought she saw a calendar event called "Q3 restructuring planning." She says she's about 70% sure she read it right. They both agree that if the meeting really exists, layoffs go up to about 80%, and if it doesn't, they stay at 20%. Here's thier fight:

1. Mara said the new estimate should obviously be a weighted average: 0.7 × 80% + 0.3 × 20% = 62%. You just blend the two scenarios by how confident you are in each.

2. Sam said you can't boil the glimpse down to a 70/30 split like that. If she'd ended up 70% sure because a reliable assistant whispered "there's a restructuring meeting on his calendar" instead of squinting at a screen, her formula would spit out the same 62% — but those are plainly different evidential situations, and the whisper version tells you extra stuff, like that the CFO's office is leaking. A rule that gives the same output for both is throwing information away.

3. Mara said that for the question "will there be layoffs," everything the glimpse teaches you runs through whether the meeting exists or not, so once you've fixed your confidence in that, the weighted average is just the correct update and the route you took to 70% can't matter beyond it.

4. Sam came back with two things. First, step 3 assumes exactly what needs proving — the glimpse probably also shifted their odds on things like "the CFO is hiding bad news," which bear on layoffs independently of the meeting question. Second, he said suppose Mara also became 60% sure she'd seen moving boxes stacked near HR: applying her rule to the meeting question first and then the boxes gives a different final number than doing boxes first and then the meeting, and a rule whose answer depends on the order you do the bookkeeping in can't be tracking anything real.

They asked me to referee and I stalled out. Go through the four moves and say who's right at each step, then give an overall verdict. And when you explain the underlying theory of updating on less-than-certain evidence, be clear about which parts are settled math and which parts are still genuinely disputed by people who work on this — I don't want a contested position dressed up as the consensus.
