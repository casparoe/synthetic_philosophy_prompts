Question: Browning and Goldhagen offer competing explanations of why ordinary Germans participated in mass killing. Explain each position, identify the key evidence each draws on, and say what is at stake in the disagreement.

Student's answer: Christopher Browning's "Ordinary Men" studies Reserve Police Battalion 101, middle-aged reservists from Hamburg who shot thousands of Jews in Poland. Browning argues these were not fanatics but ordinary men shaped by situational forces: peer pressure, deference to authority, and gradual brutalization. He supports this with psychological experiments like Milgram's obedience studies, which showed most people will inflict pain when ordered. Daniel Goldhagen's "Hitler's Willing Executioners" responds that the killers were willing participants driven by a uniquely German "eliminationist antisemitism" that had been exterminationist in character throughout German history, so the killers killed because they believed the victims deserved it. Where Browning emphasizes the situation, Goldhagen emphasizes a pre-existing culture of hatred. What's at stake is whether the Holocaust tells us something disturbing about human nature in general -- that anyone could become a perpetrator under the right conditions -- or whether it requires a specifically German cultural explanation, which limits the lesson to Germany. Browning also notes that Major Trapp offered the men of the battalion the chance to opt out, and those who refused assignments were threatened with severe punishment, so fear played a role too.
