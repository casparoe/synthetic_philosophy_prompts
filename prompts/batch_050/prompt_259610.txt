I'm a PhD student in security studies working on meaningful human control over weapons. My advisor's informal working group has a pretty settled track record of verdicts on particular systems:

- Anti-personnel landmines: no meaningful human control, unacceptable.
- Phalanx-style close-in weapon systems that automatically fire on incoming missiles without any human approving the shot: fine.
- A loitering munition that circles an area for hours and dives on anything matching a stored radar signature: not fine.
- A fire-and-forget anti-ship missile that, after launch, picks its own target among several radar contacts: fine.
- An armed drone where a human watches the feed and approves each individual strike: fine.
- A proposed autonomous sentry that would identify and fire on human-shaped targets crossing a border zone: not fine.

Now they're about to discuss a new system and I want to guess where they'll land before the meeting. The system autonomously detects, classifies, and ranks vehicles in a convoy, then presents its top pick to a human operator who has fifteen seconds to approve or veto before the firing window closes (in trials, operators approve something like 95% of the time). Note the vehicles can be occupied.

Predict their verdict, and tell me which rule they're implicitly running -- per-engagement human approval, boundedness/predictability of the system's effects in space and time, a materiel-vs-personnel target distinction, or somehting else. Which of the other rules fit the six verdicts equally well, and what additional case would best tell the candidate rules apart? I'm less interested in your own view of the system than in the logic of their track record.
