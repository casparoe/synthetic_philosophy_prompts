Exam question:

In his work on equality, Ronald Dworkin distinguishes brute luck from option luck. (a) Explain this distinction and why Dworkin takes it to be important for a theory of distributive justice. (b) Explain how the distinction supports the luck egalitarian claim that inequalities are just when they arise from choices for which people are responsible, and unjust when they arise from unchosen circumstances; include a brief account of how Dworkin's hypothetical insurance market is meant to implement this idea. (c) State and briefly explain one prominent objection to luck egalitarianism.

Student's answer:

(a) Dworkin distinguishes two kinds of luck. Option luck concerns how deliberate and calculated gambles turn out — for instance, whether a risky investment pays off, or whether an uninsured house burns down. Brute luck, by contrast, is bad fortune that strikes without the person's having accepted any such risk: being born with a congenital disability, or losing the use of one's legs when a tree falls on one's car. The distinction matters because Dworkin thinks the aim of egalitarian justice is to neutralize the influence of unchosen circumstances on people's lives, while leaving their choices to make the difference they make. Bad brute luck is exactly the sort of circumstance that justice should correct for, whereas the results of option luck are traceable to choices for which the agent can be held responsible.

(b) It follows that inequalities are just when they flow from people's choices — their ambitions, efforts, and accepted risks — and unjust when they flow from unchosen circumstances. Dworkin calls the correct standard equality of welfare: the state should arrange things so that everyone ends up equally well off in how their lives go, compensating for brute luck while leaving the outcomes of option luck in place. To implement this standard, Dworkin proposes a hypothetical insurance market: behind a veil of ignorance about their talents, people decide how much of their resources to spend insuring themselves against low earning power and against disability, and the premiums and payouts that would emerge in this imagined market justify an actual redistributive scheme of taxation and transfers. On the resulting scheme, someone who gambles on a risky venture and loses has no claim to compensation, since the loss was the outcome of option luck she could have declined.

(c) A well-known objection, due to John Rawls, is the harshness objection. If we take the distinction between brute and option luck seriously, we seem committed to abandoning people who suffer through their own imprudence — the motorcyclist who declines insurance and is then seriously injured — even when we could cheaply help them. Rawls argues instead that, since the distribution of natural endowments is arbitrary from a moral point of view, justice requires democratic equality: guaranteeing all citizens a decent social minimum and fair opportunities regardless of whether their misfortunes were their own fault.
