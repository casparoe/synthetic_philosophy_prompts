I have just come out of a long argument with a friend about whether it is rational to fear one’s own death. When I said that death can deprive someone of a worthwhile future, he replied, “Death cannot be bad for you: when it is here, you are not.” He treated this as settling both whether fear of death is irrational and whether a person has any reason to prefer a later death to an earlier one.

Do not merely rehearse the standard Epicurean position. Distinguish the precise theses that this one-liner might express: claims about who can be harmed after ceasing to exist; when a harm must be temporally located; whether something must be experienced to be bad for a person; what baseline is being used for comparison; whether “bad for” means intrinsically bad, comparatively bad, or bad relative to one’s interests; and whether the argument concerns dying, death as an event, or the subsequent state of being dead.

For each reading, classify it as trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; assess its implications for deprivation accounts, prenatal nonexistence, posthumous harms, and rational prudential preferences; and say which readings would actually support either of my friend’s conclusions. End with the strongest anti-Epicurean reply and the strongest Epicurean response to it. Begin with a two-sentence bottom line, use a numbered list, and keep the entire answer under 700 words.
