A close friend of mine teaches aesthetics and moonlights as a juror for a contemporary art prize. Over the years I have logged her verdicts on borderline cases, and her pattern puzzles me. She says Duchamp's Fountain is art, and so are Warhol's Brillo Boxes, even though she concedes that a visually indistinguishable urinal or shipping carton, produced without artistic intent, would not be. She says Cage's 4'33" is art despite offering, in her words, nothing to listen to. A spectacular sunset is not art, no matter how moving, because nobody made it. A technically flawless forged Vermeer, painted recently but passed off as seventeenth-century, is not art either, though she admits she could not justify that by pointing to any deficiency in how it looks. Finally, an avant-garde performance piece that the gallery-going public loathed and critics dismissed as a publicity stunt is art all the same, in her view, because of the context in which it was presented.

The new case I want to pose to her: a retired sailor has spent thirty years arranging driftwood, rope, and wreckage into an enormous composition on an inaccessible stretch of coastline. He regards it as his life's work, made it with complete deliberateness, revises it constantly, and describes it as communicating something about loss and the sea -- but no one else has ever seen it, he has never sought an audience, and no gallery, critic, or art institution is aware of it.

Predict her verdict and explain your reasoning. More than one rule seems compatible with her track record -- conferral of status by the art world, the maker's intentions, the work's connection to an art-historical conversation, the provision of a certain kind of experience -- and I want to know which of these best unifies her earlier verdicts, what each candidate rule predicts about the driftwood case, and where they come apart. If her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so, state which rule each possible verdict would commit her to, and describe the next case I could put to her that would most efficiently distinguish between the surviving candidates.
