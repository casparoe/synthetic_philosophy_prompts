In my graduate seminar on contractualist ethics we have spent three weeks on the aggregation problem, and I have come to suspect that the debate as it is conducted in the literature is not really one disagreement but several disputes wearing the same clothes. I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

The familiar setup: Scanlon's individualist restriction holds that the justifiability of a principle is assessed by pairwise comparison of individual complaints, so in a rescue choice between saving one stranger and saving five, the five cannot pool their complaints into a collective grievance that outweighs the single person's. On the classic picture this yields some tie-break-like procedure. The critics, building on Taurek's original provocation but reversing his conclusion, find this outcome intolerable and propose various aggregation-friendly repairs: weighting or summing complaints, appeal to ex ante perspectives, claims that in such cases contractualism should defer to impartial reasons that are not complaint-based at all. The defenders reply that every such repair either abandons what is distinctive about contractualism -- that justifiability to each person is the operative notion, not impersonally best outcomes -- or smuggles aggregation back in through a supposedly neutral device like ex ante reasoning, which they argue covertly treats interchangeable persons as a single beneficiary.

What strikes me, twenty-five years into this exchange, is that both sides have said everything many times and the same moves recur in nearly the same order: the non-aggregator produces a case where aggregation looks perverse, the aggregator produces a case where refusing to aggregate looks perverse, and each side then charges the other with begging the question by choosing their test cases in light of the verdict they already want. Nobody seems to convert.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not a disagreement about which verdicts are correct in the rescue cases at all -- both sides seem to know the menu of verdicts -- but about what would vindicate a verdict: one side takes fidelity to the separateness-of-persons structure of justification as the test, the other takes fit with robust intuitions about numbers as the test, and neither has a criterion the other accepts for ranking these. But I may be wrong. Perhaps it is a substantive dispute about the metaphysics of reasons; perhaps it is verbal, with the two sides using justification to a person in different senses; or perhaps a real empirical or formal question is buried underneath, something about interpersonal comparability of burdens or about how ex ante perspectives should be constructed.

Diagnose the disagreement using what the two sides actually argue, tell me what kind of dispute it is and why, and say what -- a distinction, a formal result, a stipulated change in the question -- would count as progress on your diagnosis, or argue honestly that none should be expected. And please close by telling me what would change your mind: which consideration or argument would overturn your diagnosis, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
