I am putting together a graduate seminar on the epistemology of anomalous inquiry, and I would like a careful counterfactual reconstruction as the centerpiece of one session.

Henry Sidgwick spent a substantial portion of his later career on psychical research, helping to found and lead the Society for Psychical Research and personally sifting reports of apparitions, mediums, and thought-transference. His motives were mixed in a way that makes him philosophically interesting. On one side stood a characteristically Victorian confidence that patient, methodical investigation by credible witnesses could settle questions that enthusiasm and dogmatism had left muddled. On the other stood the unfinished business of the Methods of Ethics: Sidgwick held that reason delivers both a principle of rational egoism and a principle of impartial benevolence, and he could find no purely philosophical way to prove they always coincide. Empirical evidence of personal survival after death, with its attendant prospects of post-mortem reward, looked to him like the one route by which that dualism of practical reason might be empirically closed.

Now suppose Sidgwick could survey the subsequent century and a half. He would find that his own Society repeatedly exposed fraud among celebrated mediums, including some figures respected investigators had vouched for. He would find the ganzfeld telepathy experiments, the meta-analyses claiming small positive effects, and the skeptical rejoinders that those effects shrink as protocols tighten and may be artifacts of selective reporting. He would find the 2011 episode in which a distinguished social psychologist published experiments purporting to demonstrate precognition in a leading journal, followed by failed replications, and the ensuing crisis over p-hacking, publication bias, and the subsequent reform movement toward preregistration and Bayesian reanalysis. And he would find that the hypothesis he most cared about — survival — has receded from serious academic consideration altogether.

What would he conclude? In particular: Would he take the record as vindicating his method while discrediting his subject matter — the investigations worked, and that is precisely why we now know there is nothing there? Or would the psychology of the replication crisis lead him to think that even his generation's best procedures were never up to the task, so that the negative result itself is on shakier ground than he hoped? Would he accept a Bayesian argument that the prior against survival was always low enough that no achievable body of testimony could overcome it, and if so, would he regard that as a defeat for his project or as a proof that the project was misconceived from the start? And what would the collapse of the survival hypothesis do to his ethics: does the dualism of practical reason become, in his hands, an argument for something else — for lowering the ambitions of moral theory, for a religious postulate of a different kind, or for revising one of the two principles?

Please give a single reasoned verdict rather than a menu of possibilities. Because this is a reconstruction, end by saying what would change it: which feature of Sidgwick's actual writings you are leaning on most heavily, where the extrapolation from his recorded views to this counterfactual is most contestable, and what consideration — about his texts, or about how one reads the modern evidence — would most plausibly move you to a different conclusion.
