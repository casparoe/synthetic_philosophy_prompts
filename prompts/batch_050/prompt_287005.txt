After a career in statistical physics, I retired three years ago and have since been working my way into philosophy. The philosophy of physics seminar at my local university has agreed to let me lead a session next month, and I intend to defend a thesis I first met in the cosmology literature: a cosmological model on which the overwhelming majority of observers are Boltzmann brains — isolated, fluctuation-born observers whose apparent memories of a low-entropy past are false — ought to be rejected as cognitively self-undermining. My case, in outline, is that if such a model were true, then our memories and present observations would almost certainly be unreliable, so the evidence we cite for the model would be spurious on the model's own terms; a theory that undercuts its evidential basis thereby gives us decisive reason to discard it, whatever its other empirical virtues.

The audience: four or five philosophers of physics conversant with the measure problem and the debate over typicality assumptions; two epistemologists who work on self-defeat and higher-order evidence; a philosopher of mind; and two cosmologists broadly sympathetic to my conclusion. I want a preparation sheet, not a defense. Give me the objections in roughly the order I should expect them, phrased as these particular people would phrase them, each with an assessment of how damaging it is, what I should concede and what I should hold, and a reply deliverable in under two minutes, with a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Please include the question I am hoping nobody asks.

Two requests concerning calibration. Where the field is genuinely divided — on the legitimacy of typicality reasoning, on whether self-underminingness bears on a theory's truth rather than merely on our entitlement to believe it — say so plainly and do not present a contested position as settled consensus. Conversely, where something is broadly agreed, do not manufacture controversy. Please write in American English.
