i run a little decision theory reading group and we just spent two sessions on the "deliberation crowds out prediction" thesis -- the idea that while you're genuinely deliberating about what to do, you can't treat your own upcoming act as an event to forecast and assign it credences, because the moment you do, the deliberation isn't really deliberation anymore (you're spectating yourself, not choosing). we had a good fight about it and i want to close with a poem that takes the idea seriously.

write me a pantoum -- the form where the second and fourth lines of each quatrain come back as the first and third lines of the next one, until the final stanza folds the opening lines back in. i want the recycling to do the actual philosophical work: the speaker keeps trying to state a probability for what they're about to do, and each prediction keeps coming back inside the deliberation and changing it, so the forecast never survives re-entry. the form is basically the argument -- nothing the speaker predicts can stay fixed once it's been predicted. the last stanza should end in an act rather than a forecast, like the poem's own deliberation finally closing, and the opening line should return in the last line but mean something different now that the choice is made.

a couple constraints: it should be accurate enough that someone who's read the literature on this (Spohn, Levi, the whole can-you-bet-on-your-own-acts debate) recognizes the problem itself and not just the catchphrase, but it also has to land as a poem for the group members who skipped the readings. no fixed length beyond what the form needs, maybe 4-6 stanzas. and please don't let it collapse into a smug "so you can't predict yourself, the end" -- the interesting part is that deliberation still works fine without self-prediction, and the poem should leave room for that.
