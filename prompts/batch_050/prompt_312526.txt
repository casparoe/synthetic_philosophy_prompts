Next month I'm giving a talk in our department colloquium series, which this semester is joint with the psych building's moral cognition lab, and I need to not embarrass myself. The paper defends a thesis I've been turning over since my dissertaton days: everyone reads Strawson's objective stance as a kind of neutral fallback -- the mode we slip into when someone is excused or exempted, regrettable maybe, but not itself up for moral assessment. I argue that's wrong. Taking the objective attitude toward another adult who is a genuine candidate for ordinary moral relationships is itself a form of moral address, a specifically chilly one, and it can be morally criticizable in roughly the way misplaced resentment is criticizable, even in cases where the other's behavior fully warrants some degree of exemption. My central exhibit is Strawson's own parent-of-the-difficult-child case from Freedom and Resentment: I claim the example, read carefully, shows that a parent who goes *wholly* objective wrongs the child, and that this is a reactive wrong rather than just a practical failure. The audience: two genuine Strawson scholars (one of whom, I'm told, holds that the objective stance is purely descriptive and can't be morally charged at all), a hard incompatibilist who will be tempted to recruit me and will be annoyed when I won't be recruited, three or four moral psychologists from the lab who think about all this in terms of attachment, emotion regulation, and clinical detachment rather than participation, and one Kantian who comes to everything and objects to everything.
