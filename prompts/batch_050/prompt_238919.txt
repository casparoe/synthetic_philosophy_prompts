I'm putting together a seminar on the epistemology of markets for my upper-level philosophy of economics course, and I built an argument that I'm now worried proves way too much. Here it is.

(1) A trade between two people is only rational for you if you expect to gain from it -- you wouldn't hand over money for shares of a company unless you thought the shares were worth more than the price.

(2) If both parties to a proposed trade are rational and this is common knowledge, then the other person's willingness to trade with you is itself information: they must expect to gain too, which means they have reasons (possibly better information than yours) for thinking the deal favors them.

(3) Updating on that fact, you should become less confident the trade favors you -- so you revise your expected gain downward.

(4) But your downward revision is itself predictable to them, so their continued willingness to trade at the same price is further information, and you revise again. Iterating, no price survives at which both parties still expect to gain.

(5) So under common knowledge of rationality, the volume of speculative trade is zero. (I gather this is roughly what the Milgrom-Stokey no-trade result and Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree theorem actually establish, so I'm not worried about the logic up to here.)

(6) Now the killer move. Nothing in the setup depended on the trade being about financial assets. Buying insurance, taking a job at a negotiated salary, buying a used car, even swapping lunch items in the cafeteria -- all of these are trades where each party's willingness is information about the other's private assessment. The iteration in (4) applies just as well.

(7) So rational agents with common knowledge of mutual rationality would never trade anything, ever. Markets, employment contracts, and insurance are all irrational. But that conclusion is obviously insane, so something in (1)-(6) has to go.

Here's what bugs me: when I poke at it, (6) feels like the culprit, but my students keep pointing out that the insurance case looks like it fits the premises just as well as the stock case -- the insurer knows more about its pricing models than I do, and their willingness to sell me fire insurance at $400/year really is evidence about my expected losses. So the "it only applies to pure speculation" response seems to need something to back it up, and I'm not sure what.

So: which step do you think should actually be rejected, why does that step look plausible enough to sneak into a seminar argument, and what do the different ways of rejecting it each cost? For instance, if the fix is that (1) conflates different notions of "expecting to gain," spell out which notions and which one the theorem actually needs. And if the fix instead requires denying common priors or smuggling in gains from trade that aren't information-based, say what that implies about how much of real-world trading volume the no-trade result genuinely threatens. I'd also like to know, for teaching purposes, whether the honest upshot is "the theorem is a narrow result about pure common-prior speculation" or "the theorem is fine and most actual traders are just irrational" -- because those make for very different week three readings.
