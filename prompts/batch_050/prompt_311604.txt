I run an undergraduate seminar on moral psychology with about eighteen students, and we have one 75-minute session to spend on the idea of standing to blame: the thought, familiar from the literature on hypocrisy, that someone who faults others for conduct they themselves engage in may lack the right to deliver that fault, quite apart from whether the criticism is true. My students reliably grasp this on the page but treat it as a mere point about rudeness or bad manners; I want them to feel, in their own behavior, the difference between a criticism being accurate and a particular person being positioned to voice it. Design a structured in-class activity built around the following mechanism I have in mind, which you should refine and repair as needed: each participant privately writes a sealed "ledger" of small, plausible self-confessed failings in a few everyday domains (keeping promises, punctuality, honesty about mistakes, fairness in shared work); the group then plays several rounds of a coordination or contribution game in which mild breaches of these norms are possible and tempting, and in which any player may spend points to publicly blame another player for a breach. The twist is that a blamed player may invoke a "standing challenge," forcing the blamer to unseal the ledger entry for the relevant domain: if the blamer's own record shows the same failing, the blame backfires and the blamer pays a steeper cost than the blamed; if not, the blame stands with extra force. Please give me complete rules, including how many rounds, what information is public versus private, a payoff scheme in points that needs no real money but makes the choices matter a little, and a round-by-round timeline that fits the 75 minutes. Crucially, arrange things so that most participants end up having blamed someone for something in their own ledger before the debrief, so that the standing question lands on them personally rather than being discussed in the abstract. The debrief should map specific moments of the game onto the philosophical distinctions I care about: the difference between the content of blame (which can remain true whoever utters it) and the standing to issue it; the difference between a hypocrite who still endorses the norm and a hypocrite who has abandoned it; and the question of whether a standing challenge silences blame or merely redirects it to better-positioned blamers. Also tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I anticipate, for instance, that students may write fake-clean ledgers, treat the unsealing as mere embarrassment rather than a normative defeat, or collude to avoid blaming at all — and how to handle each failure. Finally, be frank about which feature of the philosophical idea the activity cannot reproduce: the game makes standing an explicit, costed rule, whereas in life it is a contested normative status, and real hypocrisy typically involves professed commitments rather than a written record, so tell me what I must supply in discussion to close that gap.
