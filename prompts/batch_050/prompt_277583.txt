I TA a philosophy of probability seminar and we're doing the unit on objective chance and deference principles, and honestly the problem sets are killing me — every student writes some version of "rational credence should match known objective chance" and they're not wrong, they've got Lewis's Principal Principle basically down, but it all reads like homework. My advisor thinks verse has no place in a probability seminar, which is exactly why I want to open our last meeting by reading them a poem instead.

Here's the ask: a Petrarchan sonnet, fourteen lines, about the Principal Principle. The octave should set up the picture from the inside — give me an agent with some texture (a card sharp, a weather forecaster, someone whose living depends on getting this right) who knows nothing about how this particular coin flip or storm will turn out except the objective chance, and who shapes her own uncertainty to match it exactly. Half a heart for heads. The thing that has to come through is that this is deference, not prediction: she's not guessing the outcome, she's treating the chance as a fact about the world and letting it govern her credences while everything else stays dark to her. She's humble about the toss but not about the odds.

Then the volta: the moment she realizes the world never promised her anything back. The coin can land heads nine times running and the chance of heads on flip ten is exactly what it always was — the past doesn't owe her a correction, and a one-in-twenty chance doesn't forbid the one-in-twenty thing from happening tonight, at her table, with her money down. The closing couplet should land the asymmetry: chance constrains her credences but doesn't constrain the coin. Her humility is owed to the chances, not to fate, and thats the whole discipline. A philosopher of probability should read it and nod at the craft; my students should just think it's a good poem about standing in front of a coin that doesn't care about you.
