I'm a program officer at a small foundation that funds work at the intersection of moral psychology and social reform, and I'm putting together our next three-year initiative, which will support roughly a half-dozen researchers on the question of which moral emotion a society should try to cultivate: guilt or shame. The background motivation is familiar. Shame seems to do real work that guilt can't — it polices behavior where no one thinks they've harmed an identifiable victim (tax evasion, littering, casual cruelty in anonymous settings), and it seems to be the engine behind rapid norm shifts like the decline of drunk driving or smoking in public. But the usual critique is that shame attaches to the person rather than the act, produces defensiveness, concealment, and rage rather than repair, and is easily captured by the powerful for conformity rather than morality. Guilt, by contrast, is supposed to be the reparative emotion — but skeptics point out that guilty people often just feel bad and do nothing, and that guilt cultures can be paralyzed by scrupulosity while actual harm continues.

The initiative can only fund a few lines of work, and I've been asked to defend a priority ordering to the board. So: which open conceptual or empirical questions in this area deserve the money first, and in what order? Candidate questions I've been kicking around include: whether the act/person distinction that supposedly separates guilt from shame survives scrutiny, or whether it's a folk distinction that dissolves on inspection; whether shame can be institutionally engineered to target conduct rather than identity (and whether the attempts that claim to do this — restorative justice, corporate accountability rituals, some religious confession practices — actually succeed at it or just relabel it); whether a culture's guilt/shame balance is something that can be deliberately shifted at all, or whether it's an emergent property that policy only pretends to steer; whether online public shaming is genuinely a continuation of older shame practices or a new thing with different dynamics; and whether the empirical claims about downstream behavior (recidivism, reparative action, defensiveness) are robust enough to ground policy, or still too thin. But I'm not attached to this list — if you think the most important open question is one I haven't named, say so.

For each question you prioritize, I want the reasoning, not just the ranking: what makes it upstream of the others, how tractable it is in three years for philosophers and empirical moral psychologists respectively, and what finding or argument would move it up or down the list. I'd also like you to flag which questions you'd actively deprioritize because settling them wouldn't change what the foundation should fund. Please take a definite position — a committed ordering with stated criteria (leverage, tractability, neglectedness, whatever you actually weight), rather than a menu of plausible options for me to choose from. And give me your honest verdict, as part of that, on the framing question itself: is "which emotion should a society cultivate" even a coherent policy target, or is that where the whole enterprise goes wrong?
