I'm a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course in political philosophy, and a student came to office hours last week with an argument she had written up after our unit on global justice. She is a committed cosmopolitan, and her argument is meant to establish that conclusion; my problem is that it appears to establish considerably more, and I have been unable to show her where it slips without also giving up things she is entitled to keep. Here is the argument as she presented it. (1) Every moral obligation is either voluntarily incurred — through a promise, a contract, or some other act of consent — or else an instance of a general duty owed to everyone equally, such as the duty not to harm or the duty of easy rescue. (2) Alleged obligations to one's compatriots are not voluntarily incurred: nobody consents to being born a citizen of a particular state, and mere continued residence under a government one did not choose is not consent in the relevant sense. (3) General duties, precisely because they are owed to everyone equally, cannot justify treating compatriots better than foreigners; partial treatment requires a partial ground, and general duties supply none. (4) Therefore there are no special obligations to compatriots, and the priority that states give their own citizens in taxation, welfare, and immigration policy is unjustified favoritism. So far, so cosmopolitan. But then: (5) premises (1) through (3) apply with equal force to parents and siblings — nobody chooses their parents, and whatever duties one has to them are plainly not owed to everyone equally. (6) So there are no special obligations to one's parents either, and an adult who declines to support her aging parents and instead sends the money to highly effective overseas charities does nothing wrong. (7) That conclusion is absurd. My student, to her credit, bites the bullet on (6) and insists that filial duties really are optional supererogation. I am not willing to follow her there.

My own suspicion is that something has gone wrong by (3) at the latest, but each of the first three premises looks defensible when I stare at it individually, and I do not want to accuse her of an equivocation I cannot locate. So: which premise or step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, I would like your verdict on two escape routes I sketched and then abandoned. First, if (1) is false, then there must be some third source of obligations — benefits received, perhaps, or special causal histories, or relationships one occupies regardless of consent — but it seems that any such source generous enough to ground filial duties will also ground at least some obligations to compatriots, since citizens receive substantial benefits from their states; does rejecting (1) therefore surrender the cosmopolitan conclusion (4) along with the absurd one (6)? Second, if instead one keeps (1) and argues that an adult's ongoing relationship with her parents involves a kind of tacit voluntary endorsement that mere childhood did not, it is hard to see why the citizen who does not emigrate, votes, and uses the roads has not done the very same thing. I would strongly prefer to keep something close to (4) — at least a considerably deflated account of what we owe compatriots — while rejecting (6). Tell me whether that combination is actually available, which premise I have to give up to get it, and what I am paying.
