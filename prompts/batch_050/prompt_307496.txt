I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics, and I've been stuck on Gödel's argument from rotating universes for the ideality of time. Reconstructed as cleanly as I can manage, it seems to prove too much, and I'd like a diagnosis. The argument:

(1) If the lapse of time is objective — if events genuinely come to be and pass away rather than being tenselessly arrayed in a four-dimensional block — then this cannot depend on the contingent distribution of matter. Whether reality itself passes cannot hinge on how much dust happens to be rotating, and where.

(2) General relativity admits solutions describing rotating universes in which no global time function can be defined: there is no cosmic now, no way of slicing such a spacetime into a linear succession of world-states, and some worldlines even loop back toward the events they began from.

(3) Where no global succession of world-states exists, time cannot objectively lapse, since lapse just is the successive coming-to-be of such states.

(4) So in the rotating universes, the lapse of time is not objective but ideal — a feature of experience, not of the world.

(5) Our universe is governed by the same laws as the rotating universes; the difference lies only in the contingent distribution of matter.

(6) By (1), if lapse is ideal there, it is ideal here as well.

(7) Therefore time is ideal: the passage we seem to experience is not a feature of reality in any universe governed by general relativity, ours included.

I cannot accept (7), but each step looks locally defensible and I keep losing the thread when I try to say which one gives. I find (1) the most compelling premise — it strikes me as the heart of Gödel's insight — so I'd be glad to keep it, but I want the honest answer rather than the comfortable one.
