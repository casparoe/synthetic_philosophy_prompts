I host a long-form podcast about science and ideas, and I spent much of last year reporting a story that began as maritime heritage and ended, to my surprise, as metaphysics. A preservation trust spent three decades restoring a nineteenth-century pilot cutter, replacing her timbers one at a time as they rotted, until no original plank remained in the working vessel. What my editors didn't know when they assigned the piece is that a regional maritime museum two hundred miles away had quietly been buying up the discarded timbers for years, and has now reassembled them, in their original arrangement, into what it insists is the authentic ship. Both institutions claim the same vessel; a national heritage register, and at least one insurer, are being asked to decide which claimant is right. Listeners will recognize this as Hobbes's later sharpening of the puzzle Plutarch records — and Plutarch's telling is how I plan to open the episode:

"The ship wherein Theseus and the youth of Athens returned from Crete had thirty oars, and was preserved by the Athenians down even to the time of Demetrius Phalereus, for they took away the old planks as they decayed, putting in new and strong timber in their places, insomuch that this ship became a standing example among the philosophers, for the logical question of things that grow; one side holding that the ship remained the same, and the other contending that it was not the same."

— Plutarch, Life of Theseus

My guest is a metaphysician whose recent book defends constitutionalism about persistence: roughly, the view that a ship and the matter currently composing it are two distinct objects occupying the same place, and that the ship survives even the complete, gradual replacement of its parts because identity over time tracks continuity of form and organization rather than sameness of stuff. Draft eight to ten interview questions that do more than get her to restate this. I want questions that press where the view is actually pressed: force her to deliver a verdict on my two claimants and to explain what entitles the working vessel to the name that the reassembled timbers lack; ask how the ship and its timber can be two things rather than one when they share every part, and what grounds the difference between them; ask whether there was some particular plank-swap at which the ship would have ceased to exist had the repairs gone differently, and what she makes of there being no clean answer; and take seriously Plutarch's own framing — a logical question of things that grow — by asking whether her account of artifacts carries over to organisms, and if so what it implies about the persistence of persons, since my listeners will forgive nothing on that front. End with a question about what would move her: is the heritage register's dilemma a genuine metaphysical dispute with a right answer, or a verbal standoff that law and convention should settle — and if the latter, why write a book defending one side? For each question, give the follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. If any question as I've framed it misstates her position or smuggles in a premise a careful constitutionalist would reject, say so plainly rather than drafting politely around it. The whole set — questions, follow-ups, and any such caveats — must come to no more than 700 words, excluding the passage above.
