I'm in a reading group on philosophy of biology and we got completely stuck last week on the question of what counts as a single organism versus a bunch of organisms cooperating. So let me just dump my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and ask whether there's any coherent principle behind them. A lichen (fungus plus algae, neither can live apart in the wild) feels to me like ONE organism, not a partnership. A Portuguese man o' war, where the zooids are genetically identical and most can't survive or reproduce on their own, definitely one organism. A human plus their gut microbiome, where the bacteria are genetically diverse, come and go, and plenty live fine outside us: NOT one organism, that's an ecosystem, and I roll my eyes at "holobiont" talk here. An ant colony, where the ants are close relatives and only the queen reproduces: I want to say "superorganism" is just a metaphor and the real organisms are the individual ants. Pando, the giant aspen clone that is thousands of genetically identical stems sharing one root system: I actually think that IS one organism, even though you could cut it into pieces and each piece would live. And Dictyostelium slime mold, where genetically unrelated amoebae aggregate and a chunk of them sacrifice themselves as the stalk: not one organism, because there's obvious conflict and cheating going on inside it.

The pair I can't reconcile is the lichen versus the ant colony. If metabolic integration and mutual dependence make the lichen one organism, the ant colony has a decent amount of that too (trophallaxis, caste specialization, nobody but the queen and males reproducing), so why isn't the colony one? And if genetic homogeneity plus a single reproductive bottleneck is what does the work, then lichens should come apart into two organisms and my man o' war verdict survives, but then Pando and the man o' war are fine while the slime mold verdict is doing work by the "cheating" observation, which feels like a different criterion sneaking in. Also note my microbiome verdict seems to rest on genetics AND on the bacteria being facultative, and I can't tell which.

So: is there one principle (or a principled small set) that delivers all or most of these verdicts? Give me your best candidate stated precisely, say which of my verdicts it saves and which it has to overturn, and tell me what it predicts for two cases I didn't list: a coral head with its zooxanthellae, and Volvox. Please be upfront about how confident you are overall, and single out the one verdict of mine (or one claim of yours) that you're least sure about.
