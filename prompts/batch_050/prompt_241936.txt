Take the decisions of Western governments in March–April 2020 to impose sweeping lockdowns despite deep uncertainty about the coronavirus's fatality rate, transmissibility, and the economic and social costs of the measures themselves. Analyze this episode using the framework of decision under ignorance: were lockdowns a maximin choice (guard against the worst outcome, an uncontrolled health-system collapse), a minimax-regret choice (the regret of locking down unnecessarily being judged smaller than the regret of not locking down and being wrong), or an expected-utility calculation dressed up in precautionary language? Then run the analysis in the other direction. Rawlsian readings of the episode say policymakers effectively chose behind a partial veil of ignorance, prioritizing the worst-off — the elderly and immunocompromised — over aggregate welfare; Harsanyi-style critics say the aggregate case was there anyway. Which does the actual pattern of decisions support, given that governments protected hospital capacity more reliably than nursing homes, and that some of the worst-off under lockdown (isolated elderly, children out of school) were the same people the maximin rationale claimed to protect? Finally, what does the episode reveal about the framework itself: does it show that maximin and minimax regret diverge when the worst outcomes and the most regrettable errors come apart, that public decision-making under ignorance is really minimax regret all the way down because regret is what is politically survivable, or that the framework is silent where it matters most — over who gets to fix the outcome table?
