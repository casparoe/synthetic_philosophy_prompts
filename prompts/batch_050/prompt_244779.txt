I'm TAing a grad seminar on diachronic rationality and I have a pile of these to grade tonight, could you help me calibrate on one? The exam question was:

"Money pump arguments are often taken to show that cyclic preferences are irrational. Explain how such an argument goes, step by step. Then assess: does the argument assume the agent can't see the sequence of trades coming? If a sophisticated agent foresees the pump, does the argument collapse?"

Here's one student's answer:

"A money pump argument works by showing that an agent with cyclic preferences can be exploited into a guaranteed loss. Say the agent strictly prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. Give them C. Since they prefer B to C, they'll pay a small fee to swap C for B. Since they prefer A to B, they'll pay to swap B for A. Since they prefer C to A, they'll pay to swap back to C. They end up exactly where they started but three fees poorer, and the cycle can repeat indefinitely. Since the final state is strictly worse than the starting state — same option, less money — the agent has been dominated, which shows the preferences were irrational. This is essentially the same reasoning as the Dutch book argument, which shows that inconsistent preferences lead to sure losses.

As for foresight: the argument clearly depends on the agent not seeing the pump coming. A sophisticated agent who recognizes the sequence will just refuse the first trade, or better, form a plan at the outset and stick to it. Either way they avoid the loss. So the money pump only shows that cyclic preferences are imprudent for agents who lack foresight; it's an argument about practical vulnerability, not about the rationality of the preferences themselves, and for sufficiently sophisticated agents it collapses entirely."

Give it a grade (out of 20) and write the comments I'd put on it — point by point, what holds up and what doesn't, including anything that's subtly off rather than obviously wrong, so I can use it to benchmark the rest of the pile.
