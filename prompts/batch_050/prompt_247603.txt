And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"Strawson's Basic Argument runs as follows. When you act, you act the way you do because of the way you are -- your character, motives, and general mental makeup. So if you're going to be truly responsible for what you do, you have to be responsible for the way you are, at least in the relevant mental respects. But to be responsible for the way you are, you'd have had to have intentionally brought it about that you are that way, and you can only have done that through some earlier choice, which itself issued from the way you already were at that time. So to be responsible for that earlier choice you'd need an even earlier act of self-formation, and so on. The regress can't be terminated, since genuine self-creation, being causa sui in the relevant sense, is impossible. Hence no one is ever truly responsible for what they do.

The argument targets what Strawson calls ultimate moral responsibility, the kind of deep responsibility that would make it truly just to punish or reward someone, as opposed to the shallower everyday sense in which we hold people accountable. Strawson's point is essentially a determinist one: since our character is fixed by heredity and environment, factors entirely outside our control, we can't be responsible for it, and so can't be responsible for what flows from it. On an indeterminist picture, by contrast, there is room for the agent to intervene in this causal chain, so the regress can be broken and responsibility secured, which Strawson grants. He concludes only that determinism, if true, rules out responsibility.

It's worth noting that Strawson also thinks we plainly have no control over our character in any sense, and that compatibilists are therefore simply changing the subject when they offer weaker accounts of responsibility."
