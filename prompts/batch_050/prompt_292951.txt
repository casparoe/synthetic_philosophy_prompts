I am a novelist at work on a piece of fiction and I would like you to write the short story at the centre of it for me. The premise: a retired interpreter, who spent her career at diplomatic summits deciding when a foreign delegation's words could be taken at face value and when they were merely moves in a game, is hired by a tech company to do the same job for a new language model. The company's lawyers need to know, in writing, whether the system "believed" the false statement it made to a user, because belief would mean liability where mere malfunction would not.

The story should dramatise the philosophical problem rather than lecture about it: when we say a system believes something, are we describing a fact about its inner workings, or adopting a stance towards it because treating it as a believer lets us predict what it will do? The interpreter, drawing on her old trade, notices that the question she answered about human negotiators was never quite the factual question either — yet she also notices a difference that unsettles her: with the diplomats, she could be wrong in a way that mattered to them. Explore whether that asymmetry settles anything, or only makes the stance feel more comfortable to take with humans.

Constraints: around 1,500 to 2,000 words; no character may deliver more than two consecutive sentences of explicit philosophy; at least one scene should turn on a concrete behavioural test the interpreter devises, and the test should fail to be decisive in an interesting way; the ending should leave unresolved whether her written verdict was true, while making clear what it cost her to sign it. Write in the close third person, and in British English.
