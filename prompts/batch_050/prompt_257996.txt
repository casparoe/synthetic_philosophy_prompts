I'm taking a philosophy of mind seminar, and this week we're working through eliminativist and deflationary arguments about phenomenal consciousness. One that I've been struggling to pin down is the argument associated with Dennett's two fictional coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn, which is supposed to show that qualia, as standardly defined, are not a coherent posit. I'd like you to explain the argument to me, but at a deeper level than the summary in our textbook.

Here's my understanding of the setup, so we're on the same page. Both men used to love a particular brand of coffee and now find it unpleasant. Chase believes the coffee tastes exactly as it always did, and that what changed are his reactions: the taste itself is constant, but he has soured on it. Sanborn believes his tastes (his preferences) are unchanged, and that what changed is how the coffee tastes to him: the very flavor is different now. The twist is that there is no way, even in principle, for either man to determine which description is correct, because any evidence they might consult -- memories of past tastings, current reactions, physiological tests -- could be explained by either a change in the experience or a change in the judgments, standards, or memories brought to bear on the experience.

What I want explained is the step from this epistemic point to the stronger metaphysical conclusion. I can see why Chase and Sanborn can't know which story is right. But the argument seems to conclude that there is no fact of the matter at all -- that the question "did your qualia change, or did your relation to your qualia change?" is empty -- and from there that the ordinary conception of qualia (intrinsic, ineffable, directly apprehensible, privately known properties of experience) is incoherent rather than merely hard to verify. What exactly licenses that move? Is it a verificationist premise, and if so, does the argument stand or fall with verificationism? Or is there a subtler route: for instance, that the very idea of reidentifying a quale across time depends on memory, memory is revisable in ways that can't be checked against the quale itself, and so the notion of "the same quale again" was never doing real work to begin with?

Relatedly, I'd like you to address what seems to me the strongest response available to the qualia realist: that Dennett is simply collapsing a real distinction between an experience and one's judgments or beliefs about that experience. On this response, Chase and Sanborn just show that our access to qualia is fallible, not that there are no qualia -- the same way my fallible memory of a painting doesn't show the painting never existed. Where exactly does this response push back on the argument, and does it succeed? If it doesn't, is that because qualia are supposed to be defined as that which is directly and incorrigibly known, so that conceding fallible access amounts to giving up the notion being defended?

Finally, connect this to a puzzle I came up with while studying: suppose future neuroscience gave us a device that could read off a subject's brain state and compare it, neuron for neuron, with that same subject's brain state years earlier during a previous tasting. Would such a device settle the Chase/Sanborn question, or would it just push the indeterminacy one level up (since we'd still need to know which brain states realize the experience and which realize the judgments about it)? What does your answer to that reveal about what the argument is really targeting?

Please aim for the logical structure of the argument -- its premises, its hidden assumptions, and the exact point at which qualia realists and deflationists part company -- rather than a survey of the literature around it.
