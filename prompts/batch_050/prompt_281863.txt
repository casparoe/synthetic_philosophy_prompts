Write a dialogue between two philosophers of statistics, Ada and Benno, debating whether Bayesian convergence results vindicate the objectivity of scientific inference. Ada's position: when two agents start with different prior distributions but update on a growing shared stream of evidence, their predictive distributions merge almost surely, provided neither assigned probability zero to anything the other treats as a live possibility — so priors wash out, and honest disagreement is temporary. Benno's position: the result requires the agents to agree on the likelihoods and on what the evidence stream even consists of, which is exactly where substantive scientific disagreement lives; it governs predictions about observables rather than beliefs about unobservable theoretical entities; and it is purely asymptotic, while all actual inference happens on finite data where the ratio of priors can dominate indefinitely. Have them test the dispute against at least two contrasting cases — for instance, two agents estimating the bias of a coin versus two cosmologists with different priors over rival dark matter theories confronting the same sky surveys — and have them address whether slow convergence is still convergence and what, if anything, the theorems guarantee when likelihoods themselves are contested. Both characters must be equally competent: neither may be a caricature, each should respond to the other's actual arguments rather than restate their opening position, and each should concede at least one genuine point. They need not reach agreement, but by the end they should have located precisely where their disagreement lies.
