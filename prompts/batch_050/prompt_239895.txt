I'm a PhD student in philosophy of language working near the pragmatics literature, and I've noticed a conversational pattern I can't locate precisely in what I've read. Twice this month I've watched it happen: in a department meeting, a colleague said something like "given that we've settled on reducing admissions, we should discuss how," when no such thing had been settled; nobody objected, because objecting would have meant derailing the agenda, and twenty minutes later the chair referred to the reduction as "our earlier decision." In a reading group, a participant criticized a paper for "failing to justify its controversial premise," the premise had in fact been quietly assumed by the speaker's own framing minutes earlier, and by the end of the session everyone treated it as a shared commitment. In both cases a presupposed content passes unchallenged for purely conversational reasons, and the silence is later retroactively read as uptake or agreement. I know accommodation covers the hearer's charitable adjustment of the common ground, but here the hearers may not accept the content at all — they just judge it not worth a derailment — and the speaker, or the record, later converts that strategic silence into established common ground. Does this specific configuration have an established name, and how does it differ from ordinary accommodation, from merely informative presupposition, and from outright manipulation? I'd like the carving, not just a label: what the phenomenon is, where its edges are, and whether silence-after-presupposition should count as endorsement on standard scorekeeping models.
