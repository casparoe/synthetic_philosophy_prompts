Alright, some background first: I spent forty years in experimental physics and only picked up archaeology and philosophy of science in retirement, so forgive me if I use the wrong terms. I've been reading a lot about how archaeologists get from potsherds and postholes to what people believed and how they were organized, and I've written down where I've landed. I'd like someone to check whether the package actually holds together or whether I've walked into a contradiction.

Here's roughly what I think:

(1) Deliberate grave goods -- objects placed with the dead that took real effort to make or get -- are decent evidence that the burying community had beliefs about death, something like continued existence or continued status of the dead. Not a specific theology, but something.

(2) When an excavator can't figure out what a structure or object was for and calls it "ritual" or "cultic," that's usually a confession of ignorance dressed up as a finding. It explains nothing and couldn't be wrong, so it isn't really an inference at all.

(3) You can't get from an artifact to a belief without leaning on analogy with documented cultures, and those analogies are unreliable because human practices vary enormously across time and place. In physics we trust an inference when the instrument is calibrated against known cases; here the "instrument" never is.

(4) Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Finding no temples or writing tells you little about whether a society had priests or record-keeping, given how much simply doesn't survive.

(5) From standardized weights and measures spread over a wide area, you can confidently infer some kind of coordinating authority existed, even if you can't say what form it took.

Now, (1) feels solid to me and (2) feels like me being appropriately hard-nosed, but I have a nagging feeling they might be in more trouble together than I want to admit, and I'm not sure (3) lets me keep everything else. On the other hand I suspect some pairs that look inconsistent actually aren't once you think about it. Go through the set: which commitments genuinely pull against each other and how badly, which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and what's the cheapest single revision that fixes the most -- including what it would cost me. Please rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. Keep the whole thing under 500 words; I'm a slow reader these days.
