I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long-form magazine piece about the philosophy of mind uploading. I want to bracket the engineering question entirely: assume, for the sake of the piece, that scanning and simulating a brain works perfectly. What interests me is the conceptual terrain that remains — whether the result is a continuation of the person, a mere copy, or something stranger.

The debates I could structure the piece around include these five. First, gradual neuron-by-neuron replacement with functionally identical artificial components: does the person survive, and does it matter what happens to the displaced tissue — say, if it's kept alive and reassembled elsewhere? Second, the fading-qualia worry: even if behavior and report are unchanged throughout gradual replacement, consciousness might dim or wink out partway through, and no one, including the subject, could tell from the inside. Third, the non-destructive scan: the upload runs while the biological original is still alive, producing two apparent continuers, and the question of which one, if either, is the person. Fourth, whether personal identity is even the right thing to care about, as against psychological continuity in any number of branches. Fifth, the substrate question: whether a simulation reproduces the causal dynamics consciousness depends on, or only their abstract input-output organization, and whether that distinction is even coherent.

Which of these should anchor the piece, and in what order? The audience is educated general readers; the budget is about 8,000 words; the goal is to leave readers genuinely uncertain about the right answer rather than reassured in either direction. Defend the ranking: which questions are upstream of which (does the fading-qualia worry collapse into the substrate question, or is it independent?), which are most likely to shift a reader's considered judgment rather than provoke a gut reaction they'll discount, and which I should demote to a sidebar as overrated. If there's a question I've left out that belongs at the top, say so and rank it.

Please format the response as exactly five numbered items, one per question in your ranking, each a sentence or two naming the question and giving the reason for its position — no introduction, no conclusion.
