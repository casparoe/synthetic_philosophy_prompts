I'm a PhD student in phil mind (dissertation on the epistemology of perception, roughly), and I recently joined an interdepartmental reading group where a friend from the cog sci program shared the notes he's using to teach himself Sellars for his comprehensive exam. His summary of the "myth of the given" in Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind goes like this:

"Sellars's target is the given: the idea that there are facts about our own experience — that we're seeing something red, that we're in pain — which we can know directly, without needing any concepts or background knowledge, and which then serve as the foundation on which the rest of what we know gets built. His main exhibit is the sense datum theory, where we're directly acquainted with private inner items and everything else is inferred from them. Sellars argues this whole picture is a myth: nothing counts as knowing something unless it's placed within a network of concepts, and concepts have to be learned, so there can't be pre-conceptual awareness that justifies anything. Since there's no foundation, knowledge must instead be a matter of coherence — beliefs are justified by their relations to other beliefs, full stop. Observation reports like 'this is red' are really just very fast, unconscious inferences from raw sensation. And because sensations on their own carry no epistemic weight, the qualitative character of experience — the 'what it's like' — turns out to be irrelevant to epistemology, a leftover of the myth that serious philosophy of mind can dispense with."

Then at the end of the notes he draws a conclusion: since phenomenal character has no evidential role, anyone (and he's looking at me here) who wants to rest perceptual justification partly on how things phenomenally seem is just re-running the myth in a new costume.

Something in there rubs me the wrong way. I thought Sellars still allowed that some knowledge is non-inferential, and that he was actually pretty careful to keep a place for sensations themselves somewhere in his overall picture — but he's the one who's read the paper cover to cover and I've mostly recieved it secondhand through the McDowell/Brandom crowd. Which parts of his summary are accurate, which are standard-but-contested readings, and what would Sellars himself push back on hardest? And once you've corrected it: does his parting shot at phenomenal-seemings accounts actually follow from the myth-of-the-given argument, or is he leaning on exactly the parts of the summary that don't survive scrutiny? If it's relevant, I'm at least somewhat sympathetic to the view he's attacking, so feel free to flag it if my suspicion of the summary is just motivated reasoning on my end.
