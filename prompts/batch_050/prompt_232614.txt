After a forty-year career as an experimental chemist, I have spent my retirement reading moral philosophy, and I have recently been working through Wang Yangming. His doctrine of the unity of knowledge and action strikes me as a radical but promising solution to a problem familiar from the laboratory: people who can recite a protocol flawlessly yet fail to follow it at the bench. As I understand Wang, he holds that someone who does not act on the good does not truly know it — that knowledge and action are two names for a single reality, not two stages of a process. If that is right, then every moral failure is at bottom an epistemic failure, and the will drops out of the picture entirely: there is no gap between knowing and doing in which weakness of will could operate.

My question is practical. On Wang's account, moral education would seem to be re-conceivable as a pure knowledge-transmission problem: once genuine knowledge is installed, right action follows of itself, so habituation, exemplars, ritual practice, and the disciplining of one's resolve become superfluous — at best a stopgap for those whose knowledge is not yet complete. What, then, would be the most efficient method of transmitting moral knowledge on his view — lecture, dialectic, something closer to our scientific pedagogy? And can his position survive the modern evidence that a great deal of human behavior is driven by habit and environment rather than by what people know, so that two agents with identical knowledge routinely act differently?
