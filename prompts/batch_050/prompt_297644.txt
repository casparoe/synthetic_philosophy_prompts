So I moved apartments last month and my landlord, who never once inspected the place in four years, suddenly discovered a bunch of "pre-existing damage" the day I handed back the keys and kept half my security deposit. Me and my roommate were arguing about it and I started noticing that basically every long-term arrangement has some weird machinery specifically for how it ends: landlords hold deposits until after you've moved out, freelancers complain that the final invoice is always the one that never gets paid, construction contracts apparently hold back a chunk of "retention" money right until the end, employers want two weeks notice and dangle severance. And the strange thing is that during the relationship itself, everyone mostly just cooperates on pure trust — nobody checks anything for years. It's like cooperation is easy until somebody can see the finish line, and then both sides need handcuffs.

I've been reading a bit of game theory on my own — the centipede game and the backward induction stuff — and it seems like this whole apparatus presupposes something pretty specific: that if two rational people both know exactly when their interaction ends, cooperation unravels from the back. I'd stiff you on the last move, you know that, so you stiff me on the second-to-last move, and so on all the way down until there's no cooperation left. Deposits, escrow, milestones and notice periods all look like attempts to break that chain by making sure there's never a clean "final move" where someone can defect with impunity. But I'm not convinced that's the only theory the practice fits. Maybe it doesn't presuppose that people are cold backward-inductors at all — maybe it just presupposes that people get tempted at endings, or that trust works fine as long as nobody starts counting remaining interactions. Or maybe these institutions are just vestiges of lawsuits being expensive and don't encode any theory whatsoever.
