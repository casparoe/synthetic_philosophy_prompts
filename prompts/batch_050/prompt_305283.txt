So a few months ago I got into a dumb argument with my brother-in-law about the Parthenon Marbles (the ones Lord Elgin shipped off to London in the early 1800s, now in the British Museum, which Greece has been trying to get back basically forever) and I've been lowkey obsessing over it since. I have zero background in any of this, I work in logistics, but I ended up inventing my own little framework for when a museum should have to give something back, and I want to stress-test it. My test has three questions. One: would taking the object, the way it was taken, be considered acceptable if it happend today? Two: is there a living community right now for whom the object is part of who they are, not just something pretty to look at? Three: can the people asking for it back actually take care of it and keep it accessible to the public? My thinking was that if an object flunks question one but passes two and three, it should go back. Seemed airtight to me for like a week.

Here's where it got messy. I tried my test on the Benin Bronzes first — looted by British troops in 1897 from the Kingdom of Benin, in what's now Nigeria, and a bunch of museums (Germany, the Smithsonian, I think a museum in London too) have actually started handing them back in the last few years. Easy call by my framework: flunks one, passes two and three. But then I ran the Parthenon Marbles through it and it kind of jammed. Question one seems like a clear flunk, though I know people argue Elgin had some permission slip from the Ottoman authorities and other people say that's murky at best, so maybe don't hang everything on that detail. The real problem is question two: who's the living community? Modern Greece is a nation-state, not ancient Athens, and nobody worships Athena there anymore, so is the connection identity-constituting or is it more like really intense national pride? And whatever my test spits out there, it seems to say something weird, like either the Marbles should stay (which feels wrong given question one) or my test is too strict and tons of stuff in every big museum has to go home, which is the whole "floodgates" argument people make.

So what I'd love is a proper analysis: run both cases through my three-question test carefully, tell me where the test gives clear verdicts and where it just goes silent or contradicts itself, and whether the Parthenon case is actually a counterexample to the test or whether the test is correctly showing me the case is genuinely harder than the Benin one. Also, if the verdicts come out different for the two cases, I want to know whether that difference tracks something morally real or just vibes and politics (like, does it matter that Nigeria had a functioning institution ready to receive the bronzes while Greece is an EU country that clearly has museums?). And be honest about what a framework like mine can't settle at all — like the legal stuff, since I gather there's some British law from the 60s that blocks the museum from giving things away even if it wants to. Also please answer in American English, the British spelling in all the articles I've been reading is doing my head in.
