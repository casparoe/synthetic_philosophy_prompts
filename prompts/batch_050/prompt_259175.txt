Write a deadpan corporate white paper, around 600 words, from the marketing department of a firm called Knowledge, Inc., which sells Knowledge as a premium epistemic product and is responding to a price challenge from a cut-rate competitor, Generic True Belief Co. The white paper must justify Knowledge's price premium without ever conceding that a customer holding a true belief gets exactly where they want to go -- on any route, including the one to Larissa -- just as reliably as a customer holding knowledge. Structure it like an honest competitor comparison: product specifications, a recall history, a quality-assurance section, and a closing appeal to discerning consumers.

The humor must come from the actual literature on the Meno problem, exaggerating real moves rather than inventing ones. Among the material to work in, deadpan: the old worry that true beliefs are like Daedalus's statues, beautiful but apt to wander off unless tied down with an account (render this as an anchoring accessory, sold separately, which Generic True Belief Co. does not stock); a proud manufacturing-specs section advertising Knowledge's production process as reliably truth-conducive, with the awkward admission -- buried in a footnote -- that a competitor has argued reliability adds no value once truth is already in the box, since a working espresso machine and a lucky one produce equally hot coffee, and that this objection is known in the trade as the swamping problem; a recall notice for a batch of 1963 units found to be justified, true, and believed, yet somehow still not knowledge, with sincere apologies and a note that the defect pattern resists a general fix; and a boutique line of Knowledge hand-crafted by agents of intellectual virtue, priced to reflect that the knower deserves credit for getting things right. The white paper should never state outright that its marketing problem is the philosophical problem; a specialist reader should recognize every standard position in the genre of the joke.

The piece should stay deadpan throughout, with no winking at the reader. Where the satire leans on a contested reading -- for instance, it will be tempting to present the swamping objection as decisive against reliabilism, or the credit approach as the industry's settled answer -- the jokes should be arranged so that no contested view is presented as consensus. After the piece, add a short author's note, around 100 words and outside the satirical voice, flagging which of the positions the piece draws on are genuinely contested among epistemologists (the force of the swamping problem, whether virtue epistemology dissolves or relocates the value problem, whether the Gettier diagnosis itself is stable) and which are broadly settled (that the original tripartite analysis needed revision), so that the satire's liberties are on the record.
