I'm a high school student working on the capstone essay for my philosophy elective, which is on the situationist critique of virtue ethics. Instead of writing yet another paper arguing for one side, I want to produce the deliverable of an adversarial collaboration that never actually happened: the joint statement that a committed Aristotelian virtue ethicist and a committed situationist social psychologist could both sign after months of good-faith work together. Please draft that statement for me.

By the virtue ethicist, I mean someone who holds that robust, cross-situationally stable character traits — compassion, honesty, courage — are genuine psychological possessions of at least some people, that they explain and predict conduct, and that moral education should centrally aim at instilling them. By the situationist, I mean someone who takes the experimental record — Milgram's obedience studies, Darley and Batson's Good Samaritan experiment, and the findings that trivial variables like finding a coin in a phone booth or the smell of fresh baking measurably shift helping behavior — to show that global character traits are at best rare and at worst fictitious, and that situational features, not character, do the real causal work.

The statement should have three parts:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. These should be substantive, not platitudes. They should cover what both sides accept the classic experiments actually demonstrated; which popular glosses on those experiments both sides would reject; what each side is willing to concede (for instance, the virtue ethicist conceding that single-situation correlations between trait measures and behavior are low, the situationist conceding that the classic studies used extreme or unusual circumstances); and anything they agree on about moral education in light of the evidence.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should give the issue, the virtue ethicist's position worded so the virtue ethicist would sign it, the situationist's position worded so the situationist would sign it, and a note on whether the disagreement is empirical, conceptual, or normative. Please handle the following standard virtue-ethicist replies explicitly — judging each to be either a genuine defense, a retreat that concedes the situationist's main point, or a merely verbal maneuver — and where the two parties classify a reply differently, record that classification dispute as its own row: (a) virtues are rare, so experiments in which almost no one behaves virtuously do not threaten the view; (b) traits predict behavior in the aggregate across many occasions even when they fail to predict any single act; (c) a situation must be individuated by the agent's own construal and reasons rather than by the experimenter's physical description, so the experiments do not show what situationists claim; (d) the right lesson is a revision toward local, situation-specific traits or conditional if-then dispositions (roughly, being honest with friends but not under authority pressure) instead of global traits — plus the question of whether that revision still counts as virtue ethics.

3. Cruxes with pre-registered predictions. For each of two or three cruxes, describe a realistic, fundable study that both parties agree would bear on it — for example, an ecological momentary assessment study tracking ordinary people's honesty or helping across varied everyday contexts, or a longitudinal study testing whether childhood character ratings predict adult conduct under pressure. For each study, write down in advance the result the virtue ethicist expects and the result the situationist expects, concretely enough (in terms of rough patterns, not invented numbers) that a reader could tell afterward who had been right. At least one study must be one where the two sides' predictions genuinely diverge; where their predictions coincide, say so and explain what that implies about whether the crux is genuine.

Close with a short "What would change my mind" section, one paragraph per side, stating the single finding that would most force each party to revise its view.

The tone should be that of a real joint statement: measured, free of strawmen, and willing to say that the parties disagree about what an experiment shows where that is the honest summary. Around 1,200–1,800 words.
