I am an undergraduate taking an advanced seminar on probability and inductive inference. For the final project, I want to propose a small research agenda on the reference class problem rather than write another general survey of frequentism and subjectivism. Suppose a five-person student reading group has ten weeks and can pursue three connected projects. Which open questions should we prioritize, and in what order?

My current taxonomy is as follows:

1. The admissibility question: what makes a reference class epistemically legitimate, given that an individual belongs to indefinitely many classes? A satisfactory answer should avoid both arbitrary selection and the limiting case in which conditioning on everything known about the individual makes the relevant single-case probability trivially 0 or 1.

2. The relevance question: when statistical and causal considerations recommend different classes, which should govern? For example, a broad demographic class may support one estimate while a narrower causal-mechanism class supports another, even though the narrower class is less reliably estimated.

3. The aggregation question: when several overlapping and nonnested classes all appear relevant, should their probabilities be combined, ranked, or treated as evidence that no single probability is appropriate? I am especially interested in whether model averaging merely postpones the problem by introducing a reference class of models.

4. The subject-relativity question: does the correct class depend on the agent’s evidence, purposes, or decision stakes, or is there an agent-independent fact of the matter? If it is purpose-relative, it is unclear whether disagreement about probabilities can still be objectively criticized.

5. The diagnostic question: in applications such as medical prognosis, legal proof, and algorithmic risk assessment, does the reference class problem undermine single-case probabilities themselves, or only our methods for estimating them? Perhaps the practical problem is sometimes one of data sparsity rather than indeterminacy.

Please rank the three projects that would be most valuable for this group and defend the ordering using explicit criteria: how upstream each question is, how tractable it is without advanced technical training, how much it matters outside academic philosophy, and whether progress would solve the problem or merely relocate it. Identify at least one tempting project that you would deprioritize despite its initial appeal. Where possible, give a small example that would reveal progress or show that a proposed criterion fails.

If this taxonomy conflates genuinely different problems, or if asking for a ranking before settling the metaphysics of probability is confused, please say so directly rather than politely forcing an answer onto my framework. In that case, reorganize the agenda and explain what the more fundamental division is.
