I am preparing a handout for a graduate epistemology workshop on the regress problem, and I want it to take the form of a decision tree rather than a survey chapter. Please act as a professional epistemologist with expertise in the structure of justification, and think carefully about the logical space of positions before assigning any labels — I would like the tree built from the questions themselves, with named theories located in the space only afterward, not the other way around.

The tree should start from the shared commitment that generates the problem: a belief is justified only if there is something like a chain of reasons supporting it, and each link of that chain itself stands in need of support. From there, every branch node should be a single diagnostic question on which the positions genuinely come apart, phrased so that a reader can answer it without already knowing which theory it leads to. The questions I have in mind concern things like: whether a chain of reasons may terminate at all, and if so whether the endpoint must itself be justified and by what; whether justification may travel in a circle, and whether the size or structure of the circle matters; whether a finite mind can possess infinitely many non-repeating reasons, and whether possessing them requires being able to produce them on demand; and whether the subject's own perspective is what does the justifying or whether the reasons need only exist or obtain. Feel free to reformulate these and to add nodes I have missed, but keep each node answerable by someone who has never heard the vocabulary of the debate — use concrete setups where it helps, such as a person pressed to keep answering "and why do you believe that?", or a subject whose belief-forming process is reliable without her knowing it.

The leaves should include at least the following, and you may add others if the space requires them: internalist foundationalism with non-inferentially justified basic beliefs; externalist or reliabilist foundationalism, where the chain ends in something that is reliable rather than justified; coherentism, where the regress is absorbed into a sufficiently large and coherent web; infinitism of the sort Peter Klein defends, where the chain is infinite and non-repeating; and the skeptical outcome associated with the ancient regress argument, where no belief is justified. For any combination of answers on your tree that no one occupies, mark it as unoccupied and say in a sentence why — whether the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or apparently unexplored. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any question you are willing to put on the tree (I suspect, for instance, that modest foundationalism and certain forms of contextualism may resist separation), flag the pair explicitly and explain what further commitment would split them.

The finished tree should have roughly eight to twelve branch nodes and six to eight leaves. Present it in whatever format is clearest — indented outline, flowchart in text, or numbered branch instructions are all fine. Finally, since this handout will shape how my students first encounter the debate, I want to know where to be careful: state how confident you are in the tree's overall architecture, and single out the one node, leaf, or classification you are least sure of, with a brief note on what would change your mind.
