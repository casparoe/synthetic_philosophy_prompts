Last Saturday I spent most of a long dinner arguing with a close friend, a historian of empire, about the final chapter of John Stuart Mill's Considerations on Representative Government (1861), the chapter titled "Of the Government of Dependencies by a Free State." I do not have the book in front of me, so I will paraphrase rather than quote the passage our quarrel kept returning to: late in the chapter, Mill argues that one people's rule over another is not government in the proper sense of the word unless it is conducted for the improvement of the subject people, and that, since the home electorate and its representatives cannot be trusted to conduct such rule disinterestedly or knowledgeably, a free state holding a dependency unlike itself in civilization ought to govern it through a permanent, professionally trained, and politically insulated administrative body rather than through ordinary representative politics at home. My friend's view was that this is all of a piece with Mill's system: a liberalism grounded in improvement predicts tutelary empire for peoples deemed unready, and there is nothing more to say. I defended the opposite reading, which is now the thesis of a paper I must present: that the chapter is self-undermining on Mill's own premises. His criterion of good government, laid out in the opening chapters of the same book, is the cultivation of the active, intellectual, and moral faculties of the governed; a paternal administration that does everything for a people is, by Mill's own argument there, precisely the arrangement most calculated to let those faculties atrophy; and the civilizational ranking that is meant to exempt India from this verdict is asserted in the chapter rather than established. On my reading, the machinery Mill recommends in the final chapter is condemned by the standard he sets in the first ones.

The occasion is our department's political theory colloquium, and I know the room fairly well. Two senior Mill scholars will attend, both of whom know the textual record cold, including Mill's long career at the East India Company and his official dispatches on Indian administration; they will not be impressed by a reading that treats the chapter as if Mill had written nothing else about India. A postcolonial theorist working in the liberalism-and-empire tradition will be there too, and I expect her to be the most dangerous questioner, though not necessarily hostile to my conclusion. A historian of British India has said he will come, and the rest will be graduate students, most of them inclined to agree with me, which I suspect will only make the real opposition sharper.

Please prepare me for the question period. I want a prep sheet, not a defense: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage each would do the paper, every one phrased the way its likely asker would put it -- the Mill scholars pressing me on chapter and verse and on the Company record, the postcolonial theorist on whether my reading treats empire as a contingent mistake rather than a constitutive feature of Mill's liberalism, the historian on how the Raj actually worked. For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in two minutes, a note on where that reply is soft, and what I should concede up front rather than fight over. The objections should engage the passage paraphrased above: whether "improvement" in the final chapter means something thinner than the faculty-cultivation standard of the early ones; whether the insulated-administration proposal is a merely prudential institutional claim that my argument cannot touch; and whether the self-undermining reading is an anachronistic charity that ends up defanging the stronger critique of Mill. Finally, tell me the single question I am hoping nobody asks, and why.
