so I was reading Cicero's On Old Age (the De Senectute) and one bit from section 22 got stuck in my head. Cato is arguing against the idea that old age dulls the mind, and he says:

"Old men retain their mental faculties, provided their interest and application continue; and this is true, not only of men in exalted public station, but likewise of those in the quiet of private life. Sophocles composed tragedies to extreme old age; and when, because of his absorption in literary work, he was thought to be neglecting his business affairs, his sons haled him into court in order to secure a verdict removing him from the control of his property on the ground of imbecility, under a law similar to ours, whereby it is customary to restrain heads of families from wasting their estates. Thereupon, it is said, the old man read to the jury his play, Oedipus at Colonus, which he had just written and was revising, and inquired: 'Does that poem seem to you to be the work of an imbecile?'"

Write a short story, 800 words max (the quote doesn't count), that tests Cato's claim instead of just illustrating it. Set it today: an old person whose adult kids are moving to get control of their affairs -- guardianship hearing, power of attorney, selling the house out from under them, whatever -- and the old person pulls a Sophocles, presenting their recent work as proof they're fine. The catch I want you to lean into: the Sophocles story only works because Oedipus at Colonus happens to be a masterpiece. "Does that seem like the work of an imbecile" settles something only if the work is good *and* the room can tell. So what if the recent work is fine but clearly below the person's peak? Or great but in a way nobody in the room is equipped to judge? Or what if proving you're still sharp isn't the same as proving you should keep the checkbook? You pick which gap to live in, but somebody in the story has to actually notice it. And don't let it resolve cleanly -- no greedy villain kids with a secretly spry grandpa, no tidy judge's verdict that explains the theme at the end. the interesting version of this story is murkier than either of those.
