I help run a small philosophy reading circle at our local library — none of us has any formal training, we just work through the classics slowly — and last month we read Plato's Meno. The part that stuck with me was Socrates questioning the uneducated slave boy about geometry and claiming that he was not teaching the boy anything but rather drawing out knowledge the boy already possessed inside him. I am an HVAC technician by trade, but in my spare time I follow the discussion around modern AI systems, and lately I have been turning over a worry that strikes me as a strange mirror of that scene. The worry, as I understand it, runs roughly like this: a language model trained to predict text and to produce answers people approve of may end up with an internal picture of the world that is more accurate than anything it says out loud, because the truthful answer is not always the answer that gets rewarded. People in that field therefore ask how one might elicit what such a system latently knows — to get it to report the world as it represents it, rather than the answer it calculates the questioner wants to hear. That is, it seems to me, precisely the problem Socrates faces with the boy: how to be sure you are drawing knowledge out rather than putting it in, given that several members of our group thought Socrates' questions looked awfully leading.

What I would like is a reasoned reconstruction of where Socrates would come down on this modern project of coaxing hidden knowledge out of an AI system. Some of the tensions I would want the answer to engage: whether his method of cross-examination — pressing an answerer with counterexamples until a claim either holds up or collapses — could work on a system that generates each reply fresh to suit whoever happens to be asking, and so may have no stable position to catch in a contradiction; whether he would think it matters where the latent knowledge came from, given that he traced the boy's to the soul's prior acquaintance with truth while the model's comes from a statistical digestion of human writing, much of it wrong; whether he would find a system trained never to flatly admit ignorance a better or worse interlocutor than the confident Athenians he embarrassed; and whether the suspicion that every elicitation technique is a leading question in disguise is as damaging to the AI project as some of our group thought it was to the Meno.

Please present your answer as exactly five numbered predictions, each one or two sentences long, each addressing a distinct aspect of how Socrates would view this enterprise, with no introduction and no conclusion.
