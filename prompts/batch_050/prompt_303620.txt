I'm prepping a unit for an undergrad seminar on minds and machines, and the week after next we're screening the Star Trek: TNG episode The Measure of a Man — the one where Commander Maddox wants to disassemble Data to figure out how to mass-produce him, Data refuses, and it ends up in a hearing over whether he's Starfleet property or a person with the right to say no. Most of my students come away thinking the episode settles the question in Data's favor, and I want to push back on that, or rather I want a sharp reading I can push back *with*.

Here's the specific lens I want you to read it through: the question isn't really "is Data conscious?" but "which computations matter morally, and why?" Maddox's standards — intelligence, self-awareness, consciousness — are all functional criteria, things about what a system does. But the hearing itself mostly runs on intuition pumps: Data's poker games, his keeping Tasha's hologram, the fact that Picard can make Guinan flinch by saying "disposable people." So read the episode as an argument about moral patiency for computational systems. Does the episode actually give us a reason to think Data's computations are the morally relevant kind, or does it just make us *feel* that they are, the way we feel a dog matters more than a Roomba? And notice the wrinkle nobody in the episode touches: Data is copyable. Maddox's whole plan is premised on that. If you could instantiate a thousand Datas, does each one get the vote at the hearing? If the moral weight comes from the computation, copying seems to multiply it, and that feels wrong — but if it comes from Data's relationships and history, then the episode's criteria do no work and it's really about him being our friend, which is a terrible basis for rights.

I don't want a balanced survey of what philosophers would say on each side. Pick a definite position: either the episode gets the right answer for roughly the right reasons, or it gets the right answer for the wrong reasons (or the wrong answer, if you can defend that), and tell me which scenes actually carry the load. Also tell me the strongest place where the episode resists your reading — where an honest critic has to admit the text doesn't cooperate — because I'd rather teach that tension than pretend it's not there. End with what the episode, as fiction, lets us see about machine moral status that the standard philosophy papers on the topic tend to miss.
