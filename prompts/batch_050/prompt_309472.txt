At my reading group, Maya and Dev got stuck on this sentence from Charles Darwin’s On the Origin of Species (1859), Chapter VII, “Instinct”: "No complex instinct can possibly be produced through natural selection, except by the slow and gradual accumulation of numerous, slight, yet profitable, variations."

Maya said the sentence quietly assumes the wrong unit: “profitable” has to mean profitable to the reproducing individual, because otherwise the slight variations can’t be preserved and accumulated. So sterile worker ants are a real problem, not a cute puzzle: a worker’s awesome defensive instinct benefits sisters and the queen, while the worker leaves no descendants to “preserve” anything. She said later kin-selection talk rescues Darwin only by changing the bookkeeping from organisms to genes, and group-selection talk rescues him only by risking mushy “the colony wanted it” explanations.

Dev pushed back. He said a colony can be the thing that varies and reproduces: colonies with better soldier ratios found more daughter colonies, so worker sterility is more like a body sacrificing skin cells than like selection favoring a dead end. Maya answered that this just moves the mystery up a level: what counts as a colony “offspring,” and how do you keep inside-colony cheating from wrecking the adaptation? Dev said that objection proves too much, because organisms also have cheating cells and cancer, yet we still talk about individual organisms as units.

They both stared at me like I had brought the Darwin line on purpose. Please adjudicate the actual moves: who is right about (a) what Darwin’s sentence commits him to, (b) whether sterile castes require genes/families/colonies rather than individual workers as the unit, and (c) whether Dev’s cancer reply really answers Maya’s cheating worry. Also say what both are missing about “profitable,” heredity, and reproduction needing to line up at the same level.
