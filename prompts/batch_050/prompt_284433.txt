For my early modern philosophy course, I am designing a revision handout on Berkeley’s master argument. Build a committed decision tree classifying responses to the challenge that any supposedly unperceived object becomes an object of thought in the very attempt to conceive it. Begin from this case: a sealed box contains either a bronze cube or nothing, having been filled by a machine whose record was immediately destroyed, so no one now knows which.

Each branch should be a question answerable without already knowing Berkeleyian terminology. Separate at least these issues: whether conceiving is imagining, referring to, or perceiving; whether being represented is an intrinsic change in the object; whether inability to conceive something shows that it is impossible or merely that it cannot be used as evidence; whether the argument concerns all objects or only sensible qualities; whether its conclusion is epistemic, semantic, or ontological; and whether appeal to divine perception changes the result.

Give eight to ten leaves with descriptive names, locating both Berkeley’s intended conclusion and replies based on an act-object distinction, imagining versus referring, selective attention, phenomenalism, an epistemic-only reading, and a theistic guarantee. Note any coherent answer-combinations that appear unoccupied, and flag any leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate. For every leaf, state in one sentence its most awkward commitment. Finally, choose the exact branch where you think the master argument fails—or, if you judge that it succeeds, identify the strongest rival leaf and explain why it still loses. Do not remain neutral.
