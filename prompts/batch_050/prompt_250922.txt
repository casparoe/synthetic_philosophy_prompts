I hold the view that moral testimony is epistemically unproblematic: if a trustworthy and competent friend tells me that some policy is unjust, and I have no special reason to think she is mistaken, then believing her can put me in a position to know that the policy is unjust -- much as believing a reliable colleague's report about a historical date can give me knowledge of that date. On my view, the widely felt queasiness about moral deference (the sense that someone who says "I oppose cruelty because my advisor told me it's wrong" is doing something defective) is either a confusion, a hangover from a demandingness ideal about moral understanding that applies far beyond testimony, or at most a pragmatic point about certain conversational contexts rather than an epistemic defect.

What would count against this position? I want a genuine map of its exposure, not a summary of the debate. In particular:

1. Distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would merely be awkward. For instance: is the bare fact that most people, in surveys and in conversation, treat moral deference as fishy while treating historical deference as fine a defeater for me, or just something my view must explain? Under what conditions would a persistent intuition-asymmetry between moral and non-moral testimony genuinely threaten the view rather than merely requiring a debunking story?

2. Consider the strongest versions of the rival (pessimism about moral testimony) and ask what my view would have to concede if any of them succeeded: that testimony can transmit moral knowledge but not moral understanding, and that understanding is what morally matters; that moral beliefs require first-personal grasp of reasons in a way that blocks testimonial transmission; or that moral testimony is fine in easy cases but systematically fails in contested ones because testimony-based justification depends on prior moral assessment of the testifier's track record, which the recipient of moral testimony ex hypothesi lacks. Which of these, if established, would actually refute my position as stated, and which would only force a revision I could absorb?

3. Tell me what my view predicts that its rivals do not. If it is true, what should we expect to observe -- about how morally deferential people in fact are, about the epistemic achievements of people raised in morally decent communities who absorbed their outlook testimony-like, about experts in ethics -- that we would not expect if pessimism were true? If my view makes no such differential predictions, say so plainly.

4. Assess whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability. Defenders of my view sometimes retreat, under pressure, to claims like "testimony yields knowledge but the agent still owes it to herself to understand," or "deference is fine for propositional knowledge but not for knowing how to respond morally." Are these principled clarifications or immunizing maneuvers? What, if anything, does the view still risk?

5. Finally, say what defenders of my own position -- not only its critics -- have conceded would count against it, and give your honest overall judgment: is this a well-insulated position, and if so, is that a virtue or a symptom?

Please write in American English.
