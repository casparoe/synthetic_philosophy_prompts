The exam question was:

Read the following passage from Book III of Hume's Treatise of Human Nature (3.1.2): "Morality, therefore, is more properly felt than judg'd of; tho' this feeling or sentiment is commonly so soft and gentle, that we are apt to confound it with an idea, according to our common custom of taking all things for the same, which have any near resemblance to each other."

(a) State the thesis about moral judgment Hume defends here, and explain how contemporary expressivism inherits it. (b) Using the argument "If stealing is wrong, then getting someone else to steal is wrong; stealing is wrong; so getting someone else to steal is wrong," state the Frege–Geach problem as precisely as you can, and explain why it presses specifically on views in Hume's line. (c) Is the problem fatal to expressivism? Discuss at least one expressivist reply.

The student's answer:

Hume's claim is that a moral judgment is not really a judgment at all: to call an action vicious is not to report a fact about it but to feel, and thereby voice, a sentiment of disapprobation toward it. Contemporary expressivists inherit this directly. "Stealing is wrong" expresses disapproval of stealing rather than describing stealing, so the sentence is not in the business of stating truths.

The Frege–Geach problem arises because "wrong" also occurs in contexts where nothing is asserted or expressed. In premise one of the argument, the speaker asserts the conditional, not its antecedent; she need not disapprove of stealing at all -- she might be a nihilist testing her roommate's view. Yet the argument is plainly valid, and validity requires that "stealing is wrong" have the same content in the antecedent and in premise two; otherwise the argument merely equivocates. This is Frege's point, pressed by Geach: the same content must be able to occur asserted and unasserted, and an account that ties the meaning of "wrong" to an act of expressing disapproval cannot say what that word contributes where no disapproval is expressed. The same difficulty recurs with negation ("stealing is not wrong") and with attitude ascriptions ("Maria believes that stealing is wrong").

The problem is serious, but expressivists have replies. The simplest is to treat the embedded occurrence as reporting the speaker's disapproval: premise one comes out as "if I disapprove of stealing, then I disapprove of getting someone else to steal," which embeds in a conditional without difficulty because it is a plain factual claim about the speaker's attitudes, so the expressivist can explain validity after all. Blackburn's quasi-realism offers a more sophisticated version: moral sentences turn out to be minimally truth-apt, and once we have deflationary truth, the validity of arguments like the one above is secured, since truth is preserved in the usual way.

Whether either reply ultimately works is contested, but the objection is not obviously fatal.
