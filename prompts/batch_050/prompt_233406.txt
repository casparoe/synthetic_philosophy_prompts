I spent my career as an experimental physicist and have, since retiring, been working my way through the philosophy of mind literature. At a reading group I attend, one member -- an intelligent person whose background is in computer science rather than philosophy -- offered the following summary of what conceivability arguments in this area are supposed to establish, and I would like to know how much of it to trust:

Conceivability arguments, he said, rest on a simple principle: if you can imagine a situation coherently, without contradiction, then that situation is metaphysically possible. Philosophers defending the irreducibility of consciousness use this by asking us to picture a being physically identical to a person, atom for atom, but lacking all inner experience. Since we can picture such a being without contradiction, it is possible, and since it is possible for the physical facts to hold without the experiential facts, experience must be something over and above the physical. He added that this style of argument has a serious, perhaps fatal, problem: we now know from cases like water and H2O that some truths are necessary even though we can imagine their failing -- we can imagine water turning out to be something other than H2O, yet water could not have been anything else -- so the imagination is simply unreliable as a detector of metaphysical necessity, and the conceivability argument for dualism collapses along with it.

My own inclination is that he has the rough shape of the dialectic right but is overconfident about the rebuttal, though I cannot say precisely why. Could you audit his summary for accuracy? Which parts would a careful proponent of conceivability arguments accept as stated? Where does he conflate things that the literature keeps apart -- I have a vague sense, for instance, that what he calls imagining may not be what philosophers mean by conceiving, and that his water example may cut less cleanly than he assumes? And does his closing verdict, that the argument collapses, follow from the parts of his summary that survive correction, or does a defensible version of the conceivability route remain even after granting everything true in his objection?
