I am a philosopher of mind drafting the framing chapter of a monograph on representational realism about artificial neural networks — the question whether the internal states of trained networks genuinely represent features of the world, or whether attributions of content to them are merely a convenient fiction on the part of interpretability researchers. Before I defend any particular account, I want to fix the standard of adequacy, since the literature strikes me as systematically talking past itself: causal-informational, teleological, structural-resemblance, and deflationary accounts are each motivated by a different paradigm case, and disputes among them often reduce to competing stipulations about what a theory of representation owes us.

Please produce a specification of desiderata that a theory of representation must satisfy to be adequate to the case of deep neural networks — not a defence of any one account. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and state whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a point on which a theory might legitimately trade. Address at least the following, but treat this list as a floor rather than a ceiling:

First, which phenomena the theory must classify, and in which direction. I have in mind: the success of linear probes in decoding apparently human-interpretable features from hidden activations; polysemantic neurons and superposition, in which a single unit or direction appears responsive to many unrelated features; adversarial inputs; circuits that appear to implement in-context learning; and activation-steering results, in which adding a vector associated with some concept to intermediate activations shifts behaviour in concept-appropriate ways. A theory that cannot say whether these are representations, and why, is not yet in the running.

Second, which distinctions the theory must preserve rather than collapse. At minimum: information that is merely decodable from a network's activations versus information that plays a causal role in producing its outputs, since probe results are consistent with the former without the latter; the network's representing X versus our representing the network as representing X; representation of the current input versus representation of absent or abstract states of affairs; and genuine representation versus useful idealization.

Third, which constraints from adjacent disciplines and from the general theory of content the account must respect — most pressingly, an explanation of how a network could misrepresent, since several candidate criteria (causal covariance, decodability) appear to render error impossible and thereby disqualify themselves as criteria of content.

Three further requests. Indicate which subset of your desiderata, if any, is jointly unsatisfiable; if the full list is inconsistent, that is itself a result I would want flagged. Identify the single requirement that does the most work in excluding extant candidates, and say which family of accounts it excludes. Finally, score the main contenders — causal-informational, teleosemantic, structural-resemblance, and use-theoretic or deflationary accounts — briefly against the list, but keep this short: my interest lies in the criteria and their motivating cases, not in a ranking. A table is acceptable for the summary of requirements and for the scoring.
