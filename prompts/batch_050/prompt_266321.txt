I'm in this reading group on classical Chinese philosophy (long story) and last week two members, Priya and Dan, got into a genuine standoff over the butterfly dream in the Zhuangzi, the one where Zhuangzi dreams he's a butterfly flitting around carefree, wakes up, and isn't sure whether he's a man who dreamt he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming he's a man. Dan's position is that this is just dream skepticism, same family as Descartes: you can't rule out that your current experience is a dream, so your confidence that you're awake right now is undermined, full stop. He said the only real difference from Descartes is that Zhuangzi doesn't go on to try and build certainty back up, which makes Zhuangzi the more honest skeptic. Priya pushed back hard. She said the passage doesn't end on doubt at all -- it ends with the idea of the transformation of things, and the mood is closer to delight than anxiety, so reading it as an epistemological argument about what we can know is importing a Western frame. She also said that if Zhuangzi were making a skeptical argument it would eat itself, because the argument needs you to trust the distinction between waking and dreaming in order to doubt that same distinction.

Dan conceded the mood point a bit but said mood isn't logic: the structure of the challenge is still skeptical even if Zhuangzi shrugs where Descartes panics. He also pointed out that elsewhere in the same chapter Zhuangzi talks about how in a dream you don't know it's a dream, and how people might only realize this whole life was a dream after some great awakening -- which sure sounds like he's worried about exactly what Dan says he's worried about. Priya came back with two things. First, a skeptical argument is supposed to establish a conclusion (you can't know X), but Zhuangzi doesn't seem to be establishing anything -- he's loosening your grip on fixed boundaries between self and other, dream and waking, so the passage is doing something performative or therapeutic rather than argumentative. Second, she noted the butterfly is described as not knowing it was Zhuangzi and being perfectly happy that way, which suggests not-knowing is the point, not the problem. Dan's last move was to say that Priya's self-undermining objection actually cuts against her too: if the passage can't sustain a skeptical argument, it can't sustain her tidy non-skeptical argument either, so maybe neither of their "readings" survives and the thing just isn't an argument in either direction.

Neither would budge and the rest of us got nowhere. Who's right about which specific points here? I'd like a real adjudication -- e.g., does the passage's ending actually settle what it's doing, does the great-awakening bit support Dan as much as he thinks, does the self-undermining charge land against Priya or against both of them, and is Dan's final move a genuine escape or a cop-out? Also curious what, if anything, both of them are missing.
