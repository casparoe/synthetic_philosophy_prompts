Analyze the Boeing 737 MAX crisis as a case study in corporate moral agency. In 2018 and 2019, two crashes involving the aircraft killed 346 people. For the purposes of this analysis, treat the following as fixed: Boeing designed an automated flight-control system, MCAS, that could command nose-down stabilizer movement using input from a single angle-of-attack sensor and could activate repeatedly; early pilot materials did not clearly explain the system or its possible behavior; certification involved Boeing employees authorized to perform functions on the regulator’s behalf; Boeing faced strong commercial pressure to compete with Airbus and to avoid requirements for costly additional pilot training; and the aircraft remained in service after the first crash while the relevant risks were reassessed.

Use the following working account of institutional authorship, treating it as stipulated rather than as an established consensus theory: an organization is itself morally blameworthy when its decision-making structure integrates information and commitments across roles, enables the organization to recognize and respond to moral reasons without depending on any one employee’s private judgment, and gives it the capacity to revise its conduct when those reasons become salient.

Map the episode onto that account carefully. Which decisions or omissions, if any, are best understood as Boeing’s actions rather than as the aggregated actions of engineers, executives, test pilots, certification specialists, and managers? Where would a corporate belief or intention be located, given that relevant knowledge was distributed across departments? Could Boeing have known enough to be blameworthy even if no individual possessed the complete picture? Does fragmented knowledge weaken blame, or can an organization be culpable for creating structures that prevent knowledge from becoming decisionally effective?

Then press the account in the opposite direction. Consider the problem of many hands: if each participant performed a locally intelligible task, if some employees raised concerns while others lacked the authority to act on them, and if regulatory approval created reasonable expectations of safety, perhaps there is a responsibility gap that the idea of corporate agency merely papers over. Assess whether corporate blame identifies a genuine organizational failure, provides a useful shorthand for institutional reform, or risks absorbing blame that should remain attached to particular people. Also distinguish causal responsibility, moral blameworthiness, legal liability, and remedial responsibility rather than allowing one to stand in for all the others.

Conclude by saying what the episode shows about the stipulated account. Does it vindicate strong corporate moral agency, reveal that the account needs an additional condition, or demonstrate that institutional blame is primarily forward-looking and cannot literally resemble blame directed at a person? Your answer should identify the features of the case that the theory explains best, the features it leaves out, and the allocation of responsibility that follows most defensibly among Boeing, identifiable individuals, and the regulatory system.
