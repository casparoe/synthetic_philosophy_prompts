I'm running a little workshop for my undergrad phil club and I want to hand out a quiz called "which free will do you actually want?" — the idea being that people argue past each other about free will because they're secretly valuing different things (having real alternatives, being the ultimate source of your actions, acting from your own values rather than compulsions, deserving blame/praise, etc.), and if you sort them by what they *want* first, the compatibilism/libertarianism fight gets way less confused.

So: write me the quiz. Some specs:

- 7-9 questions. Each should be a concrete scenario or trade-off, not a "do you believe in determinism?" question. E.g. the kind of thing where you ask whether it would bother you if a neuroscientist had predicted your "spontaneous" choice an hour earlier, or whether a person who physically couldn't have resisted doing the right thing still counts as praiseworthy, or whether you'd take a pill that removes your capacity for weakness of will. Answerable by someone who's never heard the term compatibilism.
- Each question gets 2-3 answer options.
- A scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions. The leaves I want included at minimum: leeway libertarianism (wanting genuine alternative possibilities), sourcehood libertarianism (wanting to be the uncaused origin of your actions even without alternatives), Frankfurt-style hierarchichal compatibilism (wanting your first-order wants to line up with what you want to want), reasons-responsiveness compatibilism, a deflationary "I just want freedom from coercion/compulsion, metaphysics who cares" position, and hard incompatibilist skepticism (nobody has or could have the free will worth wanting, deal with it).
- If two of those leaves can't be separated by your questions, say so explicitly and explain why the distinction between them might not show up in anyone's gut reactions to cases.
- Also: if some combination of answers the quiz allows maps onto *no* position anyone actually holds, note the empty cell and take a guess why it's empty — incoherent desire, or just a view nobody's gotten around to defending.
- End with one question that isn't scored, just there to make people argue at the pub afterwards.

Keep the tone of the quiz questions kind of punchy, these are 20 year olds who'd rather be on their phones.
