I recently read a popular book about Gödel's incompleteness theorems and ended up in a long argument with a friend about what they imply for artificial intelligence. My friend, following a line of thought I understand Roger Penrose has defended, claimed the theorems show that no machine could match human mathematical insight, since we can apparently see the truth of certain statements that any given machine cannot prove. I pushed back on this, and in the course of some reading on my own I stumbled across a problem in AI safety research that struck me as even stranger than the Penrose argument -- and that, as far as I can tell, only emerged decades after Gödel's death in 1978.

As I understand the problem, it goes roughly like this. Suppose you build an AI system that does its reasoning inside some formal proof system, and you want it to be able to trust the reasoning of other instances of itself -- or of its own future self, after it has copied or modified its own code. Naively, the system would like to establish something like: whatever my proof system proves is true. But apparently a result called Löb's theorem entails that no consistent system capable of basic arithmetic can prove this kind of blanket trust in its own soundness, not even for the very same proof system it itself runs on. So a rational agent of this design seems structurally blocked from justifying confidence in successors it has built, and the researchers working on this have been driven to rather baroque workarounds -- agents trusting only subtly modified versions of themselves, replacing deductive self-trust with probabilistic self-trust, and so forth. What fascinates me is that this puts Gödel-style limitation results in almost the opposite light from how my friend used them: not as proof that machines fall short of human minds, but as proof that machines cannot even fully vouch for themselves.

So here is my question: if Gödel were alive today and confronted with this obstacle to building self-trusting agents, how do you think he would react? Would he regard Löb's theorem as little more than a corollary of his own second incompleteness theorem and find the entire engineering headache unsurprising? How might he connect it to the disjunctive conclusion he apparently drew in his 1951 Gibbs lecture -- roughly, that either the human mind surpasses anything a finite machine can do, or else there are mathematical questions that are absolutely undecidable even for us? And given his reported Platonism, his suspicion of materialist accounts of mind, and his apparent view that we grasp mathematical truths through something analogous to perception, would he take the obstacle to reveal something deep about the difference between minds and formal systems -- or would he dismiss it as a mere artifact of the decision to build agents on top of proof systems in the first place? I'd be glad if you reasoned from his broader philosophical temperament, as documented in his later writings and conversations, rather than only from the mathematics.
