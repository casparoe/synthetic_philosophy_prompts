I teach a small upper-level seminar in philosophy of language (13 students, 80 minutes) and we're on Brandom's inferentialism. They can parrot "meaning is inferential role" back at me, but they don't feel it -- underneath they still think the real meaning of a word is what's in your head, or the thing it hooks onto in the world, with inferences as decoration on top. Design a game for the session where the only way to teach or learn a word is through what follows from it and what it follows from -- no defintions, no synonyms, no pointing at anything. I want a mechanic where making a claim lands you with commitments other players can challenge ("you said X, so you're committed to Y -- or take X back"), with some visible scoreboard, so that grasping the concept just is being able to navigate that web. Give me: the actual rules and scoring, a few ready-to-use rounds including at least one nasty one (say a made-up word with rich inferential connections but nothing it refers to, so the group has to fight about whether mastering the inferences counts as knowing what it means), a timeline for the 80 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto Brandom's picture. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which part of the view the game structurally can't reproduce -- I'm guessing something about how real scorekeeping is implicit and constant rather than a scored round, but I want your honest take.
