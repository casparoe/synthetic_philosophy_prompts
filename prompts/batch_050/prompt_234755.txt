I run a small reading-and-problem group for second-year philosophy undergraduates who have all taken an introductory logic course (so they can do natural deduction with the conditional and know what a biconditional is) but have never studied the paradoxes of truth seriously. Next month I want to devote a single two-hour session to the idea that paradox can survive the removal of things people assume are essential to it: Curry's paradox shows you can derive an arbitrary sentence without ever using negation in the offending sentence, and Yablo's paradox shows you can get a liar-style contradiction from an infinite list of sentences none of which refers to itself. My own worry, from having tried this once before, is that students walked away thinking both paradoxes were just the ordinary liar in a cheap disguise, and I want to avoid that outcome this time.

Please plan the session's explanatory arc rather than giving me the explanation itself. Specifically, I want: the order in which the components should be introduced and the dependency structure behind that order — for instance, whether Curry or Yablo should come first, what each presupposes about the students' grip on the T-schema and on self-reference, and where the ordinary liar belongs given that they have all seen it casually but never formally. I want the one carrying example for each paradox and what it must have and must lack to do its job: for Curry, what the consequent should be so that the derivability of anything at all lands viscerally without letting students blame the example, and for Yablo, how to present an infinite list to finite human beings so that they engage with the structure rather than with worries about writing it down. I want the misconception map: the predictable misreadings of each paradox (for example, that Curry secretly depends on negation somewhere in the background logic, that Yablo's list is covertly circular because every sentence talks about the same collection of sentences, that these are tricks about indexicals), in the order students will voice them, and at what point in the arc each should be headed off versus deliberately allowed to surface first.

Finally, tell me what check at the end of the session would show the idea has landed — what students should be able to do or state if they have genuinely understood, as opposed to memorized, that negation and self-reference are each dispensable resources for paradox (my current idea is to ask them to state precisely which assumption each paradox puts pressure on that the other leaves untouched, but I suspect this is too easy). Also tell me what I should explicitly leave out at this level — presumably the technical business about contraction in the Curry derivation, omega-inconsistency, and the debate over whether Yablo's quantification is impredicative — and what to say to a student who pushes into that territory so they feel their question was honored without derailing the session.
