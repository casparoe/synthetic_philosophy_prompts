In discussions of literary translation, one hears again and again that a translation is like a musical performance: the source text is the score, the translator the performer, the target language the instrument, and just as no two performances of a piece are identical and none of them simply is the piece, so no translation can or should replicate the original — fidelity is a matter of interpretation, not reproduction. This analogy is rarely offered as a mere description. It is typically deployed to license the translator's liberties and to cast anyone who demands close formal fidelity — rhyme for rhyme, meter for meter — as a naïf who has confused the score with the performance.

Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot strikes me as a standing challenge to this use of the analogy. Hofstadter devoted a book to translating one small, formally rigid sixteenth-century French poem by Clément Marot dozens of times, and his insistence throughout is that the poem's formal features — its extreme brevity, its tight rhyme scheme, its compression into tiny lines — are not decorative packaging to be discarded but part of what the poem is. On his view, a translation that keeps the propositional content and drops the form has not interpreted the melody on a new instrument; it has lost the melody.

Please audit the performance analogy against this challenge. Begin with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the score, the performer, the instrument, the concert audience, a wrong note, an improvisation, a transposition into a new key? Then locate the breaks in the mapping, and — this is the part I care most about — separate the disanalogies that are load-bearing for the conclusion the analogy is used to support (that strict formal fidelity is a confusion) from the disanalogies that are real but harmless. Two candidate breaks I suspect matter: first, a musical score is composed for performance, so interpretation is part of its intended mode of existence, whereas Marot's poem was not composed to be translated; second, performances coexist with the score, which the audience can in principle consult, whereas for a reader with no French the translation does not accompany the original but replaces it. I am not confident, though, that these are the breaks that do the work — tell me if the load-bearing disanalogy lies elsewhere.

Finally, a complication. Hofstadter's own practice is anything but slavish: he freely substitutes imagery, invents cultural equivalents, and treats some constraints as negotiable while holding others inviolable. Does this make him a counterexample to the performance analogy, or its most sophisticated instance — a performer who simply regards the time signature as part of the composition rather than as a tempo marking? And if the performance analogy fails, what would you put in its place? One image I have encountered is that constrained translation is like rebuilding a ship out of timber from a different forest, plank by plank, keeping only the plan — say whether that buys anything the performance analogy lacks, or whether some third analogy better captures what is actually at stake when form itself is treated as content.
