I'm a third-year undergrad in a grad Wittgenstein seminar and my final paper is on hinge epistemology, and before I commit 6,000 words to it I want to know whether the position I've landed on actually hangs toghether. The whole paper is built around that remark in On Certainty (§152 I think) where Wittgenstein says that the propositions that stand fast for us are not ones we were ever explicitly taught, that we only discover them afterwards, and that they work like the axis of a rotating body: the axis is not fixed because anything holds it in place, rather the movement around it is what determines its immobility. That's my paraphrase, not the exact wording, but the paper treats it as the key image. Here's roughly what I want to defend:

(1) Moore's error in the proof of the external world was one of grammar, not nerve: he was perfectly right to be certain he had two hands, but his use of 'know' there misfires, because knowledge-talk only gets a grip where giving grounds and the intelligibility of doubt do, and hinge certainty has neither. (2) Hinge certainty isn't a propositional attitude at all -- it's something animal, something that shows itself in how I act and move through the world rather than a set of beliefs I hold. (Basically the Moyal-Sharrock line, though I got there before I read her.) (3) The axis remark is the canonical statement of exactly this view: the things that stand fast are the hinges, and they are held in place by nothing -- no foundation, no justification -- their standing fast is just constituted by the role they play, the movement around them. (4) Hinges are historically soft: plenty of things that once stood fast for whole cultures, like the earth not moving, were eventually given up, and in principle any hinge can shift; nothing is a hinge permanently. (5) None of this makes me a revisionist about ordinary talk -- when I say I know my own name or I know the bus comes at 8, those sentences are perfectly true; it's only Moore's philosophical use of 'know' against the skeptic that's broken. (6) The skeptic doesn't get refuted, he gets diagnosed: the brain-in-a-vat doubt is idle, it fails to engage with anything, and there's no answering it because there was never a real question. That said, I'm no dogmatist -- for all we know, our hinges could simply be wrong, and if they are, we could never find out.

So: does this set survive inspection? Please don't just flag every pair that looks iffy and call it a day. Tell me which tensions are real and which dissolve once you look closely, rank the real ones by how much damage they do to the paper, and for each one say specifically which commitment has to give and what giving it up costs me. I'd rather find out now than in my prof's margin comments.
