Here's the dispute, as best I can reconstruct it. It was about coherence arguments for expected utility maximization.

Marta's opening move: the axioms are justified because violating them makes you exploitable. If your preferences are intransitive -- you prefer A to B, B to C, but C to A -- then you can be money-pumped: someone sells you B for A plus a penny, then C for B plus a penny, then A for C plus a penny, and you're back where you started, minus three pennies, and this repeats forever. So intransitivity is irrational, full stop.

Diego's reply: that's too fast. The money pump only works if the agent keeps accepting every trade offered. Someone with cyclic preferences could simply decline to trade, or accept one swap and then stop. The pump doesn't show the preferences are irrational; it shows that a certain trading policy combined with those preferences is exploitable. So the argument smuggles in a behavioral assumption and proves less than advertised.

Marta's counter: even granting that, there's still a sure loss on the table. You paid three pennies to end up exactly where you started. Whether or not anyone actually pumps you, you'd be endorsing a sequence of moves that is strictly dominated by doing nothing. Rationality doesn't require waiting around to be exploited; the possibility of the loss is enough.

Diego's counter-counter: fine, but now notice that transitivity was never really the weak point anyway. The axiom doing the most damage is completeness -- the requirement that for any two options you either prefer one or are indifferent. There are cases where options seem genuinely incomparable, or only roughly comparable: a career as a lawyer versus one as a musician, where each small improvement to one side fails to settle it. Money-pump-style coercion doesn't touch completeness at all, because you can't pump someone merely for refusing to rank two things. So the coherence defense of expected utility is weakest exactly where it needs to be strongest.

Marta's last word: without completeness you get paralysis or arbitrariness whenever a forced choice comes up, and forced choices come up constantly. Anyway, when someone is forced to pick, their pick reveals a ranking, so the "incomparability" was never real.

Diego: a forced pick reveals a choice disposition, not a preference. If you put a gun to my head and make me choose, my choosing lawyer over musician doesn't show I preferred it -- it shows I had to pick something. You're conflating choice with preference, which is the exact confusion the completeness axiom invites.
