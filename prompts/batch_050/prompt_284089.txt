I'm a graduate student co-teaching an upper-division moral philosophy seminar this semester -- about sixteen students, one 80-minute session per week. Last week we covered deontological constraints, and I introduced what I now know is sometimes called the paradox of deontology: if there is a constraint against, say, deliberately harming an innocent person, then it seems the constraint should apply with even more force when violating it just once would prevent five other people from violating it -- yet the constraint as standardly understood forbids you from doing exactly that. It treats minimizing violations of the constraint as itself a violation of the constraint. My students copied this down dutifully and clearly felt nothing. One of them said it was "just a weird definition" and moved on. I have one session left before the unit ends, and I want them to feel, in their gut, why the paradox is genuinely paradoxical rather than a definitional curiosity.

Design me an in-class game or staged activity that does this. It should involve repeated rounds with real (small) stakes -- tokens, points, whatever doesn't need money -- structured so that participants periodically occupy the position of an agent who must choose between breaking the constraint once (e.g., directly imposing a loss on one specific classmate) and standing by while several larger losses of the same type occur that they could have prevented. Ideally the rules should be arranged so that at least some students who confidently endorse the constraint at the start end up breaking it under pressure, and some who break it end up feeling that something has gone wrong that mere arithmetic can't capture. I want roles, exact rules, a round-by-round sequence, and a payoff or penalty scheme, plus a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the components of the paradox (agent-relative versus agent-neutral reasons, using someone as a means, the difference between what I do and what I merely fail to prevent) -- and the debrief should come only after students have made and defended their choices, not as a lecture beforehand. Tell me also what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (students gaming the payoffs, rounds collapsing into jokes, one student being singled out, whatever) and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the actual philosophical puzzle the game cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply in discussion.

Write in American English.
