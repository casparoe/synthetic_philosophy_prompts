I am a PhD student in political theory writing a dissertation chapter on the decision-theoretic foundations of Rawls's original position, and I need help with an analogy I keep encountering. Please take on the role of a senior philosopher with joint expertise in rational choice theory and contemporary political philosophy — someone equally at home with Rawls, Harsanyi, and the formal literature on fair division. Think carefully, and before composing your final answer, brainstorm correspondences and disanalogies freely; then organize the results into a structured audit.

At a teaching workshop recently, a colleague defended the difference principle with the classic divide-and-choose procedure: when one person cuts a cake and the other chooses first, self-interest alone leads the cutter to cut fairly, because they do not know which piece they will end up with. Likewise, she argued, parties behind the veil of ignorance, not knowing which social position they will occupy, will be led by self-interest alone to choose fair principles. She then drew two stronger conclusions: first, that this vindicates maximin-style reasoning in the original position; second, that Harsanyi's expected-utility alternative can accordingly be set aside. I have seen this analogy in lecture notes and introductory texts, always made to do roughly this work.

Here is what I would like from you:

1. An explicit correspondence table: cutter, chooser, cake, cut, pieces, and ignorance of which piece one receives — what plays each role on the Rawlsian side (parties, social positions, primary goods, principles, the veil)?

2. A separation of disanalogies into those that matter for the intended conclusions and those that are harmless. Please address at least: whether the cake is a fixed pie (whereas Rawls allows inequalities that benefit the worst-off, i.e., the size of the "cake" depends on the "cut"), and the fact that a cutter usually knows the chooser's tastes while the veil deprives parties of knowledge of their own conception of the good.

3. My central worry: divide-and-choose embeds an adversary — the chooser will take the better piece — which makes worst-case reasoning trivially rational. Behind the veil there is no adversary, only an unknown lottery. Does the analogy covertly supply the adversary and thereby smuggle in maximin?

4. What divide-and-choose actually guarantees is envy-freeness. Is that closer in spirit to minimax regret than to maximin? If the analogy supports any decision rule, which one, and would that rule deliver the difference principle or something else?

5. Your verdict: do my colleague's two conclusions survive the audit? If the analogy cannot carry them, what (if anything) can it legitimately support, and is there a better analogy for the original position?

Aim for roughly 1,200–1,800 words, state your assumptions explicitly, and do not hedge where a definite answer is available.
