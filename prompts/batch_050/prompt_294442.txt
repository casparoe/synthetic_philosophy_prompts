I am a novelist, and for the past nine years I have sat on the acquisitions board of the small literary press that publishes my books. We have no written speech policy. When a manuscript promises trouble, we tell ourselves that we are guided by something like Mill's harm principle, though we have never settled on a formulation, and our minutes record outcomes rather than reasons. Our relevant track record is as follows.

- We published a satire of a charismatic religious leader in full knowledge that it would deeply offend his followers. It did; there were peaceful protests outside two bookshops; we expressed regret for any upset and changed nothing.
- We rejected, despite considerable literary merit, a thriller whose appendix described, accurately and in workable detail, how to synthesise a dangerous toxin from legally available materials.
- We published a novel narrated in the first person by a remorseless sexual predator, on the grounds that depiction is not endorsement and that no real victim was identifiable.
- We rejected a roman à clef in which a private individual — thinly disguised, not a public figure, and never charged with anything — was portrayed as complicit in serious crimes. Our lawyers believed the book was defensible; we declined it all the same.
- We published a polemical essay collection arguing that a well-known physician's signature treatment was fraudulent, naming him throughout. It plainly damaged his reputation; we took the view that he was a public figure and the matter one of public interest.

Now before us is an accomplished debut novel, narrated in the second person, that immerses the reader in the radicalisation of a lonely young man into a violent extremist movement. No real organisation is named; there is no operational detail whatsoever; no identifiable individual appears. But the final third is persuasive — unsettlingly so — and two readers at other houses declined it on the grounds that it functions as radicalisation literature: a manual for the disposition rather than for the hands. It reaches us six months after a real attack carried out by someone of a broadly similar profile, and it is a near-certainty that passages will circulate, out of context, among precisely the people the book depicts.

I should like to know how we ought to vote if we wish to be consistent with our own record. In particular: set out the two or three principles that would each rationalise all five of our earlier decisions at once; state what each principle predicts about the new manuscript and mark the points at which they diverge; tell me which earlier decision is the closest analog to this case, defending your choice against its nearest rival (I suspect the contest lies between the toxin thriller and the predator narrator, but persuade me either way); give me your best prediction of our verdict, with the reasons for preferring it; and describe the further hypothetical manuscript — the submission we have not yet received — that would most cleanly tell the candidate principles apart. Finally, be honest about whether the principle we would be adopting, in either publishing or rejecting this book, is still recognisably Mill's, or whether the record was never truly Millian to begin with.
