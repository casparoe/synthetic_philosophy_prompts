Last night a friend and I had a long, inconclusive argument about what makes a letterform the letter it is. Her view was deflationary: a mark is an 'a' just in case competent readers reliably read it as one, and there is no deeper fact of the matter. I came away insisting there is something more -- call it the spirit of the letter -- but I couldn't articulate it, so I tried to corner myself with cases. Here are my verdicts, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, underlies them, and whether it vindicates me or her.

1. The double-story 'a' of print and the single-story 'ɑ' of handwriting are both fully 'a'; neither is a degraded version of the other.
2. A child's wobbly, lopsided scrawl counts as 'a' -- unmistakably, despite its crudity.
3. The Greek lowercase alpha does not count as a Latin 'a', even though in many hands it is visually indistinguishable from the single-story one in case 2.
4. A mirror-reversed 'a' does not count as an 'a', full stop -- but an 'a' tilted fifteen degrees obviously does.
5. A glyph that is genuinely ambiguous in isolation, but that a reader resolves as 'a' from context (the obscured letter in 'c?t'), counts as an 'a' in that context.
6. A CAPTCHA-style 'a', warped almost beyond recognition, still counts.
7. A logotype in which the 'a' of a brand name is drawn as a mountain peak: I say this does not count, though my friend insists it's her best case.
8. An 'a' rotated a full 180 degrees does not count -- it has become, if anything, a different creature.

Note the pair I can't reconcile on any simple story: 2 and 3. The child's scrawl is farther in shape from a canonical 'a' than the alpha is, yet I accept one and reject the other. That seems to rule out anything purely visual, which is a problem for me, because my talk of a letter's 'spirit' sounded visual. It also seems to rule out my friend's view, since readers would read the alpha as 'a' without hesitation -- and yet 5 looks like a point in her favor, since context-driven recognizability is doing all the work there. And 4 and 8 together suggest orientation matters categorically in a way that shape distortion (2, 6) does not, which neither of our positions predicted.

Is there a single principle -- visual, functional, historical, intentional, or some combination -- that delivers all eight verdicts? If the best candidate requires dropping one or two of them, say which, and defend the sacrifice. Then tell me what the resulting principle predicts for three cases I haven't listed: an 'a' rotated 170 degrees; a glyph shaped exactly like our 'b' that serves as the 'a' in an independently evolved alien writing system; and an avant-garde font whose designer has placed, in the slot where 'a' belongs, a shape no reader would spontaneously identify as one. Finally, say plainly whether the surviving principle leaves any room for my conviction that there is a real essence of 'a'-ness, or whether I lost the argument.
