In my early modern philosophy course we just did Locke, and we spent a session on Molyneux's problem -- the question in that letter to Locke about whether a man born blind, who had learned to distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch, could tell which was which by sight alone the moment he gained vision, before touching them. After seminar two people from my cohort, Alex and Jordan, got into an argument about it that's still running in the group chat, and I got voluntold to settle it.

Alex's opening position was that this is simply an empirical question and science has already answered it: no. She mentioned these studies -- I think from some project in India that does cataract surgeries on people blind since birth -- where newly sighted patients apparently couldn't match shapes they'd only ever felt with shapes they were now seeing, at least not at first. So philosophers still debating the thought experiment are just ignoring data. Jordan pushed back hard: actual post-op patients are the wrong test case, because their early vision is blurry and chaotic and their brains have zero practice parsing visual input at all, so of course they fail -- that tells you something about visual calibration, not about whether touch and vision can share a shape representation. Molyneux's question, he said, is idealized: given clean, perfect first vision, is there in-principle cross-modal transfer? Alex replied that if you idealize away everything that makes the question testable, you've made it unfalsifiable and boring. Jordan said no, it becomes a question about concepts -- whether CUBE is one concept deployed by both modalities, or two separate concepts (tactile-cube and visual-cube) that have to be stitched together by association, which he claimed was Berkeley's real view anyway.

Alex's counter was that if it's about concept individuation it's *still* empirical, just a question for cognitive science instead of ophthalmology -- she half-remembered something about infants matching things they'd only sucked on with things they saw, though she couldn't reconstruct the details. Jordan's response was that even clean developmental data would only show what human brains happen to do, not what the concepts make possible: a creature could have genuinely unified shape concepts yet fail the transfer task for performance reasons, or pass it via a hardwired lookup table without really grasping the shape across modalities. At this point I honestly can't tell whether they're even disagreeing about the same question anymore. Can you go through it round by round -- who wins each exchange, whether the surgical studies actually bear on the question as Molyneux, Locke, and Berkeley framed it, and whether Jordan's shift from "what would happen" to "what the concepts are" is a legitimate refinement or just moving the goalposts? And if there's a version of the question left that philosophy rather than science has to answer, say what it is.
