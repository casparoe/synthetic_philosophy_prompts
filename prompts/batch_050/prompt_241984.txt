I teach a philosophy of mind seminar (juniors and seniors, 14 students, one 75-minute session) and we are about to spend two weeks on Dennett's multiple drafts model and his attack on the Cartesian theater. Last week I tried explaining the color phi phenomenon to my roommate — a spot appears on the left, then a differently colored spot on the right, and viewers report seeing a single spot move across and change color midway, seemingly before the second spot has appeared — and she immediately asked the obvious question: how could you see the change happen before the information causing it existed? When I gave her the two candidate explanations, one where the experience is doctored before it reaches awareness and one where the memory is rewritten afterward, she insisted there must be a fact about which one really happened to her. That insistence is exactly what I want my students to feel, then lose.

Design a classroom activity in which the students experience a version of this phenomenon themselves (no lecture up front, and they should not hear the names Dennett, multiple drafts, Orwellian, or Stalinesque until the debrief), then have them work in small groups to construct both candidate explanations of their own experience, and then — this is the hard part — have them try and fail to design an experiment or introspective test that would settle which explanation is true of their own case. I want the impasse to arise from their own attempts rather than from me asserting it. Include the materials and setup (keep it low-tech; I have a projector, paper, and stopwatches, but no lab equipment), a timeline for the 75 minutes, the debrief mapping each moment of the activity onto the multiple drafts picture and onto why Dennett thinks the question of when it becomes conscious is ill-posed, the ways this will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each, and an honest note on which feature of Dennett's view the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply by argument.

Present the whole thing as exactly five numbered sections, each two to four sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
