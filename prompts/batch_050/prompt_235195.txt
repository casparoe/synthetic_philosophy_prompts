I am taking a seminar on the intersection of philosophy of language and philosophy of mind, and for my term paper I want to evaluate proposed solutions to Moore's paradox -- why there is something deeply wrong with asserting or believing a sentence of the form "p, and I do not believe that p" (or the commissive variant, "p, and I believe that not-p"), even though such sentences can plainly be true. Before I commit to any particular solution, I want to know what I should be demanding of one. Please write the specification: the desiderata an adequate account of Moore's paradox must meet, with the case or consideration motivating each.

At minimum, make sure the list engages with the following pressure points, though feel free to add others and to reorganize:

- The truth-assertibility gap: a Moorean sentence can be true (it can in fact be raining while I fail to believe it), so whatever is wrong with it cannot be ordinary inconsistency. An account must locate the defect without overstating it.
- Person and tense asymmetries: "It is raining, but he does not believe it" and "It was raining, but I did not believe it then" are perfectly fine, while the first-person present-tense versions are not. A theory must explain why the defect is confined where it is, and ideally derive the asymmetry rather than stipulate it.
- The omissive-commissive contrast: "p and I do not believe that p" is paradoxical but consistent; "p and I believe that not-p" seems worse -- some say straightforwardly contradictory when believed, some say not. An account must either explain the difference or argue there is none.
- Assertion versus belief: some theories make the absurdity a fact about speech acts, others about states of mind. Whichever route is taken, the account must explain the residue on the other side -- why merely believing the Moorean conjunction still seems defective, or why asserting it seems worse than believing it.
- Embedding behavior: Moorean sentences appear far less objectionable under supposition, in conditionals, or in the scope of modals. A theory should predict this rather than treating it as a separate puzzle.
- Overgeneration and undergeneration: the account should not condemn sentences that are actually fine (e.g., statements about my past or unconscious beliefs, or cases where I discover my belief by observing my own behavior), and it should not fail to condemn related absurdities, such as flatly asserting what one simultaneously claims not to know.

For each desideratum, state whether you take it to be non-negotiable -- a constraint no adequate theory may violate -- or a preference characteristic of one camp (for instance, those who ground the paradox in norms of assertion versus those who ground it in the nature of belief or in the structure of self-knowledge). Crucially, I want a committed verdict, not a menu: if some pair of desiderata on the list cannot be jointly satisfied, or if satisfying one forces a specific style of solution, say so and argue for it -- that tension, if it exists, is the part of the specification I care about most. Then, briefly and secondarily, indicate how the main families of solutions fare against the list: accounts centered on norms of assertion, doxastic accounts locating the defect in belief itself, and accounts tying the paradox to the special character of first-person belief ascription. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivations; the scoring can be compressed, since the criteria are where the real work is.
