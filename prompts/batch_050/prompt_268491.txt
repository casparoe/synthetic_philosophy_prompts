I served on a jury a couple years ago and have been chewing on something ever since, and last month my board-game group (of all places) accidentally recreated the exact problem, so now I'm convinced there's an argument here that either destroys voting or I've messed something up. Here's my best reconstruction, numbered so you can point at the exact step:

(1) When a group has to decide something that hangs on several factual questions, the fair way is to take a majority vote on each question separately — each person gets an equal say on each issue, nobody's vote counts more on one question than another.
(2) So suppose a three-person panel has to decide whether a company owes damages. It owes damages only if BOTH: it made a promise (P), and it broke the promise (Q). Member 1 thinks P and Q are both true, so: damages. Member 2 thinks P is true but Q is false, so: no damages. Member 3 thinks P is false but Q is true, so: no damages.
(3) Now count. Majority says P is true (members 1 and 2). Majority says Q is true (members 1 and 3). So by voting on the questions, the group has said: the promise was made and broken — which means damages. But a majority of the panel (members 2 and 3) voted AGAINST damages when asked directly. The same votes produce both "damages" and "no damages" depending on how you tally them. So the group's verdict is inconsistent no matter what you do.
(4) And this isn't a weird one-off: I fiddled around and you can build a case like this for basically any decision that depends on more than one issue, with any number of voters. There's always some arrangement of reasonable individual views where voting question-by-question contradicts voting on the final verdict.
(5) So any time a group decides by aggregating votes on connected issues, the group's output is either incoherent, or you have to just pick one method (vote only on the verdict, or only on the questions) — and picking one means letting whoever picks the method effectively decide the outcome, since the method changes the answer.
(6) So collective decision-making by voting is either irrational or a dictatorship of whoever sets the agenda. Which means juries, boards, parliaments, all of it.

(6) is obviously nuts — juries and boards work okay-ish in practice, or at least they aren't literally self-contradictory institutions. But every premise feels innocent to me, especially (1), which is just... fairness? My friend thinks the trick is in (3), that the group never actually contradicts itself because "the group says damages" isn't real until you announce a tallying rule, but that feels like a technicality that just picks the dictatorship horn of (5). Which step do I actually give up, why does it look so harmless, and what does giving it up cost me? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and lose (6) than the other way around, but I'd like to know exactly what I'm conceding.
