An encyclopedia entry I've been reading summarizes the knowledge norm of assertion as follows: "Timothy Williamson and others have proposed that assertion is governed by a single, simple constitutive rule: one may assert something only if one knows it. Because knowledge requires certainty, it follows that most ordinary assertions are improper, and defenders of the view accept this as a welcome correction to our sloppy everyday practices. The principal rival is the truth norm, which permits one to assert whatever is true. The dispute between the two camps is therefore essentially about how confident a speaker should be before opening their mouth." I'm inclined to trust the entry, since it appears in a professional reference work, but the claim about certainty surprises me, and I had previously understood the debate to concern what makes an assertion epistemically proper rather than what speakers happen to do. Which parts of this summary are accurate, which are common simplifications, and which would a defender of the knowledge norm reject outright? In particular, is the entry's characterization of the rival truth norm fair, and does the disagreement really come down to a threshold of confidence?
