I am putting together a two-page proposal for a one-semester senior thesis in political philosophy, and I need the plan itself written. My starting point is the plural-voting scheme Mill defends in the chapter on the extension of the suffrage in Considerations on Representative Government: everyone gets a vote, but the more intelligent get several, and because he distrusts any direct examination of competence as liable to partiality and abuse, he proposes to infer intelligence from a person's occupation instead. My half-formed idea is this. The standard objection to epistocracy — that any test of competence will be captured or gamed by whoever administers it — is almost always pressed against exam-style schemes, and Mill's proxy scheme is usually dismissed as a quaint variant that the same objection refutes. I suspect the objection does not vanish under a proxy scheme but changes shape: it becomes a question about who fixes the mapping from occupations to vote-weights and how contestable that mapping is, and this difference may matter for whether competence-weighted voting is objectionable in principle or only in particular implementations. Turn this into a proposal: state the research question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; explain why treating Mill as simply an epistocrat with extra steps misses the interesting structure; describe the method, meaning what I would actually do across a fourteen-week semester (close analysis of Mill's stated rationale, reconstruction of the capture objection in both its test-based and proxy-based forms, and application of the reconstructed objection to one or two contemporary competence-weighting proposals); lay out the possible conclusions and which considerations would decide between them; state what would count as the project failing; and give an honest assessment of what could make it too big for one semester. I will supply all citations myself, so refer to positions and objections by name only and include no bibliography.
