I'm in a bioethics reading group (we're grad students, none of us philosophers by training) and last week we got into a genuinly confusing argument about the He Jiankui case. In case anyone's rusty on the details: in November 2018, at a genome-editing summit in Hong Kong, He announced that twin girls had been born in China after he used CRISPR on embryos to disable the CCR5 gene, mimicking a naturally occurring mutation that gives some people resistance to HIV. The girls' father was HIV-positive, the work was done in secret with dodgy consent paperwork, the international reaction was near-universal condemnation, and He ended up in prison. My friend's position was that obviously every framework on our syllabus condemns what he did, and the only live question is how harshly. I wasn't so sure, and I want to actually test that.

Take two theories from our reading list. First, Savulescu's principle of procreative beneficence: prospective parents have a moral reason to select, among the children they could have, the one whose life can be expected to go best, and Savulescu explicitly extends this kind of beneficence reasoning toward enhancement, not just disease avoidance. Second, the Habermasian critique of liberal eugenics: roughly, that a person deliberately designed by another can't fully see themselves as the author of their own life, because they stand in an asymmetric relation to someone else's intentions -- and Habermas draws a line between merely selecting among embryos and actively modifying one. Please analyze the He case through each theory. For procreative beneficence: is HIV-resistance editing the kind of thing the principle would endorse in principle, so that its verdict on He is at most "defensible goal, reckless execution"? Where exactly would PB locate his wrongdoing -- the risk to the children, the deception, the missing consent, the leapfrogging of safety research -- and does a principle built on the expected welfare of the future child even have the resources to condemn some of that? Also, PB is canonically about choosing among embryos, and He modified embryos rather than choosing between them, so say whether the principle strictly applies at all or whether you have to stretch it. For Habermas: does the self-authorship argument actually bite when the edit just copies a mutation plenty of people are born with anyway, and when it targets disease resistance rather than a parental taste for height or temperament? If the twins grow up healthy and unbothered, is there any Habermasian wrong left, or only the research-ethics wrongs?

Then run it the other way: what does the episode show about the theories? As I remember it, the actual condemnation leaned almost entirely on secrecy, consent violations, and danger to the kids -- basically nobody's objection was that HIV resistance is a bad trait to have. Does that mean the public verdict ran on machinery (research ethics, trust, norms about experimenting on unconsenting future people) that both PB and the liberal-eugenics debate largely ignore, and if so is that a mark against those theories or just outside their remit? Be disciplined about the mapping too: who's the choosing agent here (the parents? He himself? the state that jailed him?), what are the real options on the table, and where a theory simply doesn't speak, say so instead of forcing a verdict out of it. One last thing: keep clearly separate what's settled -- the facts of the case, what Savulescu's and Habermas's positions actually are -- from what's genuinely contested, like whether PB is sound, whether it survives this case, and whether the selection/modification distinction does any moral work at all. If the honest answer on some sub-question is that people who know this literature disagree, say that rather than handing me a fake consensus.
