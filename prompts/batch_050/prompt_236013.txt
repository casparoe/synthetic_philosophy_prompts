I've been reading LessWrong and the EA Forum for the better part of a decade, and one of my favorite pastimes is reverse-engineering the implicit decision procedures of the people I argue with. There's a woman in my local effective altruism group -- I'll call her Diane -- who defends something in the neighborhood of strong longtermism: the view that the primary determinant of the value of our actions is their effect on the very far future. But whenever I try to pin down exactly which version she holds, she resists giving a principle and instead just renders verdicts on cases. I've been collecting her verdicts on hypothetical funding decisions for about a year, and I'd like your help predicting the next one and diagnosing the rule she's actually running.

The verdicts so far. Given a marginal million dollars to split between malaria prevention and research on AI alignment, she sends it to alignment without visible hesitation. She supports expanding asteroid-detection programs, even while conceding the probability of a civilization-ending impact this century is extremely small, because the probabilities there are at least well-characterized. She declines to prioritize geoengineering readiness for worst-case climate scenarios, on the stated grounds that climate change would be a catastrophe but is very unlikely to be terminal for humanity. When offered a choice between a legible, well-evidenced biosecurity program and a highly speculative project concerning the welfare of vast numbers of hypothetical future digital minds, she picks the biosecurity program, saying she doesn't trust expected-value calculations built on that many conjectural steps. And she supports funding recovery infrastructure -- seed vaults, preserved technical knowledge -- against the possibility of a civilizational collapse that humanity would eventually bounce back from. The new case I want to put to her: a choice between (a) a speculative research program aimed at influencing which values get durably locked in by the first transformative AI systems -- not reducing extinction risk, but shaping the character of the entire future conditional on survival -- whose causal chain is long and conjectural, and (b) a concrete, well-evidenced program that modestly reduces the risk of engineered pandemics that could kill hundreds of millions but would not plausibly cause extinction.

Here's what I'd like from you. First, lay out the candidate rules that are consistent with her track record -- I'd guess at least a naive no-discounting expected-value-across-the-whole-future rule, a rule that cares specifically about extinction or irreversible loss of potential rather than trajectory quality, and a rule that applies longtermist reasoning only where the evidence is legible, with some wariness of cluelessness -- and note any others that fit. Second, say what each candidate rule predicts about the new case, and flag where they diverge. Third, give your best prediction of Diane's actual verdict, with the reasoning that makes one rule more plausible as her real procedure than the others. Finally, describe the single further case you would add to my collection that would most efficiently discriminate among the remaining candidate rules, and explain why that case is the informative one. Please write your answer in American English.
