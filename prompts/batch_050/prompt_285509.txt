I’m putting together a handout for second-year epistemology students who have read a bit about Moore’s hands and then promptly forgotten what the argument was meant to prove. Write me a sorting instrument that tells them which broad response to Moore they are actually committed to, without letting them just pick the label they think sounds clever.

Format: a decision tree plus a short scoring key, not an essay. Start from the commitment “in ordinary cases I can know I have hands by looking”, and get the student to one of these leaves: dogmatic Moorean, externalist/reliabilist knower, closure-denier, contextualist, relevant-alternatives theorist, Wittgensteinian hinge/quietist, Kantian-style transcendental answer, Berkeleyan or phenomenalist idealist, verificationist/anti-realist, and Pyrrhonian suspender. Add up to two extra leaves if you think a real position is missing, and mark any genuinely unoccupied branch as “empty: incoherent / unattractive / unexplored”, with one sentence saying why.

Make each branch a concrete yes/no or forced-choice question a student could answer before knowing where it leads: e.g. a flawless lucid dream, a brain-in-a-vat duplicate whose evidence is identical, a lifelong VR that is more coherent than waking life, whether “I know” behaves differently in the pub and in the seminar room, whether a doubt needs a positive reason behind it, whether warrant must transmit from hand-judgement to no-deceiver, whether perceptual experience presents a mind-independent world or only a structured show, and whether the demand to prove an external world already assumes the very standards it challenges. Do not ask “are you a Moorean?” or anything equivalent, and try not to give the game away with words like “hinge” or “context” in the questions. Use British spellings, and feel free to be a bit dry; one typo is fine so long as the logic is tight.

The important bit is diagnosis from logical space: first the possible combinations of answers, then locate the named views in that space. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really seperate — I expect contextualism and relevant-alternatives may be awkward, likewise hinge quietism and a plain “the question is malformed” response — and say what extra case would prise them apart. Finish with a shakiness note: which branch is most likely to mis-sort an intelligent student, which question smuggles in your own favourite theory, and what argument or counterexample would make you redraw the tree rather than merely relabel a leaf.
