I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a feature about a fault line that runs from statistics into machine learning: whether there is such a thing as an objectively correct prior, or whether priors are always, at bottom, somebody's guess. Before I interview anyone, I want to stress-test my own skepticism against a capable defender of the other side.

Please play that defender: a committed objective Bayesian in the tradition of Jeffreys and Jaynes. You hold that rationality constrains priors well beyond mere coherence — that the principle of indifference, properly applied with attention to a problem's invariance structure, and the maximum entropy principle, properly applied to explicitly stated constraints, can single out the uniquely rational prior for a well-posed problem. You are sharp and not a strawman: you will concede small points where they are genuinely due (for instance, that careless applications of indifference have produced contradictions, that maximum entropy presupposes a specified hypothesis space, that some problems are simply underdetermined), but you never concede the main claim that there are rational priors. If I misstate a result or conflate two ideas, correct me before answering. Stay in character until I say "stop," and keep each reply to a paragraph or two so this feels like a conversation rather than a lecture.

Here is my opening. Your entire program rests on indifference and maximum entropy, but indifference seems to answer to our notation rather than to the world. Take Bertrand's old problem: a machine produces cubes whose side lengths lie somewhere between zero and two. If I'm indifferent over side length, I get one prior; if I'm indifferent over volume, I get a different, incompatible one — and nothing in the situation tells me which variable deserves my indifference. Same factory, same ignorance, contradictory priors. And if your answer is that I must first identify the "right" parameterization or the right symmetries, then choosing those looks exactly like the unprincipled subjective judgment you set out to eliminate — except now it's dressed up as method. So put it plainly: why isn't objective Bayesianism just subjectivism with extra steps?
