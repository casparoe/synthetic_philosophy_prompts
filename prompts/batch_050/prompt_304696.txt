So I run a small philosophy reading group (mix of grad students and interested civilians) and we're pairing Avicenna's flying man argument with Dalton Trumbo's Johnny Got His Gun next month -- Joe Bonham wakes up in a hospital bed having lost his arms, legs, face, sight, hearing, speech, basically every channel of sensory contact, and the whole book is the inside of his head as he figures out what happened and eventually learns to communicate by tapping Morse code with his head.

Act as a philosopher of mind who also knows the medieval stuff properly (Avicenna, the reception in the Latin tradition, the obvious modern echoes in Descartes). Give us a reading of the novel through the flying man, but do it rigorously in both directions. Some things I want you to wrestle with:

- Avicenna's thought experiment is supposed to show you can be aware of yourself without being aware of your body, with all sensation stripped away. But Joe is NOT the flying man -- he still feels vibration through the bed, heat from the sun, the nurse's touch. Does the novel actually test a weaker and maybe more interesting version of the claim? Where exactly does Joe's residual sensation do philosophical work that undermines the comparison?
- Avicenna's flying man is created fully formed, with no past. Joe is drowning in memory and it's arguably memory that keeps him a self at all. Does the novel quietly commit to a diachronic picture of self-awareness that the flying man can't accommodate -- i.e., that being a self is something you sustain over time, not something you just have in an instant?
- The bit where Joe works out how to tell day from night and counts time feels like it's doing philosophy. What is it doing? Is the ability to track time from the inside a counterexample to, or a confirmation of, the idea that self-awareness needs no external world?
- Avicenna draws a conclusion about the immateriality (or at least the non-bodily nature) of the soul. Does Trumbo's materialist, anti-war humanism let the same scenario run to the opposite conclusion -- that Joe's ordeal shows a person IS what's left when you subtract the body, but in a way that's horrific rather than liberating? What does the novel add to the debate that the thought experiment on its own doesn't? Where does it resist the reading and we should be honest about that?

Before you write the reading proper, spend a few sentences brainstorming the strongest points of contact and disanalogy between the two, and flag which scenes you'd tell the group to reread beforehand. Think carefully and don't sand down the differences to make the pairing look cleverer than it is -- if parts of the comparison are a stretch, say so. No need for citations or page numbers, none of us have the same edition anyway.
