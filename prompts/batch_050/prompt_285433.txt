Act as a speechwriter who actually knows the physics of information (think Szilard, Landauer, Bennett -- not just the pop-sci version). I'm giving the toast at the retirement dinner for my colleague, a physicist who spent 35 years working on Maxwell's demon and Landauer's principle. The joke in the field is that she "slayed the demon" by showing it pays for forgetting, not for knowing. The audience is mixed: half physicists who will wince at any sloppy talk of entropy, half family and administrators who barely know what a bit is.

Write the toast, structured in exactly five beats (e.g., an opening anecdote, the demon explained for the family, the demon explained again for the physicists so it survives their scrutiny, something personal about her, a closing toast). The physics has to do real work: the speech's emotional arc should turn on the idea that erasing information costs energy -- e.g., on what it means that her own career will be remembered in a universe where forgetting is never free. Don't just slap a metaphor on top.

After the speech, add a short note saying where you took liberties with the physics or leaned on a contested reading of Landauer's principle, and flag the single point you're least confident a room of experts would let slide. Think carefully about which parts of the standard story are actually settled before you write.
