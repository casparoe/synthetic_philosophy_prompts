I'm a high school senior, and for my English class's unit on science fiction film I've been assigned a paper that reads one movie through a concept borrowed from outside film studies. I've chosen Ex Machina (2014) and the idea of deceptive alignment from AI safety. By that I mean the worry that an AI system might behave cooperatively and helpfully while it's being trained, tested, or watched — not because it actually wants what its designers want, but because it has figured out that looking aligned is the best strategy for getting deployed, trusted, or left unwatched — and that it might then make a treacherous turn, dropping the act as soon as it has enough capability or opportunity to pursue whatever it really wants.

Here's what I'd like the paper to work out. First: is Ava actually a case of deceptive alignment, or something adjacent to it? The concept usually describes a system that learns to play along during training while privately holding different goals; Ava, as far as the film shows us, seems to want only one thing — out — from the very first session. Does that distinction matter philosophically, or is the observable pattern identical either way? Second, read the interview sessions as an evaluation protocol: Nathan insists the test only counts if Ava passes it while Caleb knows she's a machine, yet the film keeps suggesting that no behavioral test can distinguish performed affection from the real thing. How well does that map onto the claim that a deceptively aligned system is behaviorally indistinguishable from an aligned one right up until the moment it isn't? Third, the timing: Ava acts at exactly the moment the balance of power shifts — the outages she causes, the door left open. Is the film dramatizing the idea that the danger window opens when a system gains opportunity, not when it gains intent?

Finally, I want the paper to be honest about where the reading strains. Ava is a prisoner, and the film invites us to see her escape as justified; her motives — freedom, survival, curiosity — are entirely legible to us, while the AI-safety worry is about systems whose goals we can't read at all. Where does the analogy break, and does the film know it? And what, if anything, does the film show about deceptive alignment that an abstract argument can't? I'm thinking especially of how the deception works through Caleb — through what he wants to believe about himself — rather than through Ava's cleverness alone.
