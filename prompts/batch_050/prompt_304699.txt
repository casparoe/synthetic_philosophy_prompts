I am preparing a position statement for a graduate seminar on natural property rights, organized around the Lockean theory of acquisition, and I would like the statement audited for internal consistency before I circulate it to the participants. My commitments are as follows.

(1) Original acquisition is grounded in labour: an unowned portion of the external world becomes one's property through the mixing of one's labour with it — clearing, fencing, cultivating, extracting — and not through mere discovery, declaration, or first sighting.

(2) Legitimate appropriation is subject to a proviso: one may appropriate only where one leaves enough, and as good, for others.

(3) The proviso is to be understood as a comparative welfare condition: appropriation is permissible so long as no one is thereby made worse off than they would have been had the resource remained unowned and held in common — and this holds even in cases where, after the appropriation, nothing unowned remains.

(4) In a pre-monetary economy, accumulation is limited by spoilage: one may not take more than one can use before it perishes. Because money exists by common consent, however, durable accumulation in a monetary economy is not subject to that limit, and large holdings amassed through voluntary exchange are legitimate.

(5) A settler who clears and cultivates a modest parcel on an uncrowded continent, with equally good land lying nearby, thereby owns it outright and may exclude later arrivals.

(6) An inventor who independently devises a useful industrial technique and then publishes it owns the technique, and may justly prevent others from using it — including others who would eventually have devised it on their own.

(7) If an entrepreneur drills and improves the last remaining unowned water source in an arid region, so that nothing as good is left for others to appropriate, but operates it so efficiently that everyone in the region, even paying his prices, ends up better off than they were when the source lay unowned and undeveloped, his appropriation is legitimate.

Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with one another, and which only appear to be? Please order the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious. For each genuine tension, identify precisely which commitments conflict, what would have to be given up to restore consistency, and what that revision would cost the overall theory. For each merely apparent tension, explain why it dissolves on inspection.

In particular, I would like to know: whether (2) and (3) can jointly be maintained or whether they are rival readings of a single proviso that issue divergent verdicts; whether each of my case verdicts in (5)–(7) is actually deliverable from the principles in (1)–(4), or whether some verdict outruns the principles meant to support it; and whether the extension of acquisition theory to intellectual production in (6) sits comfortably with the welfare condition in (3), given that the technique was never part of any commons. Finally, if the set is repairable, what is the single least costly revision that resolves the most?
