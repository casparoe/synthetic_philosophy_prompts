I am a PhD student in the philosophy of cognitive science, and I sit as the student member on my university's animal care and use committee. The committee currently protects vertebrates by statute and cephalopods by policy, but everything else is decided ad hoc, and proposals now routinely involve decapod crustaceans, insects, spiders, and planarians. I have been asked to draft a reusable triage procedure that any committee member, none of whom will have read the literature on animal consciousness, could apply to a research protocol in under an hour to determine which protections the animal in the protocol should receive.

Please write this procedure. It must be grounded in the actual theoretical situation regarding the distribution of consciousness across animals, which is that no consensus theory exists and the leading approaches (higher-order, global workspace, local recurrence, integrated information, and marker-based or indicator approaches) disagree about how far down the phylogenetic tree phenomenal experience extends. The procedure therefore cannot simply defer to "the science." It needs to do something more defensible.

Specifically, I want:

1. An ordered sequence of questions the committee member asks about the animal and the protocol, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step and where in the sequence a "no further protections required" determination is permitted to stop. The sequence should include at least: the weight placed on anatomical and neural indicators versus behavioral indicators; how to handle behavioral evidence that is ambiguous between genuine felt experience and mere nociception or adaptive inflexibility; and how the severity and duration of the proposed procedure interacts with the animal's assigned tier, so that a low-confidence animal facing a mild intervention is treated differently from the same animal facing a lethal one.

2. An explicit treatment of how the procedure handles theoretical disagreement. I want it to assign protections in a way that is robust across theories where possible, and where robustness fails I want the procedure to state which theory's verdict it is following and why. If you think a precautionary expected-welfare maximization under model uncertainty is the right formalization, say so and operationalize it; if you think that formalization fails for this setting, say why and give the alternative.

3. Explicit flags at the points where the procedure must hand the decision back to a human because the question is evaluative rather than empirical, for example the exchange rate between probability of sentience and severity of harm, or whether a small probability of intense suffering outweighs a high probability of mild discomfort.

4. Three worked applications run through the procedure in full: a Drosophila protocol involving chronic restraint and electric shock in an associative learning paradigm; an octopus protocol involving live capture, tagging, and release; and a decapod protocol in which crabs are exposed to an aversive stimulus to test avoidance learning. At least one of these should produce an outcome that a working animal researcher would find burdensome, and the procedure should own that consequence rather than soften it.

5. A final section stating what the procedure cannot do: the cases where it will misfire, the kinds of evidence that would break it, and how it should be revised as the science moves.

Keep the procedure itself free of technical theory names wherever a plain-language test would do, but the accompanying rationale should make clear what conceptual work each step is doing, so that I can defend it to both the scientists and the philosophers on the committee.
