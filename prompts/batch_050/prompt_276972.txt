I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about criminal responsibility and the brain. Last week I interviewed a defense attorney about a client of hers -- a man with a long, pretty chilling record of manipulation and violence who a court-appointed expert described as a textbook psychopath. The prosecutor's whole position basically came down to one line: "he knows right from wrong, end of story." And look, I get what he means at the level of a soundbite. The defendent could tell you perfectly well that lying and hurting people is wrong, he'd even say it himself, so lock him up.

But the more I sat with it, the more I realized I have no idea what that sentence actually claims. Like, what does "knowing right from wrong" even consist in? Does it just mean being able to state the rules, the way you could state the rules of a game you don't care about? Does it mean genuinely believing the rules are binding on you, not just conventions other people happen to enforce? Does it require *feeling* anything -- some kind of internal tug or aversion when you contemplate doing wrong -- or is pure intellectual grasp enough? Does it have to be operative in the moment of the act, or is it enough that you could recite it calmly afterwards in an interview room? And which of these does the prosecutor's argument actually need to be true in order to justify punishment?

Could you lay out the different precise claims "he knows right from wrong" could be making here, and say for each one: is it the kind of thing that's plausibly intact in someone with that profile, or the kind of thing researchers have reason to think is damaged or missing? And then -- this is the part my editor cares about -- which of these readings would actually support the conclusion that he deserves full criminal blame? Because I suspect the soundbite works by sliding between a weak reading that the evidence supports and a strong reading that the moral argument needs, but I can't quite pin the slide down myself.
