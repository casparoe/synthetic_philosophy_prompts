I host a philosophy podcast aimed at graduate students, and in a few weeks I am interviewing a metaphysician who defends origin essentialism: the Kripkean thesis that a given human being could not possibly have originated from a different sperm and egg than the ones she in fact came from. She is a careful, experienced interlocutor, and the episode will fail if the questions merely invite her to restate the intuition pump about the Queen or to re-run the standard presentation of the view. Draft eight to ten interview questions, numbered, each followed by the answer she is most likely to give and a follow-up question keyed to that answer. I want questions that press where the view is actually vulnerable, not where it is strong.

Here is what I would like covered, though you may reorder and combine as you see fit. First, the sufficiency gap: identical twins originate from the very same sperm and egg, so origins at most constrain identity without fixing it; ask her what does the rest of the work, and press in the follow-up on whether conceding this dilutes the modal intuition that made the thesis attractive. Second, the vagueness worry: describe a sorites across possible worlds in which the actual sperm is altered molecule by molecule before fertilization, so that either there is a sharp cutoff between worlds that contain me and worlds that contain a numerically different person, or origins tolerate gradual divergence and the thesis collapses; anticipate whatever reply she gives (a brute cutoff, an epistemicist line, or an appeal to biological criteria of gamete identity) and give me a follow-up against it. Third, a case I doubt she has prepared for: in vitro gametogenesis, in which eggs or sperm are derived in a laboratory from a person's ordinary somatic cells. If a child is conceived from an egg derived from her mother's skin cell, which stage counts as the essential origin — the skin cell, the derived egg, the mother? Press her on whether the view has any principled way to identify which link in a branching derivation chain is the origin, and what the thesis says if the technology one day lets one gamete be derived from two donors.

Finally, include at least one question on the argument's machinery rather than its conclusion: arguments for the necessity of origins typically proceed through branching possible worlds and the transitivity of identity, and the worry is that the same machinery proves too much — that not just the gametes but the entire causal ancestry of the zygote (the particular act of conception, the midwife, the delivery room) comes out essential. Ask where she stops the slide and why the stopping point is not arbitrary. Close the sequence with a question about defeaters: what intuition, case, or theoretical cost would lead her to abandon origin essentialism rather than absorb the cost, and follow up by asking whether a haecceitist or counterpart theorist, who can recover "I might have had different parents" in a deflated sense, has really lost anything she can prove is worth keeping.
