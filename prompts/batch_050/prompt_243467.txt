I run a 75-minute discussion section for an undergraduate philosophy of AI course, usually 24 students with a wide mix of technical backgrounds. I want one classroom game, not a list of possible activities, that makes them viscerally experience the difference between an approval-directed agent and a goal-directed agent before I put those labels on the board. The basic distinction I care about is this: one system chooses actions because it predicts that a human overseer will approve of the action or its visible results; another chooses actions because they bring about some target state of the world, whether or not the overseer recognizes that success. Please invent a complete activity with concrete roles, rules, materials, information asymmetries, rounds, and token-based scoring. It should be genuinely playable in the time available, need no computers or specialized AI knowledge, and make approval-directed behavior emerge naturally rather than having the facilitator announce it. Ideally some students should discover that they can get more approval by producing convincing-looking evidence, managing the overseer’s attention, or concealing inconvenient facts, while other students are rewarded only for an independently checkable outcome.

Give the game a definite design and defend your choices rather than offering several alternatives. Include exactly how to run it from setup through the final round, how the payoff structure differs for the two kinds of agents, what the overseers know and do not know, and how to prevent the whole thing from becoming just a generic lying game. Then provide a debrief script that ties particular events in the game to the conceptual distinction, including questions that help students distinguish bad incentives from deceptive intent. Also tell me, with a committed verdict, which one or two failures are most likely the first time I run it and how I should change the rules to handle them. Finally, be candid about what the game cannot capture about real approval-directed systems, but do not let that caveat turn the activity into a mushy illustration: I want a game whose mechanics make students feel the pull toward optimizing the evaluator rather than the world, even if that simplification leaves something philosophically important out.
