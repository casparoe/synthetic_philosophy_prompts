I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of religion, and in six weeks I'm presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on atonement theory. My paper defends and extends the argument of David Lewis's late paper on penal substitution: roughly, that punishment is only punishment at all when it is deserved by the one who suffers it, that desert is not the kind of thing that can be transferred from one person to another, and that therefore the suffering of an innocent Christ either is not punishment — in which case the doctrine has lost its name and its logic — or is punishment — in which case it is straightforwardly unjust, and no amount of divine authority makes the undeserved deserved. I then defend Lewis's own tentative positive alternative, on which the atonement should be understood not as Christ undergoing our penalty but as Christ, through the incarnation, taking up our life and bearing our sins in a second-personal way, so that what needs undoing about our wrongdoing gets undone from the inside rather than paid off from the outside. I argue this preserves most of what the tradition actually wants — real cost, real solidarity, real undoing of wrong — without the penal machinery.

The audience is the problem. About half the room will be systematic and constructive theologians: several are committed to penal substitution in something like its classic Reformed form, a few hold hybrid Christus Victor or satisfaction accounts, and at least two are openly suspicious that analytic philosophy of religion flattens doctrine by treating it as a set of propositions to be refuted. The other half are philosophers, including one person who works on the metaphysics of moral standing and collective responsibility and will have thought harder than I have about whether desert is really non-transferable, plus a couple of broadly Kantian philosophers who are sympathetic to my premises but will push on whether my positive account does any explanatory work. Nobody in the room is hostile to Christianity; the theologians will just think I've refuted a caricature, and the philosophers will think I've asserted the one premise that needs argument.

Please draft my question-period prep sheet. Give me the objections I will actually get from this specific audience — not the objections an atheist philosopher would raise — ordered by how likely they are to come up and how much damage they'd do. Phrase each one the way that sort of person would actually phrase it: the theologians will reach for Scripture, Anselm, the cry of dereliction, and the charge that I've made retributive justice the whole of justice; the philosophers will reach for vicarious liability, debt and fine-paying analogies, and cases where moral standing does seem to shift between persons. For each objection, tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold, a reply I can give in about two minutes, and where that reply is weakest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, which I suspect is this: given how sketchy Lewis's positive proposal is, what does my second-personal account explain that a plain forgiveness-plus-solidarity account doesn't — and if the answer is nothing, why did we need the machinery?
