I am a retired behavioral ecologist; for thirty years I ran operant-conditioning experiments with pigeons and later with fish, so I know my way around a Skinner box. Lately I have been reading the popular and semi-philosophical literature on the "attention economy," and I keep encountering the same argument, often from people I respect: a smartphone feed is a slot machine. The pull-to-refresh gesture is the lever pull; likes, messages, and new posts are the payouts; these arrive on an unpredictable, variable-ratio schedule, which -- as every textbook says -- produces the most persistent responding and the strongest resistance to extinction; and that is why we cannot put our phones down. The analogy is then put to work: because app designers are essentially running casinos in our pockets, persuasive design should be regulated the way gambling is regulated -- age restrictions, mandated breaks, disclosure of the "odds," perhaps liability for addiction.

I would like an honest audit of this analogy, as if refereeing a grant proposal. Specifically:

1. Lay out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the lever, the payout, the schedule, the wager, and the house. Where does the mapping hold surprisingly well?
2. Identify the disanalogies, and -- this is the part I find missing from the popular treatments -- sort them into those that matter for the regulatory conclusion and those that are merely cosmetic. Some candidates that occur to me from my own bench experience: on a real slot machine the schedule is programmed and stationary, whereas social "rewards" are generated by other people with their own reasons; slot-machine reinforcement is monolithic, but checking a phone serves habit, social obligation, boredom relief, and genuine information-seeking all at once; and slot-machine players wager money they can lose, while the alleged stake here -- attention -- is not obviously a resource one "spends" in the same sense.
3. Say what the analogy quietly imports that it never argues for: in particular, whether it smuggles in a picture of the user as a passive organism in a box, and whether it presupposes a specific (and perhaps outdated) theory of addiction.
4. Finally: does the regulatory conclusion actually survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it? If a better comparison exists for what these platforms do to us -- from ecology, economics, or anywhere else -- propose it and defend it.
