I am a retired physical chemist who has, rather late in life, taken up the philosophy of science, and I attend a monthly reading group where I am frequently out-argued by people who have been at this longer. Last month a dispute arose that I could not adjudicate, and I would like to return to the next meeting better armed. We were discussing whether general relativity's account of the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion genuinely counted in Einstein's favour, given that astronomers had known of the anomaly for decades before he published. One member waved the whole question aside with what he called the Lakatosian point: only 'novel predictions' carry evidential weight, and explaining a fact already on the books is 'mere accommodation' — worthless, or nearly so. When I protested that this standard seemed to strip Darwin of credit for explaining facts about biogeography and comparative anatomy that were known long before 1859, he replied that those cases must simply be accommodated too, and that this is the price of taking scientific method seriously.

I suspect the slogan 'only novel facts count' is smuggling together several quite different claims, and that my fellow reader needs the strong reading but is entitled at most to a weak one. Could you lay out, as a numbered list I can bring to the group, the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing — novel relative to what: the date of the prediction, the background knowledge of the scientific community, the problem the theory was constructed to solve, or the data actually used in deriving the theory? For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or plainly too strong, and whether it is the sort of thing Lakatos's methodology of research programmes actually requires or an overzealous popularisation of it. Then, for each reading, tell me whether it supports the conclusion my fellow reader wanted — that Mercury's precession could not have counted for Einstein — and separately whether it supports the more modest rule of thumb I actually find plausible, namely that a theory cooked up expressly to fit a known anomaly deserves less credit than one that stumbles onto the anomaly as an unprompted consequence. Where the readings diverge on these two verdicts, please say so explicitly rather than letting me conflate them.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — that is, which reading of 'novel facts' is the defensible core of the slogan — and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
