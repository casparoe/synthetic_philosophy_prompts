**Does the Coffee Taste the Same?**

Dennett asks us to imagine two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn, who have worked for the same coffee company for years and both agree the coffee now tastes different to them than it used to. But they disagree about what changed. Chase says the coffee tastes exactly the same as it always did — he just no longer enjoys that taste. His qualia are intact; his reactions have shifted. Sanborn says the opposite: the taste itself has changed, while his standards and preferences are the same as ever. Dennett's point is that nothing — not introspection, not behavioral tests, not neuroscience — could settle which of them is right, and that this shows the notion of a quale as a private, ineffable inner property is bankrupt. If two hypotheses are empirically indistinguishable even to the subject, there's no fact of the matter between them.

The qualia fan has a natural reply, and it's the one I want to scrutinize. Imagine a third character, Reyes, a neuroscientist who scans both men weekly. She finds that in Chase, early gustatory processing is unchanged over the years while downstream reward and valuation circuitry has shifted dramatically. In Sanborn it's the reverse: the early sensory signature has drifted, the valuation circuitry is stable. Doesn't that settle it? Chase's quale stayed put and his reaction changed; Sanborn's quale changed. The indistinguishability Dennett relied on was an artifact of limiting ourselves to armchair introspection.

I think this fails, but for a subtler reason than Dennett himself gives. The problem is that Reyes's distinction between "early sensory processing" and "downstream reaction" is doing all the work, and it is not a distinction the brain hands us for free. Processing is continuous; any cut Reyes makes between "the taste" and "the response to the taste" is a choice, not a discovery. Suppose the cut lands between cortical areas. The qualia fan then needs the quale to live on one specific side of that line — but why there? Whatever boundary you pick, Chase and Sanborn can be redescribed as differing on opposite sides of it, and the question of where the quale really sits becomes exactly as undecidable as the original question of whether the quale changed. Reyes hasn't located the quale; she's just relabeled one region of cortex as its home by fiat.

The deeper point, which I think Dennett undersells, is that the qualia realist is committed to something stronger than ineffability. They're committed to a fact — whether Chase's taste-quale is the same as it was in 1987 — that no possible evidence could reveal, including to Chase himself. That's not a mysterious fact; it's a pseudo-fact wearing the costume of a mysterious one. Genuine ignorance has the shape of "we can't check yet." Qualia talk has the shape of "there is nothing that would count as checking."

One honest concession before I close: there are cases that feel different. If Chase loses his sense of smell in an accident, we don't hesitate to say his experience of coffee changed, full stop — and the difference between that and slowly falling out of love with coffee seems real and important. I don't yet know whether Dennett's argument swallows this distinction too, or whether it spares it, and I'd welcome thoughts on whether that concession costs me the essay.
