So I'm putting together a seminar unit on moral testimony and I want to anchor it in Huck Finn instead of a journal article. Most students only know the standard reading of the raft/turn-Jim-in crisis, where Huck's sympathy beats his corrupt conscience. But I want to read the novel through the epistemology of testimony: Huck never reasoned his way to the belief that helping Jim escape is wicked, he simply absorbed it from everyone around him -- the Widow Douglas, Miss Watson, church, basically the whole antebellum South. So: did Huck *know* it was wrong, given that his belief rests entirely on testimony? If testimony transmits knowledge the way anti-reductionists say, then it seems like he knew, and the novel becomes a study in what happens when moral knowledge is motivationally inert. If you're a skeptic about moral testimony, you have the awkward result that Huck would have been epistemically better off trusting *less* of what he was told, and it was his failure as a testifier-dependent knower that saved Jim. I want a reading that takes both horns seriously, looks at the scene where Huck tears up the letter and resolves to accept damnation (don't quote it, just work from the scene), and asks what the novel shows about the difference between testimony from one person and testimony from an entire corrupt moral community -- which is something I think the phil literature mostly ignores. Also tell me honestly where the novel resists the epistemological reading, e.g. Huck's own confused self-assessment, and don't just flatten the book into an argument.

Structure it like this: "The scene and the standard reading" (short, ~1 para), "Huck's epistemic situation" (~2 paras), "What the testimony debate says back" (~2 paras, reductionist vs anti-reductionist takes), "Where the novel pushes back" (1-2 paras), and "Teaching takeaway" (a few sentences on whether this actually works for the seminar or if I should just assign a paper).
