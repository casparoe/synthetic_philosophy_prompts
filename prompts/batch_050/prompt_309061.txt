So I cover science and ideas for a general-audience outlet, and I've somehow agreed to run a 75-minute workshop at a journalism conference: 18 participants, mostly reporters and editors, no philosophy background. Design a low-tech classroom activity built around an invented breaking-news/field-reporting simulation that makes them feel the difference between a bunch of people who merely want the same thing at the same time and a group that is genuinely doing something together. The important constraint is that the activity should allow the exact same outward sequence of moves under two different rule regimes: one where everyone has an individual assignment and private score, and one where the group has formed a joint plan. Participants should discover that something real seems to have changed even when the observable behavior hasn't: suddenly there can be letting the team down, reminding someone what we decided, having authority to speak for the group, and owing an explanation if you bail. Don't name collective intentionality or any philosopher before the debrief.

Please give me concrete roles, materials, rules, a payoff scheme using points rather than money, and a minute-by-minute timeline. Build in at least three pressure points: an individually tempting defection that only counts as betrayal after the joint commitment; a case where one person has to act before checking with everyone; and a disagreement about whether the group, not just particular members, can change its mind. Include instructions for odd numbers and for participants who try to game the scoring. Then write a debrief that maps likely incidents onto joint intention, shared activity, and group agency without turning into a lecture, plus what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it. End with an honest note about which feature of real collective action the game cannot reproduce, since a newsroom game probably can't capture everything about long-lived institutions or deeply internalized group identitites.
