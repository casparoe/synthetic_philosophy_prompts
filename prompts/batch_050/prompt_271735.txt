Last night I spent like three hours arguing with a friend about whether practical stakes can change whether someone knows something. Her view was basically: if you’d double-check before acting, you didn’t really know yet. Mine was that this makes knowledge depend way too much on temperament. We kept reaching for the stock bank cases and they felt too rigged, so I want the standards first.

Write the desiderata for an adequate theory at the intersection of pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism. Fix the purpose: the theory should tell doctors, pilots, judges, and AI assistants when they’re entitled to treat p as settled for the purposes of action, rather than stop and verify. Don’t start by surveying theories; start with what any adequate view has to explain, with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

Make sure the list handles these pressure points:

- Two agents have exactly the same evidence for p, but only one faces catastrophic costs if p is false. Can one know while the other doesn’t, without making evidence irrelevant?
- The risk falls on someone other than the decision-maker: a surgeon acts on a chart, but the patient bears the cost. Does it matter who gets harmed, who benefits, and who is responsible?
- Two decisions have the same expected cost, but one is reversible and the other isn’t. Should irreversibility itself matter, or only expected loss?
- Merely mentioning a weird error possibility can’t be allowed to destroy knowledge whenever someone feels anxious. So what distinguishes a relevant alternative from a skeptical fantasy?
- If someone says at noon I know the medication is safe, then at five, after the stakes rise, says I didn’t know after all, must one of those utterances be false? Can a theory explain the shift without making the speaker contradict herself?
- Information-gathering and action can be divided across people: a nurse checks the chart, a resident approves the dose, an attending is legally responsible. Whose evidence, stakes, and role set the standard?
- A reckless person may not care about obvious risks, while an obsessive person may care about vanishingly small ones. Should knowledge depend on what the subject actually cares about, what she should care about, or what a reasonable person in her role would care about?
- The theory shouldn’t imply that p itself becomes true or false because the stakes changed, and it shouldn’t collapse knowing p into being justified in acting on p, feeling certain of p, or being permitted to assert p.

Mark which desiderata are non-negotiable and which look like preferences of particular camps. Flag any pair that seems jointly unsatisfiable, because I suspect that’s where the real result is. Then give a brief scorecard for strict invariantism, subject-sensitive invariantism, contextualism, and assessment-relativism—but keep most of the weight on the criteria, not the league table. Also distinguish carefully between saying that the word know changes its semantic standard across contexts and saying that the same relation of knowing becomes harder to stand in when more turns on it.

Aim for maybe 1200-1800 words. At the end, be explicit about how confident you are in the main claims, especially which ones are structural features of the debate and which are contested interpretations, and single out the one requirement or classification you’re least sure of.
