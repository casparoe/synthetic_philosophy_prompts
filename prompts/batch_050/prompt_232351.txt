I've settled on a resolution of Goodman's new riddle of induction, and I'd like its exposure mapped rather than its virtues rehearsed. The resolution, as I hold it, goes like this. A predicate is projectible not because it is entrenched in our actual linguistic practice -- that answer strikes me as either circular or sociological where it should be normative -- but because it figures in the simplest unified systematization of our total evidence. 'Grue' fails, on this view, not because time-indexed predicates are defective as such, but because any theory couched in grue-and-bleen vocabulary is, taken as a whole, less simple and less unified than the same theory couched in green-and-blue vocabulary. Induction is then just inference to the best-systematizing theory, and the new riddle dissolves because the grue-hypothesis loses on the same grounds any bad hypothesis loses.

What would count against this view? I'd like the defeaters sorted by how much damage they do, and I'd like you to distinguish what critics of the view would press from what a committed defender of it would themselves concede as trouble. In particular, please address at least the following candidates, plus any I've missed:

First, the language-dependence worry: if grue is taken as primitive and 'green' is defined derivatively (green before t, blue after), the simplicity comparison seems to reverse or at least become relative to a choice of primitive vocabulary. Does my position have a principled, non-question-begging way to privilege one vocabulary, or does it collapse into the entrenchment answer I was trying to avoid -- simplicity, in practice, being judged relative to the language we happen to speak?

Second, a constructed case I can't get the view to give a comfortable verdict on. Suppose condensed-matter physicists studying a class of compounds find that the genuinely simplest taxonomy of their domain is time-indexed: whether a sample is examined before or after a particular phase-transition date does real classificatory work, and the disjunctive-looking predicate enters their best-confirmed laws. My view seems committed to saying their inductive practice is rational while the emerald-grue reasoner's is not, even though the formal structure of the predicates is the same. Is the relevant asymmetry available to me -- and if it is, does securing it require smuggling in something like naturalness, at which point simplicity is no longer doing the work?

Third, whether the view buys its solution at the price of a worse problem: if induction just is inference to the simplest total systematization, then whatever justifies simplicity-preference in general has to carry the weight of the whole of induction, including the old riddle it was supposed to leave behind. Does this dissolve the new riddle only by inflating the old one?

Finally, an honest assessment of falsifiability: has this position, in the mouths of its actual defenders, been qualified to the point where no case could tell against it -- any recalcitrant predicate being absorbed by adjusting what counts as the simplest overall system? If so, is that a defect for a view of this kind, or exactly what one should expect of a constitutive claim about what induction is?

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which single result, case, or concession would most change my mind.
