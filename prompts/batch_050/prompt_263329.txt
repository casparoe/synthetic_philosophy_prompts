Last night a friend and I got into a long, unresolved argument about animal minds. It started with her dog, which she insists "knows he did something wrong" when she comes home to a shredded cushion, and ended with me defending a version of Lloyd Morgan's canon while she accused me of holding a principle that proves too much. I went home and wrote my position out as a numbered argument, and now I can see her point but not where the argument breaks. I'd like a diagnosis.

Here is the argument as I gave it to her:

(1) In explaining animal behavior, one should never attribute a psychologically higher faculty (reasoning, understanding, guilt, foresight) when the behavior can be adequately explained by a faculty lower on the psychological scale (association, instinct, reinforcement history, hardwired responses). This is just sound parsimony, and it's the same caution we apply elsewhere in science.

(2) A lower-level explanation is "adequate" in the relevant sense whenever it can account for the observable behavior in question.

(3) For any piece of nonhuman animal behavior — the dog's "guilty look," a scrub jay re-caching its food when watched, an octopus unscrewing a jar — some lower-level mechanism can, at least in principle, be specified that would produce exactly that observable behavior. The guilty look may be a conditioned response to the owner's body language; the re-caching may be a fixed action pattern triggered by being observed; and so on.

(4) Therefore, by (1)–(3), we should never attribute higher cognition to any nonhuman animal on the basis of its behavior.

(5) But the reasoning in (1)–(4) doesn't depend on anything specific to nonhuman animals. A preverbal infant's behavior — object permanence displays, pointing, apparent surprise at impossible events — can likewise always be redescribed in terms of lower mechanisms: perceptual habits, conditioned responses, preference for novel stimuli.

(6) So by the same token we should never attribute cognition or understanding to preverbal infants either.

(7) That conclusion is absurd. Infants plainly do understand some things, and developmental science is not committing a methodological sin by saying so.

My friend takes (5)–(7) to be a reductio of (1): she says the canon is an anthropic double standard dressed up as parsimony, and comparative psychology should just abandon it. I find that too drastic — surely "don't reach for the fancy explanation first" is good advice somewhere — but I can't name what, exactly, has gone wrong if (1) is kept.

So: which step do you reject, and why? In your answer, I'd like three things. First, identify where the argument's apparent force comes from — in particular, whether (2) smuggles in a reading of "adequate explanation" weaker than what the canon actually requires, and whether (3)'s "can, at least in principle, be specified" does illicit work that the original methodological caution doesn't license. Second, explain why (5) looks like a fair extension of the reasoning and whether the defender of (1) has a principled way to treat the infant case differently without begging the question in favor of human cognition. Third, tell me what rejecting your chosen step costs: if we weaken (1) to a mere heuristic about explanation quality rather than a ban on higher attributions, do we lose anything worth having in disputes like the one about my friend's dog — and is there a formulation of the canon that keeps its bite in comparative cognition without generalizing to the absurd conclusion in (7)?
