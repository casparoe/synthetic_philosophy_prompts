I write the science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine, and the intellectual tool I reach for most often is Daniel Dennett's distinction, from Darwin's Dangerous Idea, between cranes and skyhooks: explanations that lift understanding by honest, built-from-below work, versus explanatory devices that seem to descend from above and do their lifting magically. Alongside it sits his notion of greedy reductionism — the rush to explain a phenomenon at the lowest available level without paying for the intermediate floors. The trouble is that when I audit my own use of these labels, my verdicts look inconsistent, and I want to know whether a single principle underlies them or whether I am simply applauding conclusions I already liked.

Here are my verdicts on cases I have written about or edited:

1. Natural selection as the account of the vertebrate eye: a crane, and a magnificent one. Each stage is independently evidenced, and no designer is needed at any point.

2. An intelligent designer invoked as the explanation of the same eye: a skyhook, worthless.

3. A feature piece claiming that some modern preference — say, the attraction to open, grassy landscapes — is a hardwired adaptation from our savanna past: greedy, a just-so story. I have spiked such pieces.

4. A physics-based simulation that predicts a protein's folded shape directly from quantum-level interactions, bypassing the standard biochemical vocabulary of folds, chaperones, and pathways: a legitimate crane, even though it vaults clean over several intermediate levels.

5. A philosopher who says consciousness is nothing over and above patterns of neural firing, and that the residual sense of mystery is a cognitive illusion to be explained away: greedy. Something is being skipped, and the skipping is doing the work.

6. A complexity scientist who argues that the 2008 financial crisis cannot be explained by tracing the behavior of banks, traders, and regulators, because market dynamics are emergent and irreducible: a skyhook in secular dress. Here emergence functions as a permission slip to stop explaining.

7. A forensic account of that same crash which actually traces the chain of leveraged positions, margin calls, and fire sales from one institution to the next: a crane, and the best kind of journalism I know.

8. A sociologist who explains the durability of a religious congregation by its function in binding a community, and who declines to reduce this to anything in individual psychology: perfectly fine. It is not reductionist at all, and that is no defect.

The pairs that worry me: 1 and 3 are both adaptationist stories, yet I bless one and spike the other. 4 and 5 both leap across explanatory levels, yet I accept the leap in one case and condemn it in the other. 6 and 7 concern the very same event, and only the roughly reductionist one earns my praise — while 8 earns praise by refusing reduction altogether. So my operative criterion cannot be "reduce" or "do not reduce," and it cannot be "skipping levels is greedy."

What I want is this. First, reconstruct the criterion my verdicts actually embody, if there is one. Then state two or three candidate principles, each precisely enough to sort cases I have not listed, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. Then tell me which of my eight verdicts your best candidate cannot accommodate — I assume at least one must go — and why that one is the right sacrifice. Finally, apply your preferred principle to a case I have not listed: a machine-learning system identifies a molecule that proves to be an effective antibiotic, no one can interpret why the system selected it, and its developers announce that the model has discovered the drug's mechanism. Crane, skyhook, or something else?

Please structure the response in five sections, in this order: "The pattern in my verdicts" (about 100 words, reconstructing my implicit criterion), "Candidate principles" (about 300 words), "The verdict I must give up" (about 150 words), "The antibiotic case" (about 100 words), and "What would change your mind" (about 100 words — name the argument or reclassification of one of my cases that would most plausibly overturn your analysis, and say where your reasoning is most likely to fail).
