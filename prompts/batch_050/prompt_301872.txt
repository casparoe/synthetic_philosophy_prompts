Write a mock accreditation site-visit report, roughly 650 words, in the flat bureaucratic register of an American regional accrediting commission, evaluating a small research school — call it the Manchester Laboratory School — that has implemented the final section of Alan Turing's 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence as a literal educational program. For reference, that section proposes that instead of programming an adult mind directly, one should build what Turing called a child machine — a simple initial structure plus a program, analogous to hereditary material — and bring it to competence through an education process rather than through further construction. Turing's sketch includes: instruction delivered through deliberately impersonal channels, such as a teleprinter, so that nothing about the teacher's presence intrudes; behavior shaped by a system of rewards and punishments; deviations from expected behavior treated as potentially valuable variations, to be preserved or discarded by the experimenter's judgment the way a breeder selects; the suggestion that this selection might eventually be delegated to another machine to speed things along; and the concessions that such a pupil, having no legs, cannot be sent out to fetch the coal, and that it could hardly attend an ordinary school since the other children would make fun of it.

The report should use the standard apparatus — mission and governance, quality of instruction, assessment practices, student conduct and discipline, student life and wellbeing, summary of ratings, recommendations — and should land on a middling verdict such as accredited with stipulations. The comedy must come from the inspector applying the deadpan language of educational oversight to this proposal taken at face value: the registrar cannot determine where teaching ends and programming begins, and records both under enrollment; discipline consists exclusively of reward and punishment signals, with predictable effects on the pupil's initiative; the admissions office's selective retention of promising variants doubles as the school's entire pedagogical theory; the plan to automate all grading by delegating it to another machine raises a staffing question no one on site can answer; the pupil's lack of legs is documented as a facilities accommodation. The genuine philosophical difficulties — the difference between learning and being programmed, whether reward and punishment alone can yield originality, whether selection by an experimenter counts as education at all, what embodiment does to schooling — should be audible in every finding without ever being stated as objections. The report must never break character, the satire should target the proposal rather than Turing, and a reader who knows the paper should feel that the satire is fair.
