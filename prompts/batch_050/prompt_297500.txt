Two economists in my department have been going at it for two years — call them S (price-control skeptic, price-theory-first, thinks the competitive model is the right default and that long-run supply responses are where the real action is) and D (defender of at least some price controls, thinks search frictions and bargaining power mean the competitive model often isn't even the right benchmark, and that distribution counts, not just efficiency). Their chair finally forced them into an adversarial collaboration covering BOTH minimum wages and rent control at once — the idea being that any framework they produce has to handle a price floor and a price ceiling with the same tools, no special pleading for whichever policy you happen to like.

Draft the joint statement they should come back with. Specifically:

1. Agreed claims (numbered, 10-15): things both can actually sign without wincing. I'd expect these to include stuff like: the supply-and-demand diagram is a benchmark whose applicability is an empirical question, not a theorem; incidence of either policy depends on elasticities and frictions, not on who the law nominally targets; short-run and long-run effects of rent control are different objects; a wage floor and a rent ceiling aren't mirror images because employers and landlords have different adjustment margins; both policies partly transfer to incumbents at the expense of outsiders and future market participants; effects show up on non-price margins (hours, scheduling, benefits, training, quality, maintenance, conversions) and not just the headline quantity. But don't let them get away with vacuous agreed claims either — nothing both sides "agree" on should be something a random econ undergrad couldn't plausibly dispute.

2. Disagreements table: the remaining disputes, each row giving the issue, S's position worded so S would accept it, D's position worded so D would accept it, and what evidence or argument each says would move them. No strawmen. D gets to say employer market power in low-wage labor markets is widespread without S adding a sneer-word, and S gets to say rent control's long-run costs are large and well documented without D inserting "allegedly".

3. Cruxes: the 4-6 sub-questions where, if you settled them, at least one side would actually have to shift. I suspect the list includes how widespread employer market power in low-wage labor markets really is, whether housing supply in expensive cities is inelastic enough that a ceiling's textbook harms shrink, whether the people helped by each policy are the intended beneficiaries or better-off incumbents, and whether minimum wage effects hide on margins the headline employment number misses. For each crux, point to a study or natural experiment BOTH accept as bearing on it — the 1994 New Jersey/Pennsylvania fast-food comparison and the later payroll-records critique of it, the county-border-pairs designs, the Seattle study and its hours results, the San Francisco study that exploited the 1994 extension of rent control to smaller multifamily buildings, the bunching-at-the-new-wage methodology — and write down each side's reading of it, plus what result would have embarassed them.

4. A short closing section on whether the two policies stand or fall together: does holding D's view of labor markets and S's view of housing hang together, or is it incoherent? They disagree about this too, so give both answers in their own words.

Format: sections 1-4 as above, with section 2 as an actual table. The document's tone should be dry and professional even though these two clearly still don't like each other, and don't make them converge more than real people would.
