---
Historical Jesus scholars use several criteria of authenticity to sift the traditions preserved in the Gospels. The criterion of multiple attestation says that if a saying or event shows up in more than one source, it is more likely to be historical, since independent witnesses are unlikely to have invented the same thing. For example, the Lord's Prayer appears in both Matthew and Luke, so it passes this criterion with two independent witnesses.

The criterion of embarrassment holds that material the early church would have found awkward is unlikely to have been made up. Jesus' baptism by John is the standard example, since it could be read as implying that John was the greater figure, yet the tradition kept it anyway.

The criterion of dissimilarity, sometimes called double dissimilarity, counts material as authentic when it is dissimilar both to the Judaism of Jesus' day and to the concerns of the later church, since it can't easily be explained as borrowed from either side. It follows that material which can be paralleled in either Jewish tradition or church teaching cannot be attributed to Jesus himself.

Finally, the criterion of coherence allows scholars to accept further material once it fits with the core established by the first three criteria.

The criteria approach has been heavily criticized. Critics argue that the criteria are circular: to judge what coheres with Jesus' message, we already need to know what his message was. Others point out that dissimilarity, applied strictly, produces a Jesus cut off from both the Judaism he came from and the church that followed him, which is historically implausible. Research on memory has also shown that ancient communities did not preserve traditions verbatim but constantly reshaped them, so there are no uninterpreted "authentic" nuggets sitting there waiting to be extracted.

My conclusion is that the criteria cannot settle who the historical Jesus was. Since memory reshapes everything it touches and the criteria are circular, all we actually have are the church's portraits, and any reconstruction is really just a projection of the scholar doing it. Firm historical judgments about Jesus are therefore impossible, and the quest should be abandoned.
