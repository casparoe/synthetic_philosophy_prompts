Write an essay on this twist on Williamson's anti-luminosity argument. Imagine that in 2045 people can get a neural implant that lets them perfectly read off their own occurrent mental states: whenever you want, you can just check whether you're in pain, whether you feel cold, whether you believe that p, and so on. Call this "luminosity on demand". Does the implant restore the KK principle -- i.e. that if you know that p, you're in a position to know that you know that p? Reach a verdict and defend it. Along the way you should: (1) explain, in your own words, why the safety / margin-for-error considerations are supposed to make ordinary mental states non-luminous in the first place, using a gradual-change case (slowly waking up, water slowly cooling, etc.); (2) ask whether the implant genuinely escapes those considerations or merely relocates them one level up -- after all, you now have to know that the implant says you're in state S, and it's not obvious that that higher-order state is itself luminous; (3) consider whether the answer might differ between phenomenal states (pain) and epistemic states (knowing). Don't end on a shrug -- a conditional verdict like "KK comes back for states of type X but not type Y" is fine, but "it's complicated" is not.
