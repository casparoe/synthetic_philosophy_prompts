Write a sestina about the mere addition argument in population ethics -- the chain of comparisons that leads from a world of many people living excellent lives (call it A) to a vastly larger world in which every life is barely worth living (call it Z), where each step in the chain looks like an improvement or at worst a wash. The poem should assume the standard version of the argument: A becomes A+ by the sheer addition of extra people with lives worth living, at no cost to anyone already alive; A+ becomes B by a redistribution that leaves the worst-off much better and the best-off only slightly worse; and the same two moves, addition and equalization, are then repeated until only Z remains.

Constrain the poem as follows:

1. Use the strict form: six stanzas of six lines, the standard rotating permutation of the six end-words, and a three-line envoi containing all six.

2. Choose six end-words that are ordinary evaluative vocabulary rather than technical terms -- words like "worth," "more," "enough," and "better" are the right register, though the choice is yours. At least one end-word should undergo a visible change of valence across the poem: a word that functions as unambiguous praise in the early stanzas should, by sheer force of repetition and recontextualization, read as an accusation by the final stanza. The form's compulsory recurrence should do the work of showing how a judgment that is locally innocent at every step compounds into something monstrous.

3. Map the six stanzas onto six stages of the chain, including A, A+, B, and Z among them; the fourth and fifth stanzas should evoke the long, elided middle in which the two moves have been iterated past counting. Each stanza's stage should be legible to a specialist: the reader should be able to tell, from the stanza alone, which comparison is being enacted and why it seemed harmless.

4. The envoi is the only position in the form where all six end-words coexist, and you should exploit this: it should be the moment when A and Z are finally held in a single field of vision, which is precisely the vantage from which the conclusion bites, since no stepwise comparison ever reveals it. The envoi should confront the standard menu of escapes -- rejecting the transitivity of "all things considered better than," denying that mere addition is really harmless, or simply accepting the conclusion -- and it should end on one of them without pretending the choice is comfortable.

5. The poem must never name the conclusion it dramatizes, and must avoid all jargon of the field. A reader versed in the debate should recognize the argument from the poem's structure alone; a reader innocent of it should simply find a good poem about a city that kept adding rooms.

Accuracy matters: a philosopher who works on population ethics should be able to check each stanza against the corresponding step and find it right, including the asymmetry that makes the argument hard -- that the first move harms no one, the second move helps almost everyone, and yet the destination is worse than the origin.
