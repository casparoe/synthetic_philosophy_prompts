I'm in a reading group on moral psychology and we just spent two sessions on psychopathy and criminal responsibility. The whole conversation kept circling around the phrase "they know right from wrong" -- as in, psychopaths know right from wrong, so they don't deserve any excuse, unlike someone in a psychotic episode. People on both sides of the argument used the exact same phrase, which made me suspect it was doing different work in different mouths. One person said it to argue psychopaths should be punished fully, since they can recite the rules and predict what'll get them arrested; another said it to argue the opposite, that there's a sense in which psychopaths plainly do NOT know right from wrong, since they don't seem to grasp why the rules matter or feel anything toward their victims.

So here's what I want: spell out the different precise claims that phrase could be making, from the most trivial to the most demanding. Some candidate readings I half-see myself: being able to state the norms, being able to predict social or legal consequences, understanding that an act is wrong as opposed to merely punished, grasping the reasons that make it wrong (e.g. seeing the victim's suffering as a reason), being motivationally moved by that knowledge, being capable of guilt afterward. There are probably more, and some of mine might collapse into each other. For each reading, say whether it's trivial or substantive, whether the research on psychopaths (blunted affect, intact verbal performance, failure on stuff like the moral/conventional distinction) would count them as knowing or not knowing, and whether that reading would actually support the conclusion it was being used for -- excuse or no excuse. I'd especially like to know which reading the "punish them fully" side implicitly needs, and whether they can actually have it, given that the same reading might excuse people we clearly don't want to excuse. Lay it out as a menu I can bring to the group, not a single verdict.
