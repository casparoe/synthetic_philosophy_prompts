Working on a philosophy of religion essay and my study guide summarizes Hume on miracles like this: 'Hume defines a miracle as a violation of a law of nature. But laws of nature are just exceptionless regularities weve all observed, so a miracle is by definition something that contradicts the totality of human experience. That makes the very idea of evidence for a miracle incoherent: any testimony for a miracle faces the entire weight of the uniform experience that established the law, and can never win. So Hume proved miracles are impossible, and belief in them is always irrational.'

I could swear from lecture that Hume's official conclusion was more modest, and that he spends a big chunk of the essay on the track record of human testimony rather than just definitions, which this summary skips entirely. But the guide was written by someone with a PhD, so maybe I'm misremembering. Which parts of this would Hume actually endorse, which parts misstate or overstate his argument, and does the conclusion about irrationality follow from the parts he'd accept? One more thing: if the summary's reading were right, wouldn't Hume's long discussion of actual miracle reports (the healings at the Abbé Paris's tomb and so on) be a complete waste of time, since no report could ever matter? That seems like a bad sign for the summary, but tell me if I'm wrong.
