Ok so I keep circling a passage from chapter 25 of The Prince (I'm reading the W. K. Marriott translation). Machiavelli writes:

"But a man is not often found sufficiently circumspect to know how to accommodate himself to the change, both because he cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to, and also because, having always prospered by acting in one way, he cannot be persuaded that it is well to leave it; and, therefore, the cautious man, when it is time to turn adventurous, does not know how to do it, hence he is ruined; but had he changed his conduct with the times fortune would not have changed."

Build me a thought experiment that stress-tests that last conditional -- "had he changed his conduct with the times fortune would not have changed." Specifically: construct a case in which an agent (a) correctly reads that the times have changed, using only information genuinely available to him at the moment of decision, (b) actually does the thing the inflexible men in the passage can't do, i.e. abandons the mode of action that had always made him prosper, and (c) is ruined precisely because he changed, in a world where sticking to his old way would in fact have saved him. Constraints: a realistic contemporary setting (a company, a hospital, an election campaign -- no princes, no armies, no mercenaries); the agent has to be genuinely competent, not a fool who panics at the first sign of trouble; the ruin has to flow from the change itself and not from some unrelated piece of bad luck that would've sunk him either way; and keep the scenario itself to one paragraph.

Then walk me through the case. What does Machiavelli's account say about this agent before the outcome is known, and what is it forced to say after? What I'm really trying to get at is whether "conforming one's conduct to the spirit of the times" can be specified independantly of success -- because if Machiavelli can always reply, after the fact, that my agent merely *thought* he was changing with the times but was actually misreading them, the theory starts to look unfalsifiable, and if he can't say that, then the passage's conditional seems to be just false. Tell me which feature of your case is doing the philosophical work, what happens to the argument if that feature gets removed, and how someone defending Machiavelli would attack the construction. One more thing: does the verdict change depending on whether we read "fortune" in the passage as brute outcome-luck versus something more like the objective structure of the situation the agent finds himself in?
