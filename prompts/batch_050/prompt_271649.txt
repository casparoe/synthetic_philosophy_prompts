ok so i'm in an argument that keeps happening. people around me keep ending debates about whether LLMs are "really intelligent" by dropping "intelligence is what intelligence tests measure" -- sometimes said smugly, like its obviously a cheap dodge that dissolves the whole question, sometimes said approvingly, like its the hard-won operationalist insight that settles it. i genuinely can't tell which claim is supposed to be on the table.

take that one-liner apart for me. lay out the distinct precise theses it could be making, as a menu: at minimum i can see a tautology reading (true by definition, says nothing), an operational-definition reading (we're just stipulating what we mean by the word), a construct-validity reading (the tests happen to track the real thing), and a deflationary reading (there is no "real thing" beyond performance on tests, so stop asking). feel free to add readings i've missed. for each one, give a verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible, or false -- and note which of these the smug use and the approving use would each need it to be.

the part i actually care about: which reading, if any, could do the work the slogan gets asked to do in the LLM argument -- namely, blocking the move "sure, it aces every benchmark we give it, but that's not *real* intelligence"? does any defensible reading actually block that move, or does the slogan only work by sliding between two readings mid-argument?

one more thing i keep wondering and would like answered straight: if you take the deflationary reading at full strength, does the slogan eat itself? the sentence "intelligence is what intelligence tests measure" is not itself something any test measures, so by its own lights it's either meaningless or it's smuggling in exactly the kind of non-operational theorizing it forbids. tell me if that objection lands or if there's a way out.
