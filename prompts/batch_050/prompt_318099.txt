So I keep bumping into the so-called gender-equality paradox — the finding that countries ranking *higher* on gender equality tend to have *fewer* women in STEM (and in some measures more gender-typical career choices overall, like Finland and Norway being more occupationally segregated than, say, Algeria or the UAE). I'm collecting proposed explanations for it and my list is getting long. Here's what I already have, so please don't repeat any of these:

1. The artifact story: the headline stat (women's share of STEM *graduates*) is compositional — when overall female university enrollment is high, the denominator math mechanically shrinks the STEM share even if absolute numbers of women in STEM are fine. The paradox is partly a statistical mirage.
2. The economic-security story: in poorer or less egalitarian countries STEM is one of the few reliable routes to a decent income, so women pick it instrumentally; in rich welfare states the penalty for following your interests instead is small, so choices drift toward preferences.
3. The self-expression story: affluence plus post-materialist values turns career choice into identity expression, and since gendered self-concepts are still around, freer choice paradoxically produces *more* gender-typical choices.
4. The essentialism story: egalitarian countries often pair formal equality with strong "men and women are naturally different but equal" beliefs — equality gets framed as free choice between complementary roles, so the culture pushes against what the institutions push for.
5. The comparative-advantage story: girls on average are relatively stronger in reading than in math compared to boys, and people choosing freely pick their comparative strength, not their absolute one.

Give me one more explanation whose underlying mechanism is genuinely different from all five of these — not a rewording, not a special case, and not just "a bit of 2 plus a bit of 3." It has to explain the *cross-country* pattern specifically (why the gradient runs that way, not just why STEM is male-dominated somewhere or other). Then tell me: (a) at least one observable prediction your explanation makes that the five above don't, and (b) honestly, whether you think it's any good or whether it collapses into one of the listed ones on inspection. Also: I don't want to rehash whether the effect itself is real — just grant it for the sake of the exercise.
