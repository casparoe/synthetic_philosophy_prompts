I'm a high school senior doing an independent study in philosophy of mind, and my adviser assigned me Ryle's attack on Cartesian dualism. In the textbook summary I read, dualism is criticized by comparing its picture of the mind to a ghost inside a machine: the body is treated as the machine, and the mind as an invisible occupant who drives it from within. The book presents this comparison as if it settles the matter on its own -- once you see the dualist picture as a ghost in a machine, you're supposed to see that it's confused. I'm skeptical that an analogy can do that much work, and my adviser suggested I write a paper auditing it. I'd like your help thinking through the audit before I draft.

Here's what I'd like. First, lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the ghost, what plays the role of the machine, what plays the role of the ghost's inhabiting and steering, and what, in the actual dualist view, corresponds to the ghost being a separate thing that could in principle survive the machine's destruction. Second, identify where the mapping breaks. In particular: an ordinary ghost-in-a-machine story is a story about one physical thing hidden inside another, but is the dualist actually committed to that, or does the comparison mislocate the dualist's claim from the start? I also want to know whether the analogy smuggles in the conclusion -- whether calling the mind a "ghost" already assumes that anything non-physical must be spooky and illegitimate, so that the rhetorical force comes from the labeling rather than from an argument. Third, connect this to Ryle's own diagnosis, as I understand it: his charge is that dualists made a category mistake by treating the mind as the same kind of thing as the body, just made of stranger stuff. Does the ghost-in-the-machine picture accurately capture that mistake, or is it a caricature of a slightly different view -- and if the dualist says "I never claimed the mind is a hidden object at all," how much of Ryle's critique survives? Finally, a twist my adviser raised: modern talk of the mind as "software running on the hardware of the brain" is extremely common and treated as the respectable alternative. Does that analogy inherit the same category-mistake structure in disguise, or does it genuinely avoid it? Give me your verdict on how far the ghost-in-the-machine comparison can actually carry an argument, and where a critic of Ryle could push back.
