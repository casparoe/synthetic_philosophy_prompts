I run a decision theory reading group for philosophy undergrads -- about 14 people, one 75-minute session, and this is the meeting right before we do the formal decision theory stuff, so nobody's seen EDT/CDT formally yet. I want an in-class activity where they *feel* the pull of paying in a counterfactual mugging, and then the queasiness of XOR blackmail, before they ever hear either term.

Quick gloss so we're on the same page about what the activity has to recreate: in a counterfactual mugging, a trustworthy being flips a coin and tells you that had it landed the other way he'd have given you a million, but it landed this way, so would you please hand over $100 -- you get nothing either way, and yet something in you wants to pay. In XOR blackmail, someone sends you a letter saying they know whether your house has termites, and that they sent the letter iff "you have termites" and "you'd pay them off" don't match -- so paying feels like buying good news about a fact you can't influence.

The hard constraint: there's no Omega in my classroom and no perfect predictor. I want the "prediction" in the game to be something I can genuinely do -- e.g., a personality-ish questionnaire I hand out at the *previous* session and then use to forecast how each student will play, or predictions about each player made by classmates who know them. Design around that limitation rather than pretending it away.

What I need from you:
- Full rules and materials, simple enough to run off an index card. Stakes small but real (a chocolate bar, points on some meaningless-but-competitive semester leaderboard, whatever works).
- Ideally ONE underlying mechanism that, depending on what information gets revealed when, produces both a counterfactual-mugging-shaped decision and an XOR-blackmail-shaped decision, so the group sees the two problems share a skeleton. If one mechanism can't do both cleanly, say why and give me two linked activities instead.
- A debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the two thought experiments, and tells me exactly when to name them -- my instinct is to hold off until the XOR part has left everyone grumpy.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how to handle it.
- An honest note on which feature the activity can't reproduce. My specific worry: paying because a fallible human "predictor" probably got you right is just smart betting on my grading skills, not updateless reasoning, and the sharper students will definately say so. Tell me how the debrief should answer them.
