I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the ethics of automation, and for our session on responsibility gaps I want to build the meeting around a single case: the March 2018 crash in Tempe, Arizona, in which an Uber prototype self-driving vehicle, operating in autonomous mode with a human safety operator behind the wheel, struck and killed a pedestrian, Elaine Herzberg, as she crossed a multi-lane road at night pushing a bicycle. Please treat the following as the settled case materials rather than trying to recall further details: the vehicle's sensors first registered Herzberg roughly six seconds before impact, but the software's classification of what it was seeing shifted during the approach, and the system concluded that emergency braking was needed only about 1.3 seconds before impact; the Volvo's own automatic emergency braking was disabled while the test software was driving, on the design theory that the human operator was the fallback; interior video showed the operator looking down, away from the road, in the seconds before impact, and investigators concluded she had been distracted; prosecutors charged the operator with negligent homicide (she later pleaded guilty to a lesser offense and received probation), ruled that Uber bore no criminal responsibility, and Uber settled with the family and suspended its Arizona testing shortly after the crash.

Acting as a philosopher who works on moral responsibility and machine ethics, analyze this episode through the lens of the responsibility-gap thesis: the claim that increasingly autonomous systems produce outcomes for which no human is appropriately responsible, because the standard conditions of responsibility — control, knowledge or reasonable foreseeability, and the right kind of causal involvement — come apart. Be disciplined about the mapping rather than gesturing at it: name the candidate bearers of responsibility (the safety operator, the engineers who designed the fallback arrangement, Uber's management, the state that permitted the testing, and arguably the vehicle itself) and say precisely which conditions each candidate fails or satisfies. Then run the analysis in the other direction, because many philosophers' first reaction to Tempe is that it exhibits no gap at all — just an old-fashioned story of organizational corner-cutting with a scapegoated employee — so address directly whether the case confirms the gap thesis, dissolves it into ordinary diffusion of responsibility within an organization, or reveals that the thesis was always about something narrower, such as blame and punishment rather than accountability or liability. Think carefully about the distinction between no one being responsible and no one having been held responsible; I suspect the case trades on it.

Give me the analysis as exactly five numbered items, each no more than three sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the mapping of candidate bearers onto the standard responsibility conditions; (2) the operator's culpability and what moral luck does to it; (3) whether the corporation and the regulator fill the gap or merely redistribute it; (4) where the responsibility-gap lens misdescribes or fails to reach the case; (5) your verdict on what Tempe shows about the gap thesis itself.
