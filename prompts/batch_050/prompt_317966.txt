I'm a high school student preparing a debate case on public monuments, and I keep noticing a pattern I can't find a word for. Two examples. First: a city debates removing a statue of a Confederate general, and it emerges that most of the statue's loudest defenders know almost nothing about the man; their argument is simply that the statue has always been there. Second: a street named after a colonial official, where neither the people demanding the change nor the people defending the name can say who he was, and when a local historian lays out his biography, both sides agree it's beside the point. In both cases the object seems to be defended not as a commemoration of anyone but as a piece of the scenery, valuable because of its age and familiarity rather than its subject. This isn't quite the standard heritage-versus-hate framing, since that at least claims the monument honors something (a regional identity, say); here the defenders disavow any interest in what is commemorated. And it isn't simple status quo bias, because the same people cheerfully supported demolishing an old parking garage across the street. Does this configuration -- defending a commemorative object while being indifferent to, or ignorant of, what it commemorates -- have an established name in the literature on public memory? If so, how is it distinguished from ordinary attachment to heritage and from sheer inertia? If not, what would you call it, and where are its boundaries: does it require ignorance or merely indifference, and does it matter how long the object has stood there?
