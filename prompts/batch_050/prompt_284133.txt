I'm a third-year PhD student and next month I'm giving a talk in our department's works-in-progress series defending a hard line on criminal punishment: nobody ever deserves to suffer for what they've done in the basic, backward-looking sense, so retribution is off the table entirely, but we can still lock people up when they're genuinely dangerous the same way we'd quarantine someone carrying a dangerous disease. I've also bolted on a piece that's more my own -- I argue that the state owes offenders the same thing it owes the quarantined: the burden of proof on the detainer, the least-restrictive conditions compatible with safety, and active efforts at reintegration, and I claim this package is actually tougher on the state than most retributive systems, not softer on offenders. So I'm not just doing the standard "blame no one" thing, I'm trying to make the whole structure ride on the quarantine analogy carrying real weight.

The audience is the problem. It's a mixed room: two criminal law profs who are pretty openly retributivist-leaning, a handful of compatibilists who think people like me throw out something Dennett-style approaches manage to keep, at least one act-utilitarian who will think my constraints are sentimental, and a bunch of grad students who smell blood at these events. The criminology person who sometimes shows up will definitely know the actual recidivism-prediction literature better than I do. I have forty minutes of Q&A and my advisor says I got visibly rattled last time someone pushed me on a premise.

Prep me for the question period. Lead with the one-sentence bottom line -- which objection I should most fear and why -- then the rest. I want the objections I'll actually get from this specific room, phrased the way each person would phrase them, ranked by how likely they are times how damaging they'd be. For each: my best short reply, and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what to concede up front in the talk itself to defuse things, and flag the question I'm hoping nobody asks -- I suspect it's the one about what my view says about the plainly innocent person who's just very dangerous, but maybe there's a worse one I'm not seeing. Also tell me whether the compatibilists' "you could have kept responsibility talk and gotten everything you want" objection deserves more respect than I've been giving it.
