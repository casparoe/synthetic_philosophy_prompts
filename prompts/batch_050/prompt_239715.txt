Here's the passage I keep coming back to: in chapter 50 of the Han Feizi (usually translated as "Eminence in Learning"), Han Feizi argues roughly that a ruler shouldn't govern by hoping for men of rare self-cultivated virtue -- the Zeng Shens and Shi Qus of the world are maybe one in ten thousand -- but should instead arrange laws, rewards, and punishments so that the average person can't profit from doing wrong. Take that as the load-bearing claim.

Now the actual question: suppose you want a theory of political order that takes Han Feizi's wager seriously, i.e. a theory of how to design a state that works for people as they actually are, not as moral education might someday make them. Write the desiderata such a theory has to meet. For each requirement give the case or consideration that motivates it. Some things I suspect belong on the list but want you to adjudicate: it has to work when the ruler himself is mediocre, not just when subjects are; it has to say what keeps the people administering the incentives honest (who punishes the punishers?); it has to handle the fact that incentive systems get gamed by exactly the clever unvirtuous people they're built for; it has to explain how laws stay legible at scale; and it has to survive contact with the awkward historical fact that the Qin ran something close to this program and collapsed in about fifteen years.

For each item: is it a genuine desideratum or just a prejudice of the Confucian (or liberal constitutionalist) camp judging Legalism from outside? Mark which are non-negotiable and which are negotiable. And if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable -- e.g., can you have a ruler-proof system that also concentrates the two handles of reward and punishment in the ruler? -- say so, that's the part I care most about.
