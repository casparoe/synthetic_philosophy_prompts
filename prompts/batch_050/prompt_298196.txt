I'm in the second year of a history degree and we've spent the last few weeks on the French Revolution, and honestly the historiography is getting to me more then the events themselves. As far as I can tell the debate has been basically frozen since the 1980s. On one side you've got the old social interpretation, Lefebvre and Soboul and that whole tradition, where the Revolution is a bourgeois class rising up against a feudal aristocracy, driven by deep economic shifts. On the other side the revisionists, Cobban and then Furet, who pointed out that the "bourgeoisie" doing the revolting were mostly lawyers and state officials rather than industrial capitalists, that class interest can't explain the Terror, and that the Revolution was really made by political discourse and circumstance once the state broke down. And then off to the side there's Tocqueville saying the whole thing changed less than everyone assumes because the centralizing state was already built under the monarchy, plus people like Skocpol arguing it's really about state crisis and international pressure and not about class at all. Everyone seems to know all the same archival facts. Nobody has moved in decades. The debate just sort of stopped, with each camp writing textbooks that acknowledge the other side in a footnote and carry on.

My hypothesis, which I got into a stupidly long argument about with a friend who does sociology, is that this was never really a factual disagreement in the first place. I think the sides disagree about what an explanation of a revolution is even supposed to look like. The social interpretation wants deep structural causes and thinks "the discourse did it" just describes the mechanism rather than the cause. The revisionists think big structural stories are unfalsifiable moral fables, and that only agents with ideas actually do things in history. And the contingency people seem to think the whole project of explaining 1789 is a category error, that the storming of the Bastille isn't the kind of thing with a "cause" in that sense. So they're answering different questions under one name. Is that diagnosis right? And if it is, is there anything at all that would count as progress here -- a new archive, a decent model of how crowds and patronage networks actually mobilize, some sharp conceptual distinction that dissolves the thing -- or is this one of those debates where "progress" just means each generation picking whichever story flatters it's politics?

One more thing I'd like folded into the answer: the "revolution devours its own children" pattern -- the Girondins, Danton, the Hébertists, eventually Robespierre himself. Is that a datum any adequate theory of revolution has to explain (some people claim it's a structural feature of revolutions in general, not just bad luck with personalities), or is it a narrative trope we've imposed afterwards because it makes a satisfying arc? Whether either the social interpretation or the revisionists can actually account for the Terror strikes me as the one place where this debate could have been settled and conspicuously wasn't, so if there's a fault line in the whole dispute, I'd guess it's there -- but tell me if I'm wrong about that too.
