My roommate took a philosophy of mind seminar and got obsessed with the Orwellian vs. Stalinesque distinction from the color phi debate -- roughly: when someone's report of an experience doesn't match what "really happened," did they genuinely experience it and then have their memory rewritten (Orwellian), or was the false content confabulated before it ever became an experience at all (Stalinesque)? I've been quizzing her with cases and here's how she's sorted them so far:

1. Classic color phi: green dot flashed, then a red dot nearby ~50ms later, subject reports the dot moving and changing color midway. Her verdict: no fact of the matter which kind of revision happened.
2. A subject sees a dot, and a minute later a scientist scrambles their memory of its color. Verdict: Orwellian, obviously.
3. A subject is hypnotized beforehand to "remember" a dot that was never shown, and sincerely reports it. Verdict: Stalinesque, obviously.
4. A subject is asked mid-experiment (before any second stimulus arrives) what they just saw, answers, and then later misremembers their own answer. Verdict: Orwellian -- the mid-experiment probe fixed what the experience was.

New case for her: suppose the color phi illusion somehow still worked with the dots flashed a full two seconds apart (bear with me), and nobody probes the subject during the gap. Same sincere report of motion and color change. Predict her verdict, figure out which rule she's actually running on cases 1-4 (I can think of at least two that fit: one about how short the revision window is, one about whether anything probed or fixed the content), and tell me what case would force her to pick between them.
