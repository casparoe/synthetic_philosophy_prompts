I sit on the ethics committee of a secular hospital network, and several times a month we face cases in which a patient, relative, or clinician grounds a demand in a claim that God commands or forbids something — refusing blood products, declining to participate in withdrawal of care, objecting to a discharge plan. We have about thirty minutes per case, none of us is a philosopher, and our recommendation goes to the chief medical officer. I want a reusable procedure built on a passage from Plato's Euthyphro (Jowett translation), where Euthyphro states his rule for when prosecution is a sacred duty: "The real question is whether the murdered man has been justly slain. If justly, then your duty is to let the matter alone; but if unjustly, then even if the murderer lives under the same roof with you and eats at the same table, proceed against him." The rule's action-guiding part is perfectly clean, but everything turns on its antecedent — whether the act was just — which the rule does nothing to settle, and the dialogue's later, famous question (whether the pious is loved by the gods because it is pious, or pious because it is loved) suggests that appealing to what God commands only relocates that antecedent rather than answering it. Turn this into a checklist we can run on any conscience- or command-based claim: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, how to separate the factual components of the claim (which we can send out for evidence) from the evaluative antecedent (which we cannot), what the committee should do when the claimant offers divine command as settling the evaluative part, where the procedure stops with a recommendation and where it must hand off to individual judgment, and its known failure modes. Run it on two cases — an adult Jehovah's Witness refusing a transfusion, and a nurse who says God forbids her to assist in a terminal extubation — including one application where the procedure yields a result a committee member will dislike, and state plainly what the procedure cannot decide. Give the whole response as a numbered list of exactly ten items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
