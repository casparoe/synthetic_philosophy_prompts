Write a dialogue between two philosophers of biology, both equally sharp, disputing where the boundary of the organism lies. One defends an integration-first criterion: a biological individual is whatever functions as a coordinated whole with a shared fate, so a Portuguese man o' war, an ant colony, and a coral–algae holobiont all qualify to the degree that their parts cannot persist or reproduce independently. The other defends a bottleneck criterion: genuine organismhood requires a life cycle that periodically passes through a single cell (or very few cells), because only such a bottleneck suppresses within-collective selection and aligns the parts' evolutionary interests; on this view the man o' war may pass but the ant colony and the holobiont are at most organism-like, since their parts retain separable reproductive futures. Have them argue over a single hard case of your devising that sits between their paradigms (for instance, a clonal colony in which worker-like units are sterile but genetically variable due to somatic mutation), with each pressing the other on whether their criterion is a definition, an empirical hypothesis, or a covertly normative choice about which biological processes matter. Neither should caricature the other; each should answer the other's actual objections, concede at least one cost of their own view, and end by stating what observation or experiment, if any, would settle the dispute rather than merely reflect it.
