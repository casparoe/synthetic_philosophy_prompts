Write a transcript of a support-group session for superseded scientific theories -- phlogiston, the luminiferous ether, caloric, Ptolemaic astronomy, and the miasma theory of disease -- meeting in a sort of halfway house for discarded ontologies, facilitated by an earnest, slightly out-of-her-depth social worker who keeps trying to get them to process their feelings about being 'approximately true.' Around 700 words, formatted as a meeting transcript with the facilitator's session notes at the end.

The humor should come from the actual philosophy of science, not from generic jokes about being wrong. Fresnel-era ether theory should be the smug one: it got its equations carried over into the successor theory, and it lords this over the others -- this is the structural realist's favorite case, so lean on it. Caloric should nurse a complicated grievance, since the mathematics of heat flow survived even though the fluid didn't. Phlogiston should have nothing, and know it, and resent the suggestion that being 'in the vicinity of the truth' is any comfort. Ptolemy should deliver, deadpan, the line that makes every realist in the room wince: his model still works, for a certain definition of works, and that is exactly the problem with 'it works' as an argument. The session should stage, without naming them, the no-miracles intuition, the pessimistic meta-induction (the theories have all read the historical record and quote their own replacement dates the way inmates quote sentences), the divide-et-impera strategy of splitting theories into the parts that were retained and the parts that weren't, and the suspicion that 'retention' is only ever judged with hindsight, by the winners.

Stay deadpan throughout: the facilitator treats 'approximately true' as a therapeutic category and the theories treat their successor theories the way divorced people treat their exes' new partners. Do not wink at the reader or break frame. The piece should be legible and funny to someone who has never taken a philosophy of science course, while a specialist should be able to name each position as it walks through the door and should feel the satire is fair to both realists and anti-realists.

After the transcript and session notes, append a short author's note (outside the fiction, no more than 120 words) stating which historical simplifications the piece commits (for instance, whose equations it treats as straightforwardly 'retained,' and any case where the actual history is messier than the joke requires), how confident you are that the contested cases are rendered defensibly, and the single point of historical or philosophical interpretation you are least sure you got right.
