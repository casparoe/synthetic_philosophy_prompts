I am a machine learning researcher with only a lay interest in philosophy. I recently read a summary of Daniel Dennett's essay attacking the coherence of qualia, and the part that stuck with me was his thought experiment about the two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn. As I remember it: both men once loved Maxwell House coffee, and both now report that it tastes worse than it used to. For one of them, the hypothesis is that the coffee produces a different gustatory response than it used to while his standards of judgment have stayed fixed. For the other, the coffee still produces exactly the same low-level response, but his evaluative standards -- his memory of what the old taste was like, or what he wants it to be like -- have drifted, so the very same signal now reads as worse. Dennett's point, as I understand it, is that there is no further fact of the matter about which man has undergone which change: any attempt to pin down whether it was the raw taste or the judgment that shifted dissolves, because nothing in the man's access to his own experience can settle it, and so the notion of an ineffable intrinsic quale that did or didn't stay constant is doing no work.

2. Restate Dennett's claim that there is no fact of the matter in these terms. Presumably the claim becomes something like: the distinction between encoder drift and downstream drift is not well-defined for an integrated trained system, or is underdetermined by anything the system itself can report, or depends on an arbitrary choice of where to draw module boundaries.
