My wife and I have been going back and forth about whether to have a kid, and every time I try to actually weigh it -- imagine the day-to-day, talk to friends who did it, think about what we'd gain and lose -- her sister shuts me down with "you can't know what it's like until you've lived it." She says it like it ends the conversation, like the whole project of reasoning about the decision in advance is a category error. I got curious enough to poke around and found out philosophers have an actual literature on this: L.A. Paul's book Transformative Experience uses a thought experiment about being offered the chance to become a vampire, and apparently the having-kids case is one of her central examples. So the slogan is doing real work somewhere and isn't just something people say at Thanksgiving.

What I want is a disambiguation, not a defense or a takedown. Lay out the distinct precise claims "you can't know what it's like until you've lived it" could be making, from the most deflationary to the most radical. Like: is it just the trivial point that you'll lack the raw phenomenal feel beforehand (which nobody denies and which seems compatible with still reasoning fine about the decision)? Or a claim that you can't even form reliable expectations about how you'll feel? Or something about your preferences themselves changing through the experience, so the person making the decision and the person living with it have different values? Or the stronger thing I think Paul might be arguing, that standard expected-value-style deliberation actually breaks down for these choices, not just gets harder? For each reading, tell me: is it a platitude, genuinely contested, or pretty much false; and crucially, would it actually justify what my sister-in-law does with it -- namely treating advance deliberation about parenthood as pointless rather than merely imperfect?

One more thing: when you give me the verdicts, please be explicit about which parts are basically settled among people who work on this (e.g., that experience gives you something testimony can't) versus which are live disputes (like whether transformative choices really undermine rational decision theory or whether there are workarounds -- revelation approaches, deciding based on who you want to become, etc.). Don't present the contested moves as if they're the consensus view, since I may actually cite this in my next argument with her and I'd rather not overclaim.
