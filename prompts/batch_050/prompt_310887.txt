So I work at a small software company that ships a customer-support chatbot, and last week our comms team got into a genuinely heated argument about how to describe the bot's wrong answers in our docs and marketing -- are they "mistakes," "lies," something else? Someone mentioned Harry Frankfurt's On Bullshit, I read it over the weekend, and now I can't stop classifying everything. For those who haven't read it: Frankfurt's rough idea is that the liar cares about the truth and deliberately conceals it, while the bullshitter doesn't care either way -- they're just saying whatever serves their purpose, and what they say might even happen to be true. Here's where I've landed on cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, is underneath:

1. The bot confidently tells a customer our software supports a file format it doesn't support. My verdict: error, not lying. There's no belief there to misrepresent.
2. The bot invents a realistic-looking citation, with an author, journal, and page numbers, none of which exist. My verdict: this feels worse than #1, borderline lying, though I can't say why -- the mechanism producing it is presumably the same.
3. Our marketing guy writes that the product is "transformative" and "reimagines the future of work." He knows nothing about the product and doesn't want to. My verdict: textbook bullshit, Frankfurt nailed this guy.
4. A calculator app with a bug gives a wrong answer. Pure error, calling it bullshit seems insane.
5. When a user says "that's wrong," the bot replies "You're right, I apologize for the mistake." It doesn't track mistakes, it's just completing the conversational pattern. My verdict: weirdly, this one IS bullshit even though it's polite and harmless -- it's produced with total indifference to whether it's true.
6. A human intern tells a customer the same wrong thing as in #1 because she genuinely misremembered. Error, obviously. But here's the thing: I find the bot's version more blameworthy than hers, even though in both cases there's a falsehood with no intent to deceive. I can't justify the asymmetry.
7. The bot occasionally says something true just by luck, with no real grounding -- like guessing the right format by coincidence. My verdict: still smells like bullshit, which I think Frankfurt would endorse since bullshit can be true.

Is there one principle that sorts all of these -- some extension of Frankfurt's lying/bullshit/error taxonomy to systems that (arguably?) have no intentions or beliefs at all -- or do I have to give something up? I suspect #1 vs #6 can't both survive, and #2 vs #1 is shaky too. State your candidate principle(s) precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it can't accomodate, and what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed: a model that's been fine-tuned to always sound maximally confident regardless of its internal uncertainty, and a human employee who pastes the bot's output into an email to a client without checking it. Also, please be upfront about what's settled here versus genuinely contested -- e.g., whether Frankfurt's account even applies to things without mental states, and whether LLM outputs can be bullshit in his sense at all, are things people who know this literature actually disagree about, right? Don't present a contested reading as the consensus just because it makes for a cleaner answer.
