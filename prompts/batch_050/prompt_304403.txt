I write a magazine column on science and ideas, and I am preparing a reader-facing explainer on how moral revolutions actually happen -- the end of Atlantic slavery, the disappearance of dueling among European gentlemen, the abandonment of footbinding in China. The piece will include a sidebar styled as a diagnostic flowchart: readers trace their own assumptions about historical change to a named position. I would like you to design that flowchart as a decision tree, built from the logical space of the debate rather than from a list of authors.

The tree should be organized around the question of what actually does the work when a long-standing practice, already criticized for generations, finally collapses. The positions that must appear as leaves include: the Williams thesis, on which abolition followed from shifting economic interests rather than humanitarian argument; Appiah's honor-centered account, on which the moral arguments were old and the collapse came when the practice became a source of dishonor; a rational-suasion view, on which argument genuinely converts people and the timing tracks the quality or reach of the arguments; and a structural or institutional view, on which norms change only when laws, incentives, or state capacity make defection costly, whatever individuals believe. You may add further leaves if the space demands them.

Please observe the following constraints. First, build the tree top-down: each branch node should be a single diagnostic question about historical mechanism, phrased so that a reader can answer it without knowing which theory it leads to -- for instance, questions about whether private conscience can change while public conduct stays fixed, whether the timing of abolition tracks the profitability of the practice or the salience of national honor, whether argument explains why practices end but not why they end when they do. Second, map the full logical space the questions generate, and for every unoccupied combination of answers say briefly whether the position is absent because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Third, flag any pair of named positions your tree cannot cleanly separate -- I suspect, for example, that a pure interest-based account and a pure institutional account may converge on some cases -- and say what additional evidence would pry them apart. Fourth, attach to each leaf one historical episode from the three above that the position handles best and one that it handles worst.

Aim for five to seven branch questions. Keep the language at the level of an educated general reader: no citations, no bibliography, and no reliance on archival details beyond what is common knowledge about these three episodes.
