I'm running a reading group for philosophy graduate students working through the metaphysics of origins, and I want to build a diagnostic quiz titled "How essential is your origin?" -- the sort of thing members take before we start arguing, so we can see who actually holds what rather than who claims which label.

Please write the quiz and its scoring key according to these specifications.

**The positions to sort among.** The key must map answer patterns onto these five views: (a) full origin essentialism -- the particular sperm and egg you came from are essential to you, so any difference there means a different person; (b) a threshold or degree view -- your genetic material could have differed somewhat (a few base pairs, perhaps a different sperm from the same father) but there is a line past which the resulting individual would not be you; (c) counterpart-theoretic deflationism -- there is no deep fact here; whether "you could have come from different gametes" is true depends on which respects of similarity we've implicitly settled on, and the felt puzzle is semantic; (d) primitive anti-essentialism -- you are simply yourself, and you could have come from wholly different gametes, parents, species-adjacent stock; identity doesn't track origins at all; (e) biological-continuity views -- what matters is being the same organism, so what you could have survived depends on continuous biological life rather than on the identity of the originating cells.

**The questions.** Give me 8 to 10 questions. Each must present a concrete case with two to four answer options. No question may ask about the views directly, and the options must be phrased so that someone who has never heard the label "origin essentialism" can choose among them. The set of cases must include at least: one question built around monozygotic twins (same gametes, later split -- and whether "you could have been the other twin" is even a coherent answer); one sorites-style question in which a sperm's genome is edited one base pair at a time before fertilization, forcing a choice about where, if anywhere, "no longer you" sets in; one case in which the very same gametes produce an embryo that is then frozen, implanted in a different womb, and raised on another continent; one full swap -- different sperm and different egg, same parents, same day, same hospital; and one case involving a human chimera formed by the fusion of two early embryos. The remaining questions are your choice, but each must pull apart at least two of the five positions that the other questions leave together.

**The scoring key.** Map answer patterns onto the five positions. Then: (1) identify any pair of positions your quiz cannot cleanly separate, say why they converge on these cases, and sketch one additional case that would separate them; (2) list the coherent-looking answer combinations that correspond to no position on your list, and for each, say briefly whether the gap exists because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply not on my list; (3) note which single question does the most diagnostic work and which does the least.

**Tone and format.** The questions should read like a well-made magazine quiz -- brisk, concrete, no jargon in the options -- but the key can be as technical as it needs to be. Number the questions, letter the options, and keep the whole thing under 1,200 words.
