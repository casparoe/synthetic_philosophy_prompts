I am preparing the opening session of a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of infinity, and I want to distribute my own position as a target for criticism. Before I do, I would like the position audited for internal coherence. Please adopt the role of a philosopher of mathematics with particular expertise in set theory and the infinite, and think carefully rather than reaching for the standard talking points. I suggest you begin by briefly brainstorming which pairs of my commitments might interact — positively or negatively — before settling on your final assessment; then give me the audit itself.

Here is where I have landed:

(1) Cantor's diagonal argument is valid, and it establishes that there are genuinely different infinite cardinalities. I accept classical ZFC mathematics without any revisionism.

(2) Dedekind's characterization of the infinite — a set is infinite just in case it can be put in one-to-one correspondence with a proper subset of itself — is a perfectly respectable definition, and Galileo's observation about the natural numbers and their squares is a discovery about infinity, not a paradox. I do not think Euclid's maxim that the whole is greater than the part survives contact with infinite sets, and I see no need to mourn it.

(3) Hilbert's-hotel-style scenarios demonstrate that a concrete, instantiated actual infinite is metaphysically impossible. A hotel with infinitely many occupied rooms that can always accommodate infinitely many new guests by shuffling, and from which infinitely many guests can check out while infinitely many remain, is not merely unfamiliar but absurd; these features amount to a reductio of any world's containing a completed actual infinity.

(4) Whether the physical world contains infinitely many things — infinitely many particles, say, or infinitely many spacetime regions or events — is an open empirical question. Future physics could in principle settle it either way, and I would not be shocked by either outcome.

(5) Pure mathematics does not need to be instantiated in the physical world in order to be true. Mathematical existence is one thing; physical existence is another.

(6) Finitist or potentialist reinterpretations of analysis fail to capture the content of Cantor's theorem. Paraphrases in terms of unending processes or indefinitely extensible enumerations do not say what the theorem says; it is a claim about completed infinite totalities or it is about nothing.

A few instructions for the audit. First, do not flag everything: I believe at least one pair that will look tense to a careless reader is in fact perfectly consistent once the commitments are stated precisely, and I want to see whether you identify it as such and explain why the tension is merely apparent. Second, for each tension you judge genuine, state which commitments are involved, why their usual motivations pull against each other (not merely that they sound in tension), and what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me elsewhere in the list. Third, rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and be explicit about the ranking criterion. Fourth, I have no fixed view about which commitment I would surrender; do not assume one is sacrosanct.

Please close with a machine-readable final line of the following form: \answer{...} containing the commitment pairs constituting genuine tensions, ordered from most to least severe, separated by >, with each pair written as (n,m); if you judge the entire set consistent, write \answer{consistent}. For example: \answer{(3,4) > (2,3)}.
