In debates about policing and political authority, one encounters a provocative comparison associated with the sociologist Charles Tilly's work on state formation: the modern state, on this picture, resembles a protection racket. Just as a racketeer manufactures or cultivates the very dangers he then charges shopkeepers to be shielded from, governments -- especially in their formative centuries -- monopolize coercion, generate threats through war-making and internal repression, and then present taxation and obedience as the price of protection. I have seen this analogy put to two quite different uses. Abolitionists deploy it to argue that the police do not protect the public so much as sell back to us a safety they themselves jeopardize, so the institution should be dismantled rather than reformed. More moderate writers deploy it only descriptively, as a deflationary account of how states in fact arose, while insisting it carries no verdict about what states are now or what we should do about them.

Please audit the analogy as an argument, not merely as a historical observation. Begin with an explicit table of correspondences: what plays the role of the racketeer, the shopkeeper, the tribute, the threats protected against (both those the racketeer stages and those posed by rival rackets), and the refusal to pay. Then identify where the mapping breaks or strains. Consider, at minimum: whether a racketeer typically delivers genuine protection against third parties, and what follows for the analogy if states demonstrably do; the role of consent, internal accountability, and redistribution, none of which have obvious counterparts in a racket; the counterfactual baseline the analogy quietly assumes (would the threats the state protects against exist in its absence, and does the analogy smuggle in an answer?); and the disanalogy between an extortionist's discretionary violence and coercion exercised under publicly known rules. For each disanalogy, say whether it matters for the abolitionist use, the descriptive use, both, or neither -- I do not want a generic list of ways states and rackets differ, but a sorting of the differences by whether they touch the load-bearing parts of the argument.

Conclude with a committed verdict: state plainly whether the analogy can support the abolitionist conclusion it is often made to carry, whether it survives as a purely descriptive thesis with no normative residue, or whether it fails even there -- and if some conclusion about policing (reform, defunding, abolition, or none of these) can be extracted from a corrected version of the comparison, say what it is and defend it. Where the analogy misleads, propose what you take to be a more accurate one and explain why it better fits the features of the modern state that matter for the policing debate. Take a position throughout rather than presenting me a menu of options.
