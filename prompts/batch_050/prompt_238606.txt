I'm a software engineer (no philosophy training) who's been reading around the question of legal personhood for AI, and before I write up my position for an internal tech-policy working group, I'd like to know whether my views actually hang together. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Legal personhood is purely a technical device — a bundle of rights and duties the law grants where it's useful. It implies nothing about consciousness, dignity, or inner life, which is why corporate personhood has never struck me as philosophically troubling.
2. Current AI systems should not be legal persons. There's no genuine agent behind their outputs — a language model is doing sophisticated pattern-matching, not forming intentions, and responsibility-talk only makes sense where there's a real agent to point at.
3. If a future system could autonomously hold and manage assets, enter into and honour contracts, and reliably modify its behaviour in response to legal incentives, then we should grant it a limited legal personhood — on essentially the same functional grounds that justify corporate personhood.
4. When an AI system causes harm today, liability should fall entirely on the developer or deployer. Creating a suable AI "person" would mostly serve as a liability shield for the companies behind it.
5. Fining an AI system would be pointless because it can't suffer, whereas fining a corporation works because the loss eventually lands on people who can.

Which of these are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and which tensions dissolve once the terms are made precise? Please rank any real tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each one say what I'd have to give up to resolve it and what that would cost the overall position. If a single revision would clean up most of the problems, identify it — but don't assume I already know which commitment I'm least attached to.
