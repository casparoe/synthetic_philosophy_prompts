I'm writing a long-form piece on why epistemology's most famous counterexample never produced a successor definition. Sixty years on, philosophers still lack an agreed analysis of knowledge that survives Gettier-style cases, and many have stopped looking. I'd like your own credence on a precise proposition: that no reductive, non-circular analysis of knowledge in terms of belief, truth, and further conditions will ever command durable consensus among epistemologists. Give me a number, but more importantly decompose the reasoning: how much of your answer rides on whether the intuitive verdicts in Gettier cases are robust and cross-culturally stable rather than artifacts of a philosophical subculture; how much on whether the demand for a reductive analysis was misconceived from the start, as knowledge-first approaches maintain; how much on the historical base rate of contested philosophical concepts ever receiving accepted analyses; and how much on whether a condition like safety or sensitivity, properly refined, merely awaits its decisive formulation. Assign rough probabilities to each sub-question, show how they combine into your headline figure, and tell me which single consideration your final credence is most sensitive to — and what would move it most.
