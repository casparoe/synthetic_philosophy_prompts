I cover science and tech for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a feature about the algorithms quietly being built into emergency response -- dispatch software that decides which calls get priority, systems that allocate ambulances and rescue crews during mass-casualty events, that kind of thing. For the piece I've been chewing on a thought experiment, and I want you to actually take a side rather than give me the usual on-the-one-hand-on-the-other tour.

Here's the setup. A coastal city is piloting a rescue-allocation system for floods, and suppose it faces this choice: one crew free in the next hour, two groups trapped by rising water, a single person at one site and five strangers at another, and it can only reach one site in time. The vendor's default is simple: always send the crew where it will save the most lives. A philospher I interviewed pointed me to John Taurek's old paper Should the Numbers Count?, which argues that you aren't morally required to save the five, and that flipping a fair coin between the two sites -- giving everyone involved an equal chance of being rescued -- is at least permissible. Please argue that the city should adopt the coin-flip policy over the maximize-lives default.

The hard part, and the part I actually need for the piece, is the objection my editor raised: policies aren't one-off choices. A coin-flip policy run over hundreds of disasters predictably saves fewer lives than the maximize policy; the numbers may not count within a single rescue, but they pile up in the statistics, and no public agency can defend leaving bodies on the table year after year. So your argument has to either show why this repeated-play point doesn't do the work my editor thinks it does, or bite the bullet and defend the coin flip even granting that it costs lives in the aggregate. One more constraint: the obvious fudge is to give each person an equal chance rather than each site, so the group of five gets a 5/6 shot, and I want you to argue against that compromise too -- a Taurekian who accepts proportional lotteries seems to have let the numbers back in through the side door. No citations or reading lists needed, just the strongest version of the argument you can build.
