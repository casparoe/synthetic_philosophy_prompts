Two of my doctoral students, Dev and Marisol, have been arguing in my seminar on formal axiology, and their dispute has reached a standstill. I have declined to referee, partly because I think each has exposed a genuine weakness in the other's position. I would like you to adjudicate instead.

The dispute concerns a spectrum argument Dev constructed against the transitivity of 'better than.' Consider a sequence of outcomes. In the first, one person endures severe, unremitting agony for a year. In each successor outcome, the suffering's intensity is slightly reduced while the number of people who suffer it is doubled. In the final outcome, an astronomical number of people each experience a single fleeting twinge, barely noticeable and instantly forgotten. Dev then ran the following argument, and the exchange unfolded in five moves:

(1) Dev: The first outcome is clearly better than the last — one person's year of agony, however dreadful, is a far less bad outcome than a moral catastrophe distributed across trillions. Yet each outcome in the sequence also seems better than its successor: halving the intensity of a pain at the cost of doubling its bearers looks, at every step, like an improvement. These pairwise judgments together contradict transitivity. Since each comparative judgment is more secure than an abstract formal principle of axiology, we should reject the transitivity of 'better than.'

(2) Marisol: The spectrum trades on differences too fine for us to discriminate, so the pairwise judgments are exactly where we should expect our comparative faculties to misfire. 'Better' may be vague, but vagueness does not generate intransitivity — nobody concludes from the sorites that 'taller than' fails to be transitive. The argument shows, at most, that somewhere in the sequence there is a boundary our judgments cannot locate, not that there is no boundary.

(3) Dev: The vagueness reply fails, because nothing in the argument requires imperceptible increments. Rebuild the spectrum with twenty clearly distinguishable gradations of intensity, from agony down to twinge, doubling the population at each step. Each adjacent comparison still looks compelling — at every gradation, relieving that much intensity for that many people at the price of a slightly milder suffering for twice as many seems like a gain. The sorites analogy therefore misfires: the sorites depends on indiscriminability, and this argument does not.

(4) Marisol: Once the increments are noticeable, the adjacent premises are no longer compulsory — I simply deny that moving from n sufferers at a given intensity to 2n sufferers at the next intensity down is always an improvement. At some gradations aggregation wins, at others it loses, and my confidence about which is which is exactly what is under dispute. The coarse-grained version of the argument does not avoid begging the question; it just relocates it from my discriminatory limits to my contested intuitions.

(5) Dev: Then I will make a final concession that is not really a concession. Transitivity can be preserved if betterness is essentially comparative: whether one outcome is better than another can depend on which alternatives are present in the comparison, because the respects of value internal to an outcome are weighed differently against different rivals. Every pairwise judgment in the spectrum is then true relative to its own comparison, and no intransitivity ever arises. Marisol answered that this rescues transitivity by changing the subject: practical reason needs a choice-guiding relation that does not shift with the menu, and an essentially comparative 'better' cannot tell a health authority which outcome to fund when the whole spectrum is on the table at once.

Please adjudicate. Rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating; say who is more nearly right overall; and identify what, if anything, both parties are missing — in particular, whether there is a way to block the spectrum that neither of them considers. Lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then give the move-by-move assessment, with no preamble and no restatement of the dispute.
