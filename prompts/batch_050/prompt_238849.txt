I am a retired experimental physicist, and in recent years I have taken to reading political philosophy in the mornings. What first drew me in was not any grand question about justice but a humble empirical observation from my own social life. Over several decades I have noticed that people's political opinions seem to arrive in fixed packages. Knowing a dinner guest's position on, say, the legalisation of firearms lets me predict, with unsettling accuracy, his views on climate research, on the teaching of evolution, and on criminal sentencing -- topics with no causal or logical connection to one another that I can discern. I have also watched the packaging happen in real time: a former colleague of mine moved after retirement to a community with a strong political identity, and within perhaps four years his positions on a dozen unrelated questions had all migrated together, including, if I am honest, questions on which he had previously held firm and reasoned views. In my own field, when a cluster of traits travels together, one looks for a common underlying cause. I cannot find one here.

What makes this genuinely puzzling to me, rather than merely a case of groupthink, is that the contents of the packages appear to be contingent while the packaging itself is not. The bundle that marks someone as belonging to one camp in my country corresponds to a quite different bundle elsewhere, and positions that belonged to one side's package in my youth now belong to the other side's. So whatever binds a package together, it cannot be a shared first principle, since the principle would have to have changed when the contents did. There is also a directional asymmetry I keep noticing: when a party or movement revises its line on some question, most adherents revise their own view to match rather than defecting to the other side, which suggests to me that the allegiance comes first and the positions are filled in afterwards -- the reverse of the order I had naively assumed, in which a person reasons to conclusions and then joins whichever group shares them.

My question is whether this phenomenon has an established name, and I should say that a bare label will not satisfy me. I have come across words like tribalism and polarisation, but those seem to name the hostility between camps, not the internal packaging of each camp's positions, and they do not address the direction-of-causation point at all. What I want to know is: does the literature distinguish the mere fact of opinion-clustering from the stronger pattern I described, where allegiance is prior and content is contingent? Is the phenomenon treated as a failure of reasoning, as a rational economising of effort, or as something more like brand loyalty? And if there is no established concept for the exact configuration -- stable packages, arbitrary contents, reversed causation -- could you define one precisely, with boundary cases? In particular I would want to know whether it would also cover the consensus among physicists about quantum mechanics (surely not, but I would like the definition to show why not), and whether it covers my wife's conviction that all the composers I admire are overrated, which travels suspiciously well with her other tastes.
