I took a course on Indian philosophy last term and got really into Jain epistemology, and now I'm arguing about it with a friend who thinks anekāntavāda is just relativism with extra steps. I built an argument to stress-test her objection and now I'm worried it proves too much and I can't find the hole. Setup, in case it matters: the Jain picture as I understand it is that reality is manifold in character (things have indefinitely many aspects and modes), that every judgment is made from some partial standpoint (naya), and that assertions should be made conditionally, prefixed with syāt, "in a certain respect" -- with the sevenfold predication running through is, is-not, both, inexpressible, and the combinations, applied to any thing. Now the argument. (1) Every claim anyone asserts is made from some standpoint and holds only conditionally, in some respect. (2) For any non-trivial claim P about a real thing, the thing's manifold nature guarantees there is a respect in which P is assertible and a respect in which not-P is assertible. (3) So whenever two people disagree about P, the complete and correct position is to affirm both, each in its own respect -- which is literally what the sevenfold scheme prescribes. (4) But then no evidence can ever settle a disagreement: new evidence can only tell you which respect you're currently occupying, it can't make one side simply wrong. (5) Yet some disagreements obviously do get settled by evidence -- whether the well water downstream of the tannery is contaminated, whether the monsoon came early this year, whether the jar is on the table. (6) So either (1) or (2) is false, or syādvāda is a doctrine about only a special class of claims -- but then which class, and what principled line keeps the well-water question out of it?

My friend says (6) shows the whole thing collapses into either relativism or vacuity, and honestly I can't tell she's wrong. My own hunch is that (2) is the bad step, that something gets shifted between "P holds in some respect" and "P holds," but when I try to say exactly what, I end up just restating the doctrine. I'd most like to keep (1) and (5); I'd rather lose (2) or (3). There's also a sub-worry I can't put down: does the argument apply to anekāntavāda itself -- i.e., is there a standpoint from which one-sided ekānta claims are true, and if the Jaina grants that, does the doctrine eat itself, and if she refuses, isn't that refusal itself a one-sided claim? Tell me which step actually breaks, why it looked fine, and what each possible repair costs -- in particular, whether the repair leaves the Jaina anything distinctive to say against the Naiyāyika realist who just insists that some predicates hold of things simpliciter, standpoint or no standpoint.
