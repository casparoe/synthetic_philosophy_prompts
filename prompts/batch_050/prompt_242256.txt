Write a dialogue between two friends, both quantitatively trained, who are each offered the same wager by a wealthy eccentric: a fair coin will be flipped repeatedly, paying 1.2-to-1 on heads and taking the stake on tails, and they may bet any fraction of their bankroll each time — but the eccentric reserves the right to stop the game at any flip without warning, and might even stop it after a single flip. One friend is a committed Kelly bettor who holds that maximizing the expected logarithm of wealth is the only rational policy and that its asymptotic dominance over every alternative settles the matter; the other argues that Kelly's justification leans entirely on the game being repeated enough times for the growth-rate theorems to bite, and that once the number of trials is unknown and possibly one, the Kelly fraction has no special normative force and one should fall back on maximizing expected value directly, or on some explicitly risk-adjusted rule. Have them argue over concrete numbers (work out what each policy actually does for plausible stopping scenarios rather than trading slogans), press each other on whether "geometric rationality" is a discovery about rationality or merely a redescription of a taste for multiplicative survival, and confront the question of whether a strategy that is almost surely best in the limit deserves to govern a life that might end before the limit is reached. Both speakers should be equally sharp, engage with each other's actual calculations, and neither should concede the whole dispute.
