I'm preparing an interview for my podcast on AI policy. The guest is the author of a new book arguing that advanced AI is less dangerous than skeptics claim, with much of the argument resting on what she calls the strategy-stealing assumption: for any strategy a misaligned AI could pursue to acquire resources, influence, or power, there is a corresponding strategy that humans equipped with aligned AI assistants could pursue that would capture at least as much of that value for human ends -- so acting on behalf of humanity imposes no decisive competitive handicap.

I want questions that do more than invite her to restate the thesis. Draft seven interview questions, each followed by the answer she is most likely to give and a follow-up question keyed to that answer. Between them, the questions should press on at least the following:

- The status of the assumption itself: is it an empirical conjecture about compute, offense-defense balance, and human institutions, or a stipulation about what counts as a strategy -- and if empirical, what observation would overturn it?
- The alignment tax: a human executing a stolen strategy must first spend effort identifying and representing what humans actually want, while the misaligned agent optimizes a simple, fixed target directly. Does stealing still succeed when one side's objective is legible and the other's must be extracted from fragile, contested, hard-to-specify values?
- Coordination: deploying the stolen strategy requires many humans, firms, and governments to act in concert, whereas the misaligned agent faces no internal principal-agent problem. Does the assumption quietly presuppose a solution to human collective action?
- Symmetry and iteration: if humans can steal the AI's strategies, what prevents the AI from stealing the counter-strategies, and does the assumption survive several rounds of mutual adaptation?
- Winning versus ending the game: even if the assumption holds for accumulating resources and influence, does it hold for irreversible moves -- strategies available to an agent that does not need the game to continue?

Close with one question asking what evidence or argument would lead her to abandon the assumption. Keep the entire set to at most 500 words, with no preamble or framing remarks.
