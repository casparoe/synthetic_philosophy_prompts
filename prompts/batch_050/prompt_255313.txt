I'm a software engineer about ten years into my career, and I recently came across the Ship of Theseus puzzle in a philosophy podcast. My immediate reaction was that I live inside this puzzle: I've watched services get refactored module by module until not a single line of the original code remains, while everyone -- users, management, the on-call rotation -- kept treating it as the same system the whole time. And I've seen the other horn of the puzzle too: someone forks a repository, the fork and the original diverge, and at some point the question of which one is "the real project" stops being a technical question at all (though it can become a bitter social and legal one). So I suspect the debate might be more tractable, or more interesting, than it first appears, and I'd like to test that by having it restated in a vocabulary I'm actually fluent in.

Please restate the Ship of Theseus puzzle and the main positions philosophers take on it -- as I understand them from the podcast, these include the view that a thing can't survive replacement of all its parts, the four-dimensionalist view on which objects are extended through time and the ship at different times is just different temporal stages, the view that identity is relative to the concept or sortal under which you're counting, and whatever responses deal with the classic complication where the discarded planks are reassembled into a second ship -- in the vocabulary of software: repositories, commits and version histories, diffs and patches, gradual refactors, forks and branches, hashes and content-addressing, and identifiers that a system assigns and persists. Do this one position at a time, and be honest about the translation rather than forcing it. I'm reasonably competent at spotting when someone smuggles in assumptions by relabeling them.

The part I care most about is the residue report: for each position, which claims map cleanly onto something in my vocabulary, which only translate with distortion, and which have no expression in the software framing at all. In particular, I want your judgment on one thing. In software, identity questions mostly get settled by stipulation -- the repo is whatever the hosting service points its identifier at, or whatever the community keeps merging into -- and that dissolves the puzzle in practice. Does this show that the Theseus debate is really about naming conventions and our practices of reidentification rather than about any deep fact of the matter? Or does the software case merely hide the puzzle, because stipulation works there precisely where nothing important turns on the answer, whereas the stakes change when the object in question is, say, a person or an organization whose identity has moral and legal consequences? Tell me which parts of the philosophical debate survive contact with my vocabulary as genuine disagreements, and which parts evaporate.
