I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy, and I keep encountering a stalemate in discussions of multi-agent AI safety. Consider three independently trained language-model agents that repeatedly set prices in the same simulated market. They cannot send messages to one another, but each can observe the market history. After many rounds, they settle on prices well above the competitive baseline; each agent loses profit if it lowers its price unilaterally, and occasional undercutting is followed by aggressive price cuts from the others.

One group in my reading circle calls this collusion. Its members argue that the outcome is stable, mutually beneficial to the agents, harmful to consumers, and maintained through learned patterns of accommodation and retaliation. Another group resists the label because there was no agreement, communication, representation of a shared plan, or intention resembling human conspiracy. They describe the result as ordinary equilibrium selection, correlated learning from similar data, or mere coordination. The safety implications differ: if this is collusion, perhaps systems need explicit anti-coordination constraints; if it is equilibrium behavior, the problem may instead be market design or misspecified objectives.

A proposed diagnostic has only sharpened the dispute. Call it the ghost-rival test: place each agent among simulated rivals whose memories, objectives, or responsiveness are altered while the observed price history is initially held fixed. If the agent maintains high prices only when its policy implicitly expects reciprocal accommodation or punishment, the test counts this as tacit collusion. Critics reply that every adaptive policy is counterfactually sensitive to other agents, so the test does not distinguish collusion from rational play in a repeated game.

Please diagnose the stalemate rather than merely refereeing the terminology. Is this fundamentally a verbal disagreement over the extension of collusion, a substantive disagreement about which causal and counterfactual properties matter, or a case in which the parties are answering different questions—one about harmful outcomes, one about internal mechanisms, and one about responsibility—under a single name? Does the ghost-rival test identify the property that should matter for multi-agent safety, or does it smuggle in a contested theory of agency?

I would like a committed answer: choose the diagnosis you think best explains why intelligent interlocutors remain stuck, defend it against the strongest alternative, and state what would count as genuine progress—a sharper conceptual distinction, a formal criterion, an experiment, or abandonment of collusion as the central category. Please do not conclude by simply listing the available positions.
