Our town council recently debated whether to approve a chemical spraying program to control mosquitoes, and several residents opposed it simply by repeating the proverb "better safe than sorry." What struck me was how much work that one phrase did: it was treated as settling the matter without anyone having to engage with the dosage data or the studies the council had commissioned, and it seemed to license ignoring the risks of *not* spraying (mosquito-borne illness), as if caution could only point in one direction. I've been turning the phrase over since, and I can't pin down what it actually claims. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise positions that proverb could be expressing: for example, a claim that the costs of the two possible errors (acting when it's harmful, not acting when it's safe) are asymmetric; a claim about how to choose when the probabilities themselves are unknown; a claim that whoever introduces something new bears the burden of proving it harmless; or a claim that some risks may not be taken at any probability and regardless of the benefits forgone. For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or actually false, and then say which reading the residents would have needed for the phrase to justify blocking the program without engaging the evidence — and whether that reading survives scrutiny.
