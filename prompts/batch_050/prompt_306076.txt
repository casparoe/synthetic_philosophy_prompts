I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, with enough economics coursework under my belt to get into arguments like the following. Last month I had to decide between two post-degree paths: accepting a well-paid industry position that I find intellectually dull but financially secure, or staying the course toward academic work that I have always described -- sincerely, I thought -- as what I value most in life. I took the industry offer. When I confessed to a friend that I felt I'd somehow betrayed my own values, she shrugged and said something I haven't been able to shake: roughly, that my anguish is self-indulgent theater, because whatever I ended up choosing simply is what I preferred, and talk of some deeper "what I really value," floating free of what I actually do, is a story I tell myself to feel noble. On her view, the choice settles the question rather than raising one.

I want to push back, but I'm worried my objections don't cohere. Here are the facts that make me resist her conclusion: the decision was made at the end of an exhausting semester, under heavy pressure from family about financial precarity, and with the options framed to me largely in terms of risk. A month earlier, in a calmer and more reflective mood, I'm fairly sure I would have chosen otherwise, and I notice that small changes in how the choice is described still flip my inclinations back and forth. Since accepting, I've felt persistent regret of a kind I don't feel after ordinary hard trade-offs. But my friend's challenge is sharp: if I want to say I chose against my own preferences, what could "my preferences" possibly amount to, other than a pattern of choices like this one? Isn't my reflective, before-the-fact endorsement just another disposition that lost out to a stronger one?

So my request is twofold. First, philosophically: is there a defensible notion of what I value that can come apart from what I choose -- one that makes room for genuine self-betrayal, manipulation, or weakness of will, rather than dissolving them into "you preferred the pressure-relief over the scholarship, full stop"? Or is my friend's austere position actually correct, with regret being just one more competing preference rather than evidence of error? Second, practically: given that my moment-of-choice inclinations and my reflective endorsements systematically diverge, which should I treat as authoritative for the next big decision, and what concrete procedure (deliberative habits, commitment devices, choice architecture, trusted interlocutors) would you recommend for someone in my position? I'd like advice that engages the underlying conceptual issue rather than generic career counsel.
