I spent my career in experimental physics and have turned to moral philosophy in retirement, so forgive me if my taxonomy is crude. I am assembling the principal objections to the ideal of the moral saint—the person who maximizes moral goodness at every turn—and so far my list runs as follows: (1) Wolf's objection that such a person would be unattractive and one-dimensional, lacking the humor, irony, and particular enthusiasms that make a life rich; (2) the demandingness objection, that any morality whose ideal is sainthood asks more of us than a plausible morality may ask; (3) the integrity objection, that an overriding commitment to impartial morality alienates an agent from the ground projects that give her life its meaning; and (4) the crowding-out objection, that a community of saints would lose great non-moral goods—art, sport, scholarship—because no one would be left to pursue them with the requisite single-mindedness. I should like one further objection whose structure differs from all of these: not a restatement, not a special case, and not a mere combination of the four. State the objection precisely, explain how it relates to those on my list (does it reinforce one of them, presuppose one, or undercut the whole framing?), and give your honest assessment of its force. Finally, since I have learned from the sciences to ask this of every claim: say what consideration or argument would change your verdict on it, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
