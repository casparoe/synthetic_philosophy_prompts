So the dogmatism paradox has been living in my head rent free lately and I want a real fight about it, not a textbook summary. Quick version of the setup (correct me if I'm butchering it): if I know that P, then any evidence I might later encounter against P has to be misleading evidence, since P is true and the evidence points away from it. So it looks like knowing P licenses me to dismiss future counter-evidence in advance, possibly without even looking at it. Which seems insane.

Here's the position I want argued: the standard Bayesian dissolution — "you should never have credence exactly 1 in an empirical proposition, Cromwell's rule, so there's always some possible evidence that could move you, puzzle solved" — does not actually solve the paradox, it just relocates it. Argue that even a perfect conditionalizer sitting at credence 0.9999999 in P still has to regard incoming counter-evidence as almost certainly misleading, and will rationally discount it almost to zero, so the "dogmatic" behavior reappears at the level of degrees of belief instead of all-or-nothing knowledge. If you think this relocation objection fails, then argue against me instead — but in that case you have to say precisely where the Bayesian response does bite, not just gesture at conditionalization.

A few constraints:

- Don't rehash the basic Harman/Kripke exposition, I know it. Spend your words on the relocation move.
- Run the argument on testimonial evidence (a normally reliable friend tells me not-P) rather than perceptual evidence, because I think the paradox is sharpest there and perception gives people too many easy escape hatches.
- If your argument leans on a distinction between evidence being misleading and evidence being misleadingly described/acquired, make that distinction do explicit work, don't just name-drop it.
- Bonus: say what your resolution implies for the practical rationalist norm of "update on all the evidence you recieve" — does the paradox mean that norm needs an exception clause, and if so what does the clause look like?
