I'm a software engineer and I keep circling back to this thing I read about, the causal exclusion argument (I think it goes back to Jaegwon Kim). Roughly: every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, so if my decision to get coffee is anything over and above the neurons firing, then either the coffee-getting is caused twice over, which seems nuts, or the decision does no causal work at all and the neurons did everything. The reason it gets under my skin is that it sounds identical to arguing that my code doesn't do anything: every run of my program is fully explained by electrons moving around in silicon, so the sorting algorithm isn't really causing the sorted output, it's just decorative. That conclusion is obviously wrong, but I honestly can't say where the move from "fully explained at the low level" to "the high level does nothing" breaks down, or if it even does.

Write an essay on whether the exclusion argument actually shows that mental states, or higher-level properties in general, are causally inert. Go through the main escape routes -- maybe systematic overdetermination is fine, maybe causal closure is questionable, maybe the exclusion principle itself is the problem, maybe causation works in a way (interventionist, or about proportionality of cause to effect) where higher-level causes beat lower-level ones. But take a side: does the argument go through or not, and if not, which reply is the one that actually kills it? Don't just survey the options and shrug, I want a defended verdict, and say whether the sorting algorithm analogy helps or is a red herring.
