I'm a control theorist by training; I spent some years doing motor control for a robotics lab and now work on learned locomotion policies, so the vocabulary I actually think in is forward models, inverse models, state estimation, sensory prediction error, optimal control, motor primitives, and hierarchical policies. Over a reading group this term I've been working through Merleau-Ponty's account of the lived body -- motor intentionality, habit and incorporation (the blind person's cane, the typist who knows the keyboard without being able to list the keys), bodily spatiality, and his claim that the body relates to the world through a kind of practical directedness that is not representational in the way perception or thought is. My group keeps generating arguments about whether all of this is just nineteenth-century neurology redescribed, and I want to settle it for myself by doing a translation exercise and seeing what survives.

So here is the request. Restate Merleau-Ponty's position on the lived body and motor intentionality as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary: the body as a plant to be controlled, intentionality as the structure of a controller or policy, habit as cached control structure or learned subpolicies, incorporation as the extension of the plant's dynamics into the model, bodily spatiality as a body-relative coordinate frame with egocentric spatial coding, and so on -- and feel free to correct my suggested mappings if they distort him, since accuracy to the phenomenology matters more than flattering my guesses. Then give me the residue report, broken into three parts. First, which of his claims map cleanly onto standard control-theoretic notions, and whether the mapping is exact or only approximate. Second, which claims can only be translated with distortion -- I'm particularly curious whether his insistence that the directedness in skilled action is non-representational survives, given that modern motor neuroscience describes skilled action precisely in terms of internal models and predictions, and I have a standing suspicion that he would reject the representational reading while still needing something model-like to explain anticipatory grip scaling and similar results. Third, which claims have no expression at all in the control-theoretic vocabulary.

Finally, the harder judgment: for each item in the residue, say whether its failure to translate indicts the target framework (control theory genuinely cannot say something true about embodied agency, for instance anything about the first-person character of inhabiting a body, which is arguably not the kind of thing a controller description was ever meant to capture) or whether it indicts the source (the claim was doing rhetorical rather than explanatory work, or rests on a picture of computation and representation that the intervening century has made obsolete). I don't want both-sides hedging here -- I want you to commit to a verdict per item and tell me which side of the translation you think loses more.
