Here's the situation. One of my grad students is writing a paper on whether frontier AI models should be released with open weights, and her whole argument rests on an analogy she treats as basically decisive: releasing model weights is like releasing open-source software. With Linux, she points out, anyone can read the code, so bugs get found fast, nobody has to trust a single vendor, and small players can build on top of it. Same deal with open weights, she says: the whole research community can probe the model, spot dangerous capabilities before the lab does, and we avoid a world where two or three companies control the most powerful technology on the planet.

Her opponent in my reading group counters with the mirror-image analogy: handing out frontier weights is like publishing a step-by-step recipe for engineering a pandemic virus. Dual-use knowledge, harmless in careful hands, catastrophic in careless or malicious ones, and once it's out, it's out forever -- you can't recall a file that's been copied a million times.

For next week's seminar I want a proper audit of both analogies rather than another round of them trading slogans at each other. Please:

- Spell out the mapping for each analogy explicitly, element by element (a table is fine). What plays the role of the code, the bugs, the many eyes, the security patch? What plays the role of the pathogen, the recipe, the lab's containment procedures?
- For each one, say where the mapping actually breaks, and seperate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion being drawn from the ones that are just pedantic. For instance: is it relevant that you can push a security update to Linux users but not to everyone who already downloaded a set of weights? Is it relevant that a "bug" in software is a flaw waiting to be fixed, while a model's dangerous capability might be the thing working exactly as intended? Is it relevant that reading source code tells you what a program does, while staring at a few hundred gigabytes of floating point numbers mostly doesn't?
- Say what each analogy quietly smuggles in. Does the Linux analogy assume the main risks look like vulnerabilities rather than capabilities? Does the pathogen analogy assume openness has no defensive upside, or that the big labs are trustworthy custodians while the public isn't?
- Finally: once the disanalogies are all on the table, does either analogy still support the conclusion it was built to support? And if you can think of a better analogy for what releasing weights is actually like, give it and explain why it fits the case better.

Both of these students are sharp, so don't strawman either side -- the open-source analogy has genuinely earned its keep in the history of software, and the biosecurity folks have genuinely lost sleep over dual-use publishing. Casual argumentative tone is fine, as long as the analysis is doing the work.
