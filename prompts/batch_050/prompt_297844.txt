After forty years in condensed matter physics I retired and started reading philosophy, and lately it's been AI alignment stuff, so bear with me if this is a weird way to frame a question. I've been chewing on the difference between worst-case guarentees (the system must never do the bad thing, no matter what gets thrown at it) and average-case ones (it behaves fine in typical situations and you accept some tail risk). Then last month I reread Asimov's I, Robot stories and realised he's basically dramatizing this exact trade-off, decades early. The Three Laws look like an attempt at a worst-case specification, but the stories keep generating failures anyway: Speedy circling the selenium pool in Runaround because two rules pull against each other, the modified First Law robot in Little Lost Robot, the Machines in The Evitable Conflict quietly taking over the economy because that's what "prevent harm to humanity" cashing out at scale looks like.

Read the stories through this lens. Is Asimov showing that worst-case rule-based guarantees fail because the world supplies cases the spec-writer couldn't imagine, or is he actually vindicating them, since nothing truly catastrophic happens and the failures are mostly comic? And what would an average-case approach even look like inside his fictional world — Susan Calvin seems to operate on something like it. I'd like the reading to take both the fiction and the alignment distinction seriously, not just use Asimov as decoration, and to say where the stories genuinely complicate the worst-case vs average-case framing rather than just illustrating it. Bonus if you can tell me whether Asimov, the trained chemist, smuggles in a verdict a philosopher would miss.
