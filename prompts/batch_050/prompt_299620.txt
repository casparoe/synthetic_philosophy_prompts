I run a small cognitive science research group -- three postdocs and about five years of funding -- and we're deciding how to divide our effort across open questions about the structure of concepts. The field's three classic approaches (prototype theory, exemplar theory, and the theory theory) have coexisted for decades, and at a recent workshop I became convinced of something that now shapes how I want to allocate the group's time: the three theories survive partly because they've each retreated to explaining the phenomena they handle best, so that what looks like a three-way competition is really three research programs answering slightly different questions. Prototype people study typicality gradients, exemplar people study category learning in the lab, theory-theory people study explanation and induction in children, and almost nobody runs studies where all three are forced to make divergent predictions about the same data.

Given that diagnosis, I'd like a defended prioritization of open questions for my group. The candidates I have in mind include: whether prototypes and exemplars are competing accounts of a single memory system or descriptions of two distinct systems that both operate; whether any of the three views can handle conceptual combination (why "pet fish" behaves so oddly relative to "pet" and "fish"); how people who share almost no exemplars of, say, "justice" or "democracy" nevertheless manage to communicate with the same word; and why some concepts (natural kinds, apparently) resist redefinition while others (legal and bureaucratic ones) seem to be renegotiated in real time. You should feel free to add a question I'm not listing or to argue that one of mine is misconceived. For each question, I want to know: which of the three theories has the most at stake in its answer, in the sense that the wrong answer would force it into serious revision rather than minor adjustment; whether the question is actually tractable with behavioral and developmental methods in five years, as opposed to tractable only in principle; and how many of the other questions depend on it being settled first.

The deliverable I'm after is a ranked agenda with the reasoning exposed, not a survey of the literature. In particular, tell me what would change your ranking: if my diagnosis about mutual retreat is wrong -- if the three theories genuinely do make divergent predictions in some well-defined experimental space -- which of my questions suddenly becomes much more or much less worth pursuing? And if you think the whole framing of adjudicating between three rival theories of concepts is the wrong goal for a five-year group, say so and tell me what the right goal would be, since that's a decision I haven't fully made either.
