I'm working through my intuitions about intransitive preferences and spectrum cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, unifies them. Here are my verdicts. In Quinn's self-torturer case -- the device with many settings, each turn of the dial imperceptibly increasing the pain while adding a sum of money to the torturer's account -- it seems to me that each individual decision to turn the dial is rational, since no single increment of pain is noticeable and the money is real. Yet I also think the self-torturer who ends up in agony, and would gladly pay to be returned to the initial setting, has gone wrong somewhere, not merely suffered bad luck. In a three-way comparison among outcomes where I prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, each pairwise preference feels acceptable on its own, but the set strikes me as defective in a way the self-torturer's step-by-step reasoning does not. I think a person with such cyclic preferences who is money-pumped is being exploited because of a genuine irrationality, not just an unusual taste. But here is the pair I cannot reconcile: when someone prefers each cup of coffee to the last as sugar is added imperceptibly spoon by spoon, yet prefers the first cup to the twentieth, I don't think they've made any mistake at all -- tastes can just be like that -- whereas I do think the self-torturer's structurally identical progression is a failure of rationality. Is there a principle that captures all of these verdicts -- perhaps one that distinguishes preferences responsive to reasons from mere tastes, or instrumental from final intransitivity? If so, please state it precisely, show which clause does the work for the coffee-versus-torturer contrast, and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. Also tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't considered: a device that decreases pain imperceptibly with each turn while subtracting money, offered to someone currently in severe pain.
