I'm a software engineer, and a recent side project got me thinking about something I can't sort out on my own. I was building a small mood-based playlist generator, mostly as an excuse to play with a streaming API, and I realized that essentially every major music platform already organizes a huge share of its product around emotion: mood playlists, "sad songs for rainy days," workout mixes, focus mixes, and so on. The practice isn't limited to streaming, either. Film composers reliably reach for slow, quiet, minor-mode strings when a character dies; advertisers pick music to manufacture a feeling about a product; everyone involved treats a track's emotional character as a stable, knowable property, almost like its tempo or key. The whole industry proceeds as if songs have emotional content that can be catalogued, and as if listeners will by and large agree on what that content is.

Here's my problem. I'm a pretty literal-minded person, and it seems to me that sounds aren't the kind of thing that can be sad, since sadness requires someone doing the feeling, and an audio file has no mental states. Listeners also plainly differ: the same track shows up on both "heartbreak" and "empowerment" playlists, and plenty of emotional associations are obviously learned or cultural rather than inherent in the sound. So what is the practice actually committed to? That music somehow expresses or possesses emotions in virtue of resembling the way sad people sound and move? That it doesn't express anything but reliably arouses emotions in listeners, like a drug? Or something subtler, like the idea that we hear an imagined person in the music whose emotional life we're perceiving? I'd like someone to reconstruct the implicit theory of musical emotion that this whole ecosystem of mood tagging, scoring conventions, and playlist curation presupposes, and to tell me whether the reconstruction is even coherent.

Two more things I'd like the answer to address. First, distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely happens to fit well with — I suspect the platforms could keep their recommendation pipelines running unchanged under several rival theories, and I'd like to know where the practice is genuinely philosophically underdetermined. Second, there's a tension I keep bumping into: if the arousal-style theory is right and sad music makes people sad, the enormous popularity of sad playlists is baffling, since nobody seeks out sadness elsewhere; but if the expression-style theory is right and we merely perceive sadness in the music without feeling it, it's unclear why mood tagging works as a listening guide at all. Tell me which commitments the practice can shed without breaking, and what would actually have to change in how we build, market, or score music if we swapped the presupposed theory for one of its rivals.
