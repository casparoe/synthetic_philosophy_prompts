I am a retired molecular biologist who has spent the past year working through the philosophy of mind, and I keep returning to one exchange I would like examined carefully. Searle holds that intentionality comes in two kinds: original (or intrinsic) intentionality, which he says belongs to minds like ours, and derived intentionality, which belongs to things like words, maps, and thermostats, which mean what they mean only because some mind interprets them or builds them to function a certain way. Against this, Dennett offers his two-bitser thought experiment: a vending machine designed in America to accept quarters is later put into service somewhere that a physically different coin of the same size and weight is what actually triggers its mechanism. Dennett's point, as I understand it, is that nothing inside the machine settles what its internal state "really" refers to; the reference is fixed only by the selection or design history, and he argues that human intentionality is in the same position, since our brains were likewise shaped by a selection process. He takes this to undermine Searle's claim that our intentionality is original in a way the machine's is not.

Here is where I need help as an outsider. The argument is doing its work through an analogy -- the mind is to natural selection as the vending machine is to its human designers -- and I would like that analogy audited rather than either accepted or dismissed. Please give me:

1. An explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the machine, the designers, the design history, the internal state, the coin it was built for, and the coin that actually triggers it, when the analogy is applied to a human brain and its evolutionary history.

2. The disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion, separated from the ones that do not. In particular: natural selection has no intentions, foresight, or purposes, whereas the machine's designers plainly did -- so if the machine's meaning is "derived" precisely because it traces back to designers with minds, does relocating the source of derivation to a mindless process dissolve the original/derived distinction, or does it instead show that the machine case was never the right model? And does the question of what the internal state refers to admit a fact-of-the-matter answer in the brain case that it lacks in the machine case, or vice versa?

3. An assessment of whether Dennett's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Does the thought experiment actually touch Searle's core claim -- that there is something it is like for a state to be about something from the inside -- or does it at most show that ascriptions of content are interpreter-relative, which Searle could perhaps accept about the machine while denying about us? In other words: is the analogy carrying the argument, or is there an argument underneath that could be stated without it?

4. If the analogy fails or only partly succeeds, a brief statement of what a better analogy for Dennett's position would be, or an explanation of why the position resists analogical presentation.

Please answer in American English. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for citations; I want the analogy itself dissected and a verdict on what it can and cannot carry.
