I'm in an upper-level metaethics seminar and we've spent the last two weeks on expressivism. After class on Thursday two people from my cohort, Maya and Dan, got into an argument about the Frege-Geach problem that neither of them would drop, and now it's spilled over into our group chat. I stayed out of it because I honestly couldn't tell who was winning, so I want an outside referee.

Maya opened by saying expressivism is still sunk by the embedding problem: in the conditional "if stealing is wrong, then teaching your little brother to steal is wrong," the phrase "stealing is wrong" doesn't express disapproval, so when you combine it with the free-standing "stealing is wrong" the argument equivocates. Dan said this is old news — expressivists developed compositional semantics decades ago where the embedded occurrence still hooks up to the disapproval state, and validity gets reanalyzed as inconsistency of attitudes rather than preservation of truth. Maya came back with two points: first, that "consistency of attitudes" means moral arguments can be valid but never sound in the old sense, so the fix changes the subject instead of solving the problem; and second, that mixed inferences like "if stealing is wrong, I'll regret it; stealing is wrong; so I'll regret it" look weird on the attitude story because the moral claim sits inside a conditional whose other half is a plain empirical prediction. Dan's response was to offer his own toy proposal: conditionals with moral antecedents express *conditional attitudes* — roughly, disapproval of X held on the supposition that Y. Maya shot back that this misdescribes what you're doing when you entertain a moral conditional purely hypothetically, say while testing whether a theory implies something absurd, because you might not have any attitude toward stealing at that moment at all. Dan then said her whole line of attack proves too much, since the same worries would arise for normative epistemic claims like "you ought to believe what the evidence supports," which obviously embed fine. Maya's last move was that once the expressivist builds attitudes that commit you to other attitudes in exactly belief-like ways, they've reinvented belief under a new name and the view collapses into a minimal kind of cognitivism.

Please adjudicate who's right at each step of the exchange and say who's more right overall, using exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, and write it in American English.
