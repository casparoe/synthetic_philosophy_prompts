I run a study group for people who mostly came up through Overcoming Bias, the Sequences, and adjacent forums, and we're holding a mock qualifying exam in formal epistemology. Everyone in the group has Aumann's agreement theorem memorized, which is exactly the problem.

Write me one essay question on the common prior assumption that a strong candidate could answer in 75 minutes. The question must be unanswerable by reciting the theorem. It should force candidates to confront the status of the assumption itself: whether it states a norm of rationality, an empirical hypothesis about reasoners, or merely a modeling convention -- and to notice how much weight it carries. Specifically, the question should push them on the following pressure point: given any pattern of persistent disagreement, one can seemingly defend each party's rationality by positing different priors, since priors are not directly observable; so the common prior assumption appears to be the only thing standing between Aumann's theorem and vacuity. Is there any non-question-begging justification for assuming a common prior (e.g., the Harsanyi doctrine), or does the rationalist injunction to "update toward agreement" rest on a premise no one has actually defended?

Deliver three things:

1. The exam question itself, phrased the way it would appear on the paper.
2. A model answer of roughly 600 words. It must take a definite position on what the common prior assumption is and whether the agreement norm survives the pressure point -- no laying out three views and declining to choose. Give reasons for the position taken and name the strongest objection to it.
3. A marking scheme out of 25 marks, built around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth, plus a one-line note for each on the characteristic way candidates fumble it (for instance: treating the common prior assumption as an empirical claim about human cognitive uniformity; invoking Harsanyi as though it settles the normative question rather than restating it; or reading the theorem as showing that disagreement is irrational when it in fact shows only that disagreement, a common prior, and common knowledge of posteriors are jointly inconsistent).
