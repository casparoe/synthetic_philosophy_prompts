So I do the interview segment for a long-form podcast about science and big ideas, and next month we've got a historian on whose new book argues that the antebellum US Constitution was, properly read, an anti-slavery document. She's basically taking Frederick Douglass's later line after his split from the Garrisonians: the text never names slavery, the framers' euphemisms show they were embarrased by it, and the document gave the federal government plenty of tools to strangle the institution. I want the episode to do more than let her run through the greatest hits, so draft me ten or so questions with a follow-up to each one, keyed to the answer she's most likely to give.

A few spots I'd like you to press on:

- The Fugitive Slave Clause. She'll say 'person held to service or labour' could cover indentured servants and that the framers pointedly avoided the word slave. What's the follow-up that forces her to deal with the fact that a Congress full of framers passed the 1793 Fugitive Slave Act almost immediately?
- The Three-Fifths Clause. The usual defence is that counting enslaved people less would have given the South *more* power, so the compromise actually weakened slave states. I want a question that gets at whether that's really an anti-slavery argument or just a less-bad-than-the-alternative argument.
- The clause protecting the Atlantic slave trade until 1808. She'll presumably point to Congress banning it at the first opportunity. Follow-up needed on whether a 20-year moratorium on abolition is the kind of thing a genuinely anti-slavery document locks in.
- Douglass's method itself: he insisted on reading the text rather than the framers' intentions. I'd like a question probing whether that textualism is doing real work or is just convenient here, plus one on whether it matters that basically nobody at ratification read it his way.
- The obvious sceptical one: if the Constitution was already anti-slavery, why did abolition take a civil war and three constitutional amendments? And then a follow-up on whether her answer reduces the whole view to 'it could have been read that way in a better world', which is a much weaker claim than the book's title suggests.
- End with something on what historical evidence could actually change her mind, because right now I can't tell whether the view is falsifiable at all.

Keep the questions in my voice, fairly informal, and don't make them so long that she can filibuster. Cheers.
