I'm a junior at a high school named after Thomas Jefferson, and our student council has spent the past month debating whether to petition for a name change. The debate keeps collapsing into the same shallow exchange: one side says Jefferson owned slaves, the other side says he was a man of his time and we can't judge the past by present standards, and then everyone repeats themselves louder. For my philosophy elective, I want to construct something better than the actual debate we've been having. Write me a dialogue between two equally sharp interlocutors — one defending the man-of-his-time position, one rejecting it — that could serve as a model for how this conversation should go. Neither should be a strawman: the defender of contextual judgment should be the strongest version of that view, not a lazy relativist, and the critic should be the strongest version of that view, not a presentist who thinks morality was obvious all along.

Here's the twist I'd like the dialogue to be organized around, because I think it's the part our school debate has been missing. I've been developing a distinction in my notebook between two kinds of cases, which I've been calling unavailable standards and available-but-discounted standards. In the first kind, the moral standard we now apply simply wasn't accessible to anyone in the figure's world — nobody around them articulated it, and the conceptual tools for it didn't exist yet. In the second kind, the standard was fully available: contemporaries were articulating it, sometimes directly to the figure's face, and the figure even endorsed it in the abstract, but applied a kind of discount rate to it when it touched their own interests. Jefferson strikes me as the clearest second-kind case, since he wrote powerfully about the injustice of slavery while continuing to enslave people, and was challenged on it by correspondents in his own lifetime. Columbus seems relevant too, given that he was arrested and sent back to Spain in chains by the crown's own investigator, and that critics of his conduct existed within his own century, not just ours. Churchill is the hardest case for my distinction, and I want the dialogue to sit with that difficulty rather than resolve it neatly.

The dialogue should have genuine back-and-forth — each speaker responding to the other's actual last point, conceding where concession is forced. Specifically, I want the critic of the man-of-his-time defence to have to answer the obvious counter-thrust: that we ourselves discount standards we fully endorse (most of us believe animal suffering matters and still eat factory-farmed meat, believe carbon emissions harm future people and still fly), so the second kind of case describes nearly everyone, including the judges. And I want the contextualist to have to answer the question of what, exactly, the excusing factor is supposed to be if not ignorance — social pressure, financial interest, the difficulty of being better than one's neighbors — and whether those factors excuse or merely explain. The dialogue shouldn't end in tidy agreement, but by the end both speakers should have located the actual crux of their disagreement and named it explicitly, so a reader can see precisely which premise they'd have to give up to switch sides. Somewhere in the exchange, have them test my two-kind distinction against at least one case each thinks it handles badly.
