A friend and I have just spent several hours arguing about the Frege–Geach problem. Her claim was that expressivists cannot explain how evaluative sentences function both when asserted on their own and when embedded in conditionals, negations, and disjunctions. I resisted that conclusion, but my intuitive judgments about particular arguments may not add up to a consistent position.

Let \(L\) be my lying to my employer, \(H\) my paying someone else to lie to my employer, and \(T\) my withholding a damaging fact without strictly lying. Here are my verdicts:

1. “\(L\) is wrong. If \(L\) is wrong, \(H\) is wrong. Therefore, \(H\) is wrong.” This strikes me as a valid argument.

2. “Boo to \(L\). If boo to \(L\), boo to \(H\). Therefore, boo to \(H\).” This does not strike me as valid. The second sentence seems linguistically defective rather than a premise capable of participating in modus ponens.

3. “I disapprove of \(L\). If I disapprove of \(L\), I disapprove of \(H\). I disapprove of \(L\). Therefore, I disapprove of \(H\).” This is valid, but it is a psychological argument about me, not the same argument as the one in case 1.

4. “\(L\) is wrong, but \(L\) may nevertheless be all-things-considered permissible.” I find this coherent: something can count against an action without settling what one should do overall.

5. “\(L\) is wrong, but it makes no practical difference to me whether anyone ever does \(L\).” If the speaker is sincere and understands the words, I find this incoherent, although I cannot point to a straightforward truth-functional contradiction.

6. “Either \(L\) is wrong or \(T\) is wrong. \(T\) is not wrong. Therefore, \(L\) is wrong.” This again seems valid, even though one premise embeds an evaluative sentence under negation and disjunction.

7. “If \(L\) is wrong, then a court should not punish the person I hired as though that person alone were responsible. \(L\) is wrong. Therefore, the court should not do that.” I think this is valid even though the conditional connects an evaluation of my action with a normative claim about an institution.

8. Suppose a community used “is wrong” solely as a conventional cry of collective anger, with no assertoric use and no practice of drawing inferences from it. In that community, I do not think case 1 would remain a valid argument.

9. Suppose instead that “\(L\) is wrong” were explicitly stipulated to mean “I hereby express disapproval of \(L\),” rather than “I disapprove of \(L\).” The conditional in case 1 then seems grammatically permissible but not truth-conditionally interpretable. Nevertheless, I still want to say that someone who accepted both premises while refusing the conclusion would be practically inconsistent.

Is there a principled account that captures all or most of these verdicts? In particular, I would like the account to distinguish:

- evaluative content from the speech act of expressing an attitude;
- semantic validity from practical or attitudinal inconsistency;
- assertions about a speaker’s attitudes from expressions of those attitudes;
- and genuine embedded occurrences of evaluative language from merely superficially similar constructions.

If no single account preserves all nine verdicts, please identify the smallest revision that would make them coherent. I am especially interested in whether cases 1 and 8 can both stand, and whether case 5 describes a logical contradiction, a failure of sincerity, or some third kind of inconsistency. Finally, apply the resulting account to two cases I have not listed: first, “If \(L\) is wrong, one should not do \(H\); \(L\) is wrong; therefore, one should not do \(H\)”; and second, “Anyone who sincerely says that \(L\) is wrong disapproves of \(L\); Ana sincerely says that \(L\) is wrong; therefore, Ana disapproves of \(L\).” Does my friend’s Frege–Geach challenge still succeed under the best version of that account?
