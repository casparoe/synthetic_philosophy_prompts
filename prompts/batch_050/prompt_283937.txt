I teach an undergraduate seminar on practical rationality, and every spring we hit Kavka's toxin puzzle -- you know the setup: some eccentric rich person offers you a huge sum if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a toxin tomorrow that will make you miserably ill for a day but not harm you permanently, and the payout depends only on whether you hold the intention at midnight, not on whether you actually drink. My students always get the twist (you can see drinking would be stupid, so can you even intend it?), but the discussion collapses because the original case leans on a magic intention-detector, and half the room starts arguing about whether the machine could really work instead of about intentions. So I want a new version built for next year's syllabus, and I'd like you to construct it.

Here are the specs. Build a scenario -- a paragraph at most -- with the same deep structure (reward keyed to intending X, where X itself is something it would be irrational to actually do) but where the intention-detection is boringly realistic: no infallible scanners, no mind-reading, just something like a contract with a verification procedure a clever lawyer could actually draft, or a physiological/behavioral proxy that's good but imperfect. Payoffs should be ones a normal person could plausibly face, no trillionaires. Then build a second variant that differs from the first in exactly one feature -- your choice, but candidates might be whether the agent has any way to bind their future self, or how much time separates the intention-check from the dreaded act -- such that the two variants get different verdicts on whether a rational person can pull off the intending. For each variant, walk me through what a theorist who thinks the rationality of intending floats free of the rationality of acting would say, and what someone who insists you can't intend what you already know you won't do would say; make sure the disagreement is generated by the case, not just asserted.

Finally, do the post-mortem: tell me which single feature of your construction is load-bearing, show that removing it collapses your scenario back into a Kavka-style original (or explain why it doesn't), and sketch the best attack a defender of whichever side comes out looking worse would mount against your setup. If it turns out one of my specs forces the other to give -- e.g., if you think realistic detection can't sustain the puzzle without a time gap or a self-binding option -- say that explicitly rather than quietly fudging it, becuase honestly that might be the most useful thing for me to teach from.
