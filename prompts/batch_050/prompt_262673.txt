I am a novelist at work on a book about two adult sisters negotiating the long decline of their father — one who moves back to their hometown and takes on the daily care, one who sends money from abroad and feels, with some justice, that this should count. In preparing the manuscript I have been reading the philosophical literature on filial obligation, and I keep circling Jane English's proposal that grown children owe their parents nothing as a debt — that past sacrifices, once the child is grown, create no standing obligation, and that whatever children owe flows instead from the ongoing relationship, owed only in the way friendship is owed and only while the friendship exists. The view is attractive and, I think, dramatically explosive: my two sisters could each be read as living one side of it. What I want from you is help deciding which philosophical fault lines in this debate deserve to structure the novel.

Concretely: I have room for perhaps three deep disagreements to do real structural work in the book — to be things the characters are, in effect, on opposite sides of, such that the reader feels the pull of both positions. Which open questions in the filial-duties debate should those be, and in what order of importance? Some candidates I have been weighing: whether the debt model survives the objection that children never consented to being born or raised; whether the friendship model lets the dutiful-but-resentful caregiver off too easily, or instead correctly diagnoses why resentment poisons care; whether obligations survive the parent's failure or cruelty, and where exactly the line falls between an imperfect parent and one who has voided the contract; whether geographical distance, differing conceptions of a good life, or sheer inequality of sacrifice between siblings change what is owed or merely who pays it. For each question you prioritize, I want the reasoning, not just the verdict: whether it is upstream of the others (so that a character's answer to it determines her answers elsewhere), whether it maps onto conflicts people actually have rather than ones only philosophers have, and whether both sides can be inhabited sympathetically — a question where one side is simply indefensible is useless to me as drama however important it is as philosophy. And tell me which of the debate's standard implications are overrated for my purposes: which questions get heavy attention in the literature but would, if dramatized faithfully, resolve too quickly or too coldly on the page. If it helps, treat me as you would a collaborator with one book's worth of attention to spend, and tell me what would change your ranking — what fact about my characters or my readers would move a question up or down the list.
