I'm presenting at our department's philosophy of science seminar in two weeks, defending the position that Dutch book arguments don't actually justify probabilism -- the thesis that rational degrees of belief must obey the probability axioms. My argument, roughly: the arguments show at most that agents who accept bets according to their credences and who care only about money can be exploited, but real rationality isn't about monetary outcomes, the bridge principles connecting credences to betting behavior are optional and contestable, and the pragmatic framing sneaks in consequentialist assumptions that don't establish anything epistemic. I think non-probabilistic credences can be perfectly rational and the whole Dutch book tradition rests on a bait-and-switch between a theorem about bets and a claim about belief.

The audience: this is the philosophy department's weekly seminar, so I'll have maybe fifteen people. Three or four are committed formal epistemologists who treat the representation theorems and accuracy arguments as basically settled science and will know the Ramsey-de Finetti stuff cold. There are also a couple of straight philosophers of science who are sympathetic to Bayesiansim in practice but haven't thought about the foundations, two older faculty who are skeptical of formal methods generally and might actually be on my side for bad reasons, and one visiting logic professor whose deal I don't know. Nobody is a decision theorist proper.

Prepare me for the Q&A. I want the objections in roughly the order I'll actually get them, phrased the way these specific people would phrase them -- the formal epistemologists will talk about representation theorems and probably bring up accuracy dominance as the better argument, the straight philosophers of science will push from scientific practice. For each objection tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and give me a reply I can deliver in under two minutes. Also flag where my own argument is weakest -- I suspect the formal people will say I've attacked the least sophisticated version of probabilism while the accuracy-first arguments do the same work without any bets at all, and I'm honestly not sure my reply to that is good. Include the question I'm most hoping nobody asks and a sketch of what I'd say if they do.
