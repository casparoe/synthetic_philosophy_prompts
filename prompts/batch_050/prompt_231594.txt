I've been asked to give the evening lecture at a study day hosted by a defence studies department next month — the kind of place that trains serving officers — and I'm slightly regretting saying yes. My talk defends a version of contingent pacifism: roughly, that modern industrial war cannot realistically satisfy the discrimination and proportionality conditions of the just war tradition, so the tradition itself ends up condemning almost all the wars its enthusiasts want to license. The audience will be mostly serving and recently retired officers, a handful of military historians, and two or three just war theorists who will all have been to the same conferences. As a hook I want to open not with the usual pacifist horror-catalogue but with William James, from his 1910 essay The Moral Equivalent of War — not the bit everyone quotes about pugnacity being bred into our bone and marrow, but this:

"Showing war's irrationality and horror is of no effect upon him. The horrors make the fascination. War is the strong life; it is life in extremis; war-taxes are the only ones men never hesitate to pay; as the budgets of all nations show us."

My reading is that James spotted, a century early, exactly why the standard case against war falls flat in rooms like the one I'll be in: dwelling on war's irrationality and ghastliness doesn't dent its appeal, because for many people the ghastliness is the appeal, or at least inseperable from it. So my talk concedes him that, argues that a serious pacifism has to out-compete what war does for people morally rather than merely denounce it, and then tries to say what that out-competing looks like. What I want from you is my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the objections I'll actually get from this audience — not the ones my philosophy colleagues would raise over sherry — in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way a brigadier or a war studies lecturer would genuinely phrase them (in their vocabulary, not ours), ranked by how much damage each would do to the talk. For each: a two-minute reply, and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which objections I should concede straight away and which I should dig in on. I particularly want to know how they'll react to the James gambit itself, since at least a few of them will have read the essay and will know perfectly well that James was lecturing the peace movement of his day rather than joining it — so if that move is going to blow up on me, say so now rather than let me find out on the night. Finish with the weakest point in my own position — the bit I should prepare to defend properly instead of hoping nobody prods it — and the question I'm quietly praying nobody asks.
