A relative of mine, arguing for the legalization of assisted dying, returns again and again to the same comparison. When our family's dog was terminally ill — in pain, with no prospect of recovery — we did the merciful thing and had the vet end its life, and anyone who had instead kept the dog alive in that condition would have been thought cruel. To force a dying person through the same final weeks, she says, is to extend to our pets a compassion we refuse our own parents. The analogy is doing serious work in her argument: it is meant to establish not merely that assisted dying is permissible, but that anyone who has ever approved of euthanizing a suffering pet is committed to it on pain of inconsistency. I would like an audit of how far it actually holds.

Please give me the explicit correspondence first: what plays the role of the veterinarian, of the owner, of the terminal prognosis, of the judgment that continued life is a harm and death a benefit, and — crucially — of the consent that is present in the human case but absent in the animal one, where a proxy decides. Then the disanalogies, and for each one a verdict on whether it matters for her conclusion or is merely incidental detail.

One pressure point I especially want examined: in the animal case the decision is made without the patient's consent, by a party whose own interests (grief, expense, the burden of care) are entangled in it, and we regard this as unproblematic. Does that mean the analogy, taken strictly, licenses more than the voluntary, request-based regime my relative supports — while at the same time giving her less, since the proxy structure is precisely what safeguards in proposed assisted-dying laws are designed to exclude? And does the consistency charge even survive once we note that approving of euthanasia for creatures that cannot consent might commit one to nothing about creatures that can?

Finally: if the analogy cannot carry the incoherence accusation, is there a better comparison available for the conclusion she actually wants, or should she abandon the argument from consistency and rest the case elsewhere?
