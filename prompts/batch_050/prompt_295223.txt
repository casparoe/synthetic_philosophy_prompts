At a methods workshop last month, a senior economist shut down a criticism of a model in my working group with the line that the realism of a theory's assumptions is irrelevant -- all that matters is whether the predictions check out. I later learned this is a compressed version of an argument from a famous mid-century methodology essay, now circulating in my field as a one-liner that gets deployed against any objection to an idealized assumption: firms as profit maximizers, consumers as consistent, markets as frictionless. I suspect the slogan is doing more work than it can support, but I can't yet say which version of it my colleague needed versus which version he actually has.

Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing. Here is what I want for each reading:

1. State the thesis precisely enough that it has truth conditions. As candidates, consider at least: the purely instrumental reading (a model is nothing but a prediction device, so its parts are beyond appraisal); the as-if reading (agents need not actually do what the model says, so long as they behave as if they did); the negligibility reading (the false assumption does not materially affect the conclusions); the domain reading (the assumption is not a claim but a marker of the conditions under which the model is meant to apply); the robustness reading (the conclusions would survive a range of relaxations of the assumption, so its literal falsity is beside the point); and the approximation reading (the assumption is false but close enough to true for the question at hand). If there is a further distinct reading I have missed, add it.

2. For each, give a verdict: is it a platitude no one should dispute, a substantive but defensible position, or a thesis that is straightforwardly false -- and why. In particular, I want to know whether the robustness reading collapses into the negligibility reading or is genuinely stronger, and whether the as-if reading is compatible with using the model to explain rather than merely to forecast.

3. For each, say whether it would actually license the move my colleague made: dismissing the criticism of a specific assumption on the grounds that the model predicts well on historical data. Note which readings require the model's predictions to be successful in the domain where the assumption is false, as opposed to successful somewhere or other, and which readings silently shift from prediction to counterfactual -- the model will be used to answer what-if questions about a policy intervention, where a false assumption about the mechanism can change the answer even if within-domain forecasts were fine.

End with a short verdict of your own: which reading is most defensible on its merits, which reading my colleague most likely needed for his dismissal to go through, and whether the slogan survives once you require a single reading to do both jobs.
