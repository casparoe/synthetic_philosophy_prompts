So I work in distributed systems and I've been trying to make sense of an idea I keep running into in decision theory / AI-safety discussions: commitment races. The rough idea, as I understand it, is that agents who can verifiably bind themselves to a course of action early get a huge strategic advantage over agents who commit later — like the game of chicken where the first driver to visibly rip out his steering wheel wins. The worry is that this creates a race dynamic: everyone is incentivized to commit faster and more irreversibly than everyone else, even when the resulting commitments lock everyone into outcomes (mutually destructive ones, or lopsided ones) that they'd all have preferred to negotiate away from. There are proposals out there for defusing this but set those aside for now.

Here's what I want. Translate the main claims of the commitment-races picture into the vocabulary I actually think in: concurrency control. Locks, atomicity, race conditions, TOCTOU bugs, ordering guarantees, linearizability, optimistic vs pessimistic concurrency, deadlock, consensus protocols. Go claim by claim. For each one tell me: (1) does it translate cleanly — e.g. is first-mover advantage in commitment basically just "whoever grabs the lock first gets to dictate the state everyone else must respond to"; (2) does it only translate with distortion — I'm suspicious, for instance, that real commitments are nothing like atomic operations, since they have to be observed, verified, and believed by other agents, and can sometimes be revoked or misread, and I don't know whether the decision-theory version smuggles in an atomicity assumption that does all the work; (3) or does it have no expression in my vocabulary at all — candidates: the idea that the committing agent is rational and anticipates how others will reason about its commitment, the normative valence (that the race is *bad* rather than merely a liveness failure), or whatever else you think doesn't survive.

Then the part I actually care about: looking at the residue, does it point to something the commitment-races literature sees that concurrency theory is just blind to, or does it suggest the decision theorists are describing a familiar systems problem in unusually dramatic language and the exotic parts dissolve on contact? Be honest about it — don't force a translation that isn't there, and don't let my vocabulary's assumptions masquerade as translations either.
