I run the standards desk at a general-audience history magazine, and I need a procedure my editors can apply to causal sentences in drafts. Every feature contains claims like "the assassination caused the war," "overextension doomed the empire," or "the election made secession inevitable," and at present we have no shared standard for when these survive editing. Two problems keep recurring. First, our junior editors apply an informal "but for" test: mentally delete the alleged cause, ask whether the effect still happens, and if it does, strike or weaken the sentence. This test clears almost nothing in exactly the cases our writers care about most, because they gravitate toward events that historians treat as overdetermined — outcomes with several independently sufficient causes, where deleting any one of them leaves the outcome in place. Second, when a sentence fails, editors have no controlled vocabulary for the revision, so fixes are negotiated ad hoc and the magazine contradicts itself across issues.

Write a procedure an editor with no philosophy background can run on a single causal sentence in about twenty minutes, using only the draft and ordinary historical reference knowledge. It should be an ordered sequence of tests, each with a stated pass/fail criterion, and it must at minimum sort the sentence into the right one of these situations: (a) the factor was not causally relevant at all; (b) the sentence fails the counterfactual test only because the outcome was overdetermined, in which case the factor may still be a genuine cause and striking it would be an error; (c) the factor produced the outcome ahead of another process that would have produced it shortly anyway; (d) the factor was a background condition or enabling condition rather than a trigger — the tinder rather than the spark — and the sentence has conflated the two. For each situation, the procedure should specify what the sentence is permitted to say instead, using a small controlled vocabulary — something like "caused," "sufficed for," "contributed to," "triggered," "hastened," "made inevitable" — with rules mapping test outcomes to permitted verbs. Say explicitly which philosophical distinctions had to survive the simplification and which you judged safe to drop, and what to do when an editor cannot settle the relevant counterfactual at all (how much of the world do you hold fixed when you mentally delete the cause, and who decides?).

Then run the procedure on three draft sentences: "Without the assassination at Sarajevo, there would have been no First World War"; "The Western Roman Empire fell because of overextension, plague, economic decline, and barbarian pressure"; and "The printing press caused the Reformation." At least one of these should produce a verdict the writer will fight — show the procedure forcing a downgrade on a sentence that reads well and that the author will want to keep, and indicate what the editor should say in that conversation. Finally, mark the hand-off points where the procedure must stop and defer to a historian or senior editor — apportioning relative weight among contributing causes, adjudicating inevitability claims, and sentences where causal language is doing moral work ("was responsible for") — and state honestly what the procedure cannot do and the kinds of sentences on which it will reliably misfire.
