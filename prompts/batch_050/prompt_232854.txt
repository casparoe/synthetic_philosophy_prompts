I've spent years reading the rationalist blogs and forums, and one thing I keep noticing is that a particular argument has been frozen in place for well over a decade: the dispute over whether Solomonoff induction finally answers the problem of induction and vindicates Occam's razor, or whether it merely repackages them. The enthusiasts point to the universality results -- the universal prior dominates every computable distribution up to a constant factor that depends on the choice of reference machine, so on long enough data streams the initial machine-dependence washes out -- and conclude that "prefer the shortest program" is the razor, formalized, with incomputability being a practical inconvenience rather than a conceptual flaw. The skeptics reply that a prior you cannot compute is not an epistemology, that the constant factor can be arbitrarily large and so "long enough data" does real work that the theorem itself does not justify, and that any computable approximation (say, restricting to short programs with bounded runtime) smuggles back in exactly the judgment calls the formalism was supposed to eliminate. Both sides, as far as I can tell, know all the same theorems, cite the same results, and have not updated toward each other at all.

My tentative diagnosis, which I'd like you to scrutinize rather than accept, is that the two camps are answering different questions under one name: the enthusiasts are describing the ideal limit of prediction -- what a Bayesian god would converge on -- while the skeptics are asking what a bounded agent is actually licensed to believe here and now, and that "does Solomonoff solve induction?" is ambiguous between these in a way neither side has cleanly stated. Is that the right structural diagnosis, or is there a genuine substantive disagreement underneath -- for instance, a real dispute about whether asymptotic convergence results bear on finite epistemic warrant, or about whether simplicity is constitutive of rationality or merely instrumentally useful? If the disagreement is verbal, say exactly how to disambiguate the question so each side can see what the other was claiming. If it is substantive, identify the precise proposition the two sides actually disagree about, and say what would count as progress: a new theorem (and what it would have to establish), a conceptual distinction that dissolves part of the dispute, or a demonstration that one side's question is ill-posed. I'm less interested in a verdict on who's right than in a map of why smart people who share all the relevant formal machinery are still stuck -- and an honest assessment of whether the impasse is the kind that a sharper question could break, or the kind that reflects a real fork in epistemic values that no formal result can adjudicate.
