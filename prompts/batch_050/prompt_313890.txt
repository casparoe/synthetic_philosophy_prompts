I'm trying to work out what I actually think democratic backsliding is by consulting my verdicts on cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, unifies them. Hungary under Orbán since 2010 -- a new constitution, a packed constitutional court, media regulators staffed by loyalists, electoral rules redrawn to the governing party's advantage -- is clearly backsliding. Turkey after the 2016 coup attempt, with mass purges of judges and academics and a referendum concentrating power in the presidency, is clearly backsliding. Poland's PiS government taking control of the constitutional tribunal and forcing judges into retirement: backsliding, but somehow milder, and I note it was partly reversed after a change of government, which makes me think backsliding admits of degrees and can be undone. Germany dissolving a neo-Nazi party through its courts would not be backsliding in my view; that is a democracy defending itself. Yet Turkish courts banning pro-Kurdish parties does feel like backsliding, and I cannot easily say what distinguishes the two, since both involve courts restricting political competition. Had Roosevelt's 1937 court-packing plan passed, that would have been backsliding even though entirely legal -- which tells me legality isn't the test -- but then I'm unsure why I'm inclined to excuse a legislature that strips an incoming opposition governor of appointment powers before he takes office, which also feels merely hardball rather than democratic decay, and I'm not sure those two verdicts can coexist. Finally, a country whose founding constitution was already highly majoritarian and is then amended to concentrate power further: is that backsliding from a baseline, or does the baseline itself have to be democratic for the concept to apply? Is there a principle that captures most of these verdicts -- say, what makes court-packing backsliding but the German party ban not, and what makes the Polish case a matter of degree? If the cleanest principle forces me to abandon one of my judgments, tell me which one and why, and apply the resulting account to a case I haven't listed: a ruling party that loses an election and, during the transition, expands the supreme court and fills the new seats before the winners are sworn in. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- whether a single principle captures my verdicts and, briefly, what it is -- then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
