A colleague has an argument that all cultures of conflict resolution are morally on a par, and I suspect it proves too much, but I cannot locate the loose step. The background is the familiar threefold scheme: honour cultures, in which a person of standing must answer affronts personally, so that redress follows reputation and the credible threat of violence; dignity cultures, in which worth is taken as inherent, slights are beneath notice, and serious wrongs are referred to impartial third parties such as courts; and victimhood cultures, in which aggrieved parties publicise their victimhood in order to recruit third parties to their cause. His argument runs as follows:

(1) A culture's way of handling grievances is just only insofar as redress tracks desert — who was actually wronged — rather than power.
(2) In an honour culture, whether a wrong is answered depends on the victim's own strength and standing; so honour culture fails (1).
(3) In a victimhood culture, whether a wrong is answered depends on the victim's capacity to mobilise sympathetic third parties — eloquence, connections, visibility — which is power in a different currency; so victimhood culture fails (1).
(4) In a dignity culture, whether a wrong is answered depends on the victim's access to courts and agencies, which in practice tracks wealth, education and legal literacy — power again; so dignity culture fails (1).
(5) Therefore all three fail the only plausible test of a just culture of redress, and no such culture is morally superior to any other.

I want to resist (5); if honesty permits, I would rather keep (1) and the superiority of dignity culture. Yet each step looks like the same move applied three times, so I cannot reject one without an account of why the others survive. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does?
