I work as a software engineer at a company that builds physics simulators, and a side project a few of us maintain has gotten me into an argument I can't resolve. The project simulates the nervous system of the nematode C. elegans -- all 302 neurons, whose wiring is fully mapped -- coupled to a crude virtual body in a virtual environment with simulated food sources and noxious stimuli. The simulation is far from perfect: the worm's biochemistry, neuromodulation, and much of its body are abstracted away, and even its movement only roughly matches the real animal.

At a demo last week, one colleague argued that if we ever close that gap -- if the simulated worm becomes behaviorally indistinguishable from a real one -- then it deserves whatever moral consideration a real worm deserves. Her reasoning was the familiar analogy that the brain is a computer running software: the worm's mind is a program, our simulation runs the same program on different hardware, and running the same program twice gives you the same thing twice, not a picture of the thing. Another colleague pushed back with the standard counter-analogy: a simulation of a rainstorm doesn't get anything wet, so a simulation of a nervous system doesn't feel anything, no matter how faithful.

I'd like an audit of both analogies. Lay out explicitly what is supposed to map onto what in each case -- neurons to hardware, connectome to code, and so on -- and where each mapping breaks down. In particular: what does the brain-as-computer analogy quietly import that a brain may not actually have (clean separation of software from hardware, a well-defined level of abstraction at which the "program" lives, independence from the physical substrate)? And what does the rainstorm analogy quietly import in the other direction -- is the difference between simulating rain and simulating a mind one of kind, or does it just assume what it's trying to prove about which properties are substrate-dependent? Which of the disanalogies actually matter for the moral question, as opposed to being incidental? If both analogies fail, I'd like to know whether there's any principled way to pose the question -- for instance, whether "which computations, if any, have moral standing regardless of substrate" is even a well-formed question, or whether it already smuggles in a contested theory of mind.

One request about calibration: please be careful to separate what is genuinely settled here from what is contested among people who work on these problems. I gather that the worm's connectome being fully mapped is a fact, that current simulations of it are incomplete is a fact, but that the relevant background views -- functionalism about the mind, substrate independence, whether C. elegans is sentient at all, whether behavioral equivalence is the right test -- range from mainstream-but-disputed to minority positions. Please flag which is which rather than presenting any of them as the consensus, and where the answer depends on a genuinely open question, say so plainly instead of resolving it by fiat.
