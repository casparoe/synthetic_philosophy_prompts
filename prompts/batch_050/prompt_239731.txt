Last weekend I was at a cabin with two friends of mine, Maya and Daniel, both of whom have known our mutual friend Priya for about fifteen years and have, as far as I can tell, equally good track records at predicting her behavior. Priya has been offered a job in another city, and over dinner Maya said she was about 70% confident Priya would take it. Daniel said he was at 30%. Maya then proposed that, since they were equally well informed and equally reliable, the rational response for each of them was to move toward the midpoint, somewhere around 50%. Daniel refused outright, and they went back and forth for over an hour while I mostly listened.

The exchange, as I reconstruct it, went roughly like this. Maya argued that Daniel's disagreement was simply evidence, on a par with any other: if a trusted instrument reads 30 while another trusted instrument reads 70, you don't keep trusting the first one just because it happens to be yours. She added that treating your own judgment as specially privileged, when you have no independent reason to think it's better, is just a dressed-up form of bias. Daniel replied that this picture asks him to set aside his actual reasoning — he had specific grounds for his estimate, including things Priya had told him recently about hating to move — and that deferring to a bare disagreement means ceasing to think altogether. He then pressed a further point: Maya's own method is controversial even among people who study disagreement professionally, so if the method is correct, she should suspend judgment about the method itself. Maya answered that this conflated levels: they had already shared all their reasons over dinner, the disagreement had survived the pooling, and the residue left over after reason-sharing was exactly the thing her proposal was meant to handle. She also said his position had no brakes — it let him absorb contrary opinions indefinitely without ever updating, which she called a recipe for arrogance. Daniel's final move was to question whether they were peers on this occasion at all: Maya had formed her 70% estimate late the previous night, after several drinks, and he suggested her track record was no guide to her condition at that moment. Maya accused him of raising the drinks point only because it suited him, noting he had never once doubted his own estimates formed under the same conditions.

I have no stake in this and genuinely can't tell who came out ahead. Please act as a professional epistemologist with expertise in the peer disagreement literature, and think carefully before answering — it may help to first brainstorm, briefly, the strongest version of each side's position before assessing them. Then go through the exchange round by round and adjudicate each specific move: Was Maya right that a disagreement between equals functions as evidence like an instrument reading? Was Daniel right that conciliating requires abandoning one's reasoning rather than integrating new information? Does the self-application worry Daniel raised actually damage Maya's position, or is her level-distinction an adequate reply? Who has the better of the dispute about whether her late-night estimate undermines the peerhood assumption, and does Daniel's selective deployment of that point undercut it? For each step, give a verdict rather than a summary, and conclude by identifying what, if anything, both of them are missing.
