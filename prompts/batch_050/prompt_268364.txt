My criminal-law reading group is considering a hypothetical statute under which a food distributor commits a misdemeanor whenever food it sells contains a dangerous contaminant. The prosecution must prove sale and contamination, but need not prove that the distributor knew about, intended, or negligently failed to discover the contamination; evidence that the distributor used every reasonable precaution would not provide a defense. One member summarized the philosophical problem this way: “Strict liability is liability without fault: the defendant may be convicted even after doing everything a reasonable person would have done. It is therefore fundamentally different from negligence, which punishes a culpable failure to take care. Calling an offense strict-liability merely because the prosecutor need not prove intention would be imprecise, since unintentional conduct can still be negligent, and negligence is a genuine form of fault. The familiar presumption of mens rea expresses the substantive moral principle that criminal punishment always requires a culpable state of mind, not merely a rule for interpreting ambiguous statutes. A blameless defendant cannot deserve punishment, so strict-liability offenses cannot serve retributive aims. If they are defended on the ground that they deter harm or encourage firms to internalize risks, that rationale is purely consequentialist and therefore cannot be limited by proportionality or desert. Still, modest regulatory penalties might be justified when firms voluntarily enter a risky industry and are best placed to prevent the harm, provided the penalties lack the stigma and severity of serious criminal punishment.”

Is this a fair summary? Please go through its main claims and distinguish what a careful philosopher of criminal law would accept from what rests on an equivocation or overstatement. In particular, explain the difference between strict liability as to one element of an offense and liability requiring no culpability whatsoever; whether exercising reasonable care makes a defendant blameless in every morally relevant sense; how negligence relates to mens rea and fault; and why a legal presumption of mens rea should not simply be identified with the moral claim that punishment without fault is impermissible. I am also unsure about the last inference: if strict liability is justified by deterrence or risk allocation, does that really exclude limits based on desert, proportionality, or fair notice? Finally, assess whether the proposed compromise—strict liability only for modest, non-stigmatizing regulatory penalties—avoids the objection to punishing without fault or merely reduces its practical costs.
