After forty years as an experimental physicist, I have taken up philosophy in retirement and now run a small reading group. Next month we are pairing Nozick's experience machine argument with the film The Matrix, and I want a careful philosophical reading to prepare myself. Please focus on Cypher, the man who, knowing the truth, bargains to be reinserted into the simulation on condition that his memory of choosing is erased. Casual discussion treats him simply as a villain, but his choice strikes me as the film's real engagement with Nozick. Address three things: first, whether Cypher's decision is best read as a refutation of hedonism, a vindication of it, or something subtler, such as a preference-satisfaction view applied to a man who knows the preferences being satisfied will be manufactured; second, how the stipulated forgetting bears on the intuition that we want to author our lives rather than merely undergo experiences, and whether that intuition survives once the chooser cannot remember there was a choice; third, whether the film itself condemns Cypher on philosophical grounds or merely on narrative ones, given that his defection is driven less by a taste for illusion than by the genuine wretchedness of the real. Where the film complicates the standard experience machine debate, say so; where it muddles distinct questions, such as what makes a life go well versus what makes a world real, say that instead.
