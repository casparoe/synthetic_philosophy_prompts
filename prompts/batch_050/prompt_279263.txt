So I'm the de facto elections person for my grad student association (nobody else volunteered, classic), and we have to pick a voting system for choosing between three funding proposals next month. In trying to prep for this I fell down a hole reading about the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem, and I brought it to my roommate who's a philosophy undergrad, and she came back at me with an argument that has been haunting me because it seems to prove we might as well not hold the vote at all. It goes like this:

(1) Any social choice rule that takes ranked ballots, always produces a winner from at least three genuine options, is non-dictatorial, and is strategy-proof — doesn't exist. That's Gibbard-Satterthwaite. (2) Whatever system we pick for the funding vote (ranked choice, approval, Borda, whatever) is non-dictatorial and lets any of the three proposals win, so by (1) it is manipulable: there is some situation where a voter gets a better outcome for themselves by lying about their ranking. (3) A rational voter takes the better outcome when it's available, that's just what rationality means. (4) So in our election, some voter will vote strategically rather than sincerely. (5) But the output of the system is computed from the ballots cast, so if any ballot is insincere, the winner is selected on the basis of false information and therefore doesn't reflect what the association actually wants. (6) The entire point of holding a vote instead of just letting the president pick is to find out what the association actually wants. (7) So the vote cannot achieve its point, and we should save everyone the hassle and just let the president pick. Her conclusion: dictatorship is the only "honest" system, everything else is dictatorship plus theater.

I really want to keep (1) since it's a theorem, and I'd like to keep some version of (3) because throwing out rationality feels like cheating. But (7) is absurd — surely ranked choice elections aren't literally equivalent to letting one person decide. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine when I first heard it, and what it costs to reject each of the alternatives instead (e.g. if the problem is in (2)'s use of the theorem, does that mean real systems are secretly strategy-proof? if it's (5), are we saying outcomes can reflect preferences even when people lie?). Also please tell me whether any of this actually bears on what system I should recommend at the meeting, since I have to say something on thursday.
