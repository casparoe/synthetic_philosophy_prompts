I am preparing a graduate seminar handout on partiality toward compatriots, and I want to stress-test an argument I half-believe but whose conclusion I cannot accept. Call it the Entanglement Parity Argument. Please diagnose it with some precision rather than giving a tour of nationalism and cosmopolitanism.

The argument runs as follows. (1) A compatriot has a special claim on me only if some relation between us makes her claim stronger than a comparably situated stranger’s claim. (2) A relation can do that only in one of four ways: I chose or endorsed it; it is reciprocally beneficial in a way that would be unfair to free-ride on; it is coercively imposed and therefore generates remedial duties in those who benefit from or authorize the coercion; or it is partly constitutive of an identity I cannot renounce without loss. (3) Mere shared citizenship usually fails all four tests for the people with whom partiality is most often practiced: most of us did not choose our fellow citizens as such, the benefits among them are wildly uneven, state coercion falls hardest on dissenters, minorities, and the unborn future members who never authorized it, and national identity is optional enough that decent people may be alienated from it without moral defect. (4) What remains morally serious is not the passport but institutional entanglement: being locked into rules that predictably distribute opportunities, risks, emissions, security externalities, tax burdens, and rescue duties. (5) Contemporary entanglement is not contained by borders: supply chains, carbon sinks, monetary policy, antimicrobial resistance, data infrastructure, and refugee-producing wars make many foreigners about as vulnerable to my society’s choices as many compatriots are, and sometimes more so. (6) If entanglement grounds special claims, then the strength of a claim should track degree of imposed vulnerability and reliance, not nationality. (7) Therefore compatriots have no distinctive claims as compatriots; at most they are often, though not always, among the more entangled strangers. (8) But the same scalar logic cannot stop at the border of the state: my child, my elderly neighbor, and a distant garment worker each stand in some mix of chosen, beneficial, coercive, and identity-constituting relations to me, and if only degree of vulnerability and reliance fixes priority, then ordinary family and civic partiality become a bias to be justified case by case against globally stronger claims. (9) That conclusion proves too much: it would make a defensible life require continuous redistributive triage among all affected persons and would condemn as arbitrary nearly every attachment that gives agents reasons to act at all. (10) So at least one of the earlier steps must be wrong, but I cannot locate the error without either smuggling nationality back in as a primitive or making special obligations so scarce that even promises and parenthood look suspect.

If forced to keep as much as possible, I would prefer to retain (1), (4), and (9), and I am most tempted to deny (6). But I suspect the real defect may be earlier, perhaps in the menu at (2), in the move from grounding a claim to measuring its strength, or in an equivocation between claims an agent should honor and claims an institution may enforce. Please identify the exact step you would reject, explain why it initially looks compulsory, and state what your rejection costs for adjacent cases: promises, reparations for colonial or climate harm, conscription, disaster triage after a hurricane, and parents saving their own child first when a donation could save two strangers. A good answer should distinguish three things the argument may be running together—source of a duty, weight of a duty, and legitimate agent-relative prerogative—and should say whether the conclusion is invalidly reached or validly reached from a premise we should learn to live without. Please be explicit about how confident you are in the diagnosis, and single out the one point in your answer where you are least sure, including what evidence or distinction would change your mind.
