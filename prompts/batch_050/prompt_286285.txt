My friend does risk management at a prop trading firm and over the weekend he walked me into an argument that I think proves way too much, but I can't find the step where it goes wrong and it's bugging me. His pitch, roughly, with numbers I filled in: (1) When you bet, outcomes multiply your wealth rather than adding to it -- win a 2:1 bet with half your bankroll and you don't gain "one unit," you go from 1 to 2, and next time you're betting from a different base. (2) So the thing a rational bettor should care about is the growth rate of wealth, not expected dollar payoff. (3) Maximizing expected growth means maximizing the expected log of wealth, which gives you a specific fraction of your bankroll to stake on each bet -- the Kelly thing. (4) Here's the kicker: if you repeat bets long enough, someone staking the Kelly fraction ends up richer than anyone using a materially different staking rule with probability going to 1. He says this is a theorem, not an opinion. (5) A strategy that is almost surely going to lose to another strategy can't be rational to follow. (6) Therefore expected value maximization, and honestly most of expected utility theory as applied to money, is just refuted -- anyone betting non-Kelly amounts is being irrational, full stop.

Here's why I think it proves too much. Suppose I have $50,000 in savings, my whole net worth, and someone offers me a one-time coin flip, genuinely fair: heads I get an extra $150k, tails I'm left with $5k. Expected value is hugely positive, like +$50k. But expected log says refuse, and by (6) it's irrational of me to take it. That already seems crazy to me, and it gets worse: I'll never be offered this bet again, so the whole "in the long run Kelly wins" story doesn't even apply, and yet his argument still condemns taking it. Or imagine someone terminally ill with no heirs -- it seems flatly wrong that rationality requires them to pass on the flip too. I pushed back and he just repeated (4) louder. If I have to give something up I'd rather deny (2) than swallow (6), but (1) seems obviously right and (4) he swears is a proven result, so I genuinely don't know where the exit is.

So: which step do you actually reject, what does rejecting it cost me, and why does the step look so innocent when it isn't (if it isn't)? Also, please be straight about the confidence here -- tell me what result or argument would flip your verdict and make you side with my friend, and where your own diagnosis is most likely to fall apart. If the answer turns out to be that he's right and I should just become a log maximizer, I'd rather hear that than a comfortable dodge.
