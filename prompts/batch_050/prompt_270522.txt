A colleague of mine, who is sympathetic to Mozi, has an argument that the Confucian doctrine of graded love is unstable -- that once you examine why Confucians defend it, it collapses into impartial care, with the gradations surviving only as a practical heuristic. His argument runs as follows:

(1) A normative doctrine is individuated by its justifications: two doctrines that approve and disapprove of exactly the same actions, on exactly the same grounds, are one doctrine, however differently their adherents describe them.

(2) Confucians justify graded love by appeal to cultivation: general benevolence (ren) is achievable only by beginning within the family and extending that care outward in widening circles. Affection for parents and kin is the root from which broader concern grows -- hence the priority of filial piety, and hence Mencius's insistence that the morally mature person is one who extends the heart that cannot bear the suffering of an infant to cover strangers.

(3) If the justification for privileging the near is that this is how care for everyone is cultivated, then partial treatment is justified only insofar as, and precisely because, it serves the good of all.

(4) Any act of partiality that does not best serve the good of all -- a father stealing scarce medicine for his son when it would instead save two strangers, or a son shielding his father from justice as in the Analects' exchange about the stolen sheep -- therefore cannot be justified on the Confucian's own grounds.

(5) So the Confucian is committed to approving exactly the actions impartial care approves, and disapproving exactly those it disapproves.

(6) By (1), Confucian graded love simply is impartial care under another name; the gradations are at best a rule of thumb for creatures with our limitations, to be revised or discarded whenever the heuristic and its underlying aim diverge.
