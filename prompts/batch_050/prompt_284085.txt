I'm putting together the seminar handout for my final-year course on rational choice, and I want a dialogue I can use to open the session on bounded rationality. I've watched colleagues argue about this for years and it always decends into talking past each other, so I want to see it done properly. Here's the setup.

Two philosophers, both sharp, no strawmen:

Dr Ashworth takes the line that satisficing is just expected utility maximisation in a trench coat. Once you count the costs of deliberation itself as part of the decision problem, "good enough" choices fall out of maximising automatically — so there's no rival theory here, only a relabelling of the standard one.

Dr Marsh is a Simon-style satisficer who thinks this reduction is hollow. Her best argument, as I see it: maximising with deliberation costs requires solving a meta-problem (how long should I spend deciding?), that meta-problem has its own costs, and so on up — at some level the agent simply has to stop, i.e. satisfice, so satisficing is prior to maximisation rather than a special case of it.

Write the dialogue, roughly 600–900 words. Give them a concrete case to argue over — say, renting a flat in London with two weeks before you have to move, where you can't view everything and must decide when to stop looking. Things I'd like them to actually grapple with:

- whether the regress point lands, and how Ashworth should reply (does the regress terminate at a level that's cheap to optimise? does it need to terminate at all?)
- the representation worry: for any coherent stopping rule there's some utility function it can be made to maximise, so does "maximiser" explain anything here or has it become unfalsifiable
- aspiration levels: how they're set, and whether the fact that the same flat feels different depending on what you've already viewed undermines Marsh's view or is a feature of it
- whether there's any environment where Marsh's heuristics beat Ashworth's optimising even setting deliberation costs aside — the kind of case where less information genuinely helps

Make them respond to each other's actual arguments rather than give alternating speeches, and make sure each of them concedes at least one point where the other lands a blow. By the end they should have located at least one genuine crux — something such that, if it were settled, one of them would have to move. Don't have them reach agreement; a clear-eyed statement of where the disagreement lives is a much better ending than a hug.

British English throughout please — analyse, maximise, behaviour, and so on.
