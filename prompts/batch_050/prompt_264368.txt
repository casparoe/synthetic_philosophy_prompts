Three months ago I wound down a company I had spent four years building. My co-founder and I ran it cautiously -- small commitments, long deliberations -- and for most of those years the market rewarded exactly that. Then the market turned fast, a bolder competitor moved in weeks where we moved in quarters, and we were finished before we understood what had happened. A friend pressed Chapter 25 of Machiavelli's Prince on me afterward, in the old W. K. Marriott translation, and one passage has been nagging at me:

"But a man is not often found sufficiently circumspect to know how to accommodate himself to the change, both because he cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to, and also because, having always prospered by acting in one way, he cannot be persuaded that it is well to leave it; and, therefore, the cautious man, when it is time to turn adventurous, does not know how to do it, hence he is ruined; but had he changed his conduct with the times fortune would not have changed."

Sitting with that passage, I assembled an argument that I now suspect proves too much, and I would like a diagnosis:

(1) A person's success or failure depends on whether their manner of proceeding accords with the spirit of the times.
(2) A person's manner of proceeding is fixed: by nature, and by the reinforcing habit of having prospered through it.
(3) The spirit of the times is not within anyone's control.
(4) Therefore whether a person succeeds or fails is settled entirely by factors outside their control -- fixed temperament on one side, uncontrollable circumstances on the other.
(5) No one deserves praise or blame for an outcome that was settled entirely by factors outside their control.
(6) Therefore no one deserves praise for success or blame for failure.
(7) But Machiavelli himself plainly praises Julius II's impetuosity and treats ruin as something a prince could have avoided with proper virtù -- indeed, the same chapter says men can build dikes and barriers against fortune in calm weather.
(8) So either Machiavelli's text is internally contradictory, or one of (1)-(5) is false.

Conclusion (6) strikes me as the argument proving too much: it would dissolve not just Machiavelli's own evaluations but the entire practice of holding founders, generals, and statesmen responsible for anything. And yet I cannot cleanly locate the error. Steps (2) and (4) in particular feel shakier the longer I look at them, but each is grounded in something the passage actually says. If I have to choose, I would rather surrender (5), the control principle, than accept that the text contradicts itself -- but I want to know what that costs, and whether a cheaper repair exists.

Please tell me which step you reject and why it looked plausible, what rejecting it commits me to, and how the dikes-and-barriers material earlier in the same chapter bears on the argument. In doing so, separate what is settled among careful readers of The Prince from what is genuinely contested in the scholarship on fortune and virtù, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus one.
