In the years after the 2008 financial crisis, public argument over its explanation settled into two broad camps. One blamed individuals: reckless traders, greedy bankers, negligent analysts at the ratings agencies who had failed to do their jobs. The other blamed structures: bonus schemes, incentive chains running from mortgage origination to securitisation, and regulatory arrangements that would have produced a collapse no matter who sat at the trading desks. In the first chapter of Economy and Society (1922), Max Weber remarks that in discussing social phenomena we habitually speak of "social collectivities, such as states, associations, business corporations, foundations, as if they were individual persons", but stipulates that in sociological work "these collectivities must be treated as solely the resultants and modes of organization of the particular acts of individual persons, since these alone can be treated as agents in a course of subjectively understandable action".

Read Weber's precept against this episode. Which camp, if either, does it vindicate: does the greed narrative actually satisfy his demand for subjectively understandable action, or does attributing the crash to a durable vice fall short of explaining any particular act? Conversely, can the structural camp comply with the precept — is talk of incentive structures and institutional pressure merely shorthand for the acts and motives of individuals, or does it quietly treat collectivities as agents in their own right, contrary to Weber's stipulation? Notice that the post-crisis debate habitually ran explanation and blame together, as though locating the cause at the level of individuals settled culpability and locating it at the level of structure dissolved it; say whether Weber's methodological precept licenses that inference, and where it is simply silent on questions of responsibility. Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: if both camps can plausibly present their favoured explanation as meeting Weber's demand, does the episode show that the precept underdetermines social-scientific practice, or does it instead bring out what a genuinely Weberian account of the crisis would have to include that neither camp offered?
