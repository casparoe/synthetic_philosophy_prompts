I run the editorial standards desk at a mid-sized company that publishes a lot of help-center articles and product docs, and about a year ago we started letting writers draft with LLMs. Now I'm drowning in arguments about how to categorize the things that go wrong. My team currently has one bucket, "factual error," and it's causing fights because the fixes differ wildly: sometimes the model states something flatly false about a competitor's pricing, sometimes it invents a plausible-sounding feature we don't have, sometimes it produces a sentence that's technically true but assembled with zero regard for whether it's the relevant truth, and sometimes it confidently describes a real phenomenon but gets the mechanism garbled in a way no human expert would. Half my reviewers want to treat all of this as "the AI lied to us," the other half insist a machine can't lie and it's all just bugs, and the argument is eating our review meetings.

I've been reading around and found Frankfurt's old distinction between lying and bullshit — roughly, the liar cares about the truth and deliberately conceals it, while the bullshitter just doesn't care whether what they say is true or false, only whether it lands. That seems like it might carve our cases better than the lying/error binary, but when I try to actually sort a specific bad paragraph into one of those boxes I get stuck, because the categories were designed for humans with intentions and our system doesn't have any (or does it in some thin sense? I genuinely don't know). So what I want is a usable triage procedure my reviewers can run in a few minutes per flagged passage: ordered questions to ask about the output and the process that produced it, what classification each branch lands on (lie, bullshit, honest error, or whatever finer categories you think the taxonomy needs for machine output), and — this is the part I actually care about — what each classification implies for how we respond, since "retrain the model," "fix the prompt," "the writer should have checked," and "this is fine, ship it" are all live options and the label is doing the work of choosing between them.

Please be honest about where the procedure breaks down — e.g., cases where you can't tell from the text alone whether anyone in the loop was indifferent to truth, or where the intentional-language categories just don't transfer and I'm smuggling in a metaphor. Run the procedure on these three real-ish cases from our queue: (1) the model stated, citing no source, that our main competitor's product lacks end-to-end encryption, which turned out to be false; (2) it wrote a smooth, accurate-sounding paragraph about how our sync feature works that describes the architecture we deprecated two years ago, because that's what's all over the public internet; (3) it produced a paragraph that is entirely true sentence-by-sentence but answers a question nobody asked, and the writer kept it becuase it made the article look thorough. I'd also like to know which of the three gives an answer I'll find annoying.
