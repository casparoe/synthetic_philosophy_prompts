A colleague in my decision theory reading group presented the following argument and concluded that it shows expected utility theory proves too much. I can't accept the conclusion but can't locate the error. The argument:

(1) A rational agent facing a decision chooses the act with the highest expected utility, calculated on the information she currently has.

(2) Before deciding, an agent may often make an observation relevant to the decision. A theorem due to I. J. Good shows that for any such observation offered without cost, the agent's expected utility of choosing after updating on the result is at least as great as her expected utility of choosing without it -- and strictly greater whenever some possible result would change what she chooses.

(3) So whenever information bearing on a pending decision is offered for free, it is irrational to decline it.

(4) Whether information is free is a fact about its price, not about the agent, and the theorem nowhere mentions the agent's psychology. So (3) applies equally to agents who would be distressed, biased, or consumed by what they learn.

(5) Therefore it is irrational for: a patient to decline a free test for an untreatable genetic condition that would not alter any decision she faces; a journal referee to decline to learn the authors' identities before reviewing a submission, though she suspects the knowledge would skew her judgment; a chess player mid-tournament to decline a free look at a database of her opponent's opening preparation, though she knows from experience that she plays worse when she overprepares; and a member of a jury to decline to view gratuitously graphic photographs that would not change her verdict.

(6) But declining the information in at least some of these cases is not irrational.

(7) So either (1) is false, or Good's result does not establish what (2) claims, or expected utility theory simply has consequences we must learn to live with.

My colleague takes (7) to be a reductio of (1). I would like to keep (1), and I have no reason to doubt the mathematics in (2). I am also fairly attached to the verdict in (6), at least for the referee and the genetic test. Which step should I actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
