I sit on the eleven-member grants committee of a small philanthropic fund. We decide every application by straight majority vote, after hashing things out in a shared discussion thread. One member has proposed that technical grants instead go to a three-person expert panel; another is fighting the change by invoking the Condorcet jury theorem — with independent voters who are each right more often than not, a majority of eleven is nearly infallible, and shrinking the electorate throws that away. I've read the usual blogs long enough to have seen this theorem deployed casually, and I want our decision to be better than that. Draft the memo I'll circulate, recommending one of: keep majority voting, adopt the panel, or some hybrid. The memo must engage the theorem on its actual terms rather than name-drop it: our deliberation happens publicly before the vote and we all draw on the same handful of forums and prior fund writeups, so the independence premise is suspect; a couple of us have track records that are arguably worse than chance on the technical proposals; and grant quality may not be the binary right-or-wrong question the theorem assumes. Give a clear bottom line, and steelman the losing side rather than strawmanning it. If you think the Condorcet framing is simply the wrong tool here and both my colleagues are confused, the memo should say so plainly instead of politely playing along.
