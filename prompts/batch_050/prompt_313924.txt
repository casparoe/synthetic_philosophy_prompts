Two philosophers in my epistemology reading group keep having the same fight about luminosity and the KK principle. One thinks that, at least for some conditions like being in pain or seeming to see red, if you are in the condition then you are normally in a position to know that you are; she also wants to defend a carefully restricted KK principle, roughly that clear knowledge puts one in a position to know that one knows. The other thinks the margin-for-error argument shows that no interesting condition can be perfectly luminous: if knowledge requires a safety buffer, then a series of tiny changes should eventually trap the KK defender into saying someone knows at every step even after the condition has plainly disappeared. They have agreed to stop giving speeches and produce an adversarial-collaboration document they can both sign.

Draft that document. Start with numbered agreed claims about knowledge, safety, gradual change, self-knowledge, and what the KK principle does and does not say; make these substantive rather than bland peacekeeping. Then give a disagreements table in which each side’s position is stated in terms that side would accept, without sneering phrases like “magical introspection” or “skeptical overreaction.” Identify the real cruxes: for example, whether the relevant safety margin is fixed or context-sensitive, whether the argument needs exactly one reliable comparison capacity, whether “in a position to know” can iterate even when actual knowledge cannot, and whether the anti-KK argument proves too much by threatening ordinary knowledge under slow change. For each crux, describe a concrete test case both sides would accept as relevant and record what each predicts. You may use cases involving imperceptibly cooling coffee, a light dimming at dusk, a pain that fades by tiny increments, or a person checking their own confidence over many rounds, but vary at least one of them in a nonstandard way so the answer cannot simply rerun the usual example.

End with a section separating genuine disagreements from merely verbal ones, especially disagreements about what “luminous,” “clear case,” and “in a position to know” mean. Also include a short list of concessions each side might realistically make without abandoning its core view. The final text should read like something two careful opponents could actually publish jointly, not like a debate transcript and not like a referee declaring one of them the winner.
