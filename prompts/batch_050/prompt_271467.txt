I've been rereading Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot for a seminar on translation I'm co-teaching with someone from Comp Lit, and a fight keeps breaking out that I'd like settled before our next meeting, because every time it comes up we loose half the session to it. The book, if you haven't picked it up, revolves around one tiny occasional poem by Clément Marot: a get-well note to a sick young woman, written (as I remember it) in these absurdly short lines of three syllables each, rhymed couplets all the way down, nearly one long winding sentence, with Marot sticking himself into the poem by name. Hofstadter translates it dozens of times and his obsession is that the FORM has to survive: if your version has four-syllable lines or drops the rhymes, you've written a different poem, whatever "content" you carried over.

Here's the fight. My co-teacher reaches for the same analogy every time: a translation, she says, is like a cover version of a song. Johnny Cash doing Nine Inch Nails changes the arrangement, the instrumentation, the whole feel, and it's still a cover -- arguably a great one *because* it changes things. So a translation that keeps Marot's meaning but drops the three-syllable lines is just a respectful cover, and Hofstadter is a purist fetishist elevating his own taste into principle. The analogy isn't crazy and it's doing real work in her argument, but I have an itch that it proves too much.

So audit it for me. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the melody, the lyrics, the instrumentation, the tempo, the key signature. Then where it breaks. My own suspicion is that the fatal disanalogy is separability: in a song the melody is an independent layer that persists through the cover and can be recognized by anyone who knows the original, whereas a poem's meaning is carried by the very words being replaced, so there's no separable layer for the original to survive *in* -- and most readers of the translation can't even read the original, so there's nobody in the audience who hears it humming underneath the way you hear the original under a cover. If that's right, the analogy quietly smuggles in exactly the form/content split Hofstadter denies, and the conclusion was loaded in from the start. But tell me if I'm wrong, which of the other disanalogies actually matter for her conclusion and which are just pedantry, and whether the "legitimate cover, so drop the constraint-mania" verdict survives the audit or was being carried by the analogy the whole time. If the analogy is no good I'd also take a better one -- her backup is that translation is like adapting a novel for film, and I suspect that one's even worse, but you're welcome to weigh in on that too.
