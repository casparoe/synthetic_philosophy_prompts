So I'm a novelist and somehow got talked into sitting on the ethics commitee of my national writers' association, and we have our first genuinely hard case. A member published a historical novel about a famous 19th-century trial, marketed everywhere as meticulously researched, and a journalist just discovered that two things in it don't exist: a dramatic courtroom speech the book presents as drawn from the trial record, and an epigraph attributed to a real philosopher of the era that appears nowhere in anything he wrote. The author's defense, roughly: she asked an AI chatbot for help reconstructing period detail, it gave her both items with total confidence, and she "didn't check because they felt right." She also says -- and this is where it gets thorny -- that novelists invent things, that's the whole job, and that she never intended to deceive anyone so at worst it's an error, not misconduct.

Our code of conduct prohibits "fabrication presented as fact in works held out as fact-based," which nobody drafted with chatbots in mind. Half the committee wants censure plus a mandatory correction notice in future printings; the other half says you can't censure someone for being careless when fiction writers are licensed liars by trade.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before we vote. It should land on a concrete recommendation with actual consequences (or an explicit finding of no violation), and it needs to do three things honestly. First, sort out what the author did using the lying / bullshit / honest-error distinction in the Frankfurt sense -- indifference to truth versus intent to deceive versus straightforward mistake -- and figure out whether the chatbot changes who the indifference belongs to, since the machine presumably can't be indifferent to anything. Second, tell me whether the epigraph and the speech are the same kind of offense or different ones, because I suspect they're not and I can't articulate why. Third, take seriously the strongest version of the losing side: if we censure, what do we say to the member who points out that every historical novel in our catalogue contains invented dialogue dressed up as real? I'd rather lose the vote on a memo that's honest about that than win on one that pretends the line is obvious.
