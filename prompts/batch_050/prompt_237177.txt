So I TA'd a seminar on philosophy of economics and put together this argument from Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and it seems to prove way too much, but I can't find the crack in it. Numbered so it's easy to fight about:

(1) Any rational individual's preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, so they're representable by expected utility.
(2) Society's preferences over social lotteries — and basically every policy choice is a social lottery, since outcomes are uncertain — should meet the same standard of rationality. So social preferences are also representable as expected utility.
(3) Weak Pareto: if every individual strictly prefers social lottery A to social lottery B, society should prefer A to B.
(4) Harsanyi showed that (1)-(3) together imply social utility is a weighted sum of the individual utilities.
(5) So the distribution of well-being across people is morally irrelevant in itself — only the weighted total matters.
(6) So a society with obscene inequality is better than a modestly equal one whenever its weighted total is even slightly higher.
(7) So if there's a utility monster who converts resources into utility at a fantastic rate, we should hand them everything, and the rest of us should get bare subsistence.
(8) So a tiny pleasure enjoyed by enough people morally outweighs one person's agony, as long as the sum works out.
(9) At least one of (6)-(8) is absurd. So at least one of (1)-(3) is false.

Now, everyone in the seminar immediately piled on Pareto, but Pareto is the premise I'd most like to keep — giving it up feels like giving up on welfare economics entirely, since if society can prefer what literally everyone dispreferes I'm not sure what we're even doing. So I'd rather the bullet get bitten somewhere else if it can be.

Here's what I want from you: don't just lay out the menu of responses and leave me to choose. Pick the single step you'd reject — whether that's one of (1)-(3), or something in the move from (4) to (5), or one of the leaps after that — and defend the choice. Say why the step looked innocent when it isn't (or why it's genuinely fine and the real cost lands elsewhere), and be honest about what rejecting it costs me: what I can no longer say, what theorems I lose, what I have to give up in other arguments I probably like. If you think the argument is actually valid and sound and the "absurd" conclusions should just be accepted, that's a valid answer too, but then tell me which of (6)-(8) I'm supposed to swallow first and why that one.
