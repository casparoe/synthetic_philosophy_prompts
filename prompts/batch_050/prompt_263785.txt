In a jurisprudence reading group I help run, a colleague has begun using M. C. Escher's 1948 lithograph Drawing Hands — the image of two hands, each sketching the other into existence — as a model of how legal authority is grounded. Her claim is that the familiar regress of legal validity (each norm is valid because authorized by a higher norm, and so on down) dissolves once you treat a legal system as what Hofstadter, in Gödel, Escher, Bach, calls a tangled hierarchy: the constitution authorizes the officials, and the settled practice of the officials constitutes the constitution, with each level bringing the other into being. On her view, this means the system needs no foundational norm — no Kelsen-style basic norm, and no Hart-style rule of recognition resting ultimately on brute social practice — because the system is self-founding, and that self-foundation is precisely why it requires no external justification. I suspect the picture is doing her argumentative work for her, and I would like the analogy audited. Please give the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of each hand, of the lines being drawn, of the paper, and of Escher himself. Then list the disanalogies, and separate the ones that matter for her conclusion from the ones that merely reflect the fact that no analogy is perfect. In particular, press on Hofstadter's own observation that every depicted tangle presupposes an inviolate level standing outside the tangle — the level that does the actual drawing. On the most natural mapping, that inviolate level in a legal system is the ongoing practice of the officials, which looks less like self-foundation and more like exactly the Hartian picture she is trying to escape. Does the self-foundation conclusion survive once the inviolate level is on the table, or was the analogy carrying the argument all along? If you think the analogy fails her, say what picture, if any, would better capture the genuine mutual dependence between constitutions and official practice. And whatever verdict you reach, tell me what argument or observation about how legal systems actually work would change your mind, and identify where your own audit is most likely to have gone wrong.
