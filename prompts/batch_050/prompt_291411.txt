You are a philosopher of language who also has professional-level fluency in programming-language theory: type systems, design by contract, refinement types, runtime verification, certified compilation, and capability security. Think carefully, and resist the temptation to produce a tidy mapping. The point of this exercise is precisely to find out where the mapping breaks.

Some background on me: I am a programming-languages researcher. I am completely at home with preconditions and postconditions, type soundness, runtime assertion checking, proof-carrying code, and capability-based design. My grasp of philosophy of language is that of a serious hobbyist. I have been reading about the debate over the norm of assertion — the question of what condition a speaker must satisfy for an assertion to be warranted or proper — and I keep involuntarily reaching for my own vocabulary. I want you to carry out the translation systematically and honestly. By "honestly" I mean: do not smuggle in assumptions native to the computational framework and present them as translations, and flag any point where the target vocabulary itself imports a substantive philosophical commitment.

The candidate norms I have in mind are the four standard ones: the truth account (assert p only if p is true), the belief account (assert p only if you believe p), the justified-belief account (assert p only if you are justified in believing p), and the knowledge account (assert p only if you know p, the view most associated with Timothy Williamson).

Task 1 — Systematic translation. Model assertion as an exported procedure assert(p) and each candidate norm as a specification discipline: a precondition on the state of the world, a precondition on the caller's internal state, a requirement that the caller hold a certificate, and so on. Restate each of the four norms in this vocabulary. For each, report (a) what translates cleanly, (b) what must be distorted to fit, and (c) what has no expression at all.

Task 2 — Test cases. Run the translated norms against the following pressure points:

- Gettier cases: the caller holds a certificate that validates, but through a compromised or merely lucky trust chain — the right output reached via a broken dependency. Can any contract formalism distinguish knowledge from justified true belief here, or does the knowledge account resist translation at exactly this point?
- Moore-paradoxical assertions of the form "p, but I do not believe p": are these type errors, contract violations, or something the framework can only describe as a call no caller is ever in a position to make correctly?
- Lottery assertions, where the caller holds a probabilistic certificate of arbitrarily high strength: how do the translated justification and knowledge accounts differ in their treatment of statistical evidence?
- Justified but false assertions, and the distinction between violating a norm and being blameworthy for violating it: is there a contract-theoretic analogue of the difference between a violation the caller could not have detected and one it could have — something like the difference between an unavoidable runtime exception and negligence?
- Testimony: assertion seems to hand the hearer a license to re-assert. Does capability-passing capture this warrant-transfer structure, and which norm corresponds to the principle that you may only pass on a capability you actually hold?

Task 3 — Residue report. Now list what does not survive translation, and adjudicate each item. Three candidates I particularly want your judgment on: (i) the claim that the norm is constitutive of assertion rather than merely regulative — is there an analogue of the difference between a type system, which defines what counts as a program at all, and a linter, which merely flags bad style, and does that analogue do the same philosophical work? (ii) the first-personal normative force of the "only if" — a contract is a specification imposed by a designer from outside, whereas the norm of assertion is supposed to govern speakers from within the practice; (iii) the fact that a norm-violating assertion is still an assertion, whereas an ill-typed program arguably is not a program at all. For each residue item, state whether it marks a genuine limitation of the contract vocabulary or instead reveals that the original philosophical claim was doing less work than it appeared to.

Task 4 — Verdict. Which candidate norm survives translation most faithfully, and does the translation exercise itself supply a reason — one not obviously available from within the philosophy-of-language debate alone — to prefer one norm over the others?
