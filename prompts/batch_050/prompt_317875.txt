So in my gov/econ class we did this unit on the prisoner's dilemma and the evolution of cooperation, and we played an iterated tournament in class where I did terribly because I kept cooperating with people who obviously weren't going to. Anyway it's got me thinking about what I actually believe about when it's ok to "defect" in real life, so here's my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases. Slacking on a group project because the two tryhards will carry it anyway: not ok, that's free riding. Taking a slightly bigger share of the pizza when we split it evenly: fine, whatever. Cheating on a test where the whole class cheats and the curve means I'd tank my grade by being honest: still not ok, even though it feels unfair. Stopping putting in effort on the group project after my partner blew me off twice: totally fine, they defected first. Refusing to tell the teacher which friend cheated even when she asks me directly: fine, actually -- even though I just said cheating is wrong, which I realize might not make sense. Ratting out a classmate I don't like who cheated, when I wouldn't have done it for a friend: not ok, that's just using the rules as a weapon. And the one I keep flip-flopping on: doping in a sport where half the field is doping.

My tentative principle was something like "never defect first, but you can defect back after someone defects on you," but the pizza thing breaks that (no one defected first, it's still fine), and the cheating cases break it the other way because cheating first is not fine but not-reporting also feels fine even though it's kind of a second-order defection on the honest kids. Can you give me a principle (or a couple candidates) stated precisely that captures as many of these as possible, says which of my verdicts it has to give up, and tells me what to think about the doping case and about stuff I didn't ask about, like jaywalking when no cars are coming or using AI to write my english essay?
