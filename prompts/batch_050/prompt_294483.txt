I'm a novelist, and I've gotten attached to a reading of Adam Smith's impartial spectator that I use alot when thinking about character and moral judgment. My view is that the impartial spectator is less like an idealized judge looking down from nowhere and more like an internal editorial faculty built through repeated acts of imagining how one's conduct would appear to particular other people. On this account, its authority comes from surviving many socially grounded points of view, including hostile or disadvantaged ones, rather than from detachment as such. I also think good fiction can train this faculty because it makes us move between perspectives while noticing how each one distorts the others.

What would count against that position? Please distinguish textual or conceptual evidence that Smith's spectator really must be impersonal from evidence that merely complicates my stronger claim about fiction. I'd also like to know what my view predicts that a more standard ideal-observer reading does not, and what would show that it collapses into a simple conformity-to-public-opinion theory.

As a concrete test case, imagine a character who seems generous from his own point of view, vain from his servant's, threatening from a political enemy's, and pitiful from his sister's. If imagining all four perspectives makes his eventual judgment less impartial rather than more, would that refute my account, or only force a qualification? Sort the possible defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about whether the position has enough moving parts to dodge anything.
