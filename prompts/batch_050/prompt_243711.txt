Last night I got into a long and rather heated argument with a friend about Thomas Szasz. He had just read Szasz's 1960 essay "The Myth of Mental Illness" (American Psychologist) and read me the following passage from it:

"This makes it apparent that the statement that 'X is a mental symptom' involves rendering a judgment. The judgment entails, moreover, a covert comparison or matching of the patient's ideas, concepts, or beliefs with those of the observer and the society in which they live. The notion of mental symptom is therefore inextricably tied to the social (including ethical) context in which it is made in much the same way as the notion of bodily symptom is tied to an anatomical and genetic context."

My friend's gloss on Szasz, delivered with great confidence, ran roughly as follows. Szasz proved that mental illness simply does not exist, the way witches do not exist. Since diagnosing a mental symptom is just rendering a judgment -- comparing the patient's beliefs to the observer's and society's -- psychiatric diagnosis is nothing more than the psychiatrist's personal disapproval dressed up in medical language, and is therefore arbitrary and subjective. People labeled schizophrenic or depressed are not suffering from anything real; they are social nonconformists whose behavior or beliefs inconvenience others. And since there is no medical content to psychiatry at all, the entire profession is an instrument of social control, and everything done in its name, from commitment to medication, is unjustified.

I came away thinking this was a caricature, but I could not put my finger on exactly where it departs from the text, and the passage he quoted genuinely does seem to support at least some of what he said. So I would like an accuracy audit I can actually use the next time we argue. Please go through my friend's claims one by one and tell me: which of them Szasz would accept as stated; which distort him, and in what specific way -- for instance, does Szasz deny the reality of the suffering and strange behavior that gets labeled mental illness, or does he deny something narrower about how it should be classified and explained; whether the passage about judgment and covert comparison supports the conclusion that diagnosis is arbitrary, or whether Szasz is making a different point about what kind of judgment it is; and whether Szasz's target is psychiatry as such or only certain of its forms and practices. Finally, if the parts of my friend's summary that survive correction do so, do his sweeping practical conclusions follow from them, or does he need additional premises Szasz does not supply? Where the passage quoted above is doing real work in the argument, please say so explicitly rather than discussing Szasz in generalities.
