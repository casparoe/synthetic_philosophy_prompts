So I need a referee, and since my writers' group failed me I'm hoping that's you. I write novels, and the group just tore into my latest draft — not the prose, the premise. The book follows three generations of a family, and the whole thing hinges on one tiny event in chapter one: in 1954 the grandfather misses a train to Leeds because his bus gets stuck behind a broken-down coal lorry, and because of that he never meets the woman he was going to marry, marries someone else instead, and the next two hundred pages are the consequences. Everything downstream of one bad ten minutes.

At the group last week two of the others got into a real argument about whether the premise even works, and I kept quiet because I honestly coudn't tell who was winning. Roughly how it went:

1. Claire said the premise is basically a cheat. Real history doesn't pivot on missed trains — big outcomes have big causes. If tiny accidents really determined everything, the world would be unrecognisably different every other Tuesday and there'd be no patterns for historians to find, but there obviously are patterns. And if my grandfather hadn't met the woman on that train, he'd have met someone much like her at a dance the following month.

2. Marcus said that's confusing averages with events. Weather is chaotic and climate is still predictable, so the existence of big patterns says nothing about whether individual turning points turn on chance. And some very big things plainly did turn on small chances — storms wrecking fleets that would otherwise have invaded, rulers dying of a fever at exactly the wrong moment. Her view would make biography impossible, and biography is obviously possible.

3. Claire said the fleets and fevers get remembered precisely because they're rare — for every missed train that changed something there are ten thousand that got absorbed without trace, and telling stories about the hinges while ignoring all the absorptions is just survivorship bias. And anyway, even if real history occasionally pivots, fiction doesn't get to: reality doesn't have to earn belief, a novel does, and a plot that runs on coincidence is a craft failure whatever the metaphysics says.

4. Marcus said the "absorbed" point gives the game away — it concedes that systems absorb most shocks but not all, and a novelist's entire job is to write the case that isn't absorbed. And her last move quietly swapped the question: "is this realistic" and "is this satisfying" are different questions, and she can't win the first one by arguing the second.

Then it devolved into wine and I never got a verdict. Can you go through the four moves and tell me who's right at each step, what each of them is missing, and who's more right overall? And if any of it implies an answer, whether I should keep the missed train or find a sturdier hinge.
