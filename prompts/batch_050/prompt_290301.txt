I want to spar on a question in the philosophy of cognitive science, and I need a committed opponent rather than a survey. First, some shared ground in case the details aren't at your fingertips: in the Copycat research program, a program solves analogy problems in a microdomain of letter strings — abc goes to abd; what does iijjkk go to? — and its central mechanism is slippage: under the pressure of a particular problem, a description like "rightmost letter" can melt into "rightmost group," or "successor" into "predecessor," and the melted concept then guides the answer it settles on. The program's architects hold that this slippage is not window dressing but the thing itself: conceptual fluidity in miniature, the same phenomenon as a human who, mid-conversation, lets "the host" slide into "the host's husband" without ever noticing the substitution.

Play a committed defender of that claim — call the view microdomain realism: the thesis that genuine conceptual fluidity is already present in a well-built microdomain program, and that the difference between it and human conceptual flexibility is one of degree, not of kind. Here is my opening move. Every slippage your program can perform was laid down in advance by its designer: the nodes that can melt and the channels they can melt along form a fixed, hand-built menu, and the program's only freedom is which item on the menu gets selected under pressure. A child can invent a slippage nobody anticipated. Show her the string problem and she might say that abc goes to abd the way a joke goes to its punchline, and nobody put that concept in her — she built the bridge on the spot, from material that had nothing to do with letter strings. Fluid that can only flow through pre-laid pipes isn't fluidity; it's plumbing. So whatever is genuinely impressive about the program, why should we call what it does conceptual fluidity rather than a fixed lookup table with a probabilistic index?
