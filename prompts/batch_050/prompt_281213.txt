I'm part of an informal reading group that has been working through Victorian science, and we recently spent several sessions on Henry Sidgwick and the early Society for Psychical Research, which he helped found and led in the 1880s. What struck us is not whether telepathy is real but a strange pattern in how the group's investigators handled testimony, and I keep seeing the same pattern everywhere since. It goes like this. A claim is extraordinary, and the evidence for it consists of many separate reports -- a woman who dreamed of her brother's death the night he died, a medium who produced information she seemingly could not have known, a whole household that heard the same knocking. Taken one at a time, every report can be explained away: memory is reconstructive, coincidence is commoner than intuition says, some witnesses turn out to be coached or to have copied their story from an earlier published account. The skeptical investigators in Sidgwick's circle said, roughly, that a hundred weak reports are still weak -- you cannot pile up mounds of sand and call it a mountain. The defenders replied that the reports constrain one another: when many witnesses, unknown to each other and scattered across decades, converge on the same narrow kind of experience, the chance that all of them are lying or misremembering starts to look smaller than the chance that something is going on. Here is the wrinkle I have no word for. Sometimes a new report genuinely adds to the pile, and sometimes it silently subtracts from it -- for instance, when it comes out that a witness had read a popular account of similar cases before having her experience, that single disclosure retroactively infects reports you had already counted, because it shows that a mundane channel of influence existed that you had been assuming away. So the evidential weight of the collection is not a running total at all; each new item can re-rate everything before it, upward or downward, depending on what it reveals about how the reports were generated.

My question: does this phenomenon -- call it the way testimony in a dossier mutually re-evaluates itself, so that the whole is neither the sum nor the average of its parts -- have an established name, in the philosophy of testimony, in confirmation theory, in legal scholarship on corroboration, or anywhere else? I am aware this sits near several famous neighborhoods: Hume's treatment of testimony for miracles, the general question of when independent witnesses multiply rather than merely add, and the old jurisprudential problem of cumulative versus corroborative evidence. But none of those, as I understand them, is quite about the retroactive re-rating I am describing, where a single late disclosure about how earlier reports came to exist changes the value of reports already on the books, without any of those earlier reports being individually discredited. If there is a recognized concept for exactly this, please name it, explain how it is standardly analyzed, and say how it differs from the neighboring notions I have gestured at, since I suspect a good answer will need to say which of my ingredients it keeps and which it discards. If there is no established concept, say so plainly, and coin a term for it: give me a definition sharp enough that I can tell, for a contested dossier of reports -- psychic research, eyewitnesses to a historical event, adverse-effect reports for a drug -- whether the phenomenon is present, plus some boundary cases where the phenomenon shades into mere accumulation of weak evidence or into ordinary undermining of testimony. Concrete illustrations are welcome. Please write your answer in American English.
