In my philosophy of technology seminar, whenever Langdon Winner's claim that artifacts can have politics comes up, someone in class always reaches for the same comeback: technology is just a tool, like a hammer. A hammer can build a house or bash someone's head in, and we don't say the hammer has politics -- the politics is all in the user. Last week someone used it to dismiss the whole unit, including the reading about the low overpasses on Long Island parkways that buses couldn't fit under, and honestly the hammer line does seem to work against that example, since that's just a guy designing something badly on purpose.

But I'm not fully convinced the analogy carries what my classmate wants it to. Can you audit it? Give me the actual mapping first -- what plays the role of the hammer, the hand, the target -- then where it breaks. Some things I'm chewing on that you should address: a hammer sits there inert until someone picks it up, but stuff like a content-ranking algorithm or a tomato harvester designed for one kind of farm runs on its own and rearranges who has what without any single "user" deciding anything; and some technologies seem to demand certain social arrangements around them (you can't really run a nuclear plant with an anarchist commune, maybe?). Also, does the analogy sneak in the assumption that there's always a identifiable person whose intentions explain what the artifact does in the world?

Then tell me whether my classmate's conclusion -- artifacts are neutral, full stop, so blaming technology is a category error -- survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether some weaker version of it survives, and whether there's a better analogy for what technology is than the hammer.
