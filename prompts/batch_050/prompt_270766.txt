Hey, so I'm in a seminar on Scanlon's contractualism this term and last week two people in my discussion section got into a genuine argument that the TA just let run out the clock on, and it's been bugging me. Can you adjudicate it? I'll lay out the moves as best I remember them.

The case we were given: a city has to pick between two options. (A) Build a water treatment plant next to a single farmhouse, which will cause the one family living there a serious chronic respiratory condition for decades. (B) Instead, run a cheaper process that gives the tap water of two million residents a faint but permanent unpleasant taste -- each person just grumbles and mostly gets used to it, nobody's health is affected. Those are the only two options, the city has to pick one.

Move 1 (Maya): Contractualism obviously picks B. The family's complaint is severe, each resident's complaint is trivial, and Scanlon's whole thing is that complaints get compared one at a time, not summed. Two million trivial complaints stacked together still lose to one severe one. That's the point of the theory -- it protects individuals from being sacrificed to aggregate welfare.

Move 2 (Dev): That's not a selling point, that's a reductio. If numbers literally never count, then one person's broken leg outweighs a billion people's stubbed toes, and nobody could actually govern themselves by principles selected that way. His positive argument: think about which principle people could agree to in advance, not knowing which position they'd end up in. Everyone would accept a tiny chance of being the farmhouse family in exchange for certainty of avoiding the trivial harm, so a principle permitting aggregation of trivial burdens isn't one anyone could reasonably reject.

Move 3 (Maya): That "in advance" move just smuggles in expected-value reasoning, which is utilitarianism in a wig. The individualist restriction is constitutive of contractualism -- drop it and you've dropped the theory, not refined it. And Scanlon himself already handles numbers where they legitimately matter: as tie-breakers between complaints of equal seriousness. Equal complaints, count heads; unequal complaints, the worse one wins. Clean.

Move 4 (Dev): Clean until you ask where the tier boundary is. Harm is a continuum. Is one year of the respiratory condition "in a different tier" from eleven months? From ten? Any cutoff between trivial and non-trivial is arbitrary, so the tie-breaker rule is secretly doing nothing unless Maya can draw a principled line, and she hasn't. Also, he said, imagine the distributed harm is a bit worse -- each of the two million loses one night's sleep a year, forever. Still individually small, but now insisting it can't aggregate starts to feel like worshipping a rule rather than tracking what matters.

Move 5 (Maya): Moral theories are allowed to be revisionist. Maybe the right answer just is that vast numbers of trivial harms never justify seriously harming one person, and our squirming at that is a bias in favor of the many, not an argument. Dev's continuum point cuts both ways: vague boundaries don't show there are no boundaries -- baldness is a continuum too, but some people are bald.

Move 6 (Dev): The baldness reply only works if there's a fact of the matter the vague concept tracks. His worry is that Maya can't say *why* the cutoff sits where she puts it without either going arbitrary or sneaking aggregation back in ("harms count once there's enough total suffering" -- which was the thing she called utilitarianism in a wig).

Then class ended. Who's right at each step? Where does each of them overreach, what (if anything) are they both missing, and who's more right overall?
