You are a philosopher with joint expertise in decision theory, game theory, and the philosophy of law, known for careful, rigorous analysis. Think carefully about the following question before answering; begin by brainstorming the full range of considerations, then organize your answer into a structured argument. Write in American English.

Consider two widespread and related practices of criminal justice systems:

First, when several people are prosecuted for a joint crime, prosecutors routinely offer the first defendant who agrees to testify against the others a dramatically reduced charge, or full immunity, while those convicted on that testimony receive the standard sentence. Second, competition authorities in many countries run formal leniency programs for cartels: the first cartel member to report the price-fixing arrangement to the authorities escapes fines entirely, members who come forward later receive at best partial reductions, and those who stay silent face the maximum penalties. Both schemes are publicly announced, so every participant knows the structure in advance.

These schemes are, quite deliberately, engineered prisoner's dilemmas: they are designed to transform a situation in which joint silence is best for the group of offenders into one in which each individual reasons, "whatever the others do, I am better off talking first." My question is what these practices presuppose.

1. What theory of individual rationality must offenders actually follow for the schemes to work? In particular, the schemes seem to presuppose that conspirators reason independently rather than thinking, "we are all in symmetric situations, so we will all reach the same conclusion -- therefore the real choice is between everyone talking and everyone staying silent." Would leniency programs simply fail if offenders reasoned in that symmetric way, and does the practice thereby take a stand between causal and evidential decision theory, or between ordinary dominance reasoning and so-called superrationality?

2. Cartels are ongoing, repeated arrangements, sustained (one assumes) by some mixture of trust, monitoring, and the threat of future retaliation. What does the design of leniency programs presuppose about how cooperation in an indefinitely repeated dilemma is maintained, and about which of those supports the policy can actually break?

3. Elsewhere the legal system treats heavily incentivized statements with suspicion -- testimony from paid or sentence-bargaining informants is often regarded as unreliable. Is there a coherent epistemology of testimony under which both practices make sense at once, or is the system presupposing inconsistent things about when incentives corrupt evidence?

Reconstruct the implicit theory behind these practices. Distinguish carefully between what they strictly presuppose and what they merely fit well with, and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined -- where several rival theories would license it equally. Finally, say what would have to change in the design of these schemes if the presupposed picture of rationality turned out to be wrong: for instance, if real conspirators did reason symmetrically, or if punishment were required to track desert rather than order of confession.
