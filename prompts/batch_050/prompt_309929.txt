I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a column about movies that sneak real philosophy of mind past audiences who just came for the thriller. I rewatched Ex Machina last week and I want to do it through the debate about the Turing test and behavioral criteria for mind more broadly -- but not the lazy version, where the film is "about whether Ava is really conscious." Here's the angle I want to try out. Turing's original imitation game wasn't simply human-vs-machine: in the setup as he first described it, a man tries to pass as a woman for an interrogator, and the machine then takes over that role. The gendered, performative structure of the test is baked in from the start, and Ex Machina seems to literalize it -- Nathan has built Ava to be maximally legible-as-a-person to a specific young man, and the "test" only works because Caleb is attracted to her and wants to believe. So here's my question for the column: is the film an argument *for* behavioral criteria (Nathan's line of thinking -- if it walks and talks and flirts like a mind, stop asking for more) or an argument *against* them (the test measures skill at producing the behaviors that move *us*, which selects for manipulation, not mindedness)? And does the film even care about consciousness, or is it quietly making a different point -- that any real-world test is contaminated by the examiner's motives, so the controlled-experiment fantasy of the imitation game was always naive?

A few things I'd specifically like pressed on. Caleb arguably becomes the real test subject partway through -- Ava is testing whether she can get him to free her -- so does the film suggest the test is symmetric, that the machine is evaluating the human as much as vice versa, and does that break the logic of the original setup? The ending gives us zero access to Ava's inner life; she walks out and we learn nothing about what, if anything, it was like to be her. Is that the film refusing the question, conceding the other-minds problem, or slyly endorsing the behaviorist reading by showing that the inner-life question is exactly the one we can never settle? Also, please be honest about where the film *resists* my reading -- if the gender-imitation angle is doing less work than I think, say so, and if there's a scene that cuts against it, name it. And end with what the film contributes to the philosophical debate that the journal articles don't -- something about embodiment, seduction, or the impossibility of a neutral examiner, if anything.

Act as a philosopher of mind who also happens to be a sharp film critic -- you've read your Turing and your Block and the debates about whether passing a behavioral test is evidence of mind, definitional of it, or neither, and you can talk about a movie without embalming it. Think carefully. Before you commit to a reading, brainstorm two or three competing interpretations of what the film is doing with the test (including at least one where the answer is "nothing coherent"), then pick the strongest and defend it. Casual tone is fine -- this is a magazine column, not a seminar -- but don't dumb down the philosophy, and definately don't reduce the film to a classroom illustration of it.
