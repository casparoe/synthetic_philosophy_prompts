So I'm a retired organic chemist, and over the last year or so I've fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole, and the thing that got me hooked was the Libet experiments and the whole "neuroscience shows free will is an illusion" business.

Here's the situation. A local library series asked me to moderate a public Q&A with a young neuroscientist who's written a popular book arguing exactly that -- that brain signals precede conscious decisions, so conscious will is just a story the brain tells itself after the fact. He's polished and charming and has obviously done this talk a hundred times, so his standard moves are well rehearsed. I want a sequence of questions for the evening that actually pins him down instead of letting him do the TED-talk version. A few things I'd like the questions to work in, roughly escalating:

- Start with the timing data itself. As I understand it the readiness potential starts well before the reported urge to move, but Libet himself still thought we had a "free won't" -- a last-moment veto. I want a question that forces him to say whether his view covers the veto too, and if so, on what evidence, since as far as I can tell the veto case was much shakier experimentally.
- Then the interpretation problem that bugs me as an experimentalist: the readiness potential only shows up when you average over many trials, and there's this modelling work suggesting it could just be random background noise crossing a threshold rather than a "decision" forming. I want a question that makes him commit to why we should read the ramp as the brain deciding early rather than as noise plus a threshold.
- Then the ecological-validity point: these tasks involve pressing a button at an arbitrary moment for no reason, which is about as far from an actual decision (changing careers, divorcing, whistleblowing) as you can get. He'll deflect with something about "the principle is the same" -- I'd like a follow-up ready for that.
- And finally a question about what an experiment would have to look like to actually test the kind of decision-making people mean when they talk about free will, rather than wrist-flicking.

Sequence it so each answer constrains what he can say next, and give me the follow-ups for his likely replies, not just the openers. Casual is fine, I'll rewrite them in my own voice anyway.
