so i just got off three weeks of jury duty and honestly it changed my brain a little. twelve random people, none of us lawyers, most of us would rather have been anywhere else, and yet by the end we were taking it dead seriously, we asked to rehear testimony, we argued about what "reasonable" means, and we got it right i think. anyway in the courthouse cafeteria i got talking to another juror who turned out to be really into sortition -- replacing elected legislatures with bodies chosen by lottery -- and her whole pitch rested on one analogy she kept repeating: a citizens' assembly or a lottocratic legislature is just a jury scaled up. the jury works, nobody wants to abolish juries, so the same mechanism can run a country, and anyone who accepts the first but not the second is being inconsistent.

since then i've been reading around (ireland used a citizens' assembly before its abortion referendum, there was the french climate convention, etc.) and i keep seeing the jury analogy doing exactly this work -- it's the foot in the door for the whole sortition program. but the more i sit with it the more i suspect it's smuggling in a lot.

so, act as a political theorist who specializes in democratic theory and institutional design, and be careful and slow about this. before answering, brainstorm the features that make a trial jury work, then map the analogy out explicitly -- a correspondence table is fine: what plays the role of the defendant, the judge, the rules of evidence, the voir dire, the verdict, the fact that it's one case and then you go home. then tell me where the mapping breaks. some disanalogies i can already see: a jury decides one bounded question framed for it by someone else, a legislature picks its own questions; jurors can't be lobbied or bribed mid-trial because they're sequestered and anonymous, a lottocratic chamber sitting for years presumably can; jury verdicts are unanimous-ish and one-off, legislation is ongoing and revisable. but i want you to go beyond my list, and more importantly i want you to sort the disanalogies into the ones that actually threaten the inference (jury works → sortition works) versus the ones that are true but don't matter for it.

the thing i really want to know: does my cafeteria friend's inconsistency charge survive once the disanalogies are on the table? i.e. if i think the jury was legitimate, am i thereby committed to thinking a randomly selected legislature would be, or is the jury legitimate for reasons (bounded scope, adversarial lawyers doing the informing, judges policing the process, the parties' consent, peremptory challenges) that a lottocracy can't replicate? and if the analogy can't carry the weight, what's the honest version of the sortition argument -- is there a better analogy (jury *pool* selection rather than the jury itself? the Athenian boule? polling samples?) or does the case have to be made on its own merits without borrowing the jury's good reputation?
