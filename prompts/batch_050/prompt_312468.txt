I've never studied ethics, but I've been keeping a list of gut verdicts about everyday social rules, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, unifies them -- or which of my verdicts I should give up. Here they are. Not tipping at a full-service restaurant in the United States, when the service was fine: wrong, and wrong in a way that merely being rude isn't, because the staff's wages are structured around the custom. Tipping at a restaurant in a country where tipping is not expected and can cause offense: wrong, or at least you shouldn't, even though no one is harmed and the gesture is generous. Pushing ahead in a queue when nobody is enforcing it: wrong, even if you're in a hurry and everyone behind you loses only a minute. Jaywalking on an empty street where it's technically illegal: not wrong at all. Jaywalking in a city where it's illegal and locals visibly disapprove, as a tourist who could just wait: you should wait, even though it's objectively just as safe as the empty street. Wearing shoes into someone's home in a culture where that's unremarkable, when you come from a shoes-off culture: fine, no obligation to import your own custom. Ignoring a local dress expectation at a religious site you're visiting: wrong, even if you find the expectation itself old-fashioned. And the pair I can't reconcile: I think a visitor must generally follow the local convention even at some personal cost, but I also think a visitor has no obligation to follow a local convention that is itself exploitative -- say, a norm of giving small bribes to officials, or a strong expectation that foreign guests pick up every check -- and I can't say what makes those cases different from the dress code one, since in both the visitor finds the norm objectionable and the locals rely on it.

Is there a principle that delivers all of these -- something about when a convention's being in force creates a genuine obligation rather than a mere expectation, and for whom? Please state it precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it can't save and which it would have to strain to save, and say what it predicts about two cases I haven't settled: haggling in a market where haggling is the local norm, when you'd rather just pay the asking price, and standing on the wrong side of an escalator in a city whose convention you know but don't care about.
