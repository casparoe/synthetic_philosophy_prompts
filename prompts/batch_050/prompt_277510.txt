A friend of mine has recently become an enthusiastic convert to modern Stoicism, and he now deploys the dichotomy of control -- the slogan that some things are up to us and other things are not, and that peace of mind consists in concerning ourselves only with the former -- as an all-purpose argument-stopper. Most recently he used it to claim that it is simply irrational to be distressed about things like mass layoffs at his company, the outcome of an election, or whether his book manuscript gets accepted: since none of these are within his control, the Stoic maxim allegedly shows that anyone who cares about them is making a conceptual error, not just a prudential mistake. I suspect the slogan is doing far more work for him than any defensible version of it could, but I want to be precise about this rather than just accuse him of overreach.

Please disambiguate the slogan into the distinct precise theses it might be expressing, and evaluate each. I have in mind candidates along the following lines, but please correct or expand the taxonomy: (1) a metaphysical thesis about the scope of our causal powers (on which almost nothing is fully "up to us," since even my typing this sentence depends on my health, my equipment, and the absence of interruptions); (2) a thesis about what we should desire versus merely prefer, treating external outcomes as "preferred indifferents"; (3) a psychological observation that attention and emotional investment track perceived control, so that investing in uncontrollables is a recipe for frustration; (4) a decision-theoretic heuristic that effort should be allocated where the marginal return on effort is highest; (5) an ethical thesis that the only genuine good is the state of one's own character or faculty of judgment, so that external outcomes cannot truly harm us; and (6) a practical discipline of rehearsing the distinction, whatever its strict truth.

For each reading, I would like to know: Is it trivial, defensible, contentious, or false? What would have to be true for it to be defensible -- e.g., does the reading need a compatibilist or libertarian account of agency, and does the slogan smuggle in determinism when it suits and reject it when it doesn't? And, most importantly, does the reading actually license my friend's inference from "you cannot change X" to "you are wrong to care about X"? My suspicion is that even the strongest versions establish at most something about where effort should be directed or where expectations should be set, and that the jump from control to *concern* requires the much stronger ethical thesis (5), which he has never argued for and probably does not actually believe, since he would not accept that being wrongfully imprisoned could not truly harm him. Tell me whether that suspicion is correct, and which reading of the slogan he is most likely relying on at each step of his argument.
