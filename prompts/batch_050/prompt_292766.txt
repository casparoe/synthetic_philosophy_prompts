Before our institute's working group on artificial moral patients begins evaluating theories of machine consciousness, I want the standard of evaluation fixed in advance, so that we judge each contender against agreed requirements instead of letting every theory advertise its own favorite case. Write the list of desiderata that any adequate theory of which physically implemented computations carry moral standing must satisfy, with the case or consideration behind each, and with each item marked as non-negotiable for any theory whatsoever or as the commitment of a particular camp. The theory's job, for our purposes, is to say whether a given physical system's ongoing activity includes morally considerable experience, and ideally to what degree — well enough to ground decisions about which systems we may freely build, copy, pause, and delete.

Hold two background difficulties fixed, since any candidate must survive them. First, mapping triviality: for almost any sufficiently complex physical object — the molecular motions in a wall, say — and almost any simple computational description, some interpretation mapping the object's states onto the computation's states can be constructed, so a criterion phrased as 'there exists a mapping onto the right abstract machine' appears to count everything as thinking, and the theory must avoid this without collapsing into the claim that only brains count. Second, the recorded mind: a system could in principle pass any behavioral test by consulting an enormous precomputed table covering every conversational branch, reproducing the outputs of a conversant with nothing like the relevant inner goings-on, and the theory must either exclude such systems or explicitly bite that bullet and defend the taste.

Beyond these, work out what the theory must say about cases the literature tends to treat as afterthoughts: whether adding machinery that would have produced correct outputs had the inputs differed, but never activates on the actual run, restores moral status; whether it matters if the same program runs at a thousandth of normal speed, with long pauses, or distributed across continents; and whether running the identical computation twice yields one subject or two — noting that a type-identity answer (the computation is the subject, so duplicates are one) sits badly with our conviction that two biologically identical brains in identical states are plainly two subjects, while a token-identity answer lets moral weight be manufactured by copying. For each desideratum give the motivating case and your verdict on its negotiability.

Then, the part I most want: identify any subset of your desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied, and exhibit the conflict precisely. Finally, in no more than a short paragraph each, indicate how the main families of criteria — causal and counterfactual accounts of what it takes to implement a computation, global-workspace-style functional criteria, intrinsic-causal-structure criteria in the style of integrated information, and the biological-naturalist denial that computation is the right level of description at all — fare against the list. Keep the weight on the requirements; the scoring is secondary. No bibliography: I want the argument itself.
