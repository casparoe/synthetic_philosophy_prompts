You've probably heard "let justice be done though the heavens fall" (fiat justitia ruat caelum). I ran into it last week in an argument about the murderer at the door: I said obviously you lie, my friend said no, a constraint against lying doesn't dissolve just because the outcome is bad, and he dropped the slogan like that settled the matter.

Here's what bugs me. If justice matters so much that the heavens falling can't outweigh it, then surely more justice is better than less -- so why not tell one lie to prevent three other people from lying (or worse)? That's roughly what people call the paradox of deontology, and the slogan seems to just walk straight past it.

Act as a professional philosopher who is careful about distinguishing theses from each other, and think this through slowly. Lay out the different precise claims the slogan could be making -- that constraints are absolute side-constraints, that they get lexical priority over outcomes, that it's just an expression of commitment rather than a thesis at all, that consequences genuinely never matter, that the "heavens falling" case is somehow incoherent or impossible, plus any others you think are live -- and for each one say whether it's plausible or trivial or false, and crucially whether it actually answers the paradox or merely restates the constraint in a louder voice. Then tell me which reading my friend most likely meant, and whether that reading actually wins him the argument about the murderer at the door or only changes the subject.

If you think I've misframed something -- if the slogan isn't really about side-constraints, or the paradox is a bad way to put the pressure on him -- say so directly rather than politely playing along.
