I produce a university philosophy podcast on logic, and next month's guest is a logician whose recent book argues that the semantic paradoxes have been misdiagnosed for a century. Her position, in outline, is that the real culprit is not negation, not the truth predicate, and not self-reference as such, but the structural rule of contraction: the background assumption that a premise used twice in an argument may be collapsed into a single use. On her view, Curry's paradox rather than the Liar is the true test case for any theory of paradox, because a Curry sentence (one that says, in effect, that if it is true then some absurdity follows) generates triviality using nothing but a conditional, and so escapes every solution built around the behavior of negation.

Draft nine interview questions for the episode. The questions should go well beyond inviting her to restate the view. Specifically, I want at least one question on each of the following pressure points, and for every question a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give:

1. The priority claim: why take Curry as more fundamental than the Liar, given that truth-value gaps and dialetheism at least offer a coherent-looking treatment of the Liar, whereas Curry seems to make both options collapse — and a follow-up anticipating her reply that any solution validated only on negation-involving paradoxes has not solved paradox but only a special case of it.

2. The revenge worry: once her non-contractive logic is written into the theory itself, what stops us from constructing a new Curry sentence that quantifies over her consequence relation — and a follow-up for her expected response that all known revenge constructions smuggle contraction back in somewhere, pressing her to say where, exactly, in the standard revenge argument it enters.

3. The cost to ordinary and mathematical reasoning: proofs by contradiction, arguments by cases, and Cantor-style diagonalization all appear to reuse assumptions freely, so what does mathematics look like without unrestricted contraction — and a follow-up pressing on whether her line between safe and unsafe contractions is principled or merely adjusted to avoid disaster case by case.

4. Yablo's paradox: an infinite list of sentences, each saying of every later sentence in the list that it is untrue, appears paradoxical without any sentence referring to itself and without any clear point at which a premise is used twice — so does her contraction-based diagnosis even apply, and a follow-up on what it would mean for her program if the paradoxes turn out to have no common structure.

5. Falsifiability: what result — a proof, a counterexample, a development in the semantics of conditionals — would actually make her abandon the view, and a follow-up on whether a solution whose main virtue is blocking derivation of absurdity can ever be more than damage control.

Keep the questions courteous but pointed; the guest is senior, quick on her feet, and fond of answering a hard question by asking which rule of inference the questioner is willing to give up, so word each follow-up in a way that forecloses that move rather than inviting it.
