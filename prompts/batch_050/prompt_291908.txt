I work as a software engineer, and at my company we run A/B tests constantly, including on variants we privately suspect are worse for users, because the only way to find out is to ship both and measure. Nobody considers this ethically fraught. Recently I learned that in clinical research, the standard ethical justification for a randomized trial is "equipoise": a state of genuine uncertainty or disagreement in the expert medical community about which arm is superior, such that no participant is knowingly being assigned to inferior care. This struck me as odd, because if we held software to that standard, almost every experiment we run would be forbidden the moment the designer had a hunch, and yet the hunch is usually why we run the experiment.

So here is the proposition I'd like your credence on: equipoise in the expert community is what actually makes a randomized controlled trial ethically permissible, such that a trial conducted without equipoise is, for that reason alone, unethical.

Don't just give me a number. Please decompose your credence into the sub-questions it depends on, with a rough probability and a sentence of justification for each. The sub-questions I can see are: whether the relevant uncertainty has to live in the individual investigator or whether community-level disagreement is enough; whether equipoise as described ever actually obtains, given that trials are typically funded and designed by people who expect one arm to win; whether the real ethical work is done by equipoise at all, or instead by informed consent and reasonable risk-benefit ratios; and whether my A/B testing analogy is genuinely parallel or fails on some disanalogy I haven't noticed (stakes, consent quality, fiduciary duty). Feel free to add sub-questions I've missed, show me how your probabilities combine, and tell me which single sub-question your final number is most sensitive to and what argument would move it.
