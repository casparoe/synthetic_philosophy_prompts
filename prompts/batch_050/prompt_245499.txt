I have recently been put in charge of launching an internal prediction market at my company, and at the kickoff meeting a colleague who has read some economics calmly argued that the project is doomed on theoretical grounds: between rational agents with access to the same evidence, she said, no trade should ever occur, because the mere fact that someone is willing to take the other side of your bet is itself evidence that ought to move you off your position. I suspect she is right about the theorem and wrong about its application, and I want to think it through with a case built to order.

Construct a thought experiment in which two agents with a common prior, identical information, and common knowledge of each other's rationality nonetheless enter a trade that both strictly prefer to no trade. Constraints:

- No asymmetric information at any point. Neither agent knows anything the other does not, and neither can learn anything from the other's willingness to trade, because there is nothing to learn.
- No disagreement about the event the contract concerns. Both assign it the same probability, and both know that they do.
- The trade must be rational for each side in expected-utility terms, not merely excusable; neither agent may be making a mistake about anything.
- The setting should be one a normal professional could actually face (a firm, a farm, a household), the contract simple, and the scenario itself under a paragraph.

After the scenario, do the check. State each agent's situation, the contract, and the expected-utility calculation on both sides, and verify that the standard assumptions behind the no-trade results (common priors, common knowledge of rationality, no gains from trade in the absence of disagreement) are each either satisfied or explicitly identified as not doing the work. Then tell me which feature of the case is load-bearing — I expect it will be that the trade functions as insurance against pre-existing exposure rather than as a bet on a disagreement — and whether the theorem, properly stated, rules this case out or simply never applied to it. Finish by anticipating my colleague's strongest reply: that whatever holds of insurance-style trades, an actual prediction market lives on disagreement, so the case does nothing to rescue the project. Tell me honestly whether she is right about that, and what the case does and does not establish for my launch.
