A colleague of mine has an argument that purports to show there are no such things as group beliefs or group agents, and although the conclusion strikes me as absurd — we constantly hold committees, firms, and courts responsible for what they "decided" — I cannot locate the misstep. The argument runs as follows.

(1) If a collective genuinely believes a proposition, that belief must be fully determined by the beliefs of its members; there is no additional mind floating above the group in which further beliefs could reside.
(2) The only non-arbitrary way to determine collective belief from individual belief is proposition-by-proposition majority rule: the group believes whatever a majority of its members believe. Any alternative — weighting some members more heavily, requiring supermajorities, or letting the outcome on one proposition be settled by votes on others — builds in privileges or biases for which no justification can be given.
(3) Now consider a three-member hiring committee. Member 1 believes the candidate's research is excellent, believes that excellent research suffices for hiring, and so believes the candidate should be hired. Member 2 believes the research is excellent but denies that excellent research suffices, and so believes the candidate should not be hired. Member 3 believes the research is not excellent, but believes (on general principle) that excellent research would suffice, and so believes the candidate should not be hired. Each member is perfectly consistent.
(4) Applying (2): a majority (Members 1 and 2) believes the research is excellent; a majority (Members 1 and 3) believes that excellent research suffices for hiring; yet a majority (Members 2 and 3) believes the candidate should not be hired. So the committee believes the research is excellent, believes that excellent research suffices, and believes the candidate should not be hired.
(5) Anything that accepts two premises of a valid inference while rejecting its conclusion holds contradictory commitments, and nothing with contradictory commitments can be an agent, since agency requires a single coherent standpoint from which to deliberate and act.
(6) Therefore majority rule cannot constitute collective belief. By (2), no alternative can. Therefore collectives have no beliefs and are not agents, and all talk of what a committee concluded or a court held is a useful fiction at best.

My colleague says this shows that corporate responsibility is an illusion and that only individuals ever think anything. That cannot be right, but each premise looks defensible in isolation. I am most reluctant to abandon (5) — consistency seems like the minimal price of admission for agency — and I am happiest to keep (1) as well. Which step should I reject, why did it seem compelling, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? If the answer is that (2) must go, I want to know whether some principled replacement for it survives the committee case, or whether rejecting it means accepting that there is simply no fact of the matter about what the group believes until someone chooses a voting procedure.
