I've been helping a friend put together a breakup playlist (lol) and it sent me down a rabbit hole I can't get out of. Everyone — critics, composers, spotify's recommendation algorithm, my grandma — talks about instrumental music in emotional terms. A critic writes that the second movement of some symphony is "grieving," a film composer deliberately reaches for the sad strings for the funeral scene, and everyone just... knows which songs count as sad, without anyone having to explain the rule. Nobody seems to think this is loose talk, either: critics seriously argue about whether a particular performance got the sadness right, as if there were something to get wrong. But a sound wave is just pressure moving through the air. Sadness, as far as I can tell, is something a person feels, and it involves thoughts and body states and usually a reason. A squeaky door isn't sad, a dial tone isn't proud. So what is going on when a chord progression is?

So my question: what does this whole practice presuppose about what emotion-words mean when they get attached to music? I can think of a few candidates but I can't tell which one the practice actually needs. Maybe sad music is sad because it somehow resembles a sad person — their voice, the slow drooping way they move and carry themselves. Maybe it expresses the composer's own sadness (but then can a genuinely happy composer write a sad piece, or would it just be an imitation of one?). Maybe "sad" just means the music tends to make listeners sad — but then the practice is weird, because we say "that song is so sad" as praise, people deliberately seek out sad music when they're already down, and playing sad music at a funeral is supposed to help, which would be a bizarre thing to do if sad music simply made sad people sadder. Or maybe calling music sad is just a metaphor, as loose as calling the stock market nervous, and the practice presupposes nothing at all. If that's right, though, what are critics even disagreeing about when one says a performance flattened the grief in the slow movement?

Also curious about consistency: is the practice committed to one theory the whole way through, or do different bits of it quietly pull in different directions — the funeral thing seems to sit badly with the makes-you-sad theory but fine with the resemblance theory, for instance. And is some of this just underdetermined, i.e. could two rival theories both license the whole practice so we'd never be able to tell from the outside which one people actually hold? Finally, what would concretely have to change about how we talk about and use music if it turned out the emotion-words really are pure metaphor? Would anything have to change at all?
