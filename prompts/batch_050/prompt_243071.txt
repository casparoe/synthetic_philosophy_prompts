Here is the examination question (25 marks total):

Donald Davidson holds that all understanding of speech is, at bottom, a matter of radical interpretation. (a) Explain what radical interpretation is, and why Davidson thinks that even communication between speakers of the same language is, in principle, an instance of it. (8 marks) (b) Explain the role of the principle of charity in radical interpretation, and discuss whether Davidson regards charity as an optional heuristic that evidence might lead us to abandon. (8 marks) (c) Davidson asks us to imagine an omniscient interpreter. State what this thought experiment is meant to establish, and assess whether it shows that charitable interpretation guarantees the truth of most of a speaker's beliefs. (9 marks)

Here is the student's answer:

Radical interpretation is the project of interpreting a speaker from scratch, without any prior knowledge of what the speaker's words mean or what the speaker believes. The interpreter's evidence is limited to the speaker's dispositions to assent to or dissent from sentences, correlated with observable conditions in the shared environment. The interpreter's goal is to construct a Tarski-style theory of truth for the speaker's language whose theorems pair each sentence with its truth conditions, thereby yielding an interpretation of what the speaker's utterances mean. Davidson thinks all interpretation is radical in principle because even with a compatriot we cannot simply take shared conventions for granted: our knowledge that another speaker of our language means what we do is itself sustained only by the very same method of correlating utterances with circumstances, and conventions are neither necessary nor sufficient for successful communication.

The principle of charity enters because meaning and belief are interdependent: we cannot identify what a speaker believes without knowing what her sentences mean, and we cannot fix the meanings without making assumptions about her beliefs. Charity breaks into this circle by instructing the interpreter to interpret the speaker as largely a believer of truths, that is, truths by the interpreter's own lights. Charity is thus a working assumption that is justified by its practical success; if a speaker were to persistently assent to sentences that are manifestly false, charity would lapse and the interpreter could permissibly begin attributing error wholesale instead.

The omniscient interpreter is an interpreter who knows every fact about the world and about the speaker's physical and behavioral condition. Because such an interpreter knows all the physical facts, he can read off what the speaker believes and then check those beliefs directly against the facts, and Davidson's conclusion is that this procedure conclusively refutes scepticism: any speaker whatsoever must have mostly true beliefs, since the omniscient interpreter's verification would otherwise be impossible. Like Quine, Davidson also holds that because several incompatible truth theories may fit all possible evidence equally well, there is in the end no fact of the matter about what anyone's words mean.
