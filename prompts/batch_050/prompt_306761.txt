I am a novelist who also teaches an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind, and I need examination materials that will sound credible to philosophers who read my campus novel. Please draft one ninety-minute essay question on Mary’s room and the knowledge argument, together with a model answer and a detailed marking scheme.

Build the question around the following variation on the standard case. Mara has lived in an achromatic laboratory and possesses a complete, explicitly represented physical and functional theory of human color vision, including everything the relevant sciences say about her own brain when a subject sees red. Since childhood, a calibrated external device has enabled her to discriminate red objects, match color samples, and predict other people’s color-related behavior as accurately as a normally sighted person, but without producing color imagery or experience. On leaving the laboratory, Mara sees a red rose and says that she now knows what seeing red is like. The only obvious new abilities she acquires are to visualize red and to recognize red experiences from the first-person perspective as experiences of that kind.

The examination question should require students to determine whether Mara’s claim describes the discovery of a nonphysical fact, new propositional knowledge of an already known physical fact, a nonpropositional form of acquaintance, a new ability, or some combination of these. It should also require them to identify which premise of the knowledge argument each response rejects and whether the argument establishes dualism, establishes only an explanatory or conceptual gap, or fails for another reason. The question should not be answerable merely by recounting the original thought experiment or listing the ability, acquaintance, and phenomenal-concept responses.

Provide a model answer of roughly 800 words. It should reconstruct the knowledge argument precisely; distinguish epistemic novelty from ontological novelty; explain why the device does not by itself settle the dispute; examine whether visualization and first-person recognition are best understood as abilities, as applications of new phenomenal concepts, or as forms of acquaintance; and end with a defended verdict. The answer should explicitly acknowledge where a proposed response risks begging the question by defining phenomenal learning in terms favorable to itself.

Finally, provide a thirty-mark marking scheme organized around five must-hit moves. For each move, state the number of marks available, what distinguishes full from partial credit, and one characteristic way weaker candidates mishandle it. Include a separate note explaining what separates an excellent answer from a competent one—for example, distinguishing the failure of an anti-physicalist conclusion from the success of a particular physicalist reply, and resisting the assumption that all learning must be the acquisition of a new proposition. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or a reading list.
