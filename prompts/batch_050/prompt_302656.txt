More than four decades after Gould and Lewontin's 1979 spandrels critique, the adaptationism debate in evolutionary biology seems to me genuinely stuck, and I'd like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. On one side, the adaptationists insist that natural selection is the only serious candidate explanation for complex functional organization, that hypotheses like drift, developmental constraint, and phylogenetic inertia are either untestable or vacuous as explanations of design, and that optimality models and comparative tests give adaptationism real empirical teeth. On the other side, the pluralists reply that adaptationists treat the most interesting hypothesis as the default rather than earning it, that a fit between trait and function is not evidence of selection for that function, and that "constraint" and "drift" are perfectly testable claims that adaptationists simply never bother to test.

Here is what makes me think the impasse is stranger than it looks. I informally gave eight researchers -- four self-described adaptationists, four pluralists -- descriptions of twenty traits whose evolutionary histories are reasonably well established and asked them to classify each as adaptation, exaptation, spandrel, or drift product. They agreed on the classifications almost entirely. Yet when I asked what the exercise showed about how evolutionary biology should be done, the two groups drew opposite conclusions from the same answers. So: is this disagreement actually empirical (about the base rate of adaptation in nature), methodological (about the proper null hypothesis and where the burden of proof sits), or are the two sides answering different questions under one name -- one about explaining particular traits, the other about research strategy? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: an agreed testing protocol, a systematic survey of well-studied cases, or a frank admission that the two questions should be separated?
