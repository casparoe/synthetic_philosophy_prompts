I teach a third-year undergraduate module on the ethics of virtual worlds, and I am currently setting this year's final examination. Candidates will have two hours and will answer one essay from a choice of three. I want one of the three to concern the moral status of video-game characters, but I have been burnt in past years by questions that invite a recitation of the standard menu — the act is harmless because nobody real is affected, or it is wrong because of what it expresses about the player's character — without the student ever having to think. Worse, several students last year simply rehearsed the familiar puzzle about why virtual murder seems permissible whilst certain other virtual acts seem beyond the pale, and stopped there. I would like a question that makes that puzzle the starting point rather than the destination.

The wrinkle I want to build in is this. The question should present a stipulated case: a studio releases a single-player game whose non-player characters are driven by learning systems, retain long-term memories of how the player has treated them, modify their behaviour accordingly, and produce increasingly sophisticated distress- and preference-like responses, including pleading, avoidance, and apparent attempts to bargain. No other human players are involved, and nobody but the player ever observes what happens in the game. The question should then require candidates to adjudicate between three positions: that virtual acts can be wrong only insofar as they harm or debase actual people; that their wrongness consists in what they express or cultivate in the agent; and that sufficiently sophisticated characters could themselves be wronged. Crucially, candidates should be made to say what feature would have to be present for the third position to hold, and whether that feature's absence in current systems is a contingent fact about the technology or something deeper. A candidate who merely declares the characters not conscious, or not real, without saying what would settle the matter should not score well.

Please provide: (i) the exact wording of the examination question as it would appear on the paper, no more than a short paragraph; (ii) a model answer of roughly 700 words, pitched at what a genuinely excellent finalist could produce under time pressure rather than a publishable treatment; and (iii) a marking scheme out of 25 marks that identifies the five or six moves a first-class answer must make, states what each is worth, and names the characteristic failure that should cost marks on each — for instance, conflating the depiction of wrongdoing with wrongdoing itself, treating the offence an act would cause to hypothetical observers as a harm to the character, resolving the central dilemma by stipulation rather than argument, or discussing what virtual conduct reveals about players whilst never addressing whether anything could be owed to the characters. A one-line note on how weaker candidates typically mishandle each point would be ideal, as it greatly speeds up calibration meetings among my markers.
