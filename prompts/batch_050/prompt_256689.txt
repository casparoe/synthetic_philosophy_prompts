I edit school history textbooks for a mid-sized publisher and I keep having the same fight with our authors. Every chapter has sentences of the form "X caused Y" -- the assassination caused the war, the printing press caused the Reformation, the famine caused the revolution -- and our reviewers keep writing back that these are oversimplifications, while the authors argue (fairly) that fourteen-year-olds can't be expected to hold twelve interlocking causal factors in their heads at once. I want to stop refereeing this case by case, so I'm looking for a decision procedure I can hand to both sides: a checklist, in order, that a non-philosopher can run on any proposed causal sentence in about fifteen minutes, telling them when "caused" survives, when it must be downgraded to "contributed to" or "created the conditions for", when several causes should be listed and in what order, and when the sentence should be scrapped entirely. It needs to handle, explicitly, two situations I think are philosophically different but our drafts constantly blur: one where the outcome had several contributing factors that were each individually insufficient (remove any one and maybe it doesn't happen), versus one where two independent processes were each on their own sufficient and the outcome was, so to speak, guaranteed twice over. I have a suspicion the second case should be written differently from the first, but I can't articulate why, and if the procedure could explain the difference in plain terms that would already justify it.

Please ground the checklist in whatever serious work on causation and causal explanation in history actually says -- I'm told there's relevant machinery about causes as parts of sufficient conditions, about why we single out one factor among many as "the" cause, and about contrastive explanation (why did X happen rather than Y) -- but translate it so my authors don't need to know any of that vocabulary. Be honest about which steps are genuine tests and which are judgment calls wearing a lab coat, and mark the points where the checklist has to hand the decision back to an editor because it's really about what our readers need, not about the history. Then run the procedure on three real sentences from our current drafts, including at least one where it spits out a verdict I won't enjoy: first, "Gavrilo Princip's assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand caused the First World War"; second, "the Gutenberg printing press caused the Protestant Reformation"; third, "poor harvests and high bread prices caused the French Revolution of 1789". If one of those comes out needing to be rewritten in a way that makes the chapter longer and duller, say so plainly rather than fudging the procedure to flatter me -- that's the test of whether it works. Also say what the procedure can't do, and give me one invented hard case of your own that it can't settle. Please write the whole thing in British English.
