So I co-host a tiny econ-adjacent podcast — the kind of show where the whole audience has opinions about Scott Alexander posts and half of them have Manifold accounts — and we've landed a guest I'm genuinely excited about: an economist who's spent years defending futarchy, i.e. governance by conditional prediction markets, the vote-values-bet-beliefs program. She's smart and she's heard the standard objections a hundred times, which means if I ask her "but won't markets be manipulated?" I'll get a rehearsed two-minute answer and we'll have burned the slot.

Draft me eight to ten interview questions, in order, that an actually-informed interviewer would ask. Requirements:

- At least one question on the self-reference problem: conditional markets only give you useful prices if traders believe there's a real chance the policy gets adopted, so the mechanism has to actually fire sometimes even when the market says no. How does she keep that from being either toothless or a coin flip that randomly overrides good judgment?
- At least one on the metric problem: futarchy outsources values to a measurable welfare metric (the GDP+ style move), which is obvious Goodhart bait. I want her pinned down on who picks the metric, and what stops the metric itself from becoming the thing everyone — traders, agencies, politicians — optimizes into nonsense.
- At least one on the causation-vs-correlation gap: a conditional market price gives you something like P(welfare | policy adopted), but traders price in everything correlated with adoption, not just the policy's effect. Force her to say what work the market is doing that a panel of superforecasters or domain experts with actual skin in the game wouldn't.
- For each question, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — the polished one — so I'm not scrambling when she recites it.

Two more asks. First, commit: don't hand me a balanced buffet of every objection in the literature and leave me to pick. Decide which two or three lines of attack are genuinely load-bearing for the view and structure the arc around those, even if that means skipping objections some people consider central. I'd rather the questions take a position on where futarchy is actually vulnerable than be neutral survey questions. Second, no generic warmup ("so how did you get into mechanism design?") — straight into content, the audience will forgive it.

Last line of your response should be a single sentence naming the one question you'd actually expect to make her pause.
