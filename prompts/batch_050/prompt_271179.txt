So my friend Ana and I keep having these arguments about low-probability, high-stakes decisions, and I've started keeping a list of her verdicts because I can't figure out the underlying logic. Here's her track record: she says playing the lottery is a waste of money even though someone wins eventually; she pays for the fire insurance on her apartment without hesitation even though the odds of a fire are tiny and the premiums add up; she donates to disaster-preparedness charities that mostly fund asteroid-deflection and pandemic-surveillance stuff, which she defends by saying the stakes justify it even if the probabilities are miniscule; but last week a guy on the street told her he had remote control of a satellite and would crash it into the city unless she gave him $200, and she laughed in his face, and when I asked how that's different from the asteroid charity she just said "come on." She also refuses to buy a lottery ticket but does buy raffle tickets at her kid's school fundraiser, which she claims is different because she can see the drum of tickets.

Play the role of a decision theorist who's good at reverse-engineering people's implicit policies from their case judgments. Before you answer anything, brainstorm at least three different rules that are each consistent with ALL of Ana's verdicts above (e.g., a probability threshold, a source-credibility discount, a cap on how big stakes can get before they stop mattering, something about whether the probability estimate is verifiable, etc. — but come up with your own too). Then: two new cases. Case A — a respected seismologist publishes a peer-reviewed estimate that a particular fault has a 1-in-2-million chance of rupturing catastrophically next year, and asks the public to fund a very expensive mitigation project. Case B — a stranger emails Ana claiming to be an advanced AI that will, if she sends $50, prevent a simulated catastrophe affecting trillions of simulated people, with arguments she can't follow but that seem sophisticated. For each case, tell me what each of your candidate rules predicts, which rule best fits Ana's whole pattern (and why the closest earlier verdict is the right analog), your final prediction for how she'd rule, and what ONE additional case I could put to her that would most efficiently distinguish the two rules you're least sure between. Think carefully and be honest if her verdicts are just inconsistent rather than rule-governed.
