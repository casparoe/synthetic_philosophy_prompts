I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I keep running into the same problem in the literature on AI and intentionality: people argue directly about whether large language models "really" have beliefs, without first agreeing on what would settle the question. I would like the prior thing instead. Taking Dennett's intentional stance as the point of departure (though without assuming his conclusions), what are the desiderata that any theory of warranted belief-attribution to AI systems would have to meet in order to count as adequate?

Please give me the requirements list: the cases a theory must handle (the thermostat, the chess engine, the dog, the human adult, and the LLM that reports what it "thinks" — presumably not all in the same way); the distinctions it may not collapse (the predictive usefulness of adopting the stance versus the reality of the attributed states; merely carrying a representation versus having a belief with world-directed content; a pattern in the behaviour versus a pattern the system's own internal organisation exploits); and the empirical results it cannot contradict, whether from developmental psychology, comparative cognition, or mechanistic interpretability work on transformers. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and state whether it is a genuine adequacy condition or a negotiable preference of one camp. Then tell me whether the list is jointly satisfiable — if two desiderata conflict, that itself seems like a result worth knowing — and which single requirement does the most work in excluding currently popular positions.

One request about calibration: please distinguish carefully between what is actually settled among people who work on this (for instance, that stance-talk is often predictively fruitful) and what remains genuinely contested (for instance, whether a Dennettian "real pattern" view licenses belief-attributions to LLMs, or whether it in fact raises the bar for them). I would rather have the disagreement mapped accurately than have a contested reading presented as the consensus.
