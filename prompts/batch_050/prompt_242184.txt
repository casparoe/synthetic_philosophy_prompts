In my moral philosophy seminar this semester we've been working through the literature on aggregation and rescue, including Taurek's argument that the numbers shouldn't count and his suggestion that a rescuer who can save one person or five, but not both, might fairly flip a coin. Over the course of the term I've kept a running list of my classmate Mara's intuitive verdicts on cases we discussed, and I now suspect she's running a rule she couldn't state herself, since she answers quickly and then waves off requests for justification. Her verdicts so far: (i) in Taurek's own case -- David alone on one island, five people on another, equal cost and urgency to the rescuer -- she says the rescuer must save the five, and that flipping a coin would be wrong; (ii) where the choice is between preventing one stranger from losing a leg and preventing five strangers from each losing a finger, she says the rescuer must prevent the leg loss; (iii) where her own daughter and five strangers each face a broken arm, she says she may treat her daughter first; (iv) where one stranger will die and fifty strangers will each suffer a broken wrist, she says the rescuer must save the one; and (v) where two strangers face paralysis and one stranger faces paralysis, she says the rescuer must save the two.

Here is the new case I want to put to her at our study group. A relief coordinator with a fixed stock of supplies can either fly one team to a village where a single stranger will otherwise die, or fly the same team to a different village where one hundred strangers will otherwise each permanently lose the use of one hand -- all strangers to the coordinator, no special relationships on either side, comparable cost and probability of success, and no way to split the team or the supplies. I'd like a prediction of Mara's verdict, but more than that I'd like the reasoning laid out: what single rule best fits all five of her earlier verdicts, which other rules fit them just as well (I assume at least two survive, since the daughter verdict might reflect a special-obligations rider rather than anything about aggregation), and what each of those candidate rules predicts about the new case.

If her track record genuinely underdetermines the answer, please say so plainly, and tell me what principle she would be committing herself to with each possible verdict in the new case -- in particular, what she gives up about case (iv) if she says to save the hundred, and what she gives up about cases (i) and (v) if she says to save the one. Finally, since I get one follow-up hypothetical before she catches on to what I'm doing, tell me which further case would most efficiently discriminate among the surviving candidate rules, and why that case does the work.
