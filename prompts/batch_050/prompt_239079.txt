Take the Semmelweis affair -- the 1840s Vienna maternity wards, where Ignaz Semmelweis noticed that the ward run by doctors and medical students had way higher childbed fever deaths than the one run by midwives, traced it to the doctors coming straight from autopsies, instituted handwashing in chlorinated lime, and cut mortality dramatically. The medical establishment largely rejected him, he got increasingly bitter and erratic, and he died in an asylum before germ theory vindicated him.

Analyze this as a case study in peer disagreement, from the standpoint of both conciliationism and steadfastness. Do it carefully though, because the case is slippier than the pop-science version suggests:

First, get the peer structure right. Who exactly are the peers here, and of whom? Semmelweis had data; his critics had the accumulated theory of medicine of their day. Is this even a case of peer disagreement in the strict sense, or does it break the symmetry the disagreement literature assumes -- one party has a table of mortality figures, the other has something more diffuse? Can the conciliationist framework even be stated cleanly when the "evidence" on each side is this different in kind?

Second, run the analysis in the epistemic present, not in hindsight. Semmelweis's data was real but messy: his own theoretical explanation was narrow (cadaveric particles specifically, which couldn't explain all cases), he famously delayed publishing, he was by all accounts terrible at persuading people, and there was no mechanism on offer. So: what does conciliationism tell the Viennese doctors to do in, say, 1849 -- and is the honest conciliationist answer maybe "split the difference toward a practice that costs nothing anyway," i.e., just wash your hands, which complicates the idea that conciliation is about belief rather than action? And what does steadfastness tell Semmelweis to do?

Third, the hard question: does his eventual vindication do any work in evaluating what the parties should have believed at the time? Conciliationism looks terrible in retrospect here -- it would have had the one guy who was right downgrade his confidence toward the consensus that was killing people. Is that a genuine counterexample to conciliationism, or is judging it by the outcome just the historian's version of results-oriented thinking? Can the conciliationist say the view was never meant to maximize the number of times the right person stays right?

Finally, tell me whether the case actually discriminates between the two views at all, or whether -- once you grant how ambiguous the mid-century evidential situation was -- both views can tell a perfectly comfortable story, in which case the episode might show something about the disagreement literature itself rather than about either position.
