In the Antithesis of the First Antinomy in the Critique of Pure Reason, Kant argues:

"For let it be granted, that it has a beginning. A beginning is an existence which is preceded by a time in which the thing does not exist. On the above supposition, it follows that there must have been time in which the world did not exist, that is, a void time. But in a void time the origination of a thing is impossible; because no part of any such time contains a distinctive condition of being, in preference to that of non-being (whether the supposed thing originate of itself, or by means of some other cause). Consequently, many series of things may have a beginning in the world, but the world itself cannot have a beginning, and is, therefore, in relation to past time, infinite."

Analyze the twentieth-century controversy between steady-state and Big Bang cosmology — from Lemaître's primeval-atom hypothesis and the steady-state model of Hoyle, Bondi, and Gold through to the discovery of the cosmic microwave background in 1965 — as a test of Kant's First Antinomy. Kant's diagnosis was that reason, asked whether the world has a beginning, generates two equally cogent proofs and thereby convicts itself of overstepping possible experience; the quoted antithesis proof turns on the premise that a beginning must be preceded by an empty time in which nothing could determine the world to exist rather than not exist. The historical episode appears to challenge Kant twice over: relativistic cosmology rejects that premise outright, since on the standard picture time itself begins with the universe rather than containing its beginning; and the dispute was settled by observation rather than by reason's self-examination, which Kant had claimed was impossible for such questions. Work through the mapping in both directions. Does the Big Bang model vindicate Kant's thesis, his antithesis, or neither — does a beginning of time itself escape the dilemma of the quoted passage, or merely relocate it? Does the empirical resolution of the controversy show that Kant was wrong about the impotence of experience here, or was the question the cosmologists settled a different question from the one Kant's proofs addressed? And what does the episode suggest about the fate of a priori impossibility arguments — Kant's included — when they collide with a mature empirical science?
