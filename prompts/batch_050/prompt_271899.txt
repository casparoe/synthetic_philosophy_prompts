Two of my colleagues, both philosophers of physics, have been arguing for two years about what quantum gravity shows about the reality of spacetime, and I've finally persuaded them to try an adversarial collaboration. I need you to draft the document they should produce.

One defends the radical reading. In gauge/gravity duality, a gravitating spacetime is fully described by a non-gravitational quantum theory in one fewer dimension, with no locality in the emergent direction. Tensor-network models suggest that geometry is built out of quantum entanglement — that spacetime connectivity and entanglement structure may be the same thing seen two ways. And in canonical quantum gravity, the fundamental equation contains no time variable at all. Her conclusion: spacetime is not fundamental, and if the fundamental ontology is genuinely non-spatiotemporal, then spacetime as we conceived it is not real either — at best it is a useful approximation, like the solidity of a table, and the lesson of the problem of time is that temporal becoming was never in the world to begin with.

The other concedes every technical result but reads them conservatively. What is being called emergence, he says, is ordinary inter-theoretic reduction: thermodynamics reduces to statistical mechanics, and temperature did not thereby become unreal. Duality is a symmetric relation, so nothing in the mathematics privileges one side of it as the fundamental one. He calls himself a spacetime functionalist: whatever collectively plays the spacetime role — the right metrical relations, causal structure, recovery of Einstein's equations in the appropriate limit — is spacetime, whether it sits at the bottom of the theory or is woven from something else. On his view his opponent is equivocating between "not fundamental" and "not real," and the timelessness of the Wheeler-DeWitt equation is an artifact of a particular quantization scheme, not a discovery about the world.

Draft the joint statement. Specifically: a numbered section of claims both can sign, covering what is established versus conjectural (including the fact that the duality evidence is strongest for anti-de Sitter space, not our universe), what each formal result does and does not show, and what both accept about the limits of their own positions; then a disagreements table stating each residual dispute in neutral wording that both sides would accept as a fair statement of their own view; then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side (I suspect these include whether the emergence results generalize beyond anti-de Sitter space, whether functionalist criteria for "playing the spacetime role" can be made precise enough to be falsifiable, and whether any account of temporal experience can be given on a fundamentally timeless ontology); and for each crux, an argument, calculation, or conceivable observation both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. No citations or bibliography — they both know the literature and will attach references themselves. If you conclude that part of their dispute is verbal rather than substantive, identify which part and explain why.
