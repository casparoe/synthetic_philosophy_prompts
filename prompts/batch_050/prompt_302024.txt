Context: I organize an interdisciplinary seminar on geometric rationality — the cluster of ideas around maximizing expected logarithmic growth rather than expected wealth, the Kelly criterion, and the gap between time-average and ensemble-average performance in repeated gambles. I want a poem to open our first session, and I want the poem's form to carry part of the argument rather than merely decorate it.

The task: write a sestina about Kelly betting. The six end-words should be six outcomes or magnitudes drawn from a repeated multiplicative process (for example: double, halve, gain, loss, stake, ruin — though choose your own if you can do better). The sestina's fixed spiral recurrence should do real conceptual work: the same six words return in every stanza in a new order, enacting the central fact of compounding — that final wealth depends on the product of returns and not their sequence, that order changes nothing, and that only the geometric mean decides who grows and who disappears. Across the six stanzas the poem should move through, in whatever order you find natural: (i) the seduction of the arithmetic mean, and why the naive bettor who maximizes expected wealth per round is led to overbet; (ii) volatility drag, the quiet subtraction that only the geometric picture makes visible; (iii) the logarithm as the utility of someone who plans to play forever — why maximizing E[log W] is not risk-aversion smuggled in through the back door but time itself speaking; (iv) full Kelly as a speed limit, the stake above which growth declines even as expected value keeps rising; and (v) ruin as an absorbing state: in a multiplicative process a single zero is not one bad outcome among the others but the end of the sequence, and its word should recur with gathering weight. The three-line envoi should gather all six end-words and land on the asymmetry between the ensemble and the individual: the average across players can keep climbing while almost every player is broke.
