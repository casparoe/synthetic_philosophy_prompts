After seven years, my business partner and I are dissolving our consulting firm. If we agree on terms ourselves, there is $400,000 to divide; if we can't agree, the matter goes to binding arbitration whose fees will consume essentially the entire amount, leaving us both with nothing. Our attorneys have each given us an opinion on the most we could plausibly hope for in a best-case ruling: mine says I could receive at most $280,000, since I supplied most of the startup capital, while hers says she could receive at most $200,000, since she ran day-to-day operations. She has proposed that we simply split the $400,000 equally, reasoning that since we must agree or both get nothing, we are symmetrically situated and 50-50 is the natural outcome. Something about this bothers me: an equal split gives her 100 percent of her best-case outcome while giving me only about 71 percent of mine, so I would be conceding proportionally far more than she would. But I can't tell whether that observation reflects a genuine principle of fair division or is just self-serving arithmetic. Is there a defensible sense in which the split should track each party's best-case outcome rather than split the total evenly, and how strong is that case against the obvious simplicity of 50-50? Where reasonable people who study fair division disagree about the right principle, please say so explicitly rather than presenting one approach as the settled answer, and please respond in American English.
