So I'm a retired chemist (forty years of kinetics work, mostly gas-phase stuff) and over the past year I've gotten hooked on philosophy of math, and I keep tripping over something I can't find a proper name for. Let me describe it in my own terms and maybe you can tell me what the field calls it, if anything.

When I say I *know* the activation energy of some reaction, there's a complete story connecting that fact to my belief: molecules collided, photons got absorbed, my spectrometer registered something, electrons moved around in the circuitry, I read the printout. There's a physical chain running from the fact itself all the way into my head, and if the fact had been different the chain would have delivered a different belief. That's what makes it knowledge and not a lucky guess.

Now take something I know in math, like there being infinitely many primes, or that the square root of 2 is irrational. Where's the chain? Prime numbers don't emit photons. They don't collide with anything, they don't trigger my instruments, they don't do anything at all as far as I can tell. Yet my belief about them feels more secure than anything the spectrometer ever gave me. So there's a pincer movement: if real knowledge requires the facts to physically reach out and push the believer around, then mathematical knowledge looks impossible; but if mathematical knowledge is fine without any such pushing, then apparently you can know about things that couldn't affect you even in principle, which sounds like magic.

Two wrinkles so you don't answer the wrong question. First, this isn't just "how do we justify proofs" — I can check a proof line by line, that's easy. The puzzle is that even a perfect proof never put me in *contact* with anything the way the lab equipment did. Second, I've seen the move where people say numbers are just shorthand for claims about physical objects, but that can't cover the huge infinities that never show up in any lab.

So: does this exact configuration have a name of its own? I don't just want a label. Tell me what the standard problem is actually taken to be, which neighboring problems it gets lumped in with (I suspect there are two or three that look similar but aren't), and whether my wrinkle about contact-vs-justification is the core of the real problem or a seperate issue. Keep the whole answer to one page, 350 words max, and no reading list — I won't be able to check it anyway.
