When a colleague's language model fabricated a legal citation and I called the output a lie, she corrected me: "LLMs don't lie, they hallucinate." The phrase did real work in that exchange — it seemed to absolve the model, and perhaps its deployers, of a certain kind of fault — but I suspect it's doing several things at once and not all of them honestly. Please lay out the distinct precise theses this one-liner might be expressing: for instance, a claim about the absence of intent to deceive, a claim about the absence of any attitude toward truth whatsoever (in the sense Frankfurt made famous), a claim about what kind of error is involved, or merely a claim about the right metaphor. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; whether the term "hallucination" itself smuggles in assumptions (e.g., about perception or about an underlying veridical capacity) that the reading needs but cannot have; and whether that reading actually delivers the exculpatory conclusion my colleague reached for. Conclude by saying which disambiguation you think the people who repeat the slogan most likely mean, and whether it supports the use they put it to.
