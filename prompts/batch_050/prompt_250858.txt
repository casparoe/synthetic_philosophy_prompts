Something that's bugged me: almost all mainstream practical advice about uncertainty runs on expected value. Charity evaluators rank interventions by expected lives saved per dollar, financial planners compare investments by expected return (maybe with a variance penalty bolted on), and everyone gets told to take the bet whenever the odds are in their favour. But the very same people also tell you to buy insurance, diversify your portfolio, and never stake your whole bankroll on a +EV bet, all of which strictly lower your expected value. The Kelly criterion crowd, and the ergodicity economics people following Ole Peters, say this isn't some inconsistency you need utility functions to patch up: the rational target was never expected value in the first place but the time-average growth rate of your wealth, i.e. maximize expected log wealth, because you live one history sequentially, not an ensemble of parallel histories all at once.

So: what does the standard practice of advising-by-expected-value actually presuppose about rational agency? That the agent is repeatable enough times for the law of large numbers to bail them out? That aggregation across many people or many years washes risk away the same way it does within one portfolio? That risk aversion is a separate psychological taste you add on top of EV rather than something rationality itself demands? Reconstruct the implicit theory of rational choice behind the everyday practice, and be honest about where the practice strictly needs that theory versus where it would be licensed by several rivals. Then: does the Kelly/geometric view explain the insurance-and-diversification advice for free, as it claims, rather than treating it as a series of embarrassing exceptions? And what would concretely have to change -- in how charities evaluate interventions, in how robo-advisors size positions, in how we teach people to think about bets -- if the geometric view became the default instead of a footnote?
