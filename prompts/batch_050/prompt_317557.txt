I'm on the curriculum review committee for my state's high-school history standards, and I need help drafting our decision memo. Quick background: a new draft textbook chapter on the early modern period keeps assigning causal credit to non-human forces. Three passages are contested:

1. A section on the conquest of the Americas that says smallpox and measles "did most of the conquering," with population collapse figures, and only a short paragraph on indigenous political decisions and resistance.
2. A section on the 1780s that argues the Laki eruption and the resulting bad harvests "set the stage" for the French Revolution -- the words "set the stage" are doing a lot of work there.
3. A passage on the Irish potato famine that calls the blight the "cause" of the famine and the mass emigration, and mentions British policy only briefly.

The committee is split. One faction loves this stuff: they say students have been fed great-man history forever and that taking germs and climate seriously is just honest causal accounting. The other faction says the chapter slides into environmental determinism and quietly erases human agency -- the potato blight didn't starve anyone by itself, granaries stayed closed and exports continued by choice, and framing smallpox as the "conqueror" turns contingent political collapses into inevitability, which is both wrong and a bit insulting to the people who actually lived through it. Nobody on the committee can articulate the difference between "legitimate attention to non-human causes" and "determinism" in a way that would let us write an actual standard.

So: act as a professional philosopher of history / social science who moonlights on curriculum boards. Think carefully about this, and do a bit of brainstorming first -- list out the different ways a historical text can credit a non-human cause (background condition, trigger, sufficient cause, "actor" with quasi-agency) and which of them are defensible. Then write the memo, about 600-800 words, with:

- A concrete recommendation on each of the three passages: keep as-is, revise, or cut, with actual proposed rewording where you say revise.
- A general test the committee can apply to future passages -- some criterion for when non-human causal attribution is legitimate explanation vs. when it becomes determinism or agency-laundering (making political choices look like weather). Try to make the criterion do real work, not just "consider multiple factors."
- An honest treatment of the strongest point from whichever side you rule against -- if you side with the revisionists, take seriously that "smallpox conquered the Americas" is more true than the heroic-conquistador alternative; if you side with keeping it, take seriously that grammar like "the blight caused the famine" smuggles in a whole theory of what counts as a cause.

Don't just name-drop Jared Diamond or evoke "determinism bad, agency good" -- I can get that from the committee members' emails. I need the memo to actually adjudicate.
