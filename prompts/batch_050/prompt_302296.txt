My roommate took one logic class and now thinks he's dissolved free will. Here's his argument, which he claims is just Aristotle, De Interpretatione ch. 9, cleaned up:

(1) Every proposition is either true or false right now, including ones about the future.
(2) If "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is true right now, then nothing that happens from here on can make it false — you can't retroactively change what's already true.
(3) Whatever can't be made false by anything that happens from here on is already necessary.
(4) So either it's already necessary that the battle happens or already necessary that it doesn't.
(5) Deliberation only makes sense about things that aren't already settled.
(6) So the two admirals agonizing tonight are wasting their time.
(7) But the argument never uses anything special about battles or about tomorrow, so it generalizes: every future event is already necessary, and all deliberation is always pointless.

Then he adds the kicker: run the same reasoning on things Aristotle himself treats as obviously contingent — in that very chapter Aristotle talks about a cloak that could be cut up but won't be, it'll wear out first — and the argument "proves" those are necessary too. So it proves too much, and my roommate says that means we should all just be fatalists and he's fine with that.

I feel like the sleight of hand is somewhere between (2) and (4), but I can't pin it down, because (2) sounds like a tautology and (3) sounds like a definition. Which step do I actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to give up one of (1) and (5), I'd rather keep (5), but I want to know the price of each. And does Aristotle's own way out — the distinction he seems to draw between what's necessary when it is the case and what's necessary without qualification — actually block the argument, or is it just the same confusion stated in Greek?
