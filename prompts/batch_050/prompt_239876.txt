Last weekend I got into a long and somewhat heated argument with a close friend about whether democratic publics have the right — perhaps even the duty — to suppress intolerant speech, whether through law or through pressure on the platforms and institutions that amplify it. My friend's core move was that suppression is only objectionable when imposed on the people from above; when a majority itself demands that certain views be driven out of public discourse, that is not tyranny but self-government, the nation willing its own values. In On Liberty (1859), in the opening chapter, Mill describes precisely this line of thought: "What was now wanted was, that the rulers should be identified with the people; that their interest and will should be the interest and will of the nation. The nation did not need to be protected against its own will. There was no fear of its tyrannizing over itself."

I would like you to respond as a careful scholar of nineteenth-century political thought who is also conversant with contemporary debates about free speech, content moderation, and the so-called paradox of tolerance. Think carefully and begin by briefly brainstorming the relevant considerations before settling into your answer. My first question is interpretive: in the quoted sentences, is Mill speaking in his own voice or ventriloquizing a position he means to attack, and what exactly is his diagnosis of why that position goes wrong? My second question is the speculative heart of the matter. Suppose Mill could observe the present day, where the suppression of disfavored speech is frequently demanded by democratic majorities and carried out not primarily by the state but by private platforms, employers, publishers, and organized public opinion. Would he regard this as the very danger he warned about — perhaps in an even purer form, since he thought social coercion could be more pervasive than legal coercion — or would he accept my friend's argument that democratically willed, non-state suppression escapes his critique?

Finally, press on the hardest case, which is where my friend and I reached an impasse: speech that itself advocates intolerance, censorship, or the exclusion of some group from equal standing in public discourse. Does Mill's framework have the resources to license suppressing such speech without collapsing into the majoritarian logic he rejects, or is he committed to protecting even the enemies of free discussion? Where you think the text simply does not settle the answer, please say so plainly and flag the point at which reconstruction ends and guesswork begins, rather than projecting a tidy modern position onto him.
