I'm drafting the position section of a paper on corporate moral agency, prompted by a case my reading group couldn't stop arguing about: a shipping firm whose routing algorithm, cost targets, and maintenance schedules combined to produce a fatal accident, even though every engineer, manager, and executive involved behaved in a way that would seem reasonable for someone in their particular role. Nobody was reckless; something nonetheless went badly wrong, and the bereaved families blamed the company, full stop. In working out my view I've anchored myself to chapter 16 of Leviathan, where Hobbes develops his account of persons, authors, and things personated: he treats the commonwealth and similar bodies as artificial or feigned persons whose words and deeds are owned by the natural persons who authorize them, and he notes that even inanimate things, like a church, a hospital, or a bridge, can be personated by a rector or overseer who acts in their name, while insisting that such things cannot themselves be authors of anything. My question, ultimately, is whether my Hobbesian scaffolding can bear the weight I'm putting on it. Here is what I currently hold: (1) A corporation can itself be genuinely blameworthy for an outcome, and this blame can be apt even in the no-fault case above, where blame does not simply distribute down onto some culpable individual. (2) Corporate agency is entirely a matter of Hobbesian representation and authorization: there is no emergent group mind, and talk of what the company did is a convenient fiction in which the company's acts are ultimately owned by the natural persons who commissioned them. (3) Moral responsibility requires the capacity to respond to blame with genuine recognition of wrongdoing, something in the family of guilt or remorse, rather than mere outward behavioral or policy adjustment. (4) Corporations are not phenomenally conscious; there is nothing it is like to be a company, and nothing that could feel the company's guilt. (5) Fining a corporation is justified purely as deterrence and never as a matter of desert, yet the blame in (1) is genuinely deserved and not merely a useful signal. (6) A company's operative commitments, fixed by its charter and its procedures for aggregating members' judgments, can diverge from the commitments of every single one of its members, and when they do it is the company's commitments, not the members', that govern what it does.

Please audit this set for joint tenability. Identify which pairs or triples of commitments are in genuine tension and which only appear to be, and rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally serious; for each, say exactly what would have to give and what that revision would cost the overall view. In particular, tell me whether my reading of the Hobbes material in chapter 16 is doing any real work or is quietly at war with the rest: if corporate acts are always owned by natural-person authors, as Hobbes's scheme seems to require, is there any room left for blame that sticks to the company precisely when no author is at fault, and does Hobbes's point that personated inanimate things cannot be authors undercut (1), (6), or both? Finally, if you had to recommend one single revision that resolves the most severe tension at the lowest cost, say which it is and whether it forces me to abandon the Hobbesian frame or merely to reinterpret it.
