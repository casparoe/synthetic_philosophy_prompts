I'm putting together a unit for an undergrad seminar on practical rationality and I want to anchor the Buridan's ass discussion in something the students will actually feel, so I've landed on Sophie's Choice (the film mostly, though some of them will have read Styron's novel). On the face of it the famous platform scene looks like the darkest possible version of the ass case: Sophie is ordered to pick which of her two children gets sent to the gas chamber, and if she refuses to pick, both die. She can't rank them, there's no tiebreaker available to her, and yet -- unlike the ass, who starves -- she acts. She gives up her daughter. So my first question: does the scene refute the paradox, or just relocate it? The standard "solution" to Buridan situations is that any arbitrary pick is rational when the alternatives are equal, so just pick. But watching Sophie do exactly that makes "just pick" seem either obviously right (she saves one child instead of losing both) or obscene, and I can't tell which reaction is the philosophically serious one.

Here's the wrinkle I want the reading to engage with. I think there are actually two different operations getting conflated in discussions of the ass: choosing, where you have grounds that favor one option, and picking, where you select without grounds, on a whim or a coin flip or sheer willpower. The ass's problem is supposed to be that rationality only gives it choosing, and choosing is unavailable when the bales are equal. Sophie seems to prove that picking is always psychologically available even when choosing isn't -- but the film then spends its entire second half showing what the pick costs her: the Brooklyn sections, Nathan, the lies about her father, and finally her suicide. Read the film as an argument about the picking/choosing distinction: is the ending suggesting that a groundless pick between equal options is never really "resolved," that it leaves some permanent residue the ass case, being about hay, can't capture? Or is that just sentimentality, and the honest lesson is that Sophie did the rational thing on the platform and everything after is trauma rather than philosophy? Also worth addressing: unlike the ass, Sophie didn't face her options freely -- the "choice" is coerced by the officer's ultimatum -- so is this even a Buridan case at all, or does coercion change the structure enough that the analogy misleads?

I'd like a reading that takes the film seriously as philosophy but also pushes back where the film's emotional force might be doing work that argument should be doing. If there are specific scenes you'd tell my students to rewatch with the ass in mind, name them. And feel free to end by saying what, if anything, the film adds to the debate that the standard journal-article treatments of rational choice under indifference dont.
