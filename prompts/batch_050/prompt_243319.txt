An article on early modern philosophy of mathematics I recently read summarizes Hobbes's views on geometry like this: 'Hobbes held that geometrical truth is arbitrary convention, no different in kind from our choice of names for things, so on his view Euclid could just as well have been otherwise had we defined our terms differently. As an empiricist, he took geometry to rest ultimately on experience of physical bodies rather than on pure demonstration. He was also the last major philosopher to take seriously problems like squaring the circle, and the decisive refutation of his attempted proofs by John Wallis discredited his constructivist philosophy of mathematics along with them, which is why his views are now only of historical interest.' Given what I vaguely recall about Hobbes's rationalism about demonstration, something here feels off, but I'm not sure what. Is this a fair summary?
