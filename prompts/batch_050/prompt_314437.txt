A friend gave me this argument against van Fraassen's Reflection principle and I can't find the weak joint. Reflection says, roughly: if you know now that your credence in p at some later time will be r, your credence in p now should already be r.

(1) Reflection is a requirement of rationality.
(2) Tomorrow I'm going to carefully check a long proof of a math theorem T that I'm currently 50/50 on; once I've checked it, my credence in T will be about 1.
(3) I know (2) now, so by Reflection my credence in T should be about 1 already.
(4) But if my credence is already about 1, I have no reason to bother checking the proof -- and the same goes for any experiment whose result I can predict this well. So Reflection makes actually gathering evidence pointless.
(5) Separate case: next week I'll be hypnotized into being certain I'm a fried egg, and I know this now. By Reflection I should be certain now that I'm a fried egg.
(6) Both consequences are absurd, so Reflection isn't a requirement of rationality.

I suspect (4) and (5) go wrong in different places, but I can't pin either down. Which step(s) do you reject, and why did they look fine? And does one fix handle both cases, or do the math-proof case and the hypnosis case need different answers? If you think Reflection survives, state the restricted version you'd defend; if you think it dies, tell me what replaces it for judging whether my current and future selves are being coherent.
