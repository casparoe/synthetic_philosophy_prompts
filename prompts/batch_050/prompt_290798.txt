I am a novelist, and over the course of several manuscripts I have kept notes on which invented worlds my imagination — and my readers' — will admit and which it refuses. I would like to know what principle, if any, underlies my verdicts. A world in which the dead speak and gravity fails on Tuesdays: I can inhabit it fully. A world in which the narrator, in a calm and affectionate register, treats the routine humiliation of a servant class as simply what decency requires: I find I cannot enter it on the terms the text sets, and my draft readers balk even when instructed to suspend judgment. Yet a world that merely relabels things — one where cruelty is called kindness and everyone uses the words consistently — gives me no trouble at all; I translate as I read. Stranger still, I once wrote a draft in which virtue is reliably punished by the world's own machinery, where the moral order itself is unjust, and that felt imaginatively accessible, almost vivid, though it should offend me as much as the servant novel does. And when I attempted, as an exercise, a world in which two plus two equals five and characters reason soundly on that basis, the failure felt different from the moral cases: not refusal but blankness. So: is there a coherent principle that sorts these cases the way I sort them — one that explains why endorsed cruelty resists where relabeled cruelty, a corrupt cosmic order, and broken arithmetic each fail or succeed for different reasons? I want your committed answer, not a menu of positions: state the principle you would defend, say which of my verdicts it vindicates and which it forces me to revise, and then apply it to the case I am currently stuck on — a novel whose narrative voice must, for the story to work, treat an honor killing as an act of love. Should I try to overcome the resistance, honor it as data that the book's premise is false, or redesign the book around it?
