Philosophy professor here — I've taught the philosophy of emotion seminar three times now and every year I tell my students to just consult their intuitions about cases, so I figured I should finally do it myself and see where mine actually land. I'm hoping you can help me make sense of them. Take on the role of someone who's thought deeply about the main options in this literature — James-style feeling theories, Nussbaum-style judgment theories, the more recent perceptual accounts — and think carefully before answering. Here are the cases and my gut verdicts: (1) My colleague Claire is arachnophobic; she'll sincerely tell you the house spider is harmless, she's not lying or hedging, and she's still terrified of it. Verdict: that's real fear, full stop. (2) A jaded bureaucrat who coolly judges that he's been wronged by a rival — can list the wrongs on request — but feels nothing, isn't agitated, doesn't dwell on it, just files it away. Verdict: that's not anger, that's a belief. (3) An actor who works herself into a frothing rage on stage every night — pounding heart, flushed face, clenched everything — while judging nobody to have wronged her. Verdict: also not anger, just arousal plus technique. (4) My grief for my father, dead fifteen years: most days nothing is felt at all, but I'd insist the grief never went away and certain songs can bring it all back. Verdict: it's grief even on the days it's unfelt. (5) The objectless, free-floating anxiety I sometimes wake up with, before I've located anything to be anxious about. Verdict: it's an emotion, it's anxiety, even though at that moment there's no judgment about anything. (6) The flash of envy at a friend's promotion that I completely disavow — I don't for a second think her success is bad, I'd vote for it again — but it was there and it stung. Verdict: real envy, an emotion, despite the disavowal.

So: is there one principle about what an emotion is that captures all six? My own current best guess, for what it's worth, is that an emotion is an evaluative take on something that has gotten into the body somehow — but I'm pretty sure cases (5) and (4) break that in opposite directions, and I suspect (2) and (3) are doing some quiet work I've smuggled in. Here's what I'd like: first, briefly brainstorm two or three candidate principles before committing, then give me your best one (or a small menu), stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is motivated by which case. Be honest about which of my six verdicts your principle has to overturn, if any — I notice (1) says fear without the judgment counts and (3) says bodily turmoil without the judgment doesn't, and I can't currently see what distinguishes them, so if the answer is "one of those has to go," tell me which and why rather than papering over it. Finally, apply your principle to two cases I didn't list: the fear I feel during a horror film at a monster I know perfectly well isn't real, and disgust at a piece of chocolate shaped like something revolting that I know is just chocolate. Don't just name-drop theories at me — I want the principle, its costs, and its predictions.
