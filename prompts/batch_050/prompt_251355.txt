I keep going around in circles with a friend about whether DNA is literally a code or whether that's just a metaphor biologists can't seem to quit. Instead of arguing in the abstract I wrote down my gut verdicts on a set of cases, and I want to know whether there's a principle underneath them. Here's where I land:

- Morse code: obviously a literal code. Arbitrary mapping, needs a decoder, can be mis-sent or misread.
- The codon-to-amino-acid mapping: literal too, I think. It looks arbitrary in the right way — lots of alternative assignments would have worked just as well, which is exactly what you'd expect from a convention (that old 'frozen accident' thought).
- DNA as a 'blueprint' for the whole organism: metaphor, and a bad one. Nothing decodes sequence into a body plan the way ribosomes decode codons into amino acids; too many steps, too much environment.
- A stretch of DNA synthesized from scratch in a lab, never once inside a cell: still contains the same information, I want to say — same way a book in a dead language still says something even with no readers left. But I notice this sits awkwardly with my insistence that a literal code needs decoding machinery.
- Calling a mutation a 'copying error' or 'typo': I use this talk but I'm suspicious of it. 'Error' presupposes a correct text, and nothing in evolution is proofreading toward the right answer. Weird thing is, error-talk is exactly what literal codes license — you can mis-send Morse — so if I think the codon mapping is literal I'm not sure why I balk here.
- Enzyme-substrate lock-and-key, hormone binding a receptor: nobody calls this a code and that feels right — but the fit is just as evolved and just as specific as the codon table, so I can't say what the difference is.
- 'Epigenetic code' talk (methylation marks, histone modifications): my friend says if the codon table counts, this counts. I want to say no because the same mark does different things in different cell types — but then 'bank' means different things by context too, and English is literal language, so context-dependence can't by itself disqualify anything.

My tentative principle: code-talk is literal exactly when there's (a) an arbitrary, convention-like mapping and (b) machinery whose evolved job is to read it. That sorts Morse, the codon table, and the enzyme case the way I want, but I'm pretty sure it breaks on the lab-synthesized strand and maybe on the dead-language book. So: repair the principle, or replace it. If no single principle saves all my verdicts, tell me which one I should drop and why, and what the best principle says about the case I keep flip-flopping on — whether the genome is a 'program' that the cell 'runs'.

One more thing: please be upfront about which parts of this are genuinely disputed among philosophers of biology and which are close to settled. Don't present a contested position (say, that information-talk is only ever a metaphor, or that teleosemantics cleanly resolves everything) as if it were the consensus.
