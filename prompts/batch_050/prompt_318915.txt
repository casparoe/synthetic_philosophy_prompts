So I rewatched Minority Report last weekend (the Spielberg one, Tom Cruise) and afterwards got into this huge argument with my brother that I want help settling. He listens to a lot of podcasts and lately he's obsessed with this idea of "neurorights" -- the way he explained it, there's basically three rights having to do with your mind: (1) mental privacy, nobody gets to read what's in your head; (2) mental integrity, nobody gets to mess with or manipulate what's in your head; and (3) cognitive liberty, which I think means the right to actually use your own mind -- make your own decisions, change your own opinions, that kind of thing. I might be butchering the exact terms but that's the gist.

His claim was that Minority Report isn't really a free will movie at all, it's a movie about those three rights being violated, and that most people miss this because they get distracted by the whole "can the future be predicted" thing.

So: read the film through that three-part framework. Which of the three rights does PreCrime actually violate, and is it even the one you'd expect? Some stuff I'd want the reading to deal with:

- The obvious candidate is mental privacy -- the state is basing arrests on the contents of someone's mind before they've done anything. But the precogs' visions aren't exactly Anderton's thoughts, are they? Nobody reads HIS brain. So is PreCrime a mental privacy violation at all, or is it something weirder -- like punishing an intention that only exists in someone else's head?
- The precogs themselves. Agatha especially -- drugged, floating in that pool, her brain output treated as government evidence, and she clearly never consented to any of it. It seems to me SHE might be the one whose rights are most thoroughly violated (all three at once?) and the movie kind of knows it but the brother says the movie doesn't care about her, it just uses her. Who's right?
- The famous bit at the end where Anderton is standing over Leo Crow and the prediction is counting down and he chooses not to shoot. My brother says this is the movie cashing in on cognitive liberty -- the right to decide for yourself finally exercised. But is it? If the whole system already "knew" what he'd do either way, is that scene a vindication of cognitive liberty or just a lucky exception?
- Also the "minority report" itself -- the suppressed alternate vision. A dissenting prediction that gets deleted because it's inconvenient. Does the framework have anything to say about the state suppressing a mental content, or is that more of a free speech thing and the framework runs out of steam there?

Most of all I want to know where the framework breaks. It seems built for cases where a company or a government is reading or tweaking YOUR mind, and the movie's central horror is sort of orthogonal to that -- you can have perfect mental privacy and still be arrested for a future somebody else saw. If the neurorights stuff can't cover the main case, is it actually a useful framework or just a response to a narrower problem? And conversely, does the movie show something about minds and rights that my brother's three-part list is missing entirely -- like, is there a fourth right the film is gesturing at?

Be honest about where the reading strains. Don't just map every scene onto one of the three rights if some scenes don't fit. And no spoilers worries, obviously I've seen it. Definately curious whether the ending (the system shutting down because of one guy choosing differently) makes the brother's reading stronger or weaker.
