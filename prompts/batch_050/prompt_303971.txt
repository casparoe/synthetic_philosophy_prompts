So here's the situation. I spent forty years as a marine biologist and retired two years ago, and to keep my brain from turning to mush I joined a continuing-ed program at the local university. This spring I'm co-leading a seminar for other retirees on "rationality and its quirks" -- twelve people, mostly former engineers, doctors, one accountant, sharp as tacks but zero philosophy background. We meet for 90 minutes. For my session I want to do the Ellsberg paradox, the thing with the two urns where one has a known 50/50 mix of red and black balls and the other has an unknown mix, and people overwhelmingly prefer betting on the known-odds urn even though their preferences can't be made consistent with any single probability assignment to the unknown one.

Here's my problem: last year I basically *told* a group about the paradox and everyone nodded politely and said sure, that makes sense, and I could tell it didn't land at all. This time I want them to *feel* the pull of ambiguity aversion in their own gut before anyone names it. So design me an in-class activity that does that. Some constraints: real (small) stakes would be great -- I can bring a bowl of chocolate or coffee vouchers or whatever, but nothing that costs anyone actual money beyond pocket change. I can make physical urns/bags with marbles, that's easy for me. I want at least a couple of rounds, because my worry is that one round of betting is just a gamble and people won't notice anything odd about their own behavior. Ideally the setup should be arranged so that when I do the reveal, they can look back at their own choices and catch themselves being inconsistent -- like, the inconsistency should be visible in what *they* did, not in something I tell them about probability theory. A few of them have decent stats backgrounds and will try to be clever about it, so bonus points if the activity has a wrinkle that trips up even the person who thinks they know what's coming.

Also give me a debrief plan: how to walk them from "wait, why did I pick that urn" to the actual structure of the paradox (known risk vs. genuine ambiguity, and why no probability assignment rationalizes both choices at once), what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I already suspect one guy will announce he's heard of the paradox and ruin the reveal -- how do I handle that?), and be honest about which part of the phenomenon a classroom game with trivial stakes probably *can't* reproduce. One more thing: our program has a couple of members who refuse gambling-style activities on principle, so I'd love a variant framing that doesn't look like betting but still produces the same choice pattern. Please answer in American English.
