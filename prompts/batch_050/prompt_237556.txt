I've been teaching a grad seminar on philosophy of technology and I've basically talked myself into Langdon Winner's position -- the strong-ish version, where technical artifacts aren't neutral tools but can embody political properties in their very design, not just in how people happen to use them. The bridges-and-highways style cases, the tomato harvester, the whole "some technologies are inherently political in a way no use-pattern can undo" package.

Here's what I want from you: what would count *against* this view? I don't want the lazy undergrad response ("it's the user, not the artifact!") -- I've heard it, I grade it, it's boring. I want a serious map of the view's exposure. Some things I'd like covered:

1. Which possible findings -- empirical, historical, or conceptual -- would genuinely threaten it? For instance, suppose it turned out that the standard parade cases all dissolve under closer historical scrutiny, and the remaining candidates (say, a technology whose low-resource version is genuinely impossible to build, not just uneconomical) all turn out to be coercible by context. Would the committed artifact-politics person have to concede defeat there, or would they have a principled fallback? Is the fallback any good?

2. A nagging worry I have: the view might be unfalsifiable because "political" is doing too much work. If any constraint on possible action counts as political, then doorknobs have politics and the thesis is trivial; if only some constraints count, then we need an independent criterion, and doesn't that criterion do all the real philosophical work while the thesis just tags along? Tell me whether the view can be stated in a form that risks something -- what does it predict that pure social-constructivism-about-technology or actor-network-ish symmetry doesn't?

3. To make this concrete, here's a test I sketched in my seminar notes (students hated it, maybe rightly): call it the redesign test. An artifact has politics in Winner's strong sense only if there's no functionally equivalent redesign that removes the political effect while keeping the technical function. The bridges case fails this test if the same bus-exclusion could've been achieved by a toll booth. Run this test on the view: does it save the thesis by giving it teeth, or does it reveal that almost nothing passes and the thesis collapses to a claim about a handful of weird cases?

4. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one, tell me whether a defender would actually *concede* it counts against them or dismiss it as missing the point -- and whether the dismissal is legit or copium.

I'd personally be most moved by a case where the artifact clearly structures power but the "politics" turns out to be entirely in the surrounding legal/economic arrangment, such that any hardware at all would do. If you think that case is actually common, that worries me. Grade my position like you'd grade a paper that defends it: where does it bleed?
