I host a podcast on science and ideas, and my next guest is a moral philosopher defending a participation-based account of complicity: the claim that you can be complicit in wrongdoing purely by intentionally taking part in a collective venture, even when your individual contribution made no causal difference -- a single purchase of factory-farmed meat changes nothing about what gets produced, yet she wants to say the purchase can still be wrong. Draft exactly eight interview questions as a numbered list, with no introduction or conclusion. Each item should be one or two sentences and should include a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. I want the questions to press where the view is actually weakest: the causal impotence problem for individual consumers in huge markets; how she distinguishes complicity from merely benefiting from wrongdoing (paying taxes in a state that wages unjust wars, inheriting wealth built on exploitation); whether knowledge and intention are required, given that most participants in harmful institutions are inattentive rather than deliberate; and what obligations follow if she is right -- is exit enough, or must the complicit actively resist? Write in American English.
