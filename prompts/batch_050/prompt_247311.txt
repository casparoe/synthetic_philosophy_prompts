But I've been obsessed with the surprise exam paradox for years (teacher announces a surprise exam next week, students reason backwards from Friday and conclude it's impossible, exam happens Wednesday anyway). Here's my half-formed idea I want turned into a proper proposal:

In my day job, "surprise" announcements are a solved-ish engineering problem. If a scheduler wants a job to run at an unpredictable time, you don't announce "it'll be a surprise", you use randomness, commitments, hash preimages, that kind of thing. And when we reason about what nodes in a system "know", we use formal models where knowledge changes as messages arrive. So my hunch: the paradox only bites because the standard setup is vague about whether the teacher is *committed* to a policy or can adapt *after* seeing the students' reasoning. If you model it as a two-player game with an explicit knowledge-update rule, the paradox should either dissolve or reduce to a known impossibility result (like: no deterministic policy over a finite window can guarantee surprise against a player who knows the policy — basically a minimax argument).
