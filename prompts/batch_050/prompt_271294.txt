I teach a university seminar of 14 undergraduates (mixed philosophy and economics majors, 80 minutes) on rational choice under risk, and I want to run an activity before we read anything on risk-weighted expected utility. Design a staged game that gets students to feel, in their own choices, the tension that motivates Buchak-style risk-weighted expected utility theory: that a person might rationally care about how good outcomes are spread across possible worlds — weighting the worst-case scenario disproportionately — rather than simply maximizing probability-weighted averages.

Concretely, I want the following:

First, a sequence of four to six rounds of real choices with small but genuine stakes (for instance, points convertible into a snack budget, or tokens that determine a small charitable donation the class controls). The rounds should be constructed so that most students, choosing freely, will diverge from expected-value maximization in a systematic direction — e.g., repeatedly taking options whose worst-case outcome is protected even at a meaningful cost in expectation — and so that a few students instead "buy the lottery ticket." The payoff structures should be worked out numerically by you, tuned so the divergence is neither trivial nor so large that no one resists it, and each round should offer at least one choice pair where the higher-expected-value option is also the one with the worse minimum outcome.

Second, a mid-game trap: one round should be designed so that a student who simply "plays it safe" as a blanket habit — without any structure to their caution — gets burned in a way that pure expected-value reasoning would have avoided. The point is to forestall the debrief collapsing into "cautious people are risk-weighted maximizers, bold people are EV maximizers." I want students to discover that raw timidity is not the same thing as principled downside-weighting.

Third, a debrief structure of about 20 minutes that does the following, in order: has students reconstruct their own choice pattern from a record sheet they've been keeping; asks them to write down, before any theory is introduced, a one-sentence principle that would justify their choices; and only then introduces the idea of rank-dependent weighting of outcomes, mapping specific rounds onto the distinction between (a) attitudes toward risk expressed through the shape of a utility function over outcomes and (b) attitudes expressed by directly re-weighting probabilities according to outcome rank. The debrief should also surface the standard pressure point for the risk-weighting view — that it licenses violations of intuitively compelling coherence constraints (choices that look stochastically dominated, or that shift depending on how the same final outcome distribution is described) — and give me a concrete example round from the game to point to when raising it.

Fourth, tell me what will predictably go wrong: which rounds students will misread, where house-money psychology will flatten real risk attitudes, what to do if one confident student announces the expected values to the room and herds everyone, and how to handle a student who refuses to choose consistently.

Finally, an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce. In particular, address whether small classroom stakes can ever instantiate the kind of high-stakes, one-shot choice (career gambles, medical decisions) where downside-weighting is supposed to be most clearly rational, and what the facilitator must supply verbally to bridge that gap without lecturing.

Please include the full payoff tables, the choice record sheet, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the debrief prompts.
