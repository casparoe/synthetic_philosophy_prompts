Since 1969, every incoming British prime minister has, as one of the first acts of taking office, hand-written four identical sealed letters to the commanders of Britain's four ballistic missile submarines, to be opened only if a nuclear strike has destroyed the British government. The letters instruct the commanders what to do in that event; the reportedly available options are to retaliate, not to retaliate, to place the submarine under the command of a surviving ally such as the United States or Australia, or to use their own judgment. When a prime minister leaves office, the letters are destroyed unopened, so no one but the writer ever knows what any given letter said.

Analyze this institution through the lens of the counterfactual mugging problem. In that problem, a perfect predictor tells you: I flipped a coin; had it come up one way, I would have given you a large sum conditional on your paying me a small one; it came up the other way, so I ask for the small sum and offer nothing in return. Certain decision theories say to pay, because the policy of paying is the one you would have wanted to adopt before learning the coin's outcome, even though paying is now causally futile. The letter-writer who instructs retaliation seems to face a structurally similar choice: a retaliatory strike, launched after deterrence has already failed and Britain already destroyed, cannot save anyone and can only add to the destruction; yet the deterrent's value ex ante plausibly depends on the letter reading "retaliate."

Give me a careful mapping in both directions. First, make the isomorphism precise: what plays the role of the coin, of the predictor's foreknowledge, of the payment, and of the ex ante policy, and does the analogy support writing "retaliate" for roughly the reasons a proponent of paying the mugger would give? Second, identify every disanalogy you can find and say what each one does to the conclusion. Consider at least: the adversary is a fallible human government rather than a perfect predictor, and its beliefs about the letter's contents -- not the contents themselves -- do the deterring; the letter-writer and the letter-executor are different people, with the submarine commander a free agent who must choose whether to obey; and the decision is repeated across successive governments whose letters may differ, unlike the one-shot mugging. Third, note that the institution contains one feature the mugging lacks -- the options "use your own judgment" and "go to Australia" -- and ask whether these correspond to anything in the decision-theoretic landscape, for instance declining to bind oneself in advance and deferring the ex post question to a future self who will decide by ordinary causal lights. Finally, turn the analysis around: does the fact that real states handle the deterrent paradox through sealed letters, destroyed unread, and delegation to a commander on patrol tell us anything about how actual agents approximate updateless commitment -- and does the practice vindicate the decision theory that recommends paying the mugger, or reveal that the theory abstracts away exactly the features (imperfect prediction, divided agency, secrecy) on which the real institution depends?
