After a career in experimental physics I have, in retirement, begun working my way through the free will literature, and before I embarrass myself at the reading group I have joined, I would like an audit of the position I have tentatively assembled. My commitments are these:

(1) Frankfurt-style cases succeed: an agent who acts wrongly on her own, while some counterfactual intervener stood ready to force her hand had she wavered, is morally responsible even though she could not have done otherwise. So responsibility does not require alternative possibilities.

(2) Ought implies can: nobody is under a moral obligation to do what it is not within their power to do.

(3) A person is blameworthy for an action only if, in acting as they did, they failed to do what they morally ought to have done.

(4) Moral responsibility is compatible with the truth of causal determinism.

(5) When I learn that a wrongdoer's conduct was produced entirely by a neurosurgeon's direct manipulation of his brain, bypassing his own deliberation, I judge him not responsible — whatever the manipulation's outward resemblance to an ordinary causal history.

What I want is not a survey of the literature but a consistency check of this particular package. Which pairs of these commitments are in genuine tension, and which only appear to be? I ask because two of the pairings worry me when I lay them side by side, but I cannot tell whether I am conflating distinct notions of "could have done otherwise," and I suspect at least one apparent conflict dissolves once the relevant notion is made precise. Please rank any genuine tensions by severity, state for each what exactly would have to be given up to resolve it and what that concession costs, and identify the single revision — if one exists — that restores coherence at the lowest overall price. Where a tension turns on an equivocation, show the equivocation rather than merely asserting it.
