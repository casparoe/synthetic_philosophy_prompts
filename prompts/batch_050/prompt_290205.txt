I've been turning over a question about compromise between people with different values, and I have a set of verdicts I can't make fit together. I'd like to know what principle, if any, lies behind them. I have no training in philosophy; these are just cases I've thought up or watched happen.

Case 1: My neighbor prizes a tidy lawn; I want to keep my wildflower strip for the bees. We agree she stops complaining about my strip and I keep my edges mowed. Verdict: obviously fine, maybe even admirable.

Case 2: A vegetarian and a meat-eater share a household. He agrees to buy only pasture-raised meat; she agrees to stop lecturing at dinner and to cook it twice a week. Verdict: a good deal. Both think animals end up better treated than before.

Case 3: Two city councillors each think the other's pet project is a waste of money. Each votes for the other's project so her own passes. Verdict: ordinary politics, nothing wrong.

Case 4: A voter who genuinely believes a certain practice is a grave moral wrong -- on the order of killing -- is offered a deal: stop campaigning against it, and in exchange the other side will fund measures she agrees would reduce how often it happens. Verdict: she must refuse. You don't trade away the victims, even for fewer victims. But I notice this looks structurally identical to Case 2, and I can't say what the difference is.

Case 5: A company pays the leader of the campaign against it a personal sum to disband the campaign, while changing nothing about its practices. Verdict: corruption, plainly worse than Case 4, though both sides prefer the deal to the status quo.

Case 6: Two donors disagree about which of two charities to fund. If one merely thinks the other's charity is less effective, splitting the money seems fine. If one thinks the other's charity actively does harm, splitting seems to make her complicit. Verdict: the two versions really differ, though the transaction is the same.

Is there one principle that delivers all of these -- something about what you're trading (your own conduct versus someone else's fate), or about who captures the gain, or about whether the deal changes the world or only your behavior? If no single principle works, tell me which verdict is cheapest to surrender and why. And test whatever you settle on against two cases I haven't listed: buying carbon offsets for a flight you believe harms the climate, and a religious believer accepting exemptions from a law she considers unjust rather than fighting to repeal it.
