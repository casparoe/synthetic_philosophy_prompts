I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind (in a cog-sci department, which mostly means I get heckled by the ML people down the corridor), and my dissertation chapter is turning into a bit of a mess. It's on whether we're licensed to attribute beliefs to LLM-based assistants, and before I send the draft to my supervisor I want a consistency audit of the positions I've ended up committed to, because they accumulated over three chapters and I suspect they don't all fit. Here they are:

(a) I'm broadly Dennettian about belief: for a system to have beliefs just is for it to be reliably and fruitfully predictable from the intentional stance. There's no further fact 'inside' the system -- no hidden sentence-in-the-head or inner glow -- that would make it really believe something the stance misses, or fail to believe something the stance reliably delivers.

(b) My coding assistant genuinely believes things. For instance it believes my test suite uses pytest and that I hate docstrings. When I say it knows my codebase, I mean that litterally, not as shorthand.

(c) All the same, I deny it has desires or goals of its own. The helpfulness is trained-in performance. It believes things but doesn't want anything -- not even to be helpful.

(d) A thermostat believes nothing whatsoever, and neither does the predictive text on my phone. My view has to deliver that verdict; I'm not willing to bite the bullet and call them believers, even boring ones.

(e) Whether the assistant really believes is ultimately the kind of question mechanistic interpretability research could settle: once we can read the internals well enough, we'll either find belief-relevant structures or we won't, and that answers it.

(f) The fact that it contradicts itself all the time -- asserts something in one reply and flatly denies it two prompts later -- is no bar to belief attribution, since humans are inconsistent too.

(g) What a system believes can't depend on what a given interpreter happens to know (say, whether they've read the source code). Belief is a fact about the system, not about us.

So: which of these are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and what is the cheapest revision that restores consistency -- and what does it cost me? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging everything as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give. I haven't got a preferred escape route, so don't try to guess which commitment I'm attached to. Also, where relevant, seperate out what's actually settled about Dennett's own position (e.g. how he handles simple systems like thermostats, or the reality of the patterns the stance detects) from what is genuinely contested in the literature -- I don't want a contested reading of him presented to me as the received view, since my examiners will know better.
