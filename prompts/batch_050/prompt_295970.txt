I serve on the executive board of an international human rights documentation center, and we are deadlocked over the framing of the largest report our organization has ever produced. Our field teams spent three years documenting a campaign by the armed forces of a state I will call K against the Merai, an ethnic and religious minority concentrated in K's northern provinces. The established findings include roughly 40,000 civilians killed; about 700,000 people driven across the border; the systematic destruction of Merai shrines, schools, and the provincial archive holding most Merai-language records; and a state program that placed several thousand Merai children for adoption with majority-ethnicity families. The government of K maintains that this was a counterinsurgency against an armed separatist movement and that civilian deaths were incidental or unauthorized. Our investigators found no written order to destroy the Merai as such; the best evidence of purpose consists of the pattern itself and intercepted communications in which senior officers speak of permanently solving the security problem of the north.

The board must approve the report's framing next month, and three camps have formed. The first wants us to name it genocide now: they argue the pattern fits Raphael Lemkin's original conception of the crime, which encompassed the destruction of a group's cultural and social foundations and not merely mass killing, and that the child-transfer program and the archival destruction are exactly what Lemkin had in mind. The second camp insists on legal caution: the 1948 UN Genocide Convention, as courts have applied it, requires a specific intent to destroy the protected group, in whole or in part, physically or biologically, and no court has found that intent here; we should therefore document crimes against humanity, war crimes, and ethnic cleansing, note indicators of genocidal risk, and stop there. The third camp wants to abstain from legal categories altogether: our mandate is documentation, they say, and the term has become so politically loaded that deploying it invites dismissal of the entire archive as advocacy.

Draft the board's decision memo. It should recommend one of the three framings and state the bottom line up front. It should set out the general criteria an organization like ours should apply when deciding whether to use the word, rather than treating this case in isolation. It should address the divergence between Lemkin's broader sociological conception and the Convention's narrower legal category, and say which of the two an NGO's report is properly answerable to, given that we are not a court. It should take seriously the strongest argument for the losing side: that absent any judicial finding of specific intent, naming genocide overreaches our evidence, collapses a careful legal distinction into a moral epithet, and hands the perpetrator state a propaganda victory by letting it point to an unfounded accusation. Finally, it should address whether the political consequences of the label — donor pressure, Security Council dynamics, the charge that the term is applied selectively to some atrocities and withheld from others — are legitimate inputs into this decision at all, or whether letting them in corrupts the question being asked.
