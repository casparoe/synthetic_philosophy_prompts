After forty years as an experimental physicist, I have spent my retirement reading the literature on why some nations are rich and others poor — the long-running contest among institutional, geographic, and cultural explanations. I keep encountering a word I thought I owned: experiment. Economists describe certain episodes of history as "natural experiments" and treat them as the discipline's analogue of my old laboratory. The celebrated instance: because Europeans could settle safely in some colonies but not others, owing to the local disease environment, they implanted different institutions in different places; the resulting contrast in present-day prosperity is then read as the outcome of an experiment history ran on our behalf, and is taken to license the conclusion that institutions, not geography or culture, are the deep cause of national wealth. My physicist's instinct is that the word is carrying more weight here than any experiment could bear, but I am new enough to this field to distrust the instinct. I would like a proper audit of the analogy.

Please begin with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the treatment, the control group, the randomizer, the experimenter, and replication in the colonial case. Then the disanalogies, with a judgment for each as to whether it matters for the conclusion the analogy is asked to support — I suspect the absence of a genuine randomizer, the possibility that settler mortality affects income today directly through the persisting disease environment rather than only through institutions, and the non-independence of the units are not all equally damaging, and I want to know which are fatal and which are pedantic. Organize this part around a threefold distinction of my own devising, which an honest experiment normally fuses but this case may pull apart: identification — is the observed contrast confounded; extrapolation — would the estimated effect hold outside the colonies studied; and manipulability — is the cause the sort of thing anyone could even in principle administer as a dose, given that no government can run the intervention "install the institutions of a low-mortality settler colony"? Fourth: what does the word "experiment" quietly import that the target lacks — in particular, does it smuggle in the premise that the only obstacle to causal knowledge is confounding, rather than the uniqueness of history and the fact that "institutions" and "culture" are treatment variables nobody has measured the way I once measured a cross-section?

Finally, a verdict on what should replace the analogy. My candidates, drawn from sciences I know better: epidemiology before the randomized trial, which established that smoking causes cancer without ever assigning anyone to smoke, through convergence of many individually flawed designs; and the historical sciences such as geology, which reconstruct a unique past from traces by inference to the best explanation and accept weaker, more contestable conclusions. Which of these does the wealth-of-nations question actually resemble? And once the experiment analogy is retired or demoted, what survives of the institutionalist conclusion — can we know, in any defensible sense, that institutions rather than geography or culture made some nations rich, and if so, what exactly is the weakened claim?
