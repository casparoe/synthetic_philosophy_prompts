A few months ago my reading group — the same crowd that used to meet to argue about the sequences — spent two sessions on moral progress. We read Kwame Anthony Appiah's The Honor Code alongside some discussion of Eric Williams's Capitalism & Slavery, and the group split cleanly in two. One side held that the standard story Appiah attacks is basically right: moral revolutions happen because arguments accumulate and eventually convert enough people, and honor shifts are downstream of that. The other side found Appiah's pattern compelling — the arguments against dueling, footbinding, and the slave trade were all fully formed and publicly available for generations before the practices collapsed, so the arguments can't be the load-bearing cause; something about honor, status, and material interest has to change to make old arguments suddenly grip. The argument at the meetup never resolved, partly because everyone kept talking past each other with dueling anecdotes, and I'd like a cleaner treatment to circulate before our next session.

Write a dialogue between two characters, Maya and Daniel, who sit together on the grant committee of a small foundation with enough money to fund exactly one program aimed at hastening the end of industrial animal farming. Maya wants to fund a rigorous public argument campaign — the best case against factory farming, made as accessibly and relentlessly as possible. Daniel wants to fund an "honor infrastructure" project: working within one high-status professional guild to make demonstrable abstention from factory-farmed products a mark of good standing, on the model of how dueling died not when it was refuted but when participation stopped conferring honor. Daniel defends the Appiah-Williams-adjacent view that arguments rarely cause moral revolutions on their own; Maya defends the view that arguments are the real engine and that honor and interest shifts are either downstream of persuasion or ephemeral without it. Both should treat the historical cases (dueling, footbinding, the slave trade) as shared evidence to be interpreted, not as trophies to be cited.

Requirements: the two characters must be equally competent — no strawmanning, no rhetorical victory laps — and they must respond to each other's actual points rather than restating their opening positions. Each should concede at least one substantive point over the course of the conversation. They must directly engage the strongest objection against their own side: Daniel has to explain why the centuries-long availability of anti-dueling arguments isn't itself evidence that arguments eventually win, and Maya has to explain why factory farming, which unlike dueling is entangled with the everyday material interests and habits of nearly everyone, would yield to arguments when it conspicuously hasn't so far. They should also disagree about which historical case is the right analogy and let that disagreement do real work in the funding decision. The dialogue should not end in agreement or in a tidy compromise; it should end with each character's position sharpened and one specific residual crux named. Roughly 1,200–1,800 words.
