I serve on my university's animal research oversight committee, which by charter reviews work on vertebrates and has never once looked at an invertebrate protocol. That quiet arrangement ended last month, when a genetics lab sought approval for a new high-throughput Drosophila facility -- roughly three million flies a year -- involving assays that expose the flies to heat stress, starvation, chronic sleep deprivation, and repeated electric shock in learning paradigms. The principal investigator asked, mainly as a courtesy, whether he needed to submit anything to us at all. A junior member of the committee argued that we should start reviewing such work, citing the possibility of insect sentience. A senior member responded with what I'd call the scale argument turned into a threat: if there is even a one-in-a-thousand chance that fruit flies can suffer, the expected suffering in a single year of this facility dwarfs everything we regulate in the rodent wing, and a committee that reasons this way has no principled stopping point -- the cricket colony kept as feeder insects for animals we do review, the worm bins, the undergraduates' behavioral labs. Either we refuse the argument wholesale, he said, or we admit we've adopted a decision rule that will swallow every budget line on campus. I've assigned myself the job of drafting the written opinion we'll vote on, and I'm finding that I've taught the expected-value version of this argument in my applied ethics seminar for years without it ever costing anyone anything. Now it costs something.

Please draft that opinion for me, about a page long, recommending one of three options: (a) leave invertebrate work exempt, (b) extend full protocol review to it, or (c) adopt the junior member's middle-tier proposal of what she calls probability-weighted oversight -- mandatory registration, housing and euthanasia standards, and humane endpoints, but no case-by-case ethical balancing of the sort we do for vertebrates. The opinion needs a bottom line the committee can actually vote on, and it has to engage the decision-theoretic question directly rather than pretending the empirical science of fly pain will settle things for us, because it won't: is it coherent to let a very small probability of sentience, multiplied by very large numbers, drive institutional policy, and if it isn't, what principle should govern how we act under moral uncertainty about the capacity to suffer? Give the strongest losing consideration an honest hearing, including the senior member's no-stopping-point objection and the PI's point that requiring standards implies a judgment about sentience we should not pretend to have made, and that the compliance burden falls on research with real biomedical upside. Two further wrinkles the opinion must address: the facility plans a tenfold expansion within five years, so whatever we adopt now will function as precedent at a much larger scale, and our charter requires that committee decisions be publicly defensible -- the student paper will report this one.
