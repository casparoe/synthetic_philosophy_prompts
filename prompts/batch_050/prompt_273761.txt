Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary -- the ant colony in Gödel, Escher, Bach that converses at a level its constituent ants cannot comprehend -- is usually deployed as a blunt intuition pump for functionalism. I want a more surgical instrument. Construct a thought experiment, in the spirit of Aunt Hillary but not a retelling of her, that satisfies all of the following specifications:

1. Opposing verdicts. On a broadly functionalist theory of mind (mental states individuated by their causal roles), the colony-level system in your scenario has representational states -- beliefs, or something near enough -- of its own. On a competing theory, which you must name and state precisely (for instance, a biological naturalism on which mentality requires the right kind of living, unified substrate, or a view on which a mind requires a single spatially and temporally integrated locus of processing), the very same system has no colony-level mind at all; there are only ants and their interactions. Both verdicts must be derived by applying the theories to the case, never asserted by the narrator.

2. Colony-level content. The colony's representational states must be about something no individual member can represent: the colony, say, 'registers' a fact about its own condition or about its environment at a coarse grain that no ant is capable of entertaining. Explain plainly how colony-level states acquire their content and why no ant has that content.

3. One isolated variable. There must be a single, explicitly identified feature of the scenario -- temporal scale, signaling bandwidth, spatial contiguity, degree of causal integration, or something else you choose -- such that varying it flips one theory's verdict while leaving the other's untouched, and such that the entire disagreement turns on it.

4. No cheating. Realistic materials only: actual ants, or simple robots with ant-grade competence, or humans performing ant-grade duties. No exotic physics, no telepathy, no infallible predictors, and above all no stipulating that anything is or is not conscious or minded; the verdicts have to fall out of the named theories.

The scenario itself should run at most two paragraphs. After it, provide: (a) each theory's reasoning, step by step, to its verdict; (b) an identification of the structural feature doing the work, together with an account of what happens if that feature is removed -- in particular, whether the case collapses into one on which both theories agree, and whether that collapse would be a defect or the point; (c) the strongest reply available to a defender of whichever theory comes out looking worse in your case, and a verdict on whether that reply is principled or ad hoc; (d) one sentence on what a third theory of your choosing (say, an information-integration-based account) would say about the scenario and whether it sides cleanly with either party.

For calibration: I intend to use the case to test whether substrate-based intuitions about group minds track anything more than timescale prejudice, so a scenario that smuggles its verdicts in by stipulation, rather than generating them from the theories, fails.

Begin your response with the thought experiment itself -- no preface, no restatement of the specifications -- and follow it with the analysis in the order given.
