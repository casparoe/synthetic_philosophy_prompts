So I run hiring at a mid-size tech company (~700 people), and we do panel interviews: usually 5-7 interviewers per candidate, each submits an independent hire/no-hire vote, and we go with the majority. Lately people have been asking why we do it this way -- why not just let our single best interviewer decide, why 5 and not 3 or 9, why do we ban interviewers from talking to each other before voting, etc. I dimly remember from a college class that there's a theorem (Condorcet? jury something) saying majorities get arbitrarily reliable as groups grow, but also that it has assumptions that can fail badly, and I suspect some of our practices violate them.

Please write me a decision procedure I can actually run for each hiring loop that tells me: when majority voting is the right call, when I should weight some interviewers' votes more than others (I've heard optimal weighting goes by log-odds of each voter's reliability but I don't know how to do that in practice), when a vote is actively worse than just asking our best person, and when the whole setup breaks and a human just has to decide. It has to be runnable in maybe 20 minutes by a recruiting coordinator who is not going to do calculus. Where the procedure needs a number nobody can really know (like an individual interviewer's actual hit rate), say what a defensible proxy is, and be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as math. Also handle the fact that we sometimes have three-way decisions (hire for team A / hire for team B / no hire), which I gather majority rules handle poorly.

Structure the response as follows:

- Setup I'm assuming (short, ~150 words: what you take a hiring vote to be, and the two or three conditions that make majority rule trustworthy -- competence above chance, independent errors, binary choice -- in plain language)
- The procedure (the bulk of it, steps in order with a go/no-go at each: how to size the panel, whether to allow pre-vote discussion, how to estimate and weight voter reliability without real data, what to do with abstentions and three-way choices, and the specific case where average voter competence is *below* chance and the majority is worse than a coin flip)
- Worked runs (apply it to three of my real situations: (a) a standard 5-person panel with independent interviews; (b) a panel where all five interviewers sat through the same recorded presentation before interviewing, so their impressions may be correlated; (c) a three-way team A / team B / no-hire decision. For one of these the procedure should give an answer I won't like -- e.g. telling me our current practice for that case is worse than a simpler alternative)
- Where the procedure will misfire (~200 words: the failure modes it can't catch and the points where I'm fooling myself if I think the checklist settles it)

Keep the whole thing under ~1200 words if you can. No citations needed, I'd rather have something usable than something scholarly.
