In my seminar on global justice, we are treating the 1984–85 Ethiopian famine relief effort — the Band Aid and Live Aid appeals and the NGOs that channeled their proceeds — as a test case for the two dominant accounts of what rich countries owe the global poor. Take the salient facts as stipulated: the famine was substantially worsened by the Derg regime's own agricultural and military policies; later investigations alleged that portions of relief aid were taxed, diverted, or used to facilitate the regime's forced resettlement campaign; some aid reached rebel-held areas along routes that also sustained the insurgency; and millions of ordinary famine victims were nonetheless fed. Analyze the episode first through Singer's shallow-pond principle and then through Pogge's contention that our stringent duties are negative duties of justice not to uphold a global institutional order that foreseeably harms the poor. Where, concretely, would each account have instructed donors and aid agencies to act differently — does Singer's principle require giving even under known leakage, and on what reasoning; and can Pogge's framework simultaneously condemn the Derg's conduct as harm while classifying Western donations as mere charity, or does the diversion render donors participants in harm on his view? Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: does the episode merely add empirical complication that both theories anticipated, or does it expose a genuine lacuna — say, that each has surprisingly little to say about agency exercised through corrupt or coercive intermediaries?
