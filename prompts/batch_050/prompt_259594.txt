You are a moral philosopher who works on social conventions — the everyday norms around things like tipping, queueing, and crossing the street — and you are constructing a case for an article arguing that conventions do not bind everyone present in the same way. Think carefully, before you settle on a scenario, about what makes a convention binding at all; you may want to consider two or three candidate settings and test each against the constraints below before choosing the one that survives.

Construct a thought experiment in which a visitor to another country is morally required to refrain from following a local convention that the locals around her are fully entitled to keep following. This inverts the familiar when-in-Rome intuition, and the inversion has to be generated by the nature of conventions themselves, not stipulated. Constraints: the setting must be realistic and ordinary (a café queue, a crosswalk, a restaurant — no science fiction); the convention must be morally neutral, not secretly unjust or harmful to anyone; no threat of legal punishment or social sanction may do the work; and the visitor must fully understand the convention and be perfectly capable of performing it, so her obligation cannot rest on ignorance, incompetence, or misunderstanding of what the behavior means locally. The scenario itself should be one paragraph.

After the scenario, demonstrate that the case works: show that each constraint is satisfied, explain why the usual symmetry between visitor and local breaks down here, identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work and what happens to your verdict if that feature is removed, and then give the strongest objection available to someone who holds that a convention binds everyone present, local and visitor alike — along with your honest assessment of whether the case survives it.

Write the whole response in continuous prose, as you would in a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
