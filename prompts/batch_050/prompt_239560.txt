I am preparing a single ninety-minute session of a second-year undergraduate philosophy of language course, devoted entirely to Frege's puzzle about identity statements. The students have read nothing by Frege; the assigned text is the opening pages of "On Sense and Reference" (1892, trans. Max Black), which contains this passage:

"Let a, b, c be the lines connecting the vertices of a triangle with the midpoints of the opposite sides. The point of intersection of a and b is then the same as the point of intersection of b and c. So we have different designations for the same point, and these names ('point of intersection of a and b', 'point of intersection of b and c') likewise indicate the mode of presentation; and hence the statement contains actual knowledge."

Design the teaching plan for the session — its architecture, not the lecture itself — covering the following:

1. The order in which the pieces must be introduced, with the reason for each dependency. In particular: whether the puzzle (the difference in cognitive value between "a = a" and a true "a = b") must be felt before the sense/reference distinction is offered, or whether the distinction can be presented first and the puzzle derived from it; where Frege's rejection of his own earlier Begriffsschrift view — that identity is a relation between names — belongs in the sequence, given that students reliably reinvent that view on their own and need it refuted rather than skipped; and whether the passage above works better as the opening hook or as the moment where the solution clicks into place.

2. The carrying example. The passage supplies Frege's own: two descriptions of a single geometrical point. Weigh it against the standard alternative, "the morning star is the evening star," as the case around which to build the session. One wrinkle worth exploiting: many students will not know that the medians of a triangle are concurrent, so the identity in the passage states something that genuinely requires proof — which makes Frege's claim that the statement contains actual knowledge unusually vivid, but also means the example presupposes a mathematical fact some students will simply have to take on trust. Say which example you would use and why, and specify what the chosen case must have and must lack to carry the idea (must both modes of presentation be ones a single competent person could possess without suspecting they coincide? must the case avoid inviting the thought that a sense is just a definition, or a picture in the head?).

3. The misconception map: the misreadings to expect — that senses are private mental images and hence that Frege's theory makes meaning subjective; that the difference between "a = a" and "a = b" is merely a difference in the marks on the page; that the puzzle is dissolved once we say one name abbreviates a description; that an identity statement is about language rather than about the object — and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off, before it hardens into the student's settled understanding of the theory.

4. A check for understanding for the final fifteen minutes: a task the students perform, whose success or failure would show they can deploy the distinction rather than recite it — for instance, constructing a fresh case in which one object is given under two modes of presentation and explaining why learning the identity extends knowledge, or diagnosing why a purely referential account cannot explain Lois Lane's situation with Superman and Clark Kent. Tell me what a passing performance looks like and what failure modes to watch for.

5. What to refuse to cover at this level, and to say so out loud. I suspect propositional attitude contexts, empty names, and the sense and reference of whole sentences belong behind a fence, but I want your judgment on the full fence line and on how to justify it without making the theory look evasive.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single structural decision on which the whole plan hangs — then give the plan, with no preamble and no restating of this request.
