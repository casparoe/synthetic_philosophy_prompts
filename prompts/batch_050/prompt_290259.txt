I am a PhD student in political theory preparing a seminar on democratic decision-making, and I want to use the film Twelve Angry Men in a session on whether deliberation can solve the problems raised by judgment aggregation. Read the film through the discursive dilemma. In the standard dilemma, individually consistent judgments about a set of premises and a conclusion can generate an inconsistent or otherwise troubling collective judgment: a premise-based procedure may produce a conclusion that few or none of the individuals directly accept, while a conclusion-based procedure may preserve the majority’s verdict at the cost of detaching it from the group’s judgments about the supporting reasons. Construct an explicit interpretive agenda for the jury’s deliberation—for example, propositions concerning the reliability of the eyewitnesses, the evidential force of the knife, the defendant’s opportunity, and whether the prosecution has established guilt beyond a reasonable doubt—and compare how premise-based and conclusion-based procedures would resolve it. Which model better captures what happens in the jury room, and where does the mapping become artificial?

Please push the analysis beyond a simple classification. The film requires unanimity rather than majority rule, and the jurors do not merely report stable judgments: they revise their beliefs, reassess the relevance of evidence, and alter the order and framing of the questions under discussion. Does deliberation eliminate the aggregation problem by transforming the judgments to be aggregated, or does it merely relocate the problem into path dependence, agenda choice, social pressure, and the sequencing of objections? Consider also whether the jury’s eventual agreement is genuinely reason-responsive when some jurors are moved by prejudice, impatience, conformity, or resentment rather than by evidence. A careful reading should distinguish concluding that the defendant is innocent from concluding that the state has not met its burden of proof. Finally, explain what the film reveals about collective rationality that formal models of judgment aggregation tend to miss, while identifying where the analogy should be resisted rather than treating the changed verdict as uncomplicated proof that deliberation works.
