A friend who works on food ethics sent me the following argument and I cannot stop thinking about it, because the conclusion strikes me as obviously unacceptable while every step looks, at least at first glance, defensible. I would like you to tell me where it goes wrong, if it does; and if you think the reasoning is valid, tell me which premise is least costly to abandon and exactly what I would be giving up by abandoning it.

(1) If a food-production practice foreseeably and avoidably causes the death of sentient animals, those deaths count morally against the practice, other things equal -- regardless of the species of the animals and regardless of whether they live in a barn or a hedgerow.

(2) Arable agriculture foreseeably and avoidably kills enormous numbers of sentient wild animals: small mammals are shredded by combine harvesters, ground-nesting birds and reptiles are destroyed by tillage, and rodents are poisoned deliberately as pests. These deaths scale with the number of hectares under cultivation.

(3) Producing a given quantity of calories from crops requires cultivating a substantial area, whereas a single extensively grazed steer yields a very large quantity of calories and can be raised on pasture that could not grow crops anyway. So the marginal hectare devoted to grass-fed beef displaces far fewer wild-animal deaths than the marginal hectare devoted to grain or pulses.

(4) Therefore, per calorie delivered to the table, eating extensively grazed beef causes fewer deaths of sentient animals than eating plant foods, and by (1) it is, other things equal, the morally better choice.

(5) But the reasoning does not stop there. If minimizing deaths per calorie is the right principle, then we should prefer whichever food source packs the most calories into a single death, which favors the largest animals available: one whale or one elephant feeds a village. So the principle that made beef look good in (4) also makes whaling and elephant hunting look morally optimal.

(6) Yet it seems absurd to think that the morally best diet is whale meat. Hence something in the chain must give.

For what it is worth: the claims I would most hate to surrender are (1) and (2), since it seems to me that the field mouse killed by a harvester matters no less than the pig killed in an abattoir, and that food ethics which ignore wild animals entirely are missing most of the morally relevant casualties. I am more willing to doubt the empirical and comparative steps, though I note they are the ones I understand least well.

Please diagnose the argument rather than merely declaring the conclusion false. Identify the specific step or premise you think does the damage, explain why it looked innocent, and state what each of the plausible repairs would cost me. I would also ask that you indicate how confident you are in your diagnosis overall, and that you single out the single point in your analysis -- whether an empirical assumption, a conceptual distinction, or a step of the reasoning -- about which you are least sure.
