So some context: I recently went through a big falling out with my brother (money stuff, plus things said that can't be unsaid), and everyone in my family keeps telling me I have to forgive him, like it's a duty I owe, even though he's never apologized and doesn't think he did anything wrong. Around the same time, my book club picked The Tempest, and it hit me that Prospero is basically in my situation dialed up to eleven: Antonio stole his dukedom and tried to have him killed, never says sorry, stays basically silent through the whole final scene, and Prospero forgives him anyway. Or... does he? That's what I want you to dig into.

Read The Tempest through the philosophical debate about whether forgiveness can be obligatory and whether it can be unconditional. I'm thinking of the tension between views like Griswold's, where genuine forgiveness is conditional on the wrongdoer's repentance and acknowledgment, so that "forgiving" the unrepentant is really just condoning or letting go, versus the Derrida-style line that conditional forgiveness is just bookkeeping and the only true forgiveness is forgiving the unforgivable, i.e. precisely the unrepentant. Where does Prospero's pardon of Antonio land on that map? He explicitly calls his own mercy rare and virtuous, but he also extracts his dukedom back first, threatens Antonio, and pointedly refuses to actually embrace him or treat him warmly afterward -- so is that forgiveness at all, or something else wearing its clothes (forbearance, political settlement, self-mastery)? And notice the play's ending has Prospero turning around and asking the audience's indulgence to set him free, which suggests he thinks forgiveness is something that can be requested and granted almost on demand. Also please don't ignore Caliban: his quasi-repentance at the end ("I'll seek for grace" is the gist) is the one case in the play where forgiveness might meet Griswold's conditions, and I never see anyone talk about what that contrast with Antonio implies.

I'd like a reading that takes both the play and the philosophy seriously -- don't just use Prospero as an illustration of a thesis you've already settled, and if there's a scene or detail that resists the reading you're giving, say so honestly.
