I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation on the epistemology of heuristic (non-deductive) reasoning in mathematics, and for an upcoming workshop I've been asked to circulate a one-page statement of my core commitments so the other participants can probe it. Before I send it out, I'd like an audit of whether the package actually hangs together. The through-line of the dissertation is what I call the presumption of independence: roughly, the rational default of treating two phenomena as probabilistically independent when no causal or structural link between them is known. I take this to be the tacit engine behind a lot of respected heuristic argumentation, especially random-model reasoning in analytic number theory.

Here are the commitments as I currently hold them:

(1) The presumption of independence is a norm of rationality, not an empirical hypothesis: absent any known linking mechanism, one ought to set one's joint credences to factor, and only revise away from independence when evidence of dependence comes in. Its status is a priori.

(2) The presumption is what gives random-model heuristics in number theory their evidential weight. Modeling the primes as a random set of density 1/log n yields quantitative predictions (asymptotic counts for twin primes, Goldbach representations) that match the computed data remarkably well, and it is on this basis that my credence in the twin prime conjecture is around 0.95. I know the naive model needs local corrections — shared divisibility obstructions, like the fact that n and n+2 can't both be even — but I take these to be fully compatible with the presumption, since they flow from known structure rather than from discovered dependencies.

(3) Because the presumption is a priori, its justification cannot rest on its empirical track record. The success of the random model confirms the conjectures it predicts; it does not, and on pain of circularity cannot, confirm the principle that licensed the model in the first place.

(4) I also accept a Reichenbach-style principle of common cause: when a robust correlation between apparently independent variables is observed, one should infer a common cause or direct link rather than treating the correlation as brute.

(5) I'm persuaded by Bertrand-style paradoxes that there is no representation-independent notion of an uninformative prior; any appeal to indifference has to specify and defend a parametrization.

(6) The match between the model's predictions and the data is doing genuine justificatory work for my 0.95 credence in the twin prime conjecture. If the model's predictions had systematically failed at the scales we've computed, I would hold the conjecture substantially less confidently.

Here's what I'd like from you. Please respond as a senior philosopher of mathematics with strong formal-epistemology credentials, examining this statement the way a sharp external examiner would. Before the audit proper, briefly restate each commitment along with what you take to be its motivation, so I can verify you've reconstructed the package I actually hold rather than a straw version. Then: identify which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension and which pairs only appear to be; for the merely apparent ones, explain what dissolves the appearance rather than just asserting that they're compatible. Rank the genuine tensions by severity — please don't list them as if they were all equally bad — and for each one say concretely what would have to give, and what that revision would cost me for the larger dissertation project. Finally, name the single revision that would clean up the most, and tell me honestly whether the package, once revised in that way, still delivers the epistemology of heuristic reasoning that I want it to deliver.
