I am a graduate student currently taking a seminar on metaethics, and we have just spent three weeks on the Frege–Geach problem. Here is what strikes me about the state of the literature, followed by what I actually want to ask.

The problem is over half a century old, and as far as I can tell it has settled into a repeating cycle. Each new expressivist framework — Blackburn's higher-order attitudes, Gibbard's hyperplans, the hybrid theories, the more recent inferentialist treatments — arrives with a compositional construction that handles embedding, negation, and the validity of the moral modus ponens. Each time, the critics answer with one of two objections: either the construction has smuggled in the very descriptive content expressivism was supposed to avoid, or it has merely stipulated attitudes tailored to mimic the inference patterns rather than explained why those patterns hold. The expressivist rejoinder, in turn, is always that no more should be demanded: the cognitivist does not explain validity either, but takes compositional semantics for granted, so holding expressivism to a higher standard rigs the game.

My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because the two sides never agreed on what solving the problem would consist in — roughly, deriving the phenomena from independently motivated resources versus merely accommodating them — and that Dreier-style worries about creeping minimalism are this same disagreement surfacing one level up. Is that diagnosis right? If so, what would progress even look like: an agreed criterion for when a semantic story explains rather than imitates, a datum both sides accept as currently unexplained (the negation problem? Schroeder's being-for objections?), or a result about minimalism that deflates the whole dispute? If the diagnosis is wrong and there is a genuine, resolvable question underneath, identify it and explain why decades of able philosophers have failed to state it cleanly. I want a diagnosis with reasons, not another survey of the proposals.
