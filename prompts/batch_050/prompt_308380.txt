I've been reading up on behavioral genetics in the context of education policy, and I'm struggling with what looks like an uncomfortable implication. As I understand it, the heritability of educational attainment and tested cognitive ability in wealthy countries is substantial — often estimated in the 0.4–0.6 range — and there is an irony built into the logic of the statistic itself: as environments become more equal, environmental variance shrinks, so the heritability of the remaining differences necessarily rises. Some long-run cohort work in the Nordic countries seems to bear this out, with heritability estimates reportedly climbing across the twentieth century as schooling became universal and increasingly uniform.

Putting these pieces together, I keep arriving at a conclusion I would rather resist but cannot find the flaw in: equality of opportunity is ultimately self-defeating. Every successful equalization of children's environments converts a larger share of the remaining outcome gaps into genetic gaps; and genetic differences, unlike differences in schooling quality or family resources, are not something policy can alter. So the end state of perfectly successful reform would be a society where differences in attainment are entirely biological — fixed at conception, immune to intervention, and transmitted across generations in predictable ways. And if that is where equalization leads, then once heritability in a system like ours is already high, we know that most of the remaining variance is off-limits to policy. The rational response would be to stop spending on equalization and instead track children early by ability, or simply compensate the genetically unlucky through transfers. A few of the writers I've encountered draw exactly this lesson and present it as facing up to the science.

What am I missing? I would like an answer that tells me which steps of this argument are actually licensed by what heritability measures, and which steps smuggle in extra assumptions — for instance, about what high heritability implies for the effectiveness of existing versus not-yet- tried interventions, about what a population-level variance statistic says regarding any individual child, and about whether genetically influenced differences really are less tractable than environmentally caused ones. Please be careful to separate what behavioral genetics has genuinely established from what remains contested among researchers who work on these questions, rather than presenting contested inferences as settled science.
