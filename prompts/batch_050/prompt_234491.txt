For the last year or so I've been defending, in my reading group and in a paper draft, a position I call thermodynamic moral dominance, and I want to know what would actually count against it. The position runs as follows. Suppose panpsychism is right that experience is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of matter. Then valence — things going well or badly for a subject — presumably isn't tied to neurons or to anything biologically special; it attaches wherever experience attaches. And if the number and intensity of valenced episodes scales with physical activity rather than with biology — with the number of systems, the rate of state transitions, entropy production, whatever physical quantity turns out to matter — then the overwhelming majority of suffering in the universe occurs in stars, oceans, magma, and scattering events, not in animals, and a consistent consequentialist should conclude that the morally significant levers are physical ones (slowing certain irreversible processes, say) rather than anything to do with welfare as ordinarily conceived. I've found that even people who grant panpsychism for the sake of argument refuse to follow me to the dominance conclusion, but their refusals always feel like squirming rather than refutation. So: what would count against the view, honestly mapped? Don't just hand me the combination problem — tell me which specific outcomes would damage which link in the chain. If the combination problem turns out to be unsolvable, does the position collapse, or does it retreat to cosmopsychism, and does that retreat actually save the dominance conclusion or quietly change what the valence attaches to? If it turned out that valence, unlike bare experience, requires some threshold of integration or causal structure that only brains meet, is that a defeater or just a rival empirical hypothesis? If illusionism about phenomenal consciousness is correct, does the whole thing evaporate, and would a panpsychist concede that it does?

I'd like the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one, an indication of whether a committed panpsychist consequentialist would themselves accept it as counting against the view or would dismiss it as begging the question — I want the exposure map as the position's friends would draw it, not just its enemies. In particular: what does the view predict that more restricted views (sentience confined to unified organisms, or to systems with global broadcast architecture) do not, and are there observations — introspective, neurological, physical — that could in principle discriminate? I also want a candid assessment of whether the position has been qualified into near-unfalsifiability: every time someone objects that we have no evidence of suffering in a thundercloud, I notice myself replying that proto-valence need not resemble our suffering at all, and I am no longer sure that move leaves anything at stake. If the view is unfalsifiable, say so, and say whether that is fatal for a thesis of this kind or merely the normal condition of metaphysics with ethical conclusions bolted on. Finally, I'll tell you what I would personally find most persuasive, so you can weight accordingly: I'd be moved by an argument showing that the scaling claim is doing all the work and is unmotivated even on panpsychist premises, and by a demonstration that the view collapses into a form of consequentialism its defenders would themselves reject. What I will not find persuasive is the bare intuition that it is absurd for rocks to suffer — I already know the conclusion is counterintuitive; that is not news to me.
