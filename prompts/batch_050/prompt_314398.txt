As a retired natural scientist newly arrived in philosophy, I keep hearing “the future will resemble the past” used to dismiss Goodman’s grue puzzle: if emeralds have always been green, we should expect them to remain green rather than become blue after some specified future time. But the slogan seems to conceal the very issue of which respects of resemblance are evidentially relevant.

In Section IV of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, David Hume writes: “No object ever discovers, by the qualities which appear to the senses, either from the causes which produced it, or the effects which will arise from it; nor can our reason, unassisted by experience, ever draw any inference concerning real existence and matter of fact.”

Please distinguish the precise claims that the slogan might express—for example, a metaphysical uniformity thesis, a psychological description, a methodological rule, a probabilistic principle, or a claim restricted to projectible properties. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive, plausible, or false; how it relates to Hume’s claim; and whether it can rule out grue without assuming the disputed distinction between green-like and grue-like predicates. Please answer in no more than 900 words, excluding the quoted passage.
