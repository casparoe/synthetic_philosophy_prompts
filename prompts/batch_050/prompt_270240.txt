I am a novelist by trade, currently at work on a book whose central character is a lapsed seminarian, and for the past year I have been living rather closely with Dostoevsky. This spring a colleague in the philosophy department at the nearby university has invited me to set one essay question for the final examination of her upper-level seminar on freedom and the good life, on the strength of my supposed intimacy with The Brothers Karamazov. The students will have read Book V, Chapter 5, the Grand Inquisitor chapter, in Constance Garnett's translation, and they will have ninety minutes. What I do not want is the question my colleague says the chapter usually provokes, in which students competently rehearse the three temptations, declare that the Inquisitor prefers happiness to freedom, note that Christ kisses him, and conclude that Dostoevsky sides with freedom. That answer could be written by someone who had read only a summary.

The question I want should instead turn on a structural peculiarity of the chapter that my own novelist's instincts keep snagging on. Early in his preface, before the Inquisitor has spoken a word, Ivan warns Alyosha of the arrangement he has devised: "He comes on the scene in my poem, but He says nothing, only appears and passes on." Everything the Inquisitor then says — his case that the burden of free choice is a cruelty inflicted on creatures too weak to bear it, that the Church has corrected Christ's work out of pity, that mankind will be happier as a cared-for flock than as free agents tormented by conscience — is met, within Ivan's own ground rules, by a figure who is debarred from answering, and in the end answers only with the kiss. I want an exam question that makes students confront whether the silence and the kiss can function as a reply to an argument at all, and if so, what kind: Is the Inquisitor's position the sort that admits of refutation, or only of being lived against? Does Ivan's decision to give his antagonist all the arguments and his protagonist none amount to an admission that the Inquisitor has the better of the reasons, with freedom's cause resting on something that cannot be argued — and would that be a defeat or a victory for Alyosha's side? The question should force students to engage the Inquisitor's anthropological premises (his picture of human weakness, of what makes life bearable) rather than merely his conclusion, and to take seriously the possibility that the chapter is more unsettling than the pious reading allows, perhaps even that Ivan has built a trap for himself.

Please write the examination question itself (it should be self-contained and quotable as it would appear on the paper), a model answer of roughly 600 words written at the level of the best student my colleague has taught, and a marking scheme out of 20. The scheme should enumerate the specific moves an A answer must make — I want named moves, not virtues like "insightful" or "well-organized" — state what a B answer characteristically omits or flattens, and flag the two or three specific errors (for instance, treating the Inquisitor as a cardboard villain, or assuming the kiss is self-interpreting) that should cost marks even in otherwise fluent prose.
