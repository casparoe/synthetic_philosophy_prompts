I'm drafting a short essay for a political theory workshop and I want comments before I submit it. The working title is "Everyone's a Lame Duck." Here's the current draft:

---

Term limits were supposed to cure careerism. The complaint about career politicians is familiar: they optimize for reelection rather than policy, they get captured by whoever funds the next campaign, and over decades they congeal into a class apart from the people they represent. Limit their terms, the thought goes, and you get citizen-legislators who serve and go home.

But the experience with term limits (US state legislatures are the case I know best) suggests the cure has its own disease. Three side effects keep showing up. First, power migrates to whoever stays: permanent staff, lobbyists, agency bureaucrats, party leadership. Second, hard-won expertise walks out the door on a fixed schedule. Third, and most interesting to me, the last-period problem: a legislator who can't run again faces no electoral sanction, so the reelection incentive — arguably the only real accountability mechanism mass democracy has — evaporates exactly when you'd want it most.

Now here's the claim I want to defend: sortition isn't an alternative to term limits, it's their logical endpoint. A randomly selected citizens' assembly with short, non-renewable terms is a one-term limit applied to everyone, forever. Every member is a lame duck from the moment their name is drawn. If the last-period problem and the power-migration problem are real, sortition should maximize both: no member ever faces a sanction, and every member depends entirely on the permanent apparatus that briefs them.

Two replies on behalf of sortition advocates. (a) Accountability-by-sanction was never the point; the point is descriptive representation plus deliberation, and sanctions actively corrupt deliberation by giving members something to lose. (b) The principal-agent framing is wrong anyway: assembly members aren't agents of voters, they're a microcosm of voters, so there's no agency gap to police.

I think (a) abandons the very problems (capture, drift toward the permanent government) that motivated sortition in the first place, and (b) works for a jury deciding one question but not for an ongoing body that must set agendas — that's exactly where the "microcosm" members will lean on staff the way term-limited legislators do.

My proposed fix: split the job. Keep allotted bodies in the judgment seat — agenda-setting, review, veto — where sanctions genuinely do corrupt, and keep execution with professionals who ARE subject to sanctions, where sanctions discipline rather than distort. Roughly the jury/judge split, scaled up. If this works, the upshot is that sortition's fans should stop ignoring the term-limits literature, because it's the best map we have of where the bodies are burried.

---

What I want from you:

1. Attack the "limiting case" move. What's the strongest disanalogy a sortition advocate would press between a term-limited legislator and an allotted citizen? Does my essay survive it, and if not, what minimal repair would you make?
2. Is the judgment/execution fix coherent? My worry is that it smuggles electoral (or hierarchical, or market) accountability back in through the executive branch, and then I've just reinvented mixed government with extra steps. Tell me if that's fatal or fine.
3. Sanity-check the empirical-ish claims (power shifting to staff/lobbyists under term limits, last-period effects) against what you know — no citations please, just flag anything that's more contested than my confident tone suggests.

Be blunt — I'd rather hear it from you than from the workshop.
