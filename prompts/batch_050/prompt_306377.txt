In Moritz Schlick's essay On the Meaning of Life (Vom Sinn des Lebens), there is a stretch of argument I want my students to be able to reconstruct rather than merely summarize. Early in the essay, Schlick considers the familiar idea that life gets its meaning from purposes: our activities matter because of the ends they serve. He objects that any end which is itself only a means to a further end cannot confer meaning, only pass it along; and so a life organized entirely around purposes either launches an infinite regress of deferred justification or must terminate in something whose value is not borrowed from anything beyond it. Later in the essay, after dismissing the glorification of work and duty for its own sake, he locates this terminus in what he calls play, by which he means not frivolity but any activity that carries its own value within it rather than pointing beyond itself, and he goes so far as to associate the meaningful life with youth, understood as the capacity for such self-justifying activity.

Please explain, as carefully as you can, the conceptual machinery doing the work here. Specifically:

First, reconstruct the regress argument as an argument rather than a mood. What exactly is the premise about derivative versus intrinsic value, and is it the same premise as the one in regress arguments about justification in epistemology, or is the structure importantly different when the regress concerns value rather than reasons? Schlick needs the conclusion that meaning must bottom out in something valued for its own sake, but does the regress argument by itself entitle him to the stronger conclusion that this something must be an *activity* rather than, say, a state of the person?

Second, explain why Schlick's answer is not simply hedonism. He explicitly resists the idea that what matters is the pleasurable feeling an activity produces; on his view, if the value resided in a separable feeling that the activity causes, the activity would once again be a mere means and the regress problem would return. Spell out the distinction between valuing an activity *in itself* and valuing it *for its effects*, and assess whether the distinction is stable: if we ask why a bout of play is valuable and the honest answer is that it is absorbing and satisfying, has Schlick really escaped locating the value in a mental state after all?

Third, situate the essay within Schlick's own philosophical commitments. Schlick was a founder of the Vienna Circle and held that genuine propositions are verifiable, while also holding that ethics, properly understood, is an empirical, psychological investigation of what humans in fact approve and desire. Is the claim that the meaning of life is play a factual proposition about human motivational psychology, a disguised recommendation, or an expression of attitude? Does Schlick's framework allow him to say that someone who finds no intrinsic value in any activity is *mistaken* about the meaning of life, or only that such a person is differently constituted? And does the essay's evident normative fervor sit comfortably with that limitation, or is there a productive tension here that Schlick is knowingly exploiting?

I am not looking for a biographical sketch or a survey of the meaning-of-life literature. I want the argument's joints laid bare: which premise is load-bearing, which distinction is fragile, and whether the essay's conclusion is the kind of thing its author's own philosophy permits him to assert.
