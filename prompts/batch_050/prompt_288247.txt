A few months ago I got sucked into an old essay I found online, Bertrand Russell's "On Denoting" (1905), mostly because my brother-in-law bet me I couldn't make sense of a philosophy paper with no training. Near the start Russell writes: "(1) A phrase may be denoting, and yet not denote anything; e.g., 'the present King of France'." His big idea, as I understand it, is that a sentence like "the present King of France is bald" doesn't refer to some spooky non-existent person and say something about him; what it really says is that there is one and only one present King of France and that he is bald. Since there's no such person, the sentence comes out false rather than meaningless. All very tidy. Except I sat down and pushed on it, and now I seem to have proved that the theory blows itself up on its own flagship example, which can't be right, so I want someone to find the hole. Here's the argument: (1) On Russell's analysis, "the present King of France is bald" is false, since nothing is the one and only present King of France. (2) For any meaningful sentence, either it is true or its negation is true — that's just excluded middle, and Russell of all people is hardly going to deny it. (3) So "the present King of France is not bald" is true. (4) But apply Russell's own recipe to that sentence and it says there is one and only one present King of France and he isn't bald. (5) Any sentence of the form "there is one and only one F and it is G" can be true only if an F exists. (6) So Russell's analysis commits him to the existance of a present King of France — precisely the thing he denies in the passage I quoted. (7) Therefore either the theory contradicts itself, or excluded middle has to go. I'd much rather keep excluded middle than keep his analysis, but what I actually want is the diagnosis: name the exact step that fails, explain why it looked fine to me, and tell me what rejecting it costs. If you think more than one step is shaky, rank them by how expensive each is to give up, and say whether any rival treatment of empty descriptions (treating the sentence as meaningless, say, or letting there be things that don't exist) dodges my argument entirely. Please reply in British English.
