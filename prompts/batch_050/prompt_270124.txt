In the philosophy of perception, the debate over the timing of conscious experience has been stuck for decades, and I want a diagnosis of why rather than another entry in it. The standard exhibit is the color phi phenomenon: two dots of different colors flash in rapid succession at two nearby locations, and observers report a single dot moving across the gap and changing color at the midpoint. The puzzle is that the dot seems to change color before the second dot could possibly have been perceived, since the second dot is what carries the new color. Two hypotheses dominate. On the Stalinesque picture, the brain fabricates the motion-and-change sequence in advance of awareness, like a show trial staged before an audience: nothing is experienced until the full edited sequence is ready. On the Orwellian picture, the experience happens as the data arrive, but memory rewrites it before it can be reported, so the false color change exists only in recollection, like a regime doctoring yesterday's newspapers. Dennett's well-known verdict was that the distinction between these two is unanswerable in principle — there is no further fact of the matter about which is correct, only two redescritions of the same processing — and that the debate survives only because both sides assume conscious experiences have determinate onset times the way physical events do. Yet philosophers and psychologists keep writing as if the question were live, citing reaction-time studies, neural timing data, and the possibility of interrupting subjects mid-sequence.

Here is my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the impasse is not about the experiments at all but about which deliverables count as data — first-person reports of when the change was seen, third-person measurements of when the relevant processing occurred, or behavioral proxies like how fast a subject could respond to the change — and the two camps quietly privilege different ones, which is why the same studies get cited by both sides. Assess this against what the positions actually claim. Is the disagreement empirical, with a decider experiment nobody has run yet (and what would it look like — for instance, would a version where subjects press a key at the instant they see the color change, with press times clustering before the second dot appears, discriminate between the hypotheses or just add another contested datum)? Is it conceptual, turning on whether experience has a fact-of-the-matter onset time? Is it verbal, with both sides using "when it was experienced" differently? Or are the camps answering different questions — one about phenomenology, one about the availability of content for report — under one heading? In your diagnosis, say what would count as progress given the structure you find: a distinction to be drawn, an experiment, a formal result about timing and report, or an argument that no progress should be expected because Dennett was right that the question is ill-posed. Please state how confident you are in the diagnosis overall, and single out the one point in your analysis you are least sure of.
