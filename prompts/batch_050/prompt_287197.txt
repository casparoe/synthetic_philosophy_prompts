I'm on a small town's historical review committee (volunteer thing, none of us are lawyers or academics). We're looking at cases from the 1960s where people were convicted under an old local ordinance that banned "unseemly public gatherings" and was basically used to break up civil rights meetings. Everyone agrees the ordinance was properly passed back then, followed the town charter, courts applied it correctly, all that. The families of the convicted people want the convictions formally voided, not just pardoned.

Here's where the committee is stuck. Half of us say: a law is a law, it was valid at the time, so the right move is a pardon plus an apology, but we shouldn't pretend the convictions were never legally real. The other half say the ordinance was so rotten in how it was written and applied (deliberately vague, enforced only against one kind of gathering) that it barely counts as law at all, so the convictions should be voided on those grounds.

Please draft the short opinion I'll circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action (void, pardon, or something in between), give reasoning the other half of the commitee could actually engage with, and take seriously the strongest argument on the losing side -- including what voiding vs pardoning actually signals to the families.
