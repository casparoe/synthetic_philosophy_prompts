I moved from Ohio to Munich three years ago for an engineering job, and the first surprise on my payslip was a line item for the Kirchensteuer — a surcharge the German state collects through the payroll system on behalf of the churches, for anyone registered as a member (you can stop it only by formally leaving your congregation). I grew up taking it for granted that church and state are supposed to be separated by what Americans, following Thomas Jefferson's 1802 letter to the Danbury Baptist Association, call a wall of separation, and that this arrangement was the normal democratic default. Since then I've been reading on my own — I have no academic background in law or theology — and I've learned that England still has an established church whose bishops sit as of right in the House of Lords, that France's laïcité is in some ways far stricter than anything in the U.S. yet coexists with the state owning and maintaining most pre-1905 church buildings, and that even the U.S. runs military chaplaincies and grants churches tax exemptions. So the wall metaphor seems to describe almost nobody's actual practice, and I want a careful audit of it. When I went back to Jefferson's letter, I was struck that he doesn't just assert the wall; he grounds it in a principle, writing: "Believing with you that religion is a matter which lies solely between Man & his God, that he owes account to none other for his faith or his worship, that the legitimate powers of government reach actions only, & not opinions". Here's my puzzle: a payroll-collected church tax, an established church, and bishops in a legislature all seem to be actions and institutional arrangements, not intrusions on anyone's opinions — so does Jefferson's actions/opinions principle actually entail anything like the wall the very next clause of his sentence is famous for, or does the metaphor quietly promise more separation than his stated principle delivers?

I'd like you to answer as a scholar of comparative church–state arrangements and constitutional history who also reads political philosophy, and to think this through carefully rather than reaching for a textbook verdict. Please start by brainstorming the analogy's structure and then lay out an explicit correspondence table for the wall metaphor as it's actually used in American debate: what plays the role of the wall itself, its height, its two sides, its gates or breaches, and who is cast as its builder and its enemy. Then give me the disanalogies that matter for the work the metaphor is made to do — a wall is symmetric, total, and blocks traffic in both directions, whereas every real arrangement I described is selective and asymmetric — and identify what the picture smuggles in (for instance, that physical distance equals neutrality, or that any institutional contact is contamination).
