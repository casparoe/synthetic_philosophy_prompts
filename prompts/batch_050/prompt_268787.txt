Just got done with a huge argument with a friend and I need a referee. There's a pattern we kept circling and I want to know if philosophers have a name for it, or if it's actually three different things we were lumping together.

The pattern: sometimes you get a benefit by being a certain kind of person in advance, but the benfit arrives before the costly part, and nothing forces you to do the costly part when the time comes.

Three cases we fought over:

1. You're dying of thirst in the desert. A driver will only rescue you if he's convinced you're the type who'd pay him $20 once you're safe in town, and he's a great judge of character. Once you're safe, paying gains you nothing and costs you. But unless you really are the type who pays, you die out there.

2. A mom everyone knows would burn her whole life down to take revenge on anyone who hurt her kid. So nobody ever hurts the kid, and she never has to do the ruinous thing. Her willingness to do it is exactly why it stays unnecessary.

3. Someone offers you a million dollars tonight, based on a brain scan or whatever, if you genuinely intend tonight to drink a vile smoothie tomorrow morning. Money's decided tonight; nobody checks what you actually do tomorrow.

So: does this have a name? My friend says 1 is just an ordinary promise and we already understand promises, but I think the weird part is that promising is never the mechanism. And separately: in each case, what's the rational move at the moment of truth? Is "be rational now by being irrational later" a coherent plan, or does it collapse the second you actually try to intend it?
