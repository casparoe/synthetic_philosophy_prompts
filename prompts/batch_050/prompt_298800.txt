In my reading group we recently worked through the first chapter of John Stuart Mill's The Subjection of Women (1869), and two members — I'll call them Sofia and Daniel — fell into a disagreement about a passage that I've been unable to resolve to my own satisfaction. The passage runs:

"Again, in practical matters, the burthen of proof is supposed to be with those who are against liberty; who contend for any restriction or prohibition; either any limitation of the general freedom of human action, or any disqualification or disparity of privilege affecting one person or kind of persons, as compared with others. The à priori presumption is in favour of freedom and impartiality. It is held that there should be no restraint not required by the general good, and that the law should be no respecter of persons, but should treat all alike, save where dissimilarity of treatment is required by positive reasons, either of justice or of policy. But of none of these rules of evidence will the benefit be allowed to those who maintain the opinion I profess. It is useless for me to say that those who maintain the doctrine that men have a right to command and women are under an obligation to obey, or that men are fit for government and women unfit, are on the affirmative side of the question, and that they are bound to show positive evidence for the assertions, or submit to their rejection."

Their exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Sofia argued that this passage contains the whole of Mill's answer to those who justify women's exclusion by appealing to their nature, and that the answer is purely procedural. Mill doesn't claim to know what women's nature is; he reassigns the burden. Defenders of legal disqualification are on the affirmative side and must produce positive evidence, and since everything the nineteenth century believed about women's capacities was observed under conditions of enforced subordination, no such evidence could meet the standard. On her reading, the appeal to nature fails without Mill needing any thesis of his own about what women are naturally like.

(2) Daniel replied that this gives Mill an argument with an expiration date. If the case is only that the burden has not been discharged yet, then better evidence — modern psychology, cross-cultural comparison, the record of women competing on formally equal terms — could discharge it, and Mill's proceduralism would then license the very disparities he opposed. So Mill must be committed to something stronger than burden-shifting: a principled claim that evidence about a group's nature, gathered under conditions that group did not choose, can never justify legal disqualification.

(3) Sofia answered that Daniel had manufactured the expiration date by ignoring what kind of rule Mill states. The presumption is not a temporary evidentiary default but a standing requirement: no restraint not required by the general good, and no dissimilar treatment of kinds of persons absent positive reasons of justice or policy. Even perfect evidence of average differences between the sexes would not be a positive reason bearing on any individual woman, so better evidence could never discharge the burden in the way Daniel imagines. The procedural reading, she said, is stable rather than fragile.

(4) Daniel's final move was to accuse her of smuggling in her own principle. The passage expressly permits dissimilar treatment where "positive reasons, either of justice or of policy" require it, and a demonstrated group difference in average aptitude looks exactly like a reason of policy — so her claim that statistical differences cannot count as positive reasons is a gloss she has added, not something Mill says. If the passage cannot settle that question, he concluded, the burden-of-proof reading is incomplete on its own, and Mill's real answer to the appeal to nature must lie elsewhere in the essay.

I stayed out of this, and I would like an adjudication rather than a tie. Please go through the four moves in order: for each, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or mistaken, and why. Then address the underlying interpretive question: what, exactly, does this passage commit Mill to as a reply to the argument from women's nature — a procedural burden-shift, a principled exclusion of certain kinds of evidence, or neither? If you think the passage is genuinely underdetermined between these readings, say so, and identify what further consideration — from the passage itself, or from elsewhere in the essay, which you are welcome to bring in — would have to settle it. Finally, if both parties are missing something — a distinction neither drew, or a cost each reading carries that neither acknowledged — say what it is.
