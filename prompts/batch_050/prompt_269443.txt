Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against ultimate moral responsibility can be stated compactly: you do what you do because of the way you are -- your character, motives, and values; to be truly morally responsible for what you do, you would have to be truly responsible for the way you are, at least in certain crucial respects; but you could be responsible for the way you are only if you had intentionally brought it about that you are that way, and any such act of self-formation would have proceeded from a prior way of being -- prior principles of choice, prior preferences -- for which you would likewise have to be responsible. The regress never grounds out, so no one is truly morally responsible for anything.

For a seminar paper, I tried to generalize the argument to the epistemic case, and the result seems to prove too much. Here it is with numbered steps:

(1) Strawson's reasoning applies wherever an output issues from a constitution the agent did not choose and could not have chosen.

(2) Believing is such a case. What you believe is fixed by your evidence as processed through your epistemic standards, and you never chose your epistemic standards: you acquired your sense of what counts as a good reason unreflectively, from upbringing and temperament, and any deliberate attempt to revise those standards would have to proceed from standards you already possessed, which launches precisely Strawson's regress in doxastic form.

(3) Therefore no one is epistemically responsible for any belief. The notion that you can be rationally on the hook for what you believe is as illusory, and for the same reason, as the notion that you can be morally on the hook for what you do.

(4) But (3) applies to my belief in Strawson's conclusion, and to each of the beliefs that constitute this very argument, including (1) and (2).

(5) If no one is epistemically responsible for any belief, then nothing I accept can count as rationally compelling for me in the way an argument's conclusion is supposed to be -- the argument, if sound, leaves me with no rational entitlement to believe that it is sound.

(6) So the generalized Basic Argument is self-defeating: it either fails or deprives itself of any claim on our assent, and since the epistemic generalization was just Strawson's reasoning applied one level up, the original argument inherits the problem.

I take it this has gone wrong somewhere, since Strawson plainly intends his argument to be assessed on the merits and believed if the reasoning is good. My question is where. A few candidate diagnoses occur to me: that (2) equivocates between the causes of a belief and its justification; that (3) slides from not being responsible for one's epistemic constitution to one's beliefs lacking rational standing, conflating responsibility with reason-responsiveness; that (5) overreads what losing epistemic responsibility would actually cost. But I have not been able to settle which step is the culprit, or whether fixing the flaw reopens the door for a defender of ordinary moral responsibility to exploit the same fix.

Tell me which step you reject and why it looked fine on first inspection. Then price the rejection: if the flaw lies in the move from unchosen constitution to absent responsibility, does locating it there undercut Strawson's original argument as well, or is there a principled difference between the moral case and the epistemic case that keeps the Basic Argument intact while blocking my generalization? I would rather keep Strawson's conclusion about moral responsibility than keep my epistemic extension of it, so if the two stand or fall together, I want to know that plainly, and what I would have to concede to hold them apart.
