I'm a psychiatry resident who made the mistake of taking a philosophy of mind elective as my one fun credit, and now my roommate (a physics PhD student) has walked me into an argument that seems to prove my entire career is based on an illusion. It goes like this. (1) Every physical event that has a cause at all has a sufficient physical cause -- the physical world is causally closed, that's just what makes science possible. (2) My patient raising her hand, or me wincing at the dentist, are physical events, so each has a sufficient physical cause: nerve signals, motor cortex activity, muscle contractions, the whole chain. (3) But the *feeling* -- the pain, the anxiety, the desire to ask a question -- is not identical to any of that neural machinery. The usual reason given is that pain seems multiply realizable: an octopus or a hypothetical android could be in pain without having our neurons, so pain can't just BE C-fiber firing or whatever. (4) If the neural chain is already sufficient to produce the wince, then the feeling of pain causing the wince *too* would mean the wince was systematically overdetermined, like two bullets hitting the same target at once -- and ordinary events like hand-raisings aren't like that, once is enough. (5) So the pain never causes the wince, and generalizing: no mental state ever causes any physical behavior. (6) Which means talk therapy, informed consent, "she raised her hand because she wanted to ask something" -- all of it describes a causally inert shadow play running alongside the real causation.

I obviously don't want to accept (6). But I also really don't want to give up (1) -- I'm not about to tell my attending that we need to factor in non-physical forces when titrating lithium. And (3) seems well-motivated too; the multiple realizability point felt like one of the few things in the seminar that was actually convincing. So: which step do you think I should reject? Walk me through the standard exits -- I gather there are people who deny (3) and just say the pain IS the brain state, people who accept overdetermination or find some way to say the mental cause isn't really a *competitor* to the physical one, people who mess with what "cause" even means in (4) -- and for each one, tell me what it costs and whether the cost is something a working clinician should actually care about or just a metaphysician's problem. If you think one exit is clearly best, argue for it rather than just surveying.

One more constraint: my roommate insists that any solution that makes mental causation work has to generalize -- it can't just save pain and desire while letting in, I don't know, ghosts or the economy as causal agents floating above their physical bases. If that's a fair demand, explain why; if it's rigged, explain that too.
