I'm a graduate student in philosophy drafting an essay for a seminar on collective wrongdoing, and I want to use Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day as my central case study. The standard philosophical accounts of complicity seem to stumble over Stevens, the butler. On a simple counterfactual picture, you're complicit in a wrong only when your conduct makes a difference to whether it occurs; but Lord Darlington's pro-German maneuvering and his dismissal of the two Jewish housemaids would presumably have happened with or without Stevens's flawless service, so Stevens comes out innocent, which feels wrong. Christopher Kutz's alternative, built around what he calls participatory intention -- the idea that one can be accountable for a wrong simply by intending one's own contribution to a collective project, even a marginal or redundant one -- looks like a better fit, since Stevens clearly understands himself as contributing his share to what Darlington is doing in the world. But I don't want to just slot the novel into the theory as an illustration; I want to know where the theory and the novel actually illuminate each other and where they grind.

Read the novel through this lens, treating me as your audience and thinking carefully through the details of the plot before committing to a reading. Address the hard parts honestly: Stevens never disagrees with Darlington aloud, and his professional code of dignity forbids him from exercising moral judgment at all, so is his participation even intentional in the sense Kutz's account requires, or is he better described as an instrument who has outsourced his agency -- and does the novel's portrait of his self-deception (his evasive retrospective narration, his insistence that he was merely a bystander to events in the great house) support the complicity reading or undermine it by suggesting he never really formed the relevant intention? Consider also whether Miss Kenton, who protests the maids' dismissal but stays, is the more philosophically interesting case, and what the novel's quiet ending -- Stevens resolving to perfect his bantering -- implies about whether complicity of this kind is even the sort of thing one can repent. Finally, say what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that the literature on complicity tends to miss, for instance about the role of deference, professional identity, and self-narration in making participation in wrongdoing psychologically possible.

You are a philosopher who works on ethics and moral responsibility and also takes literary form seriously; be faithful in both directions, neither flattening the novel into a thought experiment nor stretching Kutz's account to fit it. Give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each two to four sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: first, the case that Stevens is complicit on the participatory-intention account; second, the strongest objection to that reading from within the novel; third, the comparison with Miss Kenton; fourth, what the novel's ending does to the complicity verdict; fifth, the single most important thing the novel teaches the philosophical literature.
