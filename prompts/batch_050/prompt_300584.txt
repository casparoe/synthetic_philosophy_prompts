I'm a retired experimental physicist (solid state, mostly) who has gotten into philosophy of science over the last couple of years. For this one, act like a professor of philosophy of science who has taught this debate for ages. Think carefully: before commiting to an answer, brainstorm a few candidate weak points in the argument below, then settle on the one you'd actually reject and defend it.

I've been reading Poincaré's Science and Hypothesis, and this passage from the chapter on hypotheses in physics stopped me cold:

"Experiment only gives us a certain number of isolated points. They must be connected by a continuous line, and this is a true generalisation. But more is done. The curve thus traced will pass between and near the points observed; it will not pass through the points themselves. Thus we are not restricted to generalising our experiment, we correct it."

My reading of this: what science really hands us is the curve — the relations, the structure — while the points themselves, the raw things, stay out of reach. I gather philosophers now call some version of this structural realism. Fine. But my son-in-law (a mathematician, annoyingly good at this) hit me with an argument that seems to prove too much. He says it's an old objection from the 1920s, someone named Newman:

(1) On this view, all a theory really tells us about the world is structural: there are some objects, and there are relations among them satisfying certain conditions — the equations.
(2) A claim of that form — there exist things, and relations on them satisfying conditions C — is made true by any sufficiently large collection of objects, because you can always define or permute relations on a set to satisfy any consistent C. That's just set theory.
(3) So the empirical content of Poincaré's view collapses into a claim about how many things there are, plus pure mathematics.
(4) Worse, two worlds with the same number of objects realize the very same structure, so the view can't even distinguish a world where like charges repel from one where they attract.
(5) So the curve-without-the-points picture is either vacuous or proves too much, and the passage is poetry, not philosophy.

I can't accept (5), but I can't find the slip either. (2) is the step that smells to me, yet he's right that it's a theorem of set theory, so I don't know what denying it even means. Which step do you reject, why did it look airtight, and what does each live repair cost — e.g., insisting the theory latches onto specific relations rather than merely saying some exist, or going further and saying structure is all there is? And be honest: does the curve/points metaphor in the passage survive your fix, or was Poincaré overstating it?
