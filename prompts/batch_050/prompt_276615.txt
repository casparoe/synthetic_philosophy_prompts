Ok, here's something I want for my grad seminar on machine consciousness and moral status. Act as a professional philosopher of mind — someone fluent in the debates about computationalism, substrate independence, and where content comes from — and build me a thought experiment to order. Take your time and think carefully. I'd like you to brainstorm two or three candidate constructions first, tell me briefly why the rejects don't work, and then develop the winner in full.

The case I need: two systems that are, from some moment onward, completely identical in their physical organization and in every computation they run — same inputs, same processing, same outputs, for years — but one of them arrived at that organization the normal way, through learning and development, and the other was manufactured directly in its final state, with all the "memories," dispositions, and internal structure loaded in at once, with none of the history that produced them in the first system. The case should force a verdict on whether the second system suffers (or enjoys, or matters at all) when it behaves exactly as if it does.

Specs:

- No cosmic accident. The state-loaded system has to be built deliberately, with technology that's a plausable extrapolation of current stuff — whole-brain-emulation-adjacent is fine. No lightning striking a swamp, no freak quantum events.
- No gradual neuron-by-neuron replacement. Fading-qualia cases are a different debate and I don't want to relitigate it.
- Don't stipulate the verdict. The case has to generate the disagreement out of the theories, not assert that one side is right.
- Keep the scenario itself to a couple of paragraphs.

Then the demonstration:

1. State the verdict a straightforward functionalist or computationalist gives about the state-loaded system, and why.
2. State the verdict of a view on which what a state means — or whether it has phenomenal character at all — depends partly on how the system came to be the way it is, and why that view denies or at least downgrades the second system's moral status. I don't need you to defend that view, just run it fairly and steelman it.
3. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing all the work, and what happens to the verdicts if I remove it. For instance: the state-loaded system then goes on to have ten years of its own ordinary history. Does the history-view's verdict change, and if so, when exactly — and doesn't that answer commit it to something odd?
4. Finish with the best counterattack from whichever side comes out looking worse: where would they say the thought experiment is an intuition pump rather than an argument, and is that charge fair?

Context, so you know what counts as cheating: I want this for a unit on whether the question of which computations matter morally can be answered by looking only at the computation, or whether you always end up smuggling in facts about origin, embedding in an environment, or how the system is interpreted. So the case is only useful to me if the divergence between the two verdicts is clean. If you think no construction can make the divergence clean without stipulating it somewhere, then don't force it — say that instead and explain exactly where the smuggling becomes unavoidable, becuase honestly that would be just as useful for the seminar.
