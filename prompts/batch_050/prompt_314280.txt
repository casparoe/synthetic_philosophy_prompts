I run a small grad-student podcast on philosophy of language (very niche, maybe 40 listeners, mostly my own cohort and one guy who keeps emailing corrections). Next week's guest is a philosopher of language who defends what I'd call the prevalence-plus-normality account of generics: sentences like "tigers are striped" or "birds fly" are true roughly when the property holds of enough normal members of the kind, with the tolerance for exceptions handled by that "normal members" clause. She's published a few papers on it and she's clearly done the interview circuit, so I want to skip the usual soft opening where she just explains the view for ten minutes while I nod.

I'd like seven or eight questions that actually put pressure on it. Somewhere in the set I want to hit the asymmetry between "mosquitoes carry malaria" (only a tiny fraction of mosquitoes actually carry malaria, but the generic sounds true) and "books are paperbacks" (most books are paperbacks, but the generic sounds false), and push on whether a prevalence-based semantics can deliver that contrast without quietly smuggling in extra machinery. I also want at least one question on "Canadians are right-handed" -- statistically true of the population, yet a terrible generic -- since that seems to me the single hardest case for any quasi-statistical story, and one on "ducks lay eggs," given that only female ducks lay eggs and the theory needs some principled way to fix the comparison class. Plus one question on the social side: the evidence that generics are acquired early and slide easily into prejudice, and whether her semantics should have anything to say about that or whether it's purely a psychology problem she can wave off.

For each question, give me the reply she'd most likely give, and then a follow-up that makes that reply harder to sustain. Flag for each one where her likely answer is weakest, i.e. where a listener should prick up thier ears. End the set with a question forcing her to name some evidence, example, or experimental result that would actually make her abandon the account, and then close with your own honest assessment of which questions you expect to land and which you're least confident about -- where your reconstruction of her position, or your guess about how she'd respond, is most likely to be off.
