A colleague argues that the old analogy "the brain is a computer" settles the case for mind uploading. His reasoning: a computer's behavior is fully determined by the computations it runs, not by whether those computations are realized in vacuum tubes, transistors, or clockwork; so if the brain is a computer, scanning it and running the same computation in silicon just is the same mind, and objections about embodiment or biology confuse the message with the medium. Audit this argument by auditing the analogy. Lay out explicitly what maps onto what (neurons, synapses, action potentials, memories, attention, and the rest), and identify where the mapping strains: the brain has no clean hardware/software separation, no stored program distinct from the machine that runs it, and no clock, and its "wiring" is itself continuously rewritten by activity. Then address the deeper worry: whether "performs computations" is an intrinsic property of a system, as being made of neurons is, or a description we impose from outside -- in which case almost any sufficiently complex physical process counts as running almost any computation, and the analogy licenses too much. For each disanalogy you raise, say whether it is merely inconvenient detail or actually load-bearing for the uploading conclusion, and state whether the analogy, suitably repaired, can still support a weaker claim worth keeping.
