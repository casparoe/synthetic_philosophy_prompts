I am a retired applied statistician -- forty years in agricultural field trials and, latterly, climate-model intercomparison -- who has lately been working through the literature on diachronic rationality, and I keep running aground on van Fraassen's Reflection principle and the debate it has generated. As I understand it, the principle says roughly that my present credence in a proposition, conditional on my credence at some future time taking a particular value, should equal that value -- that I ought to defer to my anticipated future opinion in much the way I would defer to a trusted expert. I have also gathered that it is defended by Dutch-book-style arguments and attacked by counterexamples involving foreseen memory loss, expected impairment, and situations where one rationally expects one's future self to be less reasonable rather than more. What I would like is for you to restate the whole debate -- the principle itself, the betting arguments for it, and the standard counterexamples -- in the vocabulary I actually think in: estimators, conditional expectations, calibration, proper scoring rules, sequential updating, and the reliability of a measurement instrument. Treat my future self, if you like, as an instrument whose readings I will receive tomorrow and whose calibration I must assess today. Do this as faithfully as you can, and do not quietly import assumptions from the philosophical framing and pass them off as part of the translation.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the debate translate cleanly -- I suspect the Dutch-book defences have a tidy expression in terms of scoring rules, but I want to see whether that suspicion survives scrutiny -- and which parts have to be bent to fit the statistical idiom? Most importantly, is there anything the philosophers are arguing about that has no counterpart at all in the language of estimation and calibration? I have two candidate answers in mind and would like you to adjudicate between them: either my vocabulary simply lacks the resources to express something real (a norm of self-trust across time, perhaps, or a question about what I may rationally intend for my future self, which is not obviously a question about any estimator), or the residue is a sign that part of the philosophical dispute is less substantive than it appears -- a disagreement about words that evaporates once everything is stated in terms of expected loss. I would rather be told that one of these is wrong than receive a diplomatic both-and.
