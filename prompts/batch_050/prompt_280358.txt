I'm in a college debate club and last week we were arguing about whether history degrees should be mandatory for diplomats or something like that, and someone ended the whole discussion by dropping "those who cannot remember the past are condemned to repeat it" like it was checkmate. Everyone nodded and we moved on, but the more I think about it the less I know what that's even claiming. Like, is it saying the past literally repeats? That doesn't seem true. Is it saying forgetting causes you to screw up? Plenty of people who know tons of history still make terrible calls, and the famous bad decisions usually get made by people invoking historical analogies too confidently, if anything.

Can you unpack what precise claims that sentence could actually be making, from the most defensible to the most out there? I want something like: a reading where it's basically a tautology (if you forget you did X and you do X again, that's 'repeating'), a reading where it's a causal-psychological claim about memory and decision-making, a reading where it's a claim about history having patterns that recur unless actively resisted, a strong cyclical-history reading, and whatever else you can find. For each one say whether it's trivial, plausible-but-needes-evidence, or just false, and what the mechanism would even have to be for it to work. And then the part I actually care about: which of these readings would someone need the slogan to mean for it to work as a conversation-ender about history education policy? Because I suspect the slogan only sounds decisive because it slides between a weak true reading and a strong dubious one, but I can't quite pin down the slide.
