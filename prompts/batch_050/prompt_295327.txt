In many cities, a property owner who wants to build apartments on land she owns must survive discretionary hearings at which current neighbors may object, and objections invoking neighborhood character, shadows, or parking routinely defeat projects that already comply with the written zoning code. Yet none of those neighbors needed the block's consent to move in, and the same municipalities frequently declare in their general plans that housing is a human right. What conception of ownership, and of standing to exclude, does this practice presuppose — that buying a home silently purchases a share in everyone else's land, that duration of residence confers authority newcomers lack, or that incumbents hold legitimate expectations a market transaction cannot unsettle? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether it can be made coherent alongside a right to housing, and describe what the approval process would have to look like if we instead held that ownership confers rights over one's own parcel alone — including whether a city could coherently unbundle the right to object from the land itself, say by auctioning objection rights separately, and what that auction would reveal about what neighbors were actually being sold all along.
