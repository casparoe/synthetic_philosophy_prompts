A colleague and I disagree about whether compatriots have genuine special claims. The precise proposition under dispute: in a moderately wealthy democracy, it is permissible for the state to give substantially more weight to the welfare of its own citizens than to comparably needy foreigners when allocating discretionary resources -- beyond what any fair global principle would license. She puts the probability that this is true at around 0.85; I put it around 0.3.

Give me your own credence, but decompose the reasoning rather than just stating a number. I'd like rough probabilities for at least: (a) whether co-membership in shared coercive institutions grounds associative duties independently of any general duty of justice; (b) whether, granting such duties exist, they can justify the magnitude of priority actually exercised by wealthy states, or only a much weaker tie-breaking priority; (c) whether the empirical premise embedded in the dispute holds -- that priority to compatriots and aid to foreigners genuinely trade off against each other, rather than both being expandable; and (d) whether the question has a determinate answer at all, or whether "special claims" talk conceals several distinct claims with different truth values. State how the terms combine, which single term your final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or evidence would move that term most.

Throughout, separate what is broadly agreed among political philosophers who work on this (e.g., that some special obligations to family or friends are uncontroversial) from what is genuinely contested between partialists and cosmopolitans, and don't present either camp's framework as the consensus view. Finally, given our two credences of 0.85 and 0.3, tell me which sub-question my colleague and I most likely actually disagree about.
