I teach philosophy of mind and language at a UK university, and I keep running into the same conversational move. Most recent instance: at a workshop on AI and assessment, a colleague shut down the question of whether a chatbot's confident wrong answer counted as a *mistake*, as opposed to just a low-probability string of tokens, by saying "it's just predicting the next word, there's no belief in there to be mistaken about anything." The room nodded and moved on, and I sat there thinking that one adverb, "just", was doing an enormous ammount of unexamined work.

The slogan comes in variants — "it's just autocomplete", "it's just statistics on text", the "stochastic parrot" line — but they all seem to lean on one fact about how these systems are trained and then draw wildly different conclusions from it. What I'd like from you:

First, lay out as a numbered menu the distinct precise claims the slogan could be making. Off the top of my head I can dimly make out at least: a claim about the training objective; a claim about the internal mechanism (pattern completion "all the way down", nothing else going on); a claim about grounding (no causal connection between the symbols and the world, hence no meaning); a claim about normativity (no correctness conditions internal to the system, so literally incapable of error as opposed to mere statistical deviance); and possibly a deflationary claim about what "understanding" even requires. But I suspect there are more, and I suspect some of these collapse into each other on inspection.

Second, for each reading: is it trivially true, substantive but defensible, or just false? And — this is the bit I actually need — which readings, if any, would support my colleague's move? Does any true premise about the training objective get you to "therefore it can't be mistaken"? I have a feeling the normativity reading is the only one that even comes close, and that it's the least established of the lot, but I want that argued rather than vibesed.

Third, please be careful about the epistemic status of what you say. Some of these questions are empirical and live — whether these models build internal representations that track features of the world is something interpretability researchers actively argue about — and some are properly conceptual. Mark clearly what is settled (e.g. what the training objective roughly is) versus what is genuinely contested among people who work on this stuff, and don't present a contested position, in either direction, as the consensus. I've read too many pieces that treat "they have world models" and "they're mere parrots" each as the obviously established fact, sometimes in the same paragraph.

Feel free to end with a verdict on when the slogan is doing honest work versus purely rhetorical work. Numbered menu, verdicts attached, maybe 800 words.
