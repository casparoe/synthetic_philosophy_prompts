I've come to accept a moderate adaptationism: that for most phenotypic traits of interest to biologists, treating the trait as if it were an approximately optimal solution to a design problem posed by natural selection is the best default research strategy -- not because every trait is an adaptation, but because the strategy generates testable, fruitful hypotheses even when the adaptationist answer turns out false. I take the 1979 spandrels critique by Gould and Lewontin seriously, but I suspect much of it targets a straw version of the programme, and I note that its standard illustrations (drift, pleiotropy, developmental constraint, architectural byproducts) are things a sophisticated adaptationist can absorb without abandoning the methodology. What would count against my position? I want a map of its exposure: which empirical findings in evolutionary genetics would genuinely undermine it rather than merely qualify it; whether the historical success of neutral theory at the molecular level is a defeater that generalizes to phenotypes or a special case that leaves the programme untouched; whether the fact that optimality models test a conjunction (selection pressures plus assumed constraints plus a currency of fitness) makes adaptationism unfalsifiable in practice, and whether that is a vice for a methodology rather than a theory; and whether the distinction between adaptationism as an empirical claim about nature, an explanatory claim about what biology should explain, and a methodological claim about how to do research changes what would count against each. Which of these objections do the programme's own defenders concede as real risks, and which would they dismiss as misdirected? Present your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, ordered from most to least decisive, with no introduction or conclusion.
