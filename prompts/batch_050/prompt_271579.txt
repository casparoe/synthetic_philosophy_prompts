So I had this huge argument with my mate Dan last night that carried on way past closing time and neither of us budged an inch. It started from something daft: our neighbour's hedge died over the summer while she was away, and Dan said the neighbour "killed it" by not arranging for anyone to water it. I said nobody killed it, it just died, because not doing something isn't doing anything. Then it spiralled.

His position, roughly: omissions can be proper causes. If a lifeguard clocks a drowning kid and carries on eating his sandwich, he caused that death as surely as if he'd held the kid under. The hedge case is the same shape, just less dramatic.

My position, roughly: causes are things that happen, stuff transferring energy or whatever. An absence can't be in a causal chain because there's nothing there to be in it. The lifeguard is morally monstrous, sure, but the water killed the kid. When I say "his omission caused it" I'm really just saying "he should have acted and didn't", which is a normative judgement wearing causal clothes.

Here's where it got properly heated, because we both have cases we're stuck with:

- I think the gardener who forgets to water your plant causes its death, but *I* don't cause your plant's death by also not watering it (I'm a stranger three streets away). Dan says that distinction is just social convention and has no metaphysical backbone, and I couldn't really answer that.
- Dan has to bite the bullet that every starving person on earth was "killed" by me not donating more, which he squirmed about but eventually accepted, which I think is a reductio of his whole view.
- We both agreed the signalman who doesn't pull the lever causes the crash, but disagreed about whether that's a counterexample to me (Dan's view) or just loose everyday talk (my view).
- Neither of us knew what to say about "double prevention" type cases, like when I don't lock the gate and Dan doesn't close it either and the dog gets out. Two omissions, one outcome. Who caused it?

Write a dialogue between two philosophers -- one defending each of our positions -- who pick up exactly where Dan and I left off. Both should be sharp and neither should be a pushover. They have to actually engage with each other's cases, not just restate their opening positions at each other: I want the omission-causation defender to take the "absences don't do anything physically" objection seriously, and I want the skeptic to give a real answer to the signalman case that isn't just shrugging and saying "common speech is sloppy". At least once, one of them should concede a point and adjust. And I don't want a cosy reconciliation at the end -- let them locate the actual crux of the disagreement (whether causation is purely a matter of what physically happens, or partly depends on what's normal or expected) and leave it honestly unresolved, with each of them clear about what it would cost them to give in.
