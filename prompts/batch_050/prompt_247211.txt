People argue about sortition (randomly selected citizen assemblies, like juries but for legislation) versus term limits versus just keeping career politicians, and every argument seems to assume a different answer to the prior question: what is the job of a political selection method in the first place?

Here's what I want from you. Act as a professional political philosopher who's also comfortable thinking like an engineer. Before you write the actual answer, do a short brainstorming pass where you list candidate requirements freely, then pick and organize. Think carefully, dont just pattern-match to the standard talking points.

Then write me the desiderata list: what must any method of choosing who makes political decisions actually deliver to count as adequate? I mean things like:

- the failure modes it has to guard against (capture by organized interests is the obvious one, but I bet there are others that get less airtime, like the selection method itself becoming a prize worth capturing, or competence being selected *out* over time)
- the cases it has to handle -- a polity where most citizens are disengaged, one where a faction is willing to break the rules, whatever else you think belongs
- the distinctions it can't collapse, e.g. between representing people's *preferences* and representing their *interests*, or between being responsive and being good
- which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable versus which are just the hobby horses of one camp (democratic-theory people vs. public-choice people vs. deliberative-democracy people, I gather these camps don't agree on much)

For each requirement, give the motivating case or consideration behind it, not just the label. And here's the part I most want: flag any pairs or subsets of the desiderata that you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, and say why. In chemistry we called that a constraint satisfaction problem and the infeasible region was often the interesting part. Then, briefly, score the three contenders I mentioned -- sortition, term limits, career elected officials -- against the list. Keep the scoring short; the criteria are the deliverable.
