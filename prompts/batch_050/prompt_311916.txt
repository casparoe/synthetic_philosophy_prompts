I am a philosophy professor preparing a funding application to a university grant program for a twelve-month project on artificial agency and welfare, and I need a two-page research proposal. My working idea, still half-formed, is this: most philosophical discussion of whether reinforcement learning agents could suffer fixates on the reward signal itself, treating negative reward as the obvious candidate for pain. But in the actual architecture of these systems, reward is only one of several functionally distinct quantities — there is also the learned value function (the agent's expectation of future reward) and the prediction error (the discrepancy between expected and received reward), and these come apart systematically: an agent can receive negative reward while experiencing positive prediction error, and vice versa. My suspicion is that the question "can RL agents suffer?" has no single answer because it conflates at least three candidate correlates of valence, and that the philosophically interesting question is which correlate, if any, plays the functional role that makes suffering matter morally — with the further wrinkle that something like suffering might attach not to any signal but to a structural condition, such as a persistent negative prediction error the agent's policy cannot reduce.
