I'm a software engineer with zero philosophy training and I got sucked into the rule-following paradox — the Kripke/Wittgenstein thing where everything you've ever computed is consistent with you having meant quus all along, a function that agrees with addition on every case you've actually tried but diverges on bigger inputs. It maps wierdly well onto my day job: any finite set of input-output pairs fits infinitely many functions, so what fact makes it the case that someone means one function rather than another? From what I can tell, philosophers have been stuck on this for 40-odd years. Simple dispositionalism got taken apart fast (your dispositions are finite, and they include your mistakes, so the function can't just be read off them), and since then there's a shelf of rival moves — skeptical solutions, non-reductionism, quietism — with, as far as I can see, nobody conceding anything.

I'm not asking who's right. I want a diagnosis of why it's stalled. My hunch: the camps aren't disagreeing about a fact, they're disagreeing about what would even count as an answer — one side demands that meaning be grounded in non-semantic facts, the other thinks that demand is itself the mistake. But maybe it's actually a covert empirical question (about what fixes dispositions, idk) or just two bedrock intuitions that can't be argued for. Tell me what kind of disagreement this really is, based on what the positions actually claim, and what would count as movement — a new distinction, some formal result, a reframed question — or argue that nothing would move it and say why.
