The Door They Closed: How Farming Abolished the Right to Leave

The debate over whether the agricultural revolution was a catastrophe usually runs along familiar lines. One side points to the ethnographic record: many foraging peoples work fewer hours than farmers or industrial workers, eat a more varied diet, and suffer less from epidemic disease, which led the anthropologist Marshall Sahlins to describe hunter-gatherers as the original affluent society. The other side replies that foragers faced high infant mortality, chronic food insecurity, and violent death at rates modern readers would find intolerable, and that farming, for all its costs, made possible the population densities on which civilization, science, and medicine eventually depended. Jared Diamond's well-known characterization of agriculture as humanity's worst mistake captures one pole of this dispute.

I want to suggest that both sides are arguing about the wrong variable. Calories, leisure hours, and even disease burden are, in the long sweep, secondary. The deepest transformation agriculture wrought was not dietary or epidemiological but political: it abolished fission as a solution to conflict, and thereby created the conditions in which coercive hierarchy became nearly inevitable.

Consider how disputes end in a foraging band. Mobile peoples own little, store little, and can relocate at low cost. When a conflict festers — over a marriage, a hunting territory, a bully's demands — the losing party has an option unavailable to most people since: they can simply leave, taking their kin and their labor with them. Ethnographers of foraging societies describe groups that split and recombine constantly, and this fluidity is not merely sociological trivia. It is a discipline on power. A would-be dominator in a fissioning society is a general whose army can walk away overnight. Authority remains weak because exit remains cheap.

Agriculture forecloses this option. The farmer's wealth is sunk into the ground: cleared fields, irrigation channels, stored grain, fruit trees that take years to bear. To leave is to be ruined. And so, for the first time, large numbers of people had to settle their disputes in place, among neighbors they could not escape. Someone had to adjudicate the boundary quarrel, allocate the water, protect the granary from theft, and organize the harvest labor. Adjudication requires authority, authority requires enforcement, and enforcement requires an enforcement class that does not grow its own food. Hierarchy, on this view, was not a luxury that surplus permitted, nor a conspiracy that elites imposed. It was a conflict-resolution technology for populations that had lost the ability to walk away from one another.

This reframing has, I think, a surprising implication. The standard story says inequality began with storable surplus, because surplus gave the strong something to seize. But surplus alone does not explain why anyone stayed to be dominated. Grain in a storehouse is only taxable if the taxpayer cannot leave. What agriculture really produced was not wealth but captivity — a population whose exit costs had suddenly become prohibitive. Surplus was the prize; sunk costs were the cage.

If this is right, it also suggests a coda for the present. For most of recorded history, the state has rested on the immobility of its subjects: serfs tied to land, citizens tied to territory, workers tied to factories. But exit costs are falling again in pockets of the modern economy. The remote worker, the footloose freelancer, and the firm that can relocate its headquarters with a lease negotiation all recover a fragment of the forager's leverage — the credible threat to leave. One might predict that institutions facing newly mobile constituents will behave, in small ways, more like foraging bands and less like grain states: flatter, more consensual, more anxious to please. Whether that prediction is borne out is a question I leave for another essay.
