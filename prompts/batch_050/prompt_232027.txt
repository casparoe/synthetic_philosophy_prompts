I teach game theory to philosophy graduate students and am comfortable with the standard apparatus: extensive- and normal-form representations, common knowledge of rationality, equilibrium refinements, correlated equilibrium, and the team-reasoning literature associated with Bacharach and Sugden. What I have never been able to see clearly is how the leading analytic accounts of collective intentionality — Bratman's shared intention built out of interlocking and mutually responsive individual intentions, Gilbert's joint commitment and the plural subject it constitutes, and Searle's claim that we-intentions are a primitive, irreducible mode of intentionality — relate to that apparatus.

So here is my request. Restate each of these three accounts, one by one, in the vocabulary of game theory, as faithfully as you can: players, strategies, information structures, common knowledge, equilibrium concepts, and whatever team-reasoning machinery you need. For each account, identify which of its central claims translate cleanly (for instance, whether Bratman's meshing subplans amount to something like coordination on an equilibrium plus stability conditions), which translate only under distortion, and which have no expression in that vocabulary at all. Then give me the residue report. I am especially interested in three candidates for residue: first, Gilbert's insistence that parties to a joint commitment acquire directed obligations toward one another and standing to rebuke defectors, which looks like more than the disutility of a failed coordination; second, Searle's irreducibility claim, which I suspect either collapses into a claim about how intentions are formed or asserts something a game representation cannot even state; third, the apparent asymmetry between genuinely collective action and mere strategic interdependence — whether that asymmetry survives once you have common knowledge and team reasoning on the table.

Finally, render a verdict on the residue. Where the game-theoretic vocabulary fails to express a philosophical claim, is that because the vocabulary is missing something real — a notion of obligation, of commitment, of agency — or because the philosophical claim is doing less work than its proponents suppose? I want an honest translation, not a reduction smuggled in under the guise of one: if you think Gilbert is tracking something that simply cannot be said in terms of payoffs and strategies, say so and say what it is.
