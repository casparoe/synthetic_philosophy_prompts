I am a retired physical chemist, and in my retirement I have been working my way through some philosophy of language and metaphysics, where I have run into a problem that strikes me as strangely familiar from my old life: the problem of the unity of the proposition, together with Bradley's regress. As I understand it, the difficulty is that a list of constituents — say, Socrates, mortality — is not yet the proposition that Socrates is mortal, so something must unify them; but if the unifier is itself one more constituent (a relation, a copula), then one seems to need a further unifier to bind that unifier to the original constituents, and so on indefinitely. Frege's notion of an unsaturated or incomplete function, Russell's various attempts with relating relations, and Wittgenstein's idea that the constituents hang together in a structure like links in a chain all look like attempts to stop this regress. What I would like is a careful restatement of this whole debate in the vocabulary I actually command: atoms and bonds, valence, molecular graphs, potential energy surfaces, the difference between a mere mixture and a compound, and the way structural formulas represent connectivity. A molecule, after all, is not a heap of atoms plus a bond sitting next to them like a third object; the chemist's structural formula does not treat the bond as one more atom requiring further bonds of its own. So please translate the problem, and each of the main proposed solutions, into that chemical idiom as faithfully as you can — for instance, whether Frege's unsaturatedness corresponds to open valence, whether Wittgenstein's picture corresponds to a molecular graph, and so on.

The part I care about most is the residue report. Which elements of the philosophical problem translate cleanly into chemical terms, which survive only under distortion, and which have no chemical counterpart at all? I can see one obvious candidate for a mismatch already: a methane molecule has no truth-value and makes no claim, whereas a proposition does, so whatever gives a proposition its assertoric or representational character may simply fall outside the chemical analogy. But I would like to know whether there are deeper mismatches — whether, for example, the chemist's confidence that no regress arises in bonding rests on something (the bond being a feature of the electronic structure rather than a component) that the philosopher could also say, or whether instead the chemical case dissolves the problem only because chemistry never had to answer the question philosophy is really asking. At the end, please give me your honest verdict: does the translation show that the unity problem was a confusion that good structural thinking dissolves, or does the residue reveal that my chemical intuitions are missing precisely the thing that makes the problem hard?

One request about format: please answer in continuous prose, as one would write a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables. I find that lists encourage a false sense of parallelism between items, and this is exactly the kind of question where the interesting work happens in the transitions between ideas.
