A friend of mine has been wielding what he calls a one-line refutation of liberal constitutions, built out of Sen's liberal paradox, and I can't find the step I'm actually willing to deny. His version:

(1) Sen's theorem shows that any collective decision rule satisfying weak Pareto and minimal liberty -- just two people, each decisive over one purely personal choice -- runs into inconsistency.

(2) A constitution is a decision rule chosen before anyone's actual preferences are known, so it has to work for every possible preference profile, not just the profiles people happen to end up with. That's just what the unrestricted domain condition demands, and constitutions are ex ante devices by nature.

(3) So a constitution guaranteeing both Pareto-acceptability and even minimal personal rights is defective as a rule, full stop -- whether or not anyone in the actual society turns out to be nosy about other peoples reading habits.

(4) Sen's own escape hatch -- that the paradox only bites under "meddlesome" preferences, so liberal citizens should be educated out of caring about each other's private choices -- therefore doesn't help. You can't patch a rule-level inconsistency by hoping for nice preferences, since the rule has to be settled before the preferences arrive.

(5) Real constitutions do entrench both. So real constitutions are incoherent not contingently (on the days people happen to be meddlesome) but necessarily, as rules.

(6) Hence either we drop Pareto even as an ideal -- accept that society may sometimes impose outcomes every single person dislikes -- or we drop entrenched rights. No third option.

I keep wanting to say the trouble hides somewhere between (2) and (4), but when I push there he says a guarantee that fails in some possible world is no guarantee at all, and constitution-writing is precisely the buisness of guarantees. Which step do I actually have to give up, and what does giving it up cost? I'd really like to keep both entrenched personal rights and some form of the Pareto principle if that's at all possible.
