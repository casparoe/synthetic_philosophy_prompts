everyone in constitutional theory talks about constitutions as Ulysses tied to the mast. you know the story: he knows the sirens will drive him mad, so he has the crew plug their ears with wax, tie him up, and ignore his orders to untie him no matter how much he begs. the line people draw is: a society at its founding, in its lucid moment, entrenches certain rules -- rights, amendment procedures, debt limits, whatever -- precisely so that later, when the political equivalent of siren song kicks in (moral panics, charismatic demagogues, wartime fear, inflation temptations), the society can't do what it will desperately want to do. and this gets used to answer the dead hand objection: the complaint that entrenched constitutions let long-dead founders rule the living is supposedly dissolved, because binding yourself isn't being ruled by someone else, it's rational self-government over time.

i'm skeptical the analogy is doing honest work here and i want a proper audit. give me the correspondence table first: who is Ulysses, who are the sailors with the wax, what's the mast, what's the song, what's Circe's advance warning, what are the ropes (and who's allowed to check the knots). then the disanalogies, and i specifically want you to sort them into the ones that kill the argumentative use versus the ones that are just pedantic. the ones i suspect matter: Ulysses binds his *own* future self whose underlying goals stay fixed (he wants to get home before, during, and after -- only his judgment is briefly scrambled), whereas a founding generation binds *other people* whose values may legitimately drift; Ulysses knows exactly what threat he's binding against and roughly how long it lasts, whereas constitutional designers bind against unknown threats for centuries; and the crew can be trusted to keep the wax in because they're on the same boat with the same destination, which is a strange thing to say about judges.

also: does the analogy secretly build in the answer to the hard question? like, by framing the bound party as "the same person, temporarily irrational," does it smuggle in the claim that later majorities wanting change are always the siren-addled version and never the lucid one? what would the story look like from the perspective of a later generation that thinks *it* is Ulysses-in-his-right-mind and the founders were the ones hearing things?

finally: if the mast story can't carry the legitimacy of strong entrenchment, is there a better analogy in the neighborhood? i keep reaching for something like a psychiatric advance directive (patient precommits to treatment against a future incompetent self) or a one-way ratchet or a slow thermostat, but each of those has its own problems -- tell me which comes closest and what it gets right that the ship doesn't, or whether the whole family of self-binding metaphors is the wrong frame for what constitutions do.
