You are a philosopher with expertise in applied ethics, brought on as an advisor to a mid-sized technology company whose product line includes "digital afterlife" services: chatbots and voice interfaces that simulate deceased people, built from their texts, emails, voice recordings, and social media posts. Our grief counseling partners, our legal team, and our engineering leads keep arguing past one another, so I have been asked to chair a standing review board that will approve or reject each proposed griefbot product, roughly two or three cases a month. What I need from you is a decision procedure the board can actually run on a proposal, one that operationalizes what the ethics of simulating the dead has to say — posthumous consent and what counts as a reasonable substitute for it, the interests the dead might retain in how they are represented, the psychological effects on the bereaved (both the comfort of continued bonds and the risks of dependency, distorted memory, and stalled grieving), and the difference between a memorial and a simulation that claims to *be* the person.

Think carefully before you write, and begin with a short brainstorm of the distinct moral concerns a griefbot can raise, since the procedure should be organized around them. Then give me the procedure itself: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a rejection, and where it must hand the decision back to the board because the question is one of values rather than one the checklist can settle. Be explicit about which distinctions have to survive the simplification — for instance, consent given before death versus consent inferred by family members, an adult user who commissioned the bot for herself versus children who did not choose it, a static archive with a face versus a generative model that will say things the person never said — and which details of the underlying theory can safely be dropped for practical use. The board has lay members, about ninety minutes per case, and the authority to require design changes (disclaimers, usage limits, data deletion options) as a middle path between approval and rejection.

Finally, run the procedure on three cases, including at least one where it delivers an answer our marketing department will dislike: (1) a widow who wants a chatbot of her late husband, trained on twenty years of their private messages, for her own use; (2) the estate of a recently deceased, moderately famous musician that wants to license an interactive voice avatar fans can talk to, with revenue shared between the estate and our company; (3) a grieving father who wants a bot of his teenage daughter, who died suddenly, built partly from her social media and partly from fabricated personality details he would supply — and her surviving sister, who is sixteen, objects. Close by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do and where it is most likely to misfire.
