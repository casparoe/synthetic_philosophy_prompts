I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine, and next month I've agreed to give a forty-minute talk plus Q&A at the philosophy meetup that meets in the basement of the public library — mostly sharp amateurs, a couple of grad students, nobody who's read the wellbeing literature systematically. I want to build the whole talk around tranquilism: roughly, the view that what ultimately matters is only the absence of craving or disturbed wanting, so that a state of untroubled contentment is fully as good as a state of bliss, because whatever seems better about the bliss is really just the relief of some craving. I got into an argument about this with my editor over coffee last week and realized I couldn't actually explain why the view isn't just sadness with extra steps, which seems like a bad sign. The audience will arrive assuming that wellbeing talk means happiness talk, so the talk lives or dies on how I get them from there to the idea that pleasure might only ever matter as the removal of a lack.

Don't give me the talk itself — plan it for me. What has to be in place before the central claim is even intelligible rather than just bleak-sounding (my guess is the distinction between pleasure and the relief of a desire has to come first, but maybe something even earlier?), and in what order should the pieces go, with the reason each depends on the last. Give me the one example that should carry the whole thing: I was thinking of contrasting a blissful but craving-riddled person with a serenely contented one, but tell me if that's the wrong vehicle, or what the example needs to include and exclude so it doesn't smuggle the conclusion in. Then map the objections they'll raise in the order they'll acually raise them — my predictions are: it's just rebranded Buddhism; so being numb or depressed is the ideal?; it implies nobody should bother with fun or with having kids — and say exactly where in the sequence each one should be disarmed rather than left for Q&A. Finally, one question I can put to the room at the end that shows whether they got the distinction rather than just absorbed a mood, and what I should deliberately leave out for this audience (the population-ethics implications seem like a trap, but correct me).
