I am a novelist, and I have come to suspect that my profession and the philosopher's are running the same con with different packaging. Philosophers present short fictions -- a violinist wired to a stranger's kidneys, a runaway trolley, a color scientist who has never seen red -- and treat the reader's response to them as evidence; I present long fictions and treat the reader's response as the point. Before I finish a book in which several set-piece scenes are designed to function as extended thought experiments, I want to know what I should be asking of a theory of how such devices work on a mind. Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of how thought experiments (and, by extension, persuasive fictions) alter belief has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

The list should cover, at minimum: which canonical episodes it must get right, including both the successes (Galileo on falling bodies, Einstein's trains and elevators) and the suspected swindles (where a vivid narrative seems to smuggle in the very assumption it claims to test); which distinctions it cannot collapse, such as between a thought experiment that generates new information and one that merely rearranges what the reader already believed, or between an intuition elicited and an intuition endorsed on reflection; what it must say about the role of narrative particulars -- names, faces, order of presentation -- that logic says should be inert but experience says are not; how it must accommodate the embarrassing fact that framing, wording, and even the reader's native language shift verdicts on famous cases; and what it predicts about disagreement, since a device whose output varies across audiences can hardly be a neutral measuring instrument. For each requirement, say why it belongs on the list and what would count against a theory that failed it.

Then mark each desideratum as non-negotiable or as a prejudice of a particular camp -- I expect, for instance, that "must reduce to an argument reconstructible in premises and conclusions" is a demand of one school rather than a datum. If some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable -- if, say, respecting the experimental philosophy results while preserving the evidential force of the classic cases cannot both be done -- say so plainly, since that would itself be the most useful finding. Finally, tell me which single desideratum does the most work in excluding candidate accounts, and score the main families of views briefly against the list: the picture of thought experiments as disguised arguments, the picture of them as direct apprehension of possibility, and the picture of them as literary persuasion wearing a lab coat. Keep the scoring terse; the specification is what I will actually build the book on.
