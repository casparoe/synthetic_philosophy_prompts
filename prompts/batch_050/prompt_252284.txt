Something that's been bugging me since my philosophy of biology seminar: the instructor keeps ending arguments by saying that good science has to "carve nature at its joints." Last week she used it to shut down my question about whether the DSM's category of depression is a real category or just a useful bureaucratic label, and I've seen the same line deployed about Pluto losing planethood, about whether race is a biological category, about "species" themselves. But I honestly cant figure out what the slogan actually commits anyone to. What are the distinct, precise claims someone could be making with it -- from the almost-trivial (some classifications just work better for some purposes) to the strong realist reading (nature has a mind-independent structure and any taxonomy that fails to match it is objectively wrong) and whatever lies in between? For each reading, say whether it's trivial, substantive-but-defensible, or overreach. More importantly: which reading would my instructor actually NEED for the slogan to settle my DSM question rather than just gesture at it, and does that reading survive the observation that sciences like psychiatry and economics seem to build their categories partly around our interests and interventions rather than around joint-structure? Also, does the slogan even make sense if the joints are supposed to come at many grains at once (phylogenetic vs. ecological vs. genetic kinds all cross-cutting)? Lay the readings out as a list with verdicts that I can bring to office hours, and flag any reading that makes the slogan true but useless for her argument.
