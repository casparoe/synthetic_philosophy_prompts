I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm writing a piece on why sad music makes people feel good (the whole "sad song paradox" thing). While researching I kept running into Eduard Hanslick's 1854 book On the Musically Beautiful, which everyone in the philosophy of music seems to treat as the big formalist manifesto. From what I can tell, Hanslick argued that music can't express emotions at all — that feelings are just a physiological side effect and the actual content of music is purely its formal structure. But that seems obviously refuted by, like, every film score ever? Composers clearly succeed at writing sad music, audiences reliably agree on which pieces are sad, and streaming services literally tag songs by mood.

So my question for the article: where did Hanslick go wrong? Was he just working before we had the psychology to understand how musical features (minor keys, slow tempo, descending lines) encode emotions, or was there some cultural reason he wanted to deny expression? I want to explain to readers how his theory got overturned and what replaced it. Also, if you could point me to the experiment that first demonstrated that listeners attribute emotions to music, that would help my fact-checker.
