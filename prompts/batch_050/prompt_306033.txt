In a policy fellowship I'm part of, the same debate keeps recurring in analogy form. One faction argues that transformative AI should be understood on the model of the printing press: a general-purpose technology for producing and distributing information, which triggered real turmoil (religious wars, heresy panics, destabilized authorities) but which no sane person now wishes had been suppressed, licensed, or centrally controlled. From this they conclude that preemptive regulation of AI is a category error — you would have been regulating literacy. The opposing faction insists the right model is nuclear weapons: a dual-use capability with abrupt destructive potential, developed under great-power rivalry, where the relevant policy instruments are secrecy, export controls, test bans, and nonproliferation treaties, and where waiting for the technology to mature before governing it means governing the ashes. Occasionally someone splits the difference with the Industrial Revolution — wrenching but net-positive, and manageable by ordinary labor and welfare policy — but the press and the bomb are the two analogies doing real argumentative work in the room.

I'd like a rigorous audit of both analogies rather than a meditation on them. For each, give me the explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the printing press's low marginal cost of reproduction, the church's lost monopoly on textual authority, the decades-long diffusion lag, the reactor's dual-use fuel cycle, the arms race's verification problem, and so on. Then separate the disanalogies into those that matter for the specific conclusion each analogy is being used to license and those that are merely cosmetic. In particular, examine the features each analogy quietly imports: the printing-press picture assumes the artifact is passive and that humans remain the sole agents acting on the information; the nuclear picture assumes a handful of legible state actors, a single catastrophic use-case, and a capability that cannot be copied from leaked materials. Which of these imported features does AI actually possess, and does either conclusion — near-laissez-faire from the press camp, or treaty-style control from the bomb camp — still stand once the disanalogies are priced in, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion the whole time? If neither analogy survives intact, say what better comparison class, if any, would bear the weight, or whether the honest answer is that transformative AI lacks a usable historical analogue and policy must be argued from first principles instead.

Structure the response as: two correspondence tables, a ranked list of load-bearing disanalogies for each analogy, and a closing assessment. End with a single machine-readable last line of the form Verdict: <one of "printing press analogy survives", "nuclear analogy survives", "both survive in weakened form", "neither survives; argue from first principles">, followed by one sentence justifying the verdict.
