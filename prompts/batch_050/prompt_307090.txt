I'm in a phil of science / phil of psychiatry course and we're doing a unit on classification in the human sciences, mostly centered on Hacking's idea of "looping effects" -- the thought that when you classify people (e.g. as having a certain disorder), the classified people can become aware of the label and change in response, so the category and the people in it end up influencing each other. Our prof asked us to build our own decision tree of the positions in this debate and I keep making mush, so I want you to write one I can compare mine against.

The tree should start from something like "psychiatric categories (depression, ADHD, autism, schizophrenia etc.) are at least somewhat useful clinically" and end at named positions. The leaves I want covered at minimum are: a hard medical-realist view (disorders are natural kinds, labels just discover them), a strong social-constructionist view, Hacking's own interactive-kinds/looping position, a "symptom network" style view (disorders are just clusters of mutually reinforcing symptoms, no underlying entity), and an eliminativist/dimensional view (scrap categories, use spectra or trait scores instead). Feel free to add one or two more if you think the space demands it.

Some constraints so it actually sorts people rather than just labeling them:

- Each branch node should be a question someone could answer without knowing the positions, e.g. things like: would the category still carve anything real if patients never learned their own diagnosis? Does it matter whether the label changes the person's behavior vs just changing how doctors see them? Is the clustering of symptoms caused by one underlying thing or by the symptoms causing each other? Use concrete mini-cases where you can (I liked ones about a kid whose behavior shifts after getting an ADHD label, vs. a community where a diagnosis arrives and the illness itself starts presenting differently).
- Include at least one question about what Hacking calls the difference between indifferent kinds and interactive kinds, but phrase it as an answerable question, not jargon.
- I'd also like you to include one hypothetical node we invented in my tutorial: a "one-way loop" where a label changes the people being classified, but the changed people never feed back to change the diagnostic criteria themselves. Ask whether that's stable or whether it inevitably becomes a full loop, and say which leaf that lands you in.
- Note any combinations of answers that are logically available but nobody seems to occupy, and say briefly whether the spot is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Same for any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate -- flag them instead of fudging a question.
- Keep it to maybe 6-8 branch questions total so it fits on one page of a handout.

One thing I genuinly can't figure out and want the tree to make progress on: does the looping story actually threaten the reality of the categories, or does it just mean the targets are moving while still being real? If your tree ends up treating that as THE master question that everything hangs off, say so and build it that way; if you think it's actually a side issue and the real splits are elsewhere, build the tree that way and justify it in a sentence or two at the end.
