I'm putting together the syllabus for a graduate seminar on the legal-philosophical legacy of the postwar tribunals, and before we read any of the standard secondary literature I want to hand my students a specification: what would a theory of retroactive criminal punishment in international law actually have to deliver in order to count as an adequate response to Nuremberg and Tokyo? Here is the dialectical situation as I understand it. The defense at Nuremberg pressed the nullum crimen sine lege objection: the Charter's counts, above all crimes against peace and crimes against humanity, criminalized conduct that had not been defined as an individual crime when committed. The tribunal's reply, roughly, was that this maxim is not an absolute limit on jurisdiction but a general principle of justice, that the defendants could not seriously have believed that invading neighboring states and massacring civilian populations were lawful, and that the Charter gave expression to international law already in force, with the Kellogg-Briand Pact doing work for the aggression charge. At Tokyo, Justice Pal's dissent rejected all of this: he would have acquitted every defendant, treating the proceedings as victors' justice in legal costume, and he pointed to what the tribunal would not look at -- Allied conduct, including the atomic bombings, that sat awkwardly next to the charges. Meanwhile the prosecution of Katyn was left in Soviet hands, and the Charter itself stripped defendants of the superior-orders defense while allowing it in mitigation. Every justification of the tribunals I know concedes something here and fights over the rest, and they never state their full standard.

So write the desiderata. What must any adequate theory of the legitimacy of these proceedings be able to do? I want the requirements list, with the concrete case or consideration motivating each: which features of Nuremberg and Tokyo it must be able to vindicate, condemn, or explain rather than ignore (the retroactivity problem, the tu quoque problem raised by the firebombings and the atomic bombs, the selective assignment of Katyn, the rejection of act-of-state immunity and superior orders, the treatment of conspiracy, the fact that only losers stood trial); which distinctions it must respect rather than collapse (retroactive clarification of existing law versus retroactive creation of new law; legality as a constraint on punishment versus legality as one virtue among others; procedural fairness versus substantive justice; selectivity in enforcement versus partiality in judgment; holding that a trial was unjust versus holding that the defendants did not deserve punishment); and which commitments it must avoid smuggling in (for instance, assuming that what was morally monstrous was thereby already criminal, or that codification after the fact retroactively validates itself). For each desideratum, say whether you take it to be non-negotiable for any theory worthy of the name or a partisan demand of one camp -- natural lawyers, positivists, realist skeptics -- and why.

Then three further things. First, flag any subset of the desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied; I have in mind, for example, the tension between holding that the tribunal merely applied pre-existing law, holding that the Holocaust's organizers richly deserved punishment, and holding that no one may be punished under law created after the act -- if some such triad is genuinely inconsistent, that is the result I most want surfaced. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding the familiar defenses and critiques of the tribunals. Third, and briefly, since I will do the detailed scoring in class: indicate how the main contenders -- a natural-law vindication, the positivist concession that the law was frankly retroactive but a lesser evil than impunity, Radbruch's formula about intolerably unjust law, a purely procedural defense of the trials' fairness, and the full victors'-justice critique -- stand against the list, in a sentence or two each. Fix the purpose as follows: the theory must be one we could hand to a future tribunal facing conduct not yet codified as crime, not merely a rationalization of 1946.
