I'm in an intro philosophy of logic seminar and this week we read Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (the dialogue he published in Mind in 1895, where the Tortoise keeps refusing to draw Z from Euclid's premises). At one point, after Achilles gets him to grant a hypothetical C saying that Z must follow from A and B, the Tortoise just digs in again: "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?" My flatmate read the piece over my shoulder and got completely obsessed with it, and now he's going around claiming Carroll actually proved that deduction is impossible. Last night he wrote out his reasoning on our whiteboard and I honestly can't find the hole in it, which is bugging me because the conclusion is obviously insane. Here's his argument, roughly as he had it:

(1) No matter how many propositions you accept — A, B, C, D, however many hypotheticals you write down — it is always logically possible to accept all of them and still not accept Z, since each new hypothetical is just one more thing you'd have to accept before Z is forced on you. That's exactly what the Tortoise keeps showing. (2) So no finite list of accepted propositions can ever make it the case that you must accept some further proposition. (3) Therefore accepting premises, by itself, never justifies accepting a conclusion. (4) If an inference from premises to a conclusion is ever to be justified, the justification has to come from somewhere else — i.e., from some further piece of reasoning. (5) But that further reasoning is itself an inference, so by the same argument it needs justifying too, and so on forever. (6) An infinite regress of justifications justifies nothing. (7) So no deductive inference is ever justified, and nobody is ever actually rationally required to accept the conclusion of a valid argument. He says this includes modus ponens itself, and that the whole of logic rests on an illusion we just agree not to inspect.

Now, I obviously want to keep the conclusion false — I want it to be true that if you accept that things equal to the same are equal to each other, and that the two sides of the triangle are equal to the same, you really must accept they're equal to each other, full stop, no matter how stubborn you are. But when I press him on the individual premises each one sounds weirdly plausible, and the step from the Tortoise's point to (3) and (4) especially feels airtight when he says it fast. Which premise or step should I actually be rejecting here? Can you walk me through exactly where the argument goes wrong, why that step looks fine at first glance (I suspect it has something to do with how he's treating those hypotheticals, but I can't articulate it), and what it costs to reject it — like, if I say the regress stops somewhere, do I have to say some principles of inference are just self-evident and need no justification, and is that a bullet worth biting? I'd also be curious whether the Tortoise passage itself, quoted above, actually supports his (1) or whether he's reading more into it than Carroll gives him.
