I chair the clinical ethics committee of a regional cancer centre, and a case has landed with us that has split the palliative team down the middle. Mrs D is a 71-year-old with metastatic breast cancer and severe bone pain that had resisted every conventional measure. Four months ago, as a last resort, she underwent a bilateral anterior cingulotomy at another trust — a neurosurgical procedure occasionally used for intractable pain, which interrupts part of the circuitry thought to give pain its affective charge. Since the operation a strange pattern has emerged. She consistently rates her pain at 7 or 8 out of 10 on the standard numeric scale, yet says, calmly and repeatedly, that it no longer troubles her: it is there, it is intense, and she simply does not mind it. She declines her breakthrough doses, reads and chats through procedures that used to leave her in tears, and has asked that her background opioid regimen — which now causes sedation, constipation, and contributed to two falls — be tapered away entirely, a request her son strongly supports. At the same time, the ward nurses report that she still winces, guards her left side, and braces herself before turning in bed.

The disagreement before the committee is about what her case tells us to do, both for her and in general. Her palliative consultant argues that her testimony should simply govern: the entire point of analgesia is to relieve suffering, she tells us sincerely and with intact capacity that she is not suffering, and continuing a burdensome opioid regimen is harm without benefit — tapering is the only defensible course. Several nurses and one committee member take the opposite view: the wincing and guarding show the pain is still doing its work on her, and the flatness may reflect what the surgery has done to her capacity to care about things rather than evidence that the pain has become harmless. There is also a policy question riding on the case: our unit assesses pain by asking patients to rate its intensity from 0 to 10, and Mrs D's case raises the question of whether we should instead — or also — ask how much the pain bothers them, and which of the two questions should drive dosing decisions when the answers come apart.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting, of roughly 500 to 700 words. It should recommend a concrete course of action for Mrs D — taper, maintain, or maintain with conditions — with a timeframe and a review point, and it should say what the committee should take her case to show about our assessment policy. I want the opinion to engage directly with the underlying question of what makes pain bad: whether its badness lies in the sensation, in its being minded, in what it does to a person's functioning, or somewhere else, and what follows when those things dissociate. It should give a fair and serious hearing to the strongest argument on the losing side — I suspect that is the consultant's position, but the draft should treat it as a live possibility rather than a straw man — and it should state what further evidence, if any, would change the recommendation. No literature review or citations; this is a decision document, and the reasoning has to stand on its own.
