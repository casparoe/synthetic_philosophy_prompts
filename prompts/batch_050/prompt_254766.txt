I sit on the programme committee of a public policy institute, and we are hosting an event on the regulation of prostitution later this year. Two of our panellists — a sociologist who advocates the Nordic model (decriminalise the sale of sex, criminalise its purchase, and fund exit services, along Swedish lines) and a public-health researcher who favours New Zealand-style full decriminalisation — have been arguing past each other for over a year, mostly in email threads the rest of us have stopped reading. I have asked them to produce a short joint document ahead of the event, and both drafts have failed in the same way: each author states the other side's position in terms the other side would never accept, so the documents read as two polemics stapled together. Draft the document they should have produced.

It should contain, in order: (i) a numbered list of claims both can sign — some empirical, some normative — such as claims about the harms both sides want reduced, the conditions under which much prostitution actually takes place, and the limits of the available data, all phrased so that neither side has to concede anything it denies; (ii) a statement of the genuine disagreements, each in wording both parties would accept as a fair description of their own view, which means avoiding the slogans each camp uses to caricature the other; (iii) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I suspect these include whether purchasing sex is intrinsically exploitative or only contingently so, whether suppressing demand reduces total harm or mainly displaces it into more dangerous forms, and how to weigh the welfare of people who would remain in the trade under each regime against that of people who would never enter it — but correct me if you think the cruxes lie elsewhere. (iv) For each crux, a concrete comparison or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it — for instance, the contrast between the legalised regimes of Germany and the Netherlands and the Swedish or Norwegian arrangements, or New Zealand's own post-reform reviews — with each side's prediction about that evidence recorded in advance, so that neither can move the goalposts afterwards. Note that both camps cite the German experience selectively, so the document should also say what each would accept as a fair characterisation of it.

Close with a section identifying which of their disagreements, if any, are verbal rather than substantive — I have a suspicion that part of the fight is over what counts as coercion and what counts as harm reduction — and explain why. Do not adjudicate between the two positions or declare a winner: the aim is a text both panellists could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made as exact as possible.
