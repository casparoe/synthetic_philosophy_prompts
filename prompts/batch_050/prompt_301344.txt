I am preparing a graduate-level seminar in nineteenth-century philosophy, but the relevant session is also open to advanced undergraduates, so I must plan it as a mixed audience: students who have read the aesthetic sections of The World as Will and Representation with varying degrees of care, some of whom have musical training and some none. The session, ninety minutes, is devoted to Schopenhauer's doctrine of music — the claim that music stands apart from the other arts because it does not copy the Platonic Ideas by which the Will is objectified, but is instead a copy of the Will itself. Act as a specialist in Schopenhauer who is also an experienced teacher, and before producing the plan, think carefully and do a round of explicit brainstorming about what makes this doctrine difficult to teach; then give me the plan itself.

I do not want you to explain the doctrine to me — I know it. I want a plan for how I should explain it. Specifically:

First, the dependency structure. What must be in place, in what order, before the claim about music can even be heard as a claim rather than as rhapsody? My instinct is that one cannot reach music until the students have the Will as thing-in-itself, the grades of its objectification, and the account of the other arts as presenting Ideas at those grades — but tell me whether that is right, whether anything else must precede it (I suspect the will-less subject of aesthetic contemplation has to be introduced somewhere, and I am unsure where), and where the sequence most often collapses for students.

Second, the carrying example. Choose one example that will bear the weight of the session and argue for it: what it must have and what it must conspicuously lack. My inclination is a movement of purely instrumental music in the classical style rather than a song, precisely because Schopenhauer's point is threatened the moment words or a programme are attached; but consider whether a simpler example — a scale, a cadence, the resolution of a suspension — might serve better at the start, with the symphony kept for later. If you reject either, propose your own and say why.

Third, the misconception map. Enumerate the misunderstandings this audience predictably brings or forms — my own list runs: that the doctrine says music expresses the composer's emotions; that it is merely a florid way of praising music's power; that it privileges programme music, when the opposite is true; that the claim about music as copy of the Will is compatible with the aesthetic release from willing, with no tension to be felt. For each, tell me the exact point in the sequence at which I should head it off, and how — in particular, whether the fourth should be corrected at all, or whether the students should be made to sit with the tension that music both copies the Will and suspends it, as one of the genuine difficulties of the system.

Fourth, the check for understanding. Give me one question to pose at the end whose answers would distinguish a student who has grasped the doctrine from one who has merely absorbed the vocabulary. Tell me what a good answer contains and what a confident but empty answer sounds like.

Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level, and say explicitly that I am leaving it out: for instance, Schopenhauer's own remarks on opera and on particular composers, the arithmetic view of music associated with Leibniz, and whatever else you judge a distraction for a first encounter.
