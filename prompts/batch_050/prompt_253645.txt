I am a retired experimental physicist, and in the last few years I have been reading metaphysics in my spare time with the enthusiasm of a late convert. Coming from a background in relativity, I initially found the perdurantist picture of persistence almost embarrassingly natural: of course objects are four-dimensional "worms" extended through time, just as they are extended through space, and talk of a persisting object is really talk of the maximal spacetime region it occupies. What has surprised me is that the philosophical literature does not seem to treat the matter as settled by physics, and I keep running into positions — endurantism, and more recently something called "stage theory" or "exdurantism" — that I have not succeeded in distinguishing cleanly. I would be grateful for an explanation that takes my physics background seriously rather than working around it.

My first confusion is about what the argument from relativity is actually supposed to show. I understand that the relativity of simultaneity undermines a privileged, observer-independent "now," and that this is often taken to favor the four-dimensional view. But it seems to me that an endurantist could simply accept the block universe — the four-dimensional manifold with all its events — while denying that material objects have temporal parts located at the various slices of it. Is there a further premise needed to get from the structure of spacetime to the ontology of persisting objects, and if so, what is it? I suspect I am missing a step, because the move from "no absolute simultaneity" to "objects perdure" strikes me as a non sequitur as it stands, yet clearly better philosophers than me have found it compelling.

My second confusion concerns the two rival versions of four-dimensionalism. As far as I can tell, both the worm theorist and the stage theorist accept that there exist instantaneous object-stages, but they disagree about which entity ordinary names pick out: the worm theorist says I am the whole temporally extended sum, while the stage theorist says I am a single stage, with claims about my past and future handled by counterpart relations among stages rather than by identity over time. What I cannot see is what turns on this choice. Could you explain, using one concrete and sustained example — say, a person across a single ordinary day, or the classic case of a statue reshaped from one lump of clay — what each theory says, which sentences come out true on each, and why the stage theorist thinks the counterpart machinery is worth the trouble? In particular, I would like to understand how the stage theorist's semantics for "I was a child" or "the bridge will collapse" actually works, and why this is meant to help with puzzles about coincidence and counting. A worked example done carefully would be far more useful to me than a survey of the literature.
