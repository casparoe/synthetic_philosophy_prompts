I'm a third-year undergraduate taking an upper-division seminar on philosophy of language, and I also work two afternoons a week as a paid peer tutor for the introductory course. The single most common failure I see in intro essays is students treating "knowable independently of experience" and "true in all possible worlds" as the same property, so Kripke's examples of the contingent a priori and the necessary a posteriori read to them as wordplay rather than discoveries. Our seminar instructor has suggested that the two-dimensional framework -- evaluating a sentence twice over, once by considering a scenario as actual (would the sentence turn out true if that scenario is how things in fact are?) and once by considering it as counterfactual (does the sentence hold of that situation, given how things actually are?) -- is the cleanest way to keep the two axes apart. What I want is a procedure I can teach to my tutees and use when marking their exercises: an ordered sequence of steps that takes a candidate sentence and returns its position on both dimensions, including preliminary steps for fixing contextual parameters (who is speaking, when, which term is being used rigidly and which as a description), instructions for how to treat natural kind terms differently from definite descriptions and indexicals, and a rule for what to do when a student constructs a scenario that is coherent in words but arguably impossible. Assume the user has read Naming and Necessity and one encyclopedia-level summary of the framework, and that the whole procedure must be runnable on a single sentence in about fifteen minutes, since that is what an exam question allows.

Please build the procedure so it is honest about its own machinery: state explicitly which steps are mechanical and which require a judgment call, and in particular mark the point where the "considered as actual" test quietly presupposes that we can settle, from the armchair, what a term would pick out in an arbitrary epistemic scenario -- an assumption I gather is contested, along with whether the framework explains the modality or merely redescribes it. I would like the procedure to flag those hand-off points rather than hide them, since I would rather tell a tutee "here the framework runs out and philosophers argue" than have them think the test is broken. Also tell me which simplifications are safe for a first pass (I suspect fixing the speaker and time before either evaluation is one) and which would gut the framework entirely.

Then run the completed procedure on four sentences, showing every step: "water is H2O," "Hesperus is Phosphorus," "I am here now" (uttered by a particular person at a particular time and place), and "the bar serving as the standard of length is one meter long at noon on the day the standard is fixed." Include at least one worked case where the procedure delivers a verdict a competent student will dislike -- for instance, a stipulated definition coming out contingent, or a sentence that feels impossible to get wrong coming out as merely a priori -- and say whether that verdict should be presented to students as a feature of the framework or as a known trouble spot. Finally, name a class of sentences where you would expect the procedure to fail or to produce results that practitioners dispute (my own guesses are mathematical truths and mind-brain identity claims, but correct me), so that I can keep such sentences out of intro exercises.
