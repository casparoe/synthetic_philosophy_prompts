My decision theory reading group has spent the last two meetings on the Death in Damascus case, and somewhere in the third hour someone muttered that Death must run the most ruthlessly efficient scheduling office in existence. I want to turn that aside into a piece for our end-of-semester zine. For anyone not in the group: in the usual telling, a merchant's servant in Baghdad bumps into Death in the market, reads her startled gesture as a threat, borrows a horse, and flees to Samarra — where, Death explains to the merchant, the two of them had an appointment that very evening, her startle having been surprise at finding the servant in Baghdad at all. In the decision-theory version the details are sharpened: Death keeps an appointment book written in advance and predicts infallibly (or near enough) where each client will be on the appointed night, so the servant's choice of city cannot change what the book says, even though the relevant entry is, in effect, a record of that very choice. The case is notorious for having no comfortable resting point. Whatever the servant resolves to do, his resolving is evidence that Death waits wherever he is heading (or staying), so no option remains attractive on the supposition that it is taken; and yet the book is already written, so there is a real sense in which his deliberation changes nothing.

Write the FAQ page of Death's Office of Appointments: deadpan bureaucratic customer-service prose, roughly 700 words, six to eight numbered questions. The humor must come from the decision theory itself rather than generic gothic jokes about scythes and robes — a decision theorist should laugh, not wince. Concretely, I want the office's answers to keep exploiting, at escalating levels, the fact that the predictor has already priced in the client's reasoning: the startled gesture in the marketplace was anticipated and is in the book; the plan to do the opposite of whatever one was going to do is in the book; the letter of complaint one is currently drafting is in the book. Include at least one query from a self-identified causal decision theorist who argues that, the book being already written, staying and fleeing are causally on a par and so the whole service is a scam — and give the office a reply that is funny precisely because it is not clear he is wrong. A testimonials section (five stars, "I stayed put in Damascus and lived") should appear and then quietly fall apart on inspection, along survivorship-bias lines. The piece should end with the office politely declining to advise the servant where to spend the night, on grounds that get at why the case offers no stable option, without ever using the word ratifiable.

Since this is satire rather than argument, I don't need a verdict, but please append a short afterword, outside the word count, saying where the piece takes liberties — for instance, whether your Death is an infallible predictor or merely an extremely reliable one, and which decision-theoretic premise the jokes lean on most heavily. In that same afterword, say how confident you are that the satire is fair to the causalist rather than a caricature of him, and single out the one joke you are least sure is defensible on the merits.
