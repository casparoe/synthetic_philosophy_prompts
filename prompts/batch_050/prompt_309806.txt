I have recently been appointed to my university's animal research ethics committee, and my first batch of protocols has forced a question on me I had only ever treated abstractly. One protocol proposes lesion experiments on honeybees; another uses only flatworms; a third, submitted half-jokingly by a philosophy colleague, asks whether we would need approval to run aversive-feedback experiments on a sophisticated thermostat or a robot vacuum, and if not, why the flatworms are different. I need to give new committee members a way to locate where they actually stand on gradations of moral status before we discuss any specific protocol.

Please construct a decision tree that sorts a person into a position on the moral status of marginal cases, built from the logical space of commitments rather than from a list of named theories. Start with the questions that do the dividing, then place the actual positions at the leaves. I would like six to eight branch points and no more than a dozen leaves. Some constraints:

- Each branch must be labeled with a concrete diagnostic question someone can answer without knowing any terminology -- for example, a question about whether a being with no experiences but some responsiveness to damage merits any direct consideration, rather than a question about whether one is a sentientist. Where a question can be sharpened by a case, use one: the bee, the flatworm, the thermostat, a deep-sleeping patient, or a future AI with self-monitoring but (we stipulate) no phenomenal feel.
- The tree must make room, as leaves, for at least the following, though you need not use these labels: the view that moral status tracks degree of sentience continuously, with no floor; the view that sentience is binary and everything sentient counts fully; the view that a threshold of cognitive sophistication beyond mere sentience is required; the view that status depends on species membership or relationship to us rather than on any capacity; and a precautionary view on which uncertainty about whether a system feels anything itself generates obligations.
- Note explicitly which positions the tree cannot separate on the basis of their answers alone (I suspect the precautionary view will collapse into at least one other leaf), and say what further question would be needed to pry them apart.
- For any combination of answers that no one seems to occupy, mark the leaf as unoccupied and diagnose briefly whether it is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.
- Each occupied leaf should carry one sentence stating the commitment of that position that my committee members will find most awkward when applied to the honeybee protocol.

The audience is intelligent non-philosophers -- biologists, veterinarians, one lawyer -- so keep the tree usable as a one-page handout, but feel free to add a short appendix explaining any load-bearing distinction (such as phenomenal versus merely functional responsiveness) in two or three sentences each.
