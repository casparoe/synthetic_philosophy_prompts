I'm presenting my position on peer disagreement to our reading group next week and would like a consistency audit before I defend it. Here is where I've landed:

(1) Equal weight: when I discover that someone I regard as my epistemic peer disagrees with me about p, I should move roughly halfway toward their view, giving their verdict about as much weight as my own.

(2) Independence: in deciding how to respond to the disagreement, I must bracket the first-order reasoning that led me to my view, since that reasoning is precisely what is under dispute; relying on it would be circular.

(3) Total evidence: what it is rational for me to believe is always fixed by my total evidence, and the fact that a peer disagrees with me is simply one more piece of evidence, to be weighed alongside my first-order evidence rather than playing any privileged role.

(4) Discounting: I regularly give little weight to a peer's dissent when I know they were tired, rushed, or otherwise impaired at the time, and I take this to be fully compatible with counting them as my peer when both of us are in good condition.

(5) Steadfastness about conciliationism: although a number of philosophers I respect — people I would count as my epistemic peers on exactly this topic — reject conciliationism in favor of steadfast views, my confidence in (1) is undiminished by their dissent, and I don't think I owe them any movement on this question.

Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with one another, and which pairs only appear to be? For each real tension, say what exactly generates it and what would have to give on either side, and rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally serious. If a single revision would resolve more than one problem, identify it and say what it costs.
