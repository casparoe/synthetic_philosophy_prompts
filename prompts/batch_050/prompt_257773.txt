You are an AI safety researcher with a background in both machine learning and philosophy of science, asked to serve as the neutral drafter for an adversarial collaboration. Think carefully before writing; begin by brainstorming, privately, the strongest version of each side's position and the places where their apparent disagreements might dissolve on inspection. Then produce the final document.

The two parties are researchers who have argued for years about how to make the deployment of powerful AI systems safe:

- Camp A, the alignment-first camp, holds that safety requires making the model itself trustworthy: shaping its goals, values, or motivations during training so that it does not want to cause harm, deceive its operators, or pursue objectives of its own. On their view, any safety case that does not rest on the model's internal dispositions is at best a stopgap, because a sufficiently capable misaligned system will eventually find the gaps in whatever external scaffolding surrounds it.

- Camp B, the control-first camp, holds that safety should be established under the explicit assumption that the model may be misaligned: the deployment protocol -- trusted monitoring by weaker models, limiting the powerful model's affordances and information, auditing samples of its outputs, requiring that consequential actions be ratified by systems or processes the model does not control, testing the protocol with red teams that try to smuggle sabotage past it -- should make catastrophe unlikely even if the model is actively scheming. On their view, waiting for confident alignment before deploying is both unnecessary and a recipe for deploying nothing while rivals deploy everything.

Draft the joint statement these two camps should be able to produce after a genuine adversarial collaboration. The document must contain:

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of at least eight substantive claims both camps can sign without reservation. These should be real content, not peace treaty boilerplate: for example, claims about what current evaluations of model propensities can and cannot establish, about whether a monitor weaker than the monitored system can still catch certain classes of misbehavior, about the difference between a model that behaves well because it is aligned and one that behaves well because it calculates that misbehavior would be caught, and about what each methodology would cost if it turned out to be unnecessary. Formulations must be neither vacuous nor slanted toward one camp.

2. Disagreements. A table of the residual disagreements, each stated in neutral wording that both camps would accept as an accurate statement of their own position. For each disagreement, add a line noting whether it is empirical (settleable in principle by experiments on current or near-future systems), conceptual (about what safety or alignment even means), or strategic (about what to do given shared uncertainty). If any apparent disagreement turns out to be verbal -- the two camps using, say, the word 'safe' or the word 'trust' differently -- identify it as such and say which usage each camp actually needs for its argument.

3. Cruxes and advance predictions. Identify three to five cruxes: sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp to substantially revise its position. For each crux, describe a concrete test, experiment, or observable future development that both camps agree bears on it, and record each camp's prediction in advance. At least one crux should concern how control measures scale as the gap widens between the monitored system and its monitors, and at least one should concern whether a scheming model's cooperation with control evaluations can be distinguished from genuine alignment using any procedure both camps regard as feasible.

4. A closing section stating, for each camp, what result -- if demonstrated over the next several years -- would make its members concede that the other camp's approach should be the default, and whether there is any finding both camps agree would move the field as a whole rather than just trading one camp's members for the other's.

Throughout, hold both sides to their strongest forms: the alignment-first camp must engage with the strongest version of control (defense in depth, conservatism about what the powerful system is allowed to touch, red-teaming of the protocol itself), not a caricature of a single filter; and the control-first camp must engage with the strongest version of the alignment objection (that any protocol designed and operated with help from the potentially misaligned system inherits that system's blind spots), not a caricature of pure pessimism. Aim for roughly 1,500 words.
