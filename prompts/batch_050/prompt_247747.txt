I've settled on a pretty deflationary reading of Kavka's toxin puzzle: it doesn't reveal anything deep about the rationality of intentions, it just shows that you can't form an intention at will when you already know you won't carry it out. On this view the whole paradox evaporates once you keep "reasons to intend" and "reasons to act" cleanly seperated, and the puzzle is really about the limits of direct voluntary control over your own mental states, not about whether rational intention can come apart from rational action.

Tell me what would count against this reading. Not just "here's what Kavka fans say back" -- I want to know which cases or arguments would actually force a deflationist like me to concede that the puzzle tracks something about rationality itself, and which ones we'd reasonably wave away. Does the deflationary reading make any prediction the substantive reading doesn't (e.g. about variants of the case -- perfect self-knowledge, a pill that causes amnesia, a payoff for drinking rather than intending)? And be honest about whether my position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if every alleged counterexample gets reclassified as "just more evidence you can't intend at will," is that a feature or a bug? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.
