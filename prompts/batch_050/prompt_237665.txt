Exam question: Explain what Wittgenstein means by a hinge proposition in On Certainty, and how the notion is supposed to undercut both the Cartesian skeptic and G. E. Moore's attempted refutation of skepticism. In your answer, discuss whether hinge propositions are known, justified, or believed, and explain the significance of the river-bed metaphor.

Student answer: In On Certainty, Wittgenstein develops the notion of hinge propositions in order to show that radical doubt fails. Hinge propositions, such as that I have two hands or that the earth existed long before I was born, are the fixed points around which our questioning and doubting revolve. Just as a door cannot turn without its hinges, doubt cannot get going unless some things are exempt from doubt. Wittgenstein's point is that these hinges are known with certainty: each of us has accumulated overwhelming evidence for them over the course of a lifetime, and this evidence is what makes doubting them irrational. Moore was therefore right to insist that he knew he had two hands, although Wittgenstein regards Moore's proof of an external world as unnecessary rather than mistaken. Doubt, for Wittgenstein, always requires grounds, and a doubt without grounds is not a genuine doubt at all. The river-bed metaphor illustrates this structure: the river of our empirical thoughts flows along a bed of hardened propositions that remain permanently fixed, since they have been confirmed beyond any possibility of revision. This shows that skepticism is self-undermining, because the skeptic must already rely on the hinges in order to formulate his doubts.
