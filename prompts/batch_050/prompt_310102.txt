My office-mate has spent the semester presiding over our vagueness seminar, and I have been keeping a record of her rulings. Her verdicts so far:

1. The forced march: start with a heap of ten thousand grains and remove one grain at a time. Verdict: the tolerance principle -- the claim that removing a single grain from a heap always leaves a heap -- is not exceptionlessly true. Somewhere in the series the inference must break, even though no one can say where.

2. The borderline case: a man whose hair count falls squarely in the disputed region. Is he bald? Verdict: unanswerable -- and not merely unanswered. No inquiry, however ideal, could put us in a position to say.

3. The same man, the disjunction: is he either bald or not bald? Verdict: yes, plainly. That much is simply true.

4. The existential question: is there, somewhere in the sorites series of heights, a last height that counts as tall? Verdict: yes, there is a boundary somewhere; it is just that its location is not the sort of thing anyone could know.

5. The identity case: two cartographic societies draw the boundaries of the same mountain a kilometre apart. Is their mountain identical to ours? Verdict: either it is or it isn't. The uncertainty concerns which object our word has latched onto, not some third, indeterminate status of being.

6. The legal case: a bylaw bans vehicles from the park, and the court must rule on an electric skateboard, a borderline vehicle. Verdict: whichever way the court rules, it will be making law rather than discovering what the bylaw already settled. There is no prior fact of the matter for the court to get right or wrong.

Next week's case is this. A science museum puts up a plaque beside a display of a borderline pile of sand, reading: this collection ceases to be a heap at exactly 47 grains. Forty-seven is not a number anyone could defend on independent grounds; it sits in the middle of the disputed range. Three visitors argue about the plaque. The first says it states something false. The second says it states something that is either true or false, though no one could ever know which. The third says it states something that is neither true nor false -- while conceding that the claim that some number is the cutoff is straightforwardly true.

Predict her verdict on the plaque, and say which position her record commits her to: epistemicism, supervaluationism, a degree-theoretic or many-valued view, or something else. In doing so, lay out what each candidate view predicts about the plaque, identify which of the six earlier verdicts do the most discriminating work, and say honestly whether any verdict sits uncomfortably with your attribution -- I suspect one of them does, but I won't say which. Finally, name the single further case that would settle it between the two strongest candidates. It should not be the museum case with a different number; find a case where the two views' deeper commitments -- about whether there is a hidden fact of the matter, or about the logic of the boundary claims themselves -- come apart.
