For most of my adult life I've had a background intuition that the morally relevant thing about my choices is what they actually cause, but over the past year of thinking on my own -- no training in philosophy, just reading discussions online and turning it over -- I've become convinced the picture is more tangled. Here is my version of the puzzle. Suppose the universe (or the multiverse, or just the future) contains an astronomical number of agents in situations structurally similar to mine. Then my choice in a one-shot dilemma seems to be evidence about what all those similar agents choose in theirs, even where I can causally affect none of them. And separately: once the population at stake is large enough, even minuscule probabilities seem to dominate any calculation, so that a one-in-a-billion chance of affecting a trillion lives outranks a certainty of helping my neighbor. I gather there are named positions on how to handle these two pressure points, but I don't trust my own grasp of the labels, and I don't want the labels doing the work.

I run a small online reading group of fellow non-specialists, and I want to build a "which position do you actually hold?" decision tree that sorts our members by their real commitments rather than by which terms they've heard. Please construct it for me, with these constraints:

1. The tree should start from a minimal commitment nearly everyone in the group accepts -- something like "rational choice means weighing outcomes by their likelihood and size" -- and branch from there into at most nine binary or three-way questions, ending in five to seven leaves.

2. Every branch node must be a concrete case or claim a person can react to without knowing any position's name. Good candidates for the diagnostic material, since these genuinely pull people apart in my experience:
   - A case where acting benefits you slightly and predictably burdens one distant stranger you'll never meet, but where your choice is also evidence that a vast number of similar agents facing the identical choice act the same way.
   - A case distinguishing correlation that exists because the other agent literally shares your decision procedure (a copy, a simulation, a twin raised on identical reasoning) from correlation that is merely statistical (people with similar temperaments and information).
   - A "tiny probability, astronomical stakes" case, to test whether someone follows expected value all the way down or rounds small probabilities away.
   - A case testing whether uncertainty about whether the correlation is even real licenses ignoring it.

3. Among the leaves, please make room for at least: the pure causalist who treats evidence about others as changing predictions but never reasons; the cooperator who requires a shared decision procedure but not mere similarity; the broad cooperator for whom any robust correlation suffices; the person who accepts the logic in principle yet distrusts the empirical premises so thoroughly that it never changes their behavior; and whoever remains once the tiny-probability question splits the cooperators. Use standard labels where you're confident they fit, but define each leaf in plain functional terms and invent a clear name where the standard one is unreliable.

4. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions in that space. Where a coherent combination of answers seems to correspond to no real position, note it and diagnose briefly whether the gap exists because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

5. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and say what further question -- if any -- would split them.

6. End with a short note on which single question in your tree you expect to do the most sorting work in practice, and why.
