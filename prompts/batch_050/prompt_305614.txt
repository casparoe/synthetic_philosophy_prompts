I've been in a running argument with a friend about how rational agents should handle tiny probabilities of enormous stakes, and I want to stop arguing ad hoc and figure out what principle, if any, I actually hold. So here are my gut verdicts on a set of cases, and I'd like you to tell me what decision rule unifies them, what it implies for cases I haven't listed, and which of my verdicts has to go if the rule is to stay simple. First, the easy ones: offered a fair coin that pays 2 units of good on heads and nothing on tails, against a guaranteed 1 unit, I'm happy to gamble, and I'd say the same at 100 against 50. Offered the St. Petersburg gamble at any large fixed price, I refuse, and I think refusing is correct despite the gamble's infinite expected value. A stranger on the street promises me 10^20 units of good tomorrow if I hand over $20 today; I assign some nonzero credence to his being able to deliver, but I keep my money and feel entirely justified. So far, so ordinary.

The harder ones: suppose a well-funded research program claims that one particular policy has a one-in-a-trillion chance of producing a galaxy-wide utopia of astronomical value, and the calculation shows this dwarfs every mundane use of the same resources. My verdict is that we are not required to fund it, and that a theory which says we are has been exploited by the size of the number rather than guided by it. But now: astronomers verify, with excellent instruments and independent replication, that an extinction-sized asteroid has a one-in-a-million chance of striking Earth in forty years, and a deflection mission costing a large fraction of world GDP would reduce that chance to near zero. Here my verdict flips completely: fund the mission, and anyone who invoked my previous case to dismiss it would be making a serious mistake. I cannot currently say what distinguishes the two, since both involve small probabilities and vast stakes, and that embarrasses me. One more: a friend tells me that every time he crosses the street there is some minuscule chance, via chaotic causal chains, that he sets off a catastrophe, and he crosses anyway without a moment's guilt. I think he's right to, and I don't think this is mere squeamishness about low numbers.

So: is there a coherent principle behind these verdicts -- some combination of bounded utility, discounting or ignoring sufficiently small probabilities, and treating the trustworthiness of a probability estimate differently from its size -- or am I just being inconsistent? Please state the best candidate principle precisely, show which of my cases motivates each of its clauses, and be honest about which verdicts it cannot save. Then tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't decided: a one-percent chance (not tiny, not large) of an outcome a million times better than the alternative, and a tiny but rigorously established probability of a payoff that is large but not astronomical. Finally, if my asteroid verdict and my utopia verdict genuinely cannot both stand under any simple principle, tell me which one you'd overturn and what I lose by overturning it.
