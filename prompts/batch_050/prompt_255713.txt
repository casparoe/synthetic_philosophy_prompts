A close friend of mine, who calls herself a tranquilist, has a settled pattern of verdicts about well-being and creation that I want to probe. Her judgments so far: (1) a couple who create a child destined for a wonderful life do nothing wrong, but declining to create the child is no loss either — the merely possible child is not missing out on anything; (2) it would be seriously wrong to create a child whose life will contain even a single hour of severe agony, no matter how much joy surrounds that hour; (3) if a perfectly contented, wholly desireless person is offered a pill that grants an hour of intense pleasure with no side effects and no craving afterward, they gain nothing by taking it — they were already as well off as a person can be; and (4) she donates to anesthesia and pain-relief charities but says that organizations spreading delight through art, however lovely, are not doing the same kind of moral work. Now I want to know what she will say about this case: a healthy, contented person is offered a drug that first induces a powerful but harmless craving for exactly one hour and then fully satisfies it, leaving the person precisely as contented as before. I can see at least two rules that fit her existing track record and pull apart here — one on which craving is itself a disturbance that nothing can compensate, another on which the satisfaction of an existing craving is the one thing that does add genuine value — but I want the analysis done properly. Please reply with exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: the first three should each state a candidate rule consistent with all four verdicts and say what it predicts about the craving-then-satisfaction drug; the fourth should give your best guess at her verdict and the reason you favor that rule over its rivals; the fifth should describe one further hypothetical case that would most efficiently distinguish the remaining rules from each other.
