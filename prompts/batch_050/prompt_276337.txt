I spent four decades as an experimental chemist and came to philosophy only in retirement, through a reading group that meets at the continuing-education program of our local university. For the past month we have been stuck on Warren Quinn's self-torturer case and on the broader question of whether rational preferences can be intransitive, and what I want is not another rehearsal of the case but a diagnosis of why we cannot move past it.

The setup, as I understand it: a person is fitted with a device offering a long sequence of settings, each delivering a slightly more intense painful shock than the last, with adjacent settings differing so little that no one could feel the difference between neighbors, and each advance up the dial carrying a payment. Moving from any setting to the next looks plainly worth doing, since one trades an imperceptible increment of pain for real money; yet the person who keeps advancing ends up in severe, constant agony, holding a sum he would immediately surrender to be back at the start.

The group has hardened into three camps. One holds that every individual step is rational and that the case therefore shows rationality can be self-defeating when applied choice by choice. A second insists that rationality must govern the whole sequence, so the agent is required to adopt and hold a policy rather than evaluate steps locally. A third claims the entire puzzle rests on a fiction, since in practice people would notice the drift and stop. We have gone around this triangle four times now, and nobody has conceded anything.

I will confess my working hypothesis, as a scientist, so you can assess it rather than leave me guessing whether it is naive. In measurement, indiscriminability is not transitive: sample A can be indistinguishable from B, and B from C, while A and C are easily told apart, and I gather mathematical psychologists formalized this long ago under the name of semiorders. If felt indifference between adjacent pains is intransitive in just this way, then perhaps the puzzle is partly an empirical artifact — a mistaken picture of how discrimination actually works — rather than a deep result about the structure of rationality.

So here is what I am asking. What kind of disagreement is this, really? A disguised empirical question about perceptual thresholds that psychology could in principle settle? A normative dispute about whether rationality attaches to single choices or to whole sequences, where each side simply asserts its premise? Or a verbal tangle in which the camps mean different things by "preference" and "rational"? And given your diagnosis, what would progress even look like — a cleaner distinction, an experiment, a formal theorem — or is this the sort of impasse one should simply learn to expect, and if so, why?
