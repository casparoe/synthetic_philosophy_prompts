I keep losing arguments with a friend who's a committed modal rationalist — she thinks ideal conceivability is a reliable guide to possibility, roughly in Chalmers' sense, and I want a case to hit back with. So: build me a thought experiment in which something is ideally conceivable (holds up under ideal rational reflection, not just prima facie "sure, I can picture it") but is nonetheless, you'd argue, impossible. Constraints: no zombies, no water/H2O or Hesperus/Phosphorus, no Twin Earth, and no leaning on some math conjecture that happens to be false (like someone in 1990 "conceiving" of a counterexample to Fermat's last theorem). Set it in something like ordinary life or near-future science rather than outright fantasy if you can.

After the scenario, do the full check. Say why it clears the bar for ideal conceivability — and specify whether it's negatively conceivable, positively conceivable, or both, since I'd rather the case not collapse if she grants me only one of them. Then argue for the impossibility. Then diagnose: where would she attack? Does the case actually pressure the conceivability-possibility link, or does it work by smuggling in an a posteriori necessity she can just shrug and accept, in which case be honest that all I've got is another Kripke case wearing a hat. Finally, tell me which single feature of the scenario is doing the work and what happens to the argument if I take that feature out.
