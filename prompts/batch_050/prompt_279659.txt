I'm in a reading group on Zeno and infinity, and last week I tried to defend Thomson's conclusion about his lamp, but my arguement ended up proving way more than I wanted and now I can't locate the mistake. Here's the argument as I gave it. (1) Take a lamp that's on at t = 0 and toggle it at t = 1, t = 1.5, t = 1.75, and so on, so that infinitely many toggles happen strictly before t = 2 (we granted the idealization -- the question is whether the setup is coherent, not whether you could build the switch). (2) Whatever else is true, at t = 2 the lamp is either on or off; it's an ordinary lamp, not some quantum thing. (3) It can't be on at t = 2, since for every moment before 2 at which it was on, it was switched off again before 2 arrived. (4) It can't be off at t = 2, since for every moment before 2 at which it was off, it was switched on again before 2. (5) Contradiction, so the lamp schedule can't be completed -- Thomson was right that the supertask is impossible. (6) But notice that nothing in steps (2)-(4) used anything specific to lamps. The same template applies to a bouncing ball whose bounces halve in height and duration so that infinitely many bounces finish before t = 2: for every moment before 2 at which the ball was above the floor there's a later moment before 2 at which it touched the floor, and vice versa, so by (3)-(4)-style reasoning the ball can be neither in the air nor on the floor at t = 2 -- yet it obviously just sits there. (7) And it applies to Achilles passing the infinitely many points between himself and the tortoise. (8) So my reasoning seems to show that continuous processes completing infinitely many stages are impossible across the board, which would mean either motion is impossible or space and time aren't really infinitely divisible. Everyone agreed (8) is way too strong -- physics is not going to fall to a lamp argument -- but nobody could agree on which step fails.

For what it's worth, I'd like to keep (5) if I can, since the lamp genuinely seems like it couldn't have a settled final state, and I'd obviously like to keep Achilles catching the tortoise. So which step actually goes wrong, why did it look fine, and what does it cost me to reject it? I have a vague feeling the trouble is in (3)-(4), but whenever I say that out loud it sounds like I'm just denying that the lamp has to be in some state at t = 2, which (2) already ruled out.
