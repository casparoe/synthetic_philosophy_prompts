I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am drafting a long-form piece on the rhetoric of AI existential risk. In reporting it, I keep encountering two stock analogies that are made to do heavy argumentative lifting, and I want to understand how much weight each can actually bear.

The first analogy, popularized by technology-industry figures, holds that developing advanced AI is like summoning a demon: the summoner is confident that the pentagram and the incantations will keep the entity bound, and the summoner is fatally wrong. This analogy is typically deployed to argue for slowing or halting frontier AI development outright. The second analogy, favored in policy and diplomatic circles, holds that advanced AI is like nuclear weapons: a strategically decisive technology whose existence triggers arms races and which therefore requires treaties, verification regimes, and nonproliferation frameworks. This analogy is typically deployed to argue for international governance and managed competition between states.

Note that the two analogies pull toward different conclusions -- the demon picture toward cessation, the nuclear picture toward regulation of an ongoing enterprise -- and they seem to locate the danger in different places. Please audit both, treating them as arguments rather than decorations. For each one, give me an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the demon, the summoning circle, and the summoner's confidence; what plays the role of the warhead, fissile material, deterrence, and the test ban. Then identify where each mapping breaks. Some candidate pressure points I have in mind, though you should not restrict yourself to them: nuclear weapons do not pursue goals, self-improve, or attempt to deceive their handlers, while the demon analogy presupposes an agent with intentions -- and it is contested whether current trajectories in AI will produce anything of the kind; conversely, the nuclear framework presupposes a small number of state actors, weapons whose testing is visible and whose materials are chokepointed, and a stable logic of deterrence, none of which may transfer to software.

For each analogy, please tell me: which features of the source domain it quietly imports that the target may lack; which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion it is used to support and which are superficial; and whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Finally, my working hypothesis is that the demon analogy captures the control problem (can we reliably constrain a system more capable than ourselves?) while the nuclear analogy captures the racing and instability problem (what happens between competing developers and states?), and that the choice between them is less a disagreement about analogies than a disagreement about which failure mode is primary. Assess whether that division of labor holds up, and whether any better analogy -- from any domain -- would serve the public conversation more honestly than either of these.
