I lead a QA team at a company that prepares regulatory filings. Each filing is basically a huge list of claims -- material specs, test results, dates, supplier certifications -- and my reviewers check each claim individually and only sign off on it if they're very confident in it (say 99%+ per claim). The problem is a filing can have 2,000+ claims, so even if every single claim passes review, statistically a few of them are probably wrong. I've basically been living the preface paradox: I'd stake my reputation on any given line, but I'm close to certain each filing contains *some* error, and my legal department keeps asking me "so are you certifying this filing is accurate or not?" and I don't have a principled answer.

There's also the flip side, which I gather is the lottery paradox: for any specific error someone proposes ("claim #483 might be wrong"), my reviewers can usually say with justification "no, that one's solid" -- but it feels like that same move, applied everywhere, would let me conclude the whole filing is error-free, which I know it isn't.

Write me a procedure my team can actually run when deciding whether to certify a filing. It should specify: when per-claim confidence is enough and when we need some separate batch-level check, how to think about aggregating individual confidences without pretending the math is more exact than it is, whether the "expected number of errors" should matter more or less depending on what kind of error it is, and at what point the question stops being about statistics and becomes a judgment call about acceptable risk that has to go to a human (and which human). Flag honestly which steps are real procedure and which are judgement calls in a trenchcoat.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a routine filing of 2,500 low-stakes claims each verified at 99.5%, (2) a filing of 300 claims where one particular claim, if wrong, could get someone hurt and the rest just cost money, and (3) a filing where my most careful reviewer swears every claim is individually certain and refuses to acknowledge the batch-level worry at all -- tell me what the procedure says to do about her, because she's usually right and it's infuriating.
