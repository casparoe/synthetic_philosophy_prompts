I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy preparing a proposal for a twelve-month research fellowship, and I would like you to turn a half-formed idea into a polished document of roughly two pages.

The idea: the debate over the sunk cost fallacy assumes, almost without exception, that the person deciding whether to continue is the person who paid. Yet many of the cases where sunk-cost reasoning has real grip are not like that. A government presses on with a war because, its officials insist, withdrawal would mean the dead had died for nothing. A student finishes a degree her parents impoverished themselves to fund. A junior scholar spends years completing a deceased mentor's unfinished manuscript. In each case the cost is sunk for someone else, and continuing is framed not as recouping a loss but as honoring a person. My hunch is that these cases are either the fallacy dressed in moral language, or they reveal a distinct normative structure — something to do with debts to the dead, or with the idea that a sacrifice acquires its meaning from what it is later made to produce — which the standard textbook examples (the concert ticket in the snowstorm, the failing factory) simply never make visible.

The proposal should state the question precisely; explain why both the orthodox view that rationality requires ignoring sunk costs and the main revisionist defenses (sunk costs as evidence about one's own past judgment, or as commitment devices) leave this class of cases untouched; describe what I would actually do, in phases across the twelve months, including how purely philosophical work makes progress here — for instance, by constructing paired cases that differ only in who bore the cost and testing candidate principles such as the claim that a cost is sunk only relative to the agent who paid it; say what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and keep the scope realistic for one researcher and one year. No bibliography or citations, please — I will supply those myself.
