I retired from my university's chemistry department about two years ago and started sitting in on the philosophy department's lunchtime reading group. They vote on cases — which of two outcomes is worse — and I've been keeping minutes, because I think they've walked into a contradiction and none of them see it yet. Here's their record so far:

1. One hour of truly excruciating pain is worse than a month of dull backache. (Unanimous.)
2. I then constructed a long spectrum, step by step: each step halves the intensity and doubles the duration, from the hour of agony down to roughly thirty years of a faint recurring itch. Asked about each adjacent pair, they said the milder-and-longer one was *not worse* — every single link, no exceptions.
3. But when I put the two endpoints side by side — one hour of agony vs. thirty years of the itch — they voted that the thirty years of itch is worse, and were pretty confident about it.

To me this smells like one of two things: either there's a hidden threshold somewhere in the middle of the spectrum (one of the links actually flips, but the pairwise difference is too small to notice), or they've quietly given up on "worse than" being transative without realizing it. Maybe there's a third option I'm missing, like the endpoints just being incomparable somehow.

For next week I've proposed two new cases. First: redo the comparisons but skip every other rung, so each pair sits two steps apart and the intensity gap is big enough to actually feel. Second: offer them a chain of trades — swap your current suffering package for the next rung down plus a chocolate bar, repeated all the way down the spectrum.

Predict how they vote on each of the two new cases, tell me which rule they're actually running, and name the one further case that would settle it between your top two candidate rules. Possibly relevant: these are people who hate being money-pumped almost as much as they hate being wrong.
