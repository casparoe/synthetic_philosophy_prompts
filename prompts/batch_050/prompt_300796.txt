Last night a friend and I argued for hours about Gödel and the philosophy of time, and he kept invoking a popular science book whose account ran roughly as follows: 'In 1949 Gödel found solutions to Einstein's field equations describing rotating universes that contain closed timelike curves, so that a sufficiently patient traveler could journey into her own past. Because telescopic observations show that our universe is not rotating, Gödel concluded that time is an illusion and its apparent flow purely subjective. Einstein was deeply unsettled by this result but could find no mathematical error in it.' My friend takes this to establish that physics has shown there is no objective present, and I pushed back, but I couldn't say precisely where the summary goes wrong. Is it a fair account? I suspect it conflates Gödel's actual conditional argument — about what the mere physical possibility of such worlds would show about the nature of time in any world governed by the same laws, ours included — with a flat assertion that time is unreal, and that it misrepresents what the non-rotation of our universe does and does not matter for his conclusion. Please separate what is accurate in the summary, what is a common simplification, and what Gödel did not in fact claim — and tell me whether my friend's conclusion about the present survives the corrections.
