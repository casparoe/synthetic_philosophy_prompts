Since retiring from a career in experimental biochemistry, I have taken up philosophy with the enthusiasm of a late convert, and I now convene a monthly discussion group — nine of us, mostly retired engineers, physicians, and one former patent attorney — that meets for roughly ninety minutes. Our current preoccupation is the so-called responsibility gap for autonomous systems: the worry that when a sufficiently self-directing machine causes harm, no human being may turn out to be blameworthy in the morally relevant sense, even though the harm plainly cries out for someone to answer for it.

I would like you to design a structured activity for our group that makes the participants feel the force of this gap in their own deliberations, rather than merely reading about it. The design should respect the following constraints:

1. No lecture or framing up front. The group must not hear the phrase "responsibility gap" or any philosopher's name until the debrief. They should be presented with a realistic scenario — I would prefer something set in a domain they know from life, such as a hospital, a municipal works department, or an insurance office, rather than a self-driving car, which they will have half-formed opinions about already.
2. The scenario must be engineered so that blame genuinely cannot settle anywhere, not merely so that it is hard to find. Each candidate bearer of responsibility (the procurement officer who chose the system, the engineers who built it, the staff who trained it on historical data, the operator who oversaw it, the regulator who approved it) must have a defeasible excuse of a recognizably legitimate kind — one concerning what they could reasonably have known, the other concerning what was within their control. The harm must remain serious and uncompensated.
3. The activity must require a real decision from the group, with mild structure to force disagreement: for instance, a fixed pool of blame or liability points that must be distributed among the parties (or assigned to no one), allocated by each participant privately and then defended publicly. The group should discover, in the course of defending their allocations, that every assignment they make feels unjust to someone, including themselves.
4. Include a timeline broken into phases, the written materials each phase requires (you may draft the scenario text itself), the rules of the allocation procedure, and at least one moment where the facilitator deliberately tightens the screws — e.g., revealing a further fact that undermines the allocation the group had begun to converge on.

Then provide a debrief plan: which moments in the activity correspond to which components of the philosophical problem (the epistemic condition on blame, the control condition, the intuition that retribution or compensation is owed regardless), what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run with this kind of audience, and an honest statement of which feature of the responsibility gap the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must simply state.

After the prose, give me a structured summary as a JSON object with the following fields: "phases" (an array of objects, each with "name", "duration_minutes", "materials", and "facilitator_actions"); "blame_candidates" (an array of objects, each with "role", "legitimate_excuse_epistemic", "legitimate_excuse_control", and "why_participants_will_still_want_to_blame_them"); "debrief_mapping" (an array of objects, each pairing an "activity_moment" with the "philosophical_component" it instantiates); "predictable_failures" (an array of strings); and "irreducible_gap" (a string stating what the activity cannot show).
