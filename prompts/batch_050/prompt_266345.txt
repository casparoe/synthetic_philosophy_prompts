Last month I got into a long argument with a coworker that has been nagging at me ever since. I had mentioned an idea I came across on a philosophy podcast -- attributed, I believe, to a philosopher named Scheffler -- roughly the thought that most of us would fall into despair if we learned that humanity would end shortly after our own deaths, even if our own lives remained completely untouched (the podcast used the scenario of a world where no more children are born, so everyone alive gets to live out their days, but there is no next generation). I said this shows that human extinction would be bad in a way that goes beyond the deaths it involves. My coworker dismissed the whole thing with one sentence: "If there's nobody left, there's nobody to be harmed -- no one will be around to miss it." He took this to settle that extinction cannot be worse than the sum of the deaths that bring it about, and that my despair reaction is just sentimentality. I've been turning his sentence over since, and the more I look at it the more claims I think it might be smuggling, but I lack the training to pull them apart cleanly.

Here is what I'd like. Act as a philosopher who works on population ethics and the value of humanity's future, and take your time -- I'd rather you brainstorm widely first and then organize than jump straight to an answer. Unpack my coworker's one-liner into the distinct precise claims it could be making. As a starting point for your taxonomy (feel free to correct or extend it), I can dimly see several candidates: a plain truism that there will be no suffering or grief after everyone is gone; a stronger claim that nothing can be bad unless it is bad *for* someone who exists to undergo it; a claim that you cannot harm people by preventing them from existing, since there is no one to compare against; a claim that the badness of any event is exhausted by the bad experiences it contains, so two scenarios with identical experiences are equally bad; and maybe just a rhetorical point about who could legitimately complain. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or actually false, and what its strongest form would look like.

Then the part I care about most: which of these readings does my coworker's dismissive use actually require? My suspicion is that he is surfing from the platitude to the conclusion via one of the contentious middle readings, but I can't quite locate the slide. If the strongest defensible version of his slogan does *not* get him his conclusion, explain where the gap is. And finally, does our imagined despair at humanity's end -- the podcast's observation -- count as evidence that one of his underlying claims is wrong, or is he entitled to wave it off as a feeling that proves nothing about value? Please lay the readings out as a clearly labeled menu I could bring back to him, with a verdict attached to each.
