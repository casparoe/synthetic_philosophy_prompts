I'm a second-year psychology student who's somehow ended up in a continental philosophy module (long story, needed the credits), and we're doing Sartre at the moment. In Existentialism is a Humanism there's this bit where he's responding to people who call his philosophy too gloomy, and he says the real reason people object is basically the opposite: "For at bottom, what is alarming in the doctrine that I am about to try to explain to you is – is it not? – that it confronts man with a possibility of choice." I keep turning this over because it seems almost backwards from everything I've learned in my actual subject. In psych we're taught that people find too many options paralysing, that they gravitate towards defaults, that they'll go to quite impressive lengths to avoid re-examining a decision they've already made. Sartre seems to be saying the terror of choice is fundamental to the human situation, whereas my lecturers talk about it more like a bug in our decision-making that supermarkets exploit.

So here's what I'd like, and it might be a bit of an odd exercise. Restate Sartre's whole picture of radical freedom and bad faith in the vocabulary I actually work in: cognitive dissonance, motivated reasoning, self-serving attributions, internal vs external locus of control, choice overload, learned helplessness, that sort of thing. Do it as faithfully as you can, ie don't just loosely say "bad faith is kind of like rationalisation" – tell me exactly which of his claims map onto which concepts. But the part I really care about is the residue report at the end. Which bits of his view translate cleanly, which bits only translate if you distort them, and is there anything he's saying that has no counterpart at all in empirical psychology? My gut feeling is that bad faith is just motivated reasoning wearing a fancy French outfit, and that his thing about anguish is roughly what we'd call decision-related anxiety, but I suspect I'm flattening something. If the residue shows that psychology is missing something he captured, say so – and if it shows he was dressing up a fairly ordinary bit of self-deception in grand metaphysical language, say that instead. Also, where does that line about choice being alarming land once you've done the translation? Is it a testable claim, a phenomenological observation, or something else?
