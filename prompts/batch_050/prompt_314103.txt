**Money pumps don't show what you think they show**

The money pump argument against intransitive preferences is famous: if you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, someone can trade you around the cycle, charging you a penny each step, until you're broke and right back where you started. Conclusion: intransitive preferences are irrational. QED.

I think this argument is way less interesting than its reputation, because it smuggles in an assumption nobody defends: that the agent never notices the pattern.

Think about what has to be true for the pump to run. You go around the loop once: A to B to C to A, three pennies lighter. For the pump to keep running, you have to make the same trades again. And again. Any agent capable of holding stable, structured preferences is also capable of noticing, after one loop, that they're being cycled. The pump doesn't exploit intransitivity. It exploits *pattern-blindness* -- a complete failure to update your strategy in response to what the environment is doing to you.

But pattern-blindness is irrational for anyone, whatever their preferences look like. A fully transitive agent who never updates can be got in other ways -- sell them things in frames, exploit the fact they don't learn which contexts make them regret purchases, whatever. The exploitability tracks the blindness, not the intransitivity. So the money pump argument at most proves a conditional: if you live in an adversarial sequence-offer environment AND you're constitutionally incapable of noticing sequences, then transitivity is prudentially advisable. That's a long way from a norm of rationality.

Now, the standard rejoinder: rationality norms shouldn't be contingent on how friendly your environment is. The pump reveals an internal defect; the pennies just make the defect visible. But I want to press on "internal defect." What's the defect, exactly, if it costs you nothing unless someone is actively pumping you? The defender needs a cost that isn't exploitation, and the only candidate I can see is this: intransitive preferences make it undefined what you actually *want* in a three-way choice. There's no best option. That's a real problem -- but notice it's a problem about the coherence of desire, not about money, and the pump was supposed to be the argument, not a colorful illustration of a different argument.

Here's the part I actually want to develop, and where I might be getting carried away. Everything above is about another agent pumping you. But the most common money pump in real life is run by your own future self. My preferences drift: I buy the guitar, two years later I'd prefer the money and the closet space. Past-me and present-me have, jointly, executed a trade cycle and lost value -- someone (the guitar store) extracted the difference. If money pumps ground a rationality norm, then preference change over time should be irrational too, since it's diachronically exploitable in exactly the same way. Almost nobody wants that conclusion. The usual response is "but those are different preferences at different times, not one intransitive preference set" -- fine, but that response individuates preferences by time-slice, and once you do that, the synchronic intransitive agent can play the same game: treat each step of the cycle as a fresh choice situation. Either the diachronic agent is one self with exploitable cross-time structure, or she's a succession of agents each locally fine -- and the intransitive agent can claim whichever one lets her off the hook.

So my tentative conclusion: money pump arguments don't establish a norm against intransitivity. They establish that certain preference structures interact badly with certain environments, which is a claim about strategy, not rationality. The real argument against intransitivity has to be the boring one about there being no best option.
