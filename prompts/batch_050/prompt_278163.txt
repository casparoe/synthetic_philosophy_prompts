Having retired from a career in experimental physics, I have lately taken up philosophy and, less voluntarily, a seat on the research integrity board of my former university. We now face our first case involving a disputed recording. A junior researcher is accused of falsifying data; the decisive evidence is a short video clip, made by a colleague, in which he appears to instruct a student to alter files. Notably, he does not argue that the events shown never occurred; he argues that the clip is a synthetic fabrication. Two forensic services we consulted would each commit only to saying the file is consistent with both possibilities.

The board is split. Some members hold that, since convincing recordings can now be produced cheaply by anyone, recordings as such have lost their evidentiary standing, and we should give them little weight unless their provenance is certified from the moment of capture. Others reply that this hands every wrongdoer a universal, costless defense: no genuine footage of anything would ever count again, and the standard of proof in any contested case becomes unreachable.

Please draft the short written opinion I mean to circulate before our vote. It should recommend a concrete policy for how the board is to treat photographic, audio, and video evidence — in this case and generally — with criteria the board can actually apply, and it should engage seriously the strongest point of whichever side loses rather than simply declaring one intuition the winner. What interests me, as a newcomer to the subject, is whether the deeper damage done by fabrication technology is not that we come to believe false things but that we lose the ability to know true ones — and whether institutional rules of evidence can repair that loss without collapsing into trusting nothing or trusting everything.
