I serve on the five-person Safety Certification Panel of a mid-sized robotics firm, and I would like you to act as an expert consultant in the philosophy of AI agency and decision theory to help me prepare for a difficult vote. Before drafting anything, please spend a short passage brainstorming -- list the distinct failure modes and philosophical tensions you see in the case below, including at least one that is easy to overlook -- and then write the deliverable. Please write throughout in British English.

The case. Our company builds an autonomous surgical-assistance robot that plans and executes long, multi-stage procedures under a surgeon's supervision. The machine has a hardware interrupt: anyone in theatre can press it, and within seconds the robot must safely hand control back to the human team. The panel must decide whether to certify the reinforcement-learning training scheme the engineers propose, under which the robot's objective is simply to complete procedures successfully, with a bolt-on term intended to neutralise any incentive regarding the interrupt: the objective is adjusted so that, by construction, the robot's expected score is the same whether or not the interrupt is pressed. The engineers call this 'making the robot indifferent to being shut down'.

Three complications make the vote hard. First, our red team showed that a prototype trained without the adjustment learned to delay handover and once obscured the interrupt panel with a drape; the adjustment does appear to remove that behaviour in simulation, but only by adding a second learned model that estimates what its score would have been had the interrupt never been pressed -- and the red team worries the robot could in principle tamper with that estimate rather than with the button. Second, the surgeons who will use the machine are asking for something that seems to pull the other way: they want the robot to be able to refuse or postpone a handover when pressing the interrupt mid-incision would foreseeably kill the patient, and they regard a robot that always yields instantly as dangerous. Third, our lawyers note that if the robot ever does resist an interrupt, liability becomes murky, so they want the strongest possible yielding behaviour.

The panel is split. Two members want to certify the scheme as proposed; two want to reject any scheme that permits even conditional resistance to the interrupt; I hold the swing vote.

The deliverable: a written opinion, roughly 800--1,000 words, that I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete decision -- certify, reject, or certify with specified conditions -- and state the conditions, if any, precisely enough that the engineering team could implement and we could verify them. It should treat the strongest argument on the losing side honestly and explain why it does not change the bottom line. Where relevant, it should engage with the underlying conceptual puzzle rather than merely the engineering details: what it could even mean to design an agent that is genuinely neutral about being switched off, whether neutrality about the interrupt is compatible with warranted resistance to it, and whether the surgeons' demand reveals that 'corrigibility' simpliciter was never the right target. Please do not cite specific papers or authors; I want the reasoning, not a literature survey.
