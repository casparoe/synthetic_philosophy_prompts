So I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a big feature on government "nudge units" and behavioral policy. Before I lock in my editorial line I want to check my own views actually hang together, because my editor will definately poke holes in them. Roughly where I stand:

1. Opt-out defaults are a legitimate tool for governments — auto-enrollment into retirement savings, opt-out organ donor registries, that kind of thing. Paradigm cases of acceptable policy.
2. The state has no business overriding a competent adult's informed choices for their own good, which is why I oppose stuff like soda size bans and cigarette prohibition.
3. The main empirical reason defaults work is that most people are inattentive and never make an active choice at all — the default just becomes the outcome.
4. It's straightforwardly wrong when companies exploit consumer inattention — pre-ticked boxes, auto-renewal traps, making cancellation a maze — even when everything was technically disclosed in the fine print somewhere.
5. I'm fine with mandatory calorie labels but against sugar taxes, since the first just informs people and the second constrains them.

Which of these are genuinely in tension, and which only look like it? Rank the tensions by severity, say for each one what would have to give, and then tell me the single cheapest revision. I'm not especially attached to any one of these, so don't pull punches.
