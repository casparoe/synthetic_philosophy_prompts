At a department seminar, two evolutionary biologists argued over a hypothetical case: a ring of salamander populations encircling a valley, where each population interbreeds freely with its neighbours, but where the ring closes, the two endpoint populations live side by side without ever hybridizing. (1) Ana said the whole ring is one species: gene flow connects every population to every other, and any cut along the ring would be arbitrary, since there is no place where interbreeding actually stops. (2) Bruno said the endpoints are two species: where they meet they behave exactly like any two good sympatric species -- no hybrids, distinct courtship calls -- and a criterion that ignores what organisms do where they actually encounter each other is not about species at all. (3) Ana replied that Bruno's criterion is worse than arbitrary, it is incoherent: interbreeding is not transitive, since A breeds with B and B with C while A cannot breed with C, so "same species if connected by interbreeding" fails as a grouping rule, and any alternative based on assortative mating leaves the vast majority of populations, which never occur sympatrically, simply unclassifiable. (4) Bruno replied that Ana's criterion has the mirror-image defect: it makes species identity hostage to external accidents, since a single landslide wiping out one intermediate population would create two species overnight with no change whatsoever in the organisms themselves, and meanwhile it lumps together, as one species, animals that could never produce offspring.
