I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a course on the philosophy of history, and we've just spent three weeks on the debate between Great Man accounts of historical explanation and structural or materialist ones. Our seminar's organizing counterfactual has been whether there would have been a Nazi Germany — or something morally equivalent under a different name — if Hitler had been killed in the First World War. For an end-of-term handout, I'd like to turn that debate into a decision tree that sorts members of our reading group by what they actually believe about historical causation, rather than by which label ("structuralist," "contingency theorist") they find flattering.

Please build the tree as follows. Start from the acceptance of the counterfactual as posed above, and make the very first node the question of whether the counterfactual is even assessable — that is, whether someone who thinks the past is too sensitive to initial conditions for "rewind the tape" questions to have answers belongs on the tree at all, and if so, whether they count as taking a position on the debate or as declining it.

Every subsequent branch should be phrased as a question a person could answer honestly without knowing which position it leads to. At minimum, the tree should contain nodes separating the following commitments, though you may reorganize or subdivide them if the logical structure demands it: (a) whether the morally salient features of the outcome are a genocidal antisemitic regime specifically, or any authoritarian nationalist regime in interwar Germany — i.e., how much of "Nazi Germany" has to recur for the counterfactual to come out one way; (b) whether the structural conditions (Versailles, Weimar's legitimacy deficit, the Depression, existing völkisch movements) made some such regime overdetermined, probable-but-avoidable, or merely possible; (c) whether a different individual could plausibly have filled Hitler's functional role, or whether something about him — as a focal point solving a coordination problem among competing far-right factions, say — was doing causal work no available substitute could have done; and (d) whether the causal responsibility of individuals, as opposed to conditions, is even the kind of thing that admits of degrees, or whether assigning causal weights to people versus structures is a category error.

Each leaf should be a named position (Great Man contingency, inevitabilist structuralism, an accelerationist view on which Hitler hastened but did not cause the outcome, a replacement-leader view, counterfactual skepticism, and any others the space generates). Flag any two leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate and say why. If the question-space contains an unoccupied combination — one nobody in the debate seems to hold — note it and diagnose briefly whether the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored. Aim for eight to twelve branch nodes, and add a one-line note at each node on which historical facts the answer hinges on, so the handout shows students where the philosophy ends and the historiography begins.
