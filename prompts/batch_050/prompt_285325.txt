So I'm a novelist, halfway through my third book, and I've been chewing on an idea I half-remember from Gödel, Escher, Bach. Hofstadter argues that any message comes in layers: there's the actual content, and then there's stuff wrapped around it that signals hey, this is a message, and here's how to decode me. His example is a record sent into space -- the grooves are just grooves unless whoever finds it also figures out how to build a record player. Meaning, on this picture, doesn't sit inside the message alone; it shows up in the interaction between the message and whatever machinery decodes it.

Now compare two pictures of what my job as a novelist even is:

Picture one: the meaning is in the book. The text is the complete message, and good writing is basically lossless encoding -- if I do my job right, anyone who can decode the text at all gets what I meant, and misreadings are decoder failures, not author failures.

Picture two: the reader brings the record player. The book is a record, not a player, and most of the real work happens in machinery I don't control -- the reader's culture, language, assumptions about what a story even is. My job is to cut grooves that work well with the players my actual readers happen to have.

Which picture is closer to the truth? A few specific things I want you to grapple with rather than hand-wave past:

- Prefaces, genre conventions, epigraphs, covers, the sheer fact of something being sold as "a novel" -- these all look like attempts to smuggle the decoding instructions into the package itself. How far can that go? Can a text ever fully bootstrap its own reader, or is there always something the reader has to supply that no amount of framing can provide? Where's the the actual limit?

- Does the answer change with the reader's distance from me? Someone in my own city right now vs. a reader in 300 years vs. a culture with a completely different storytelling tradition. Is picture one truer for near readers and picture two truer for far readers, or is that too easy a compromise that dodges the real question?

- Practically: which picture makes me a better writer? Note that the truer picture and the more useful picture might not be the same one. A novelist who believes meaning is fully in the text will write differently -- better? worse? -- than one who believes readers co-author everything.

One constraint: don't just split the difference and say it's both, it's an interaction, etc. Everyone says that. Tell me which picture you'd actually put your weight on, and then tell me where the losing picture still bites.
