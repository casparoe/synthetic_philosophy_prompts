So I'm a PhD student (political theory, but I hang around the ML ethics people a lot) and at our reading group last week two people got into an argument about the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness that never got resolved, and I want an outside verdict. Quick setup: we were discussing the well-known result that, roughly, when base rates differ between groups, a risk score can't simultaneously satisfy calibration within each group and equal false positive / false negative rates across groups, except in degenerate cases like perfect prediction.

Person 1's take was that the theorem settles basically nothing normatively. Her argument: the result is just arithmetic, a consequence of the fact that two groups have different base rates, and nobody ever thought you could make all the fairness metrics come out equal at once -- practitioners always knew you pick one metric and justify it. So citing "impossibility" as if it were some deep insight about fairness is grandstanding; the real work is the ordinary normative question of which metric fits which decision context, and the theorem adds zero to that. She also said the framing lets companies off the hook -- they wave the theorem around, say "fairness is mathematically impossible, oh well," and then quietly pick whichever metric is cheapest or best for their exposure, dressed up as a principled compromise.

Person 2 pushed back hard. He said the theorem does real work precisely because it blocks the "just optimize everything" response that's natural to engineers and to regulators writing guidance, and that before these results people genuinely did treat the metrics as jointly achievable goals. He also made a subtler point I couldn't fully follow: he claimed Person 1's "it's just the base rates" line actually concedes his position, because once you see the tension is driven by base-rate differences, you realize the metrics are encoding different judgments about whether those base-rate differences are themselves legitimate inputs -- so the impossibility result is a diagnostic that forces the normative disagreement into the open rather than a triviality. She replied that a result "forcing a conversation" is not the same as the result being substantive, a horoscope can force a conversation too. He said that's uncharitable, that diagnostic value IS substantive value in a field that was running on confused formalizations. It kind of stalled there.

Who's right about what? I'd like a point-by-point adjudication: is the impossibility result arithmetically trivial in a way that deflates it, or does triviality-claims like hers undersell what the formalization actually showed? Does citing the theorem function as an excuse for companies, or is that a separable rhetorical misuse? And on their core clash -- whether "it forces the normative question" counts as the result doing substantive work -- which side has the better of it, and is there something both of them are missing?
