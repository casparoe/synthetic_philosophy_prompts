Is there an established concept for the following pattern, and if not, what should it be called? I work in communications, and over the years I have watched the same thing happen to three colleagues. Each was asked to defend a version of events they knew at the outset to be false — a sales figure, the cause of a failed product, the timeline of a resignation. That first step is ordinary lying, and I am not asking about it. What interests me is what came next. After a year or two of repeating the story, each seemed to lose access to the truth altogether: not merely refusing to admit the facts, but apparently unable to retrieve them even in private settings where nothing was at stake, and one of them began genuinely reorganising his files and diary around the false version. By the end, being corrected did not produce the embarrassment liars show, but sincere moral outrage. So the pattern is: a strategic lie, knowingly adopted, which then digests its own author, until the distinction between liar and believer no longer applies. It is not hypocrisy, since nothing is concealed. It does not seem to be ordinary self-deception either, because there was no antecedent wish or fear doing the work — the false belief was installed from the outside, as a job requirement. And it is not quite Orwell's doublethink, since they never hold truth and falsehood side by side. If the nearest concepts are the ones I have mentioned, please explain precisely where my case diverges from each; if nothing established fits, define the phenomenon properly, with boundary cases — for instance, what would show that someone had merely become a practised liar rather than undergone the transformation I am describing.
