Back when I was still working I followed the Van Meegeren affair with some interest -- Dutch painter, forged Vermeers around the war, his big religious canvas was praised by leading experts as among Vermeer's finest, and after he confessed (partly to save his skin, since one of his "Vermeers" had ended up with Göring and he was facing a collaboration charge) the very same paintings were suddenly seen as obviously second-rate. The paint never changed; only the label did.

Take this episode and run it through the debate between perceptual formalism about aesthetic value -- the worth of a picture is all there on the canvas, in what you can see -- and the view that aesthetic value essentially involves the object's actual history and authorship. Do the mapping carefully. What does each side predict about the valuations before and after the exposure? Does the swing embarrass the formalists, or can they absorb it (snobbery, expectation shaping perception itself, the crowd correcting for prestige rather than for beauty)? And in the other direction: what does the episode do to the historical view? Doesn't it prove too much -- if authorship can flip a masterpiece into a curiosity, it seems we can't trust our own eyes at all, which is an odd result for a theory of *aesthetic* value.

One more thing, since I'm an old experimentalist: is there a clean version of this as an actual experiment (same image, different provenance story) whose outcome would settle something, or would it just re-run the same ambiguity under lab conditions?
