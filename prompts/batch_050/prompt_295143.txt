So I got into this huge argument with my roommate last night that went til like 2am, over whether the state should be allowed to require a license before people have kids, the same way it licenses drivers or people who cut hair. His pitch was basically: driving badly mostly endangers strangers and we license that without blinking, parenting badly wrecks a specific defenseless person for decades and we just... let anyone do it, no questions asked. He threw in this thing he'd read, I think from a legal philosopher named Feinberg, about kids having a right to an "open future" -- the idea that parents owe their children a childhood that doesn't slam doors shut, and that this right belongs to the kid, held against the parents, not just a vague social hope. His claim was that if you take that right seriously, some minimal licensing (screening out the clearly dangerous, maybe requiring basic preparation) is not just permitted but required, the way we'd think the state must step in if a parent wanted to deny a kid all schooling.

My pushback was mostly vibes at first, which he rightly called out, so let me steelman it: predicting who will be an abusive parent is noisy and the false positives fall on the poor and minorities, the state is terrible at raising kids so removal isn't obviously better than a mediocre home, reproduction feels like a more fundamental interest than driving, and there's something creepy about the government deciding who gets to create a person. He said all of that is about *this* licensing scheme, not licensing in principle, and that I'd happily support intervention after abuse is proven, so I already accept the state standing between parent and child -- I'm just insisting the evidence arrive in the form of a harmed kid first. That one stung, ngl.

So here's what I want: your actual credence that *some* form of parental licensing -- set aside whether it's politically feasible, just whether it would be morally justified in principle if a reasonably competent state administered it -- is correct, and more importantly the decomposition. Break it into the sub-questions: how much weight the child's-right-to-an-open-future idea can really bear (does it support prospective screening or only after-the-fact intervention?), how predictive we could actually be about serious maltreatment, whether creating a person is the kind of interest the state may condition at all, and whether "the state does it badly" is an objection to the principle or only to us. Rough probability on each, how they combine into your final number, and which sub-question your answer is most sensitive to -- i.e., where should my roommate and I go read more carefully if we want to figure out which of us is wrong?
