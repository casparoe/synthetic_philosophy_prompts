I'm writing a term paper for a philosophy of language seminar and want to build it around my own judgments about generic sentences — bare-plural and indefinite-singular statements of the form "Ks are F." Here are the sentences and how each strikes me:

1. "Ducks lay eggs." True, even though roughly half of all ducks are male and never lay anything.
2. "Birds fly." True, despite penguins and ostriches.
3. "Mosquitoes carry malaria." True, even though only a minuscule fraction of mosquitoes actually carry the parasite.
4. "Pit bulls maul children." False, or at least something I'd push back on if someone asserted it — even if you stipulated for the sake of argument that pit bull attacks occur at roughly the same rate among pit bulls as malaria-carrying does among mosquitoes. This one and (3) are the pair I can't easily reconcile: structurally the cases look parallel, but my verdicts differ.
5. "Books are paperbacks." False, even in a market where most books sold are in fact paperbacks.
6. "Doctors are men." False, even in a country where most doctors are men. This feels like the same phenomenon as (5).
7. "Drivers stop at red lights." True, even though everyone routinely sees violations — it seems to express a norm rather than a statistic, unlike the others.
8. "Sharks attack swimmers." I go back and forth. It sounds more acceptable than (4) but the underlying statistics are comparably tiny, and I can't tell whether my hesitation tracks a real difference or just which group is being generalized about.

What I'd like from you: first, try to find a single principle of generic truth-conditions (or, if one won't do it, a small set of clauses) that delivers all of these verdicts, stating it precisely enough that the case motivating each clause is identifiable. The interesting question is whether there is a non-ad-hoc way to separate (3) from (4) — something in the neighborhood of "generics about dangerous properties are true at low prevalence" gets (3) but threatens to overturn (4), so you will have to either complicate the principle to save both or tell me honestly which verdict you think has to go and why. Second, apply the resulting principle to two cases I haven't listed and tell me what it predicts: "Snakes are slimy" (in fact snakes' skin is dry) and "Teenagers are irresponsible." Third, since the paper is partly about the method itself, briefly address whether verdicts like (4)'s should even be treated as data about semantics, or whether they belong to pragmatics or social cognition instead — I have a view but don't want to anchor you, so I'll leave mine out.

Two requests about presentation. Please don't cite or name specific published theories or philosophers; describe positions by their content and I'll supply the references myself, since I need to check them against the seminar reading list anyway. And please end with a note on how confident you are in the principle you've landed on, singling out the specific claim or case treatment you are least sure of and saying what kind of evidence or argument would change your mind about it.
