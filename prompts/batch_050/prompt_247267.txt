Last night I had a long and rather heated argument with a friend about whether culture can genuinely cause economic outcomes, and I want to stress-test the position I found myself defending.

My friend's view is a thoroughgoing scepticism: shared values, beliefs, and norms never do real causal work in explaining why some societies prosper and others stagnate. On her account, whenever it looks as though a society's values explain its economic trajectory, one of three things is actually going on: the values are post-hoc rationalisations of material interests (people believe whatever justifies what they were going to do anyway); the real cause is some institutional or material factor that happens to correlate with the values (geography, legal origin, disease environment, colonial history); or the alleged causal story is just an untestable narrative that survives because any outcome can be retrofitted to the culture in question. She allowed one further, subtler objection that I found harder to shake: even where values clearly do influence behaviour, the influence always runs through concrete mechanisms — child-rearing practices, schooling, religious organisations, workplace norms enforced by sanctions — and once you have specified those mechanisms, "culture" is merely an umbrella label for the bundle, not itself a cause. Calling culture a cause, she said, is a bookkeeping convenience, like saying "the weather" ruined the picnic.

I defended the opposing view: that durable, transmitted systems of value can be genuine difference-makers — that two populations facing similar material conditions can diverge economically because of what they regard as honourable, obligatory, or admirable, and that this is a causal claim in the same respectable sense as claims about institutions or policies. I invoked the broad Weberian idea that an ethic can shape economic conduct, though I do not want to defend Weber's particular historical thesis, only the general causal category.

Here is what I want from you. Tell me what would count against my position. I do not want the generic culture-versus-institutions survey; I want a map of my position's exposure. Specifically:

1. Which observations, findings, or arguments would actually threaten the claim that culture can be a cause? Please rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about what makes each one damaging — does it refute the claim, restrict its scope, or only embarrass particular applications of it?

2. What would the sophisticated defenders of my position — people who work on culture and economic development for a living — themselves concede as genuine threats, as opposed to what only hostile critics press? In particular, how much damage does the identification problem do (reverse causation from prosperity to values, omitted third factors, the difficulty of separating transmission from selection), and is there any evidence that defenders treat as load-bearing rather than merely suggestive?

3. Address my friend's bookkeeping objection directly. If every causal claim about culture decomposes into claims about specific transmission mechanisms, does anything remain for "culture" to be the cause of? Is there a principled difference between a genuine higher-level cause and a mere umbrella label, and if so, what would settle which side of the line culture falls on? I would especially like to know whether this objection is actually specific to culture, or whether it generalises to institutions, policies, and other things my friend is happy to call causes — in which case it may prove too much.

4. Finally, assess whether my position has been qualified into unfalsifiability. If any failed prediction can be met by redescribing the relevant culture more finely, or by saying the values were not really internalised, or by pushing the effect further into the past, then perhaps my friend wins by default. Say plainly whether you think the position risks anything, and if it does not, whether that is fatal for a claim of this kind or simply the normal condition of macro-historical hypotheses.

One more thing: I found myself most moved, during the argument, by cases of apparent persistence — values that seem to track ancestry rather than current circumstances. If that is the strongest card in my hand, say what would have to be true for it to trump her objections, and what evidence would show that such persistence is real causation rather than the shadow of something else.

Please write your answer in British English.
