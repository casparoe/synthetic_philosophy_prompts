I want to do a little reflective equilibrium exercise about mechanisms. Context: I've been thinking about anomaly detection — anything that's supposed to flag when "the mechanism" is misbehaving presupposes an answer to "what counts as the same mechanism", and I've realized my intuitions there are a mess. Here are my verdicts on some cases; I want to know what principle, if any, is behind them.

Cases (same mechanism, or a different one?):

1. A watchmaker replaces a worn gear in a mechanical watch with a newly machined identical gear. Same mechanism, obviously. Not even an event worth logging.
2. A watchmaker rips out the whole escapement and installs a quartz circuit. The hands sweep exactly as before, it keeps the same time. My verdict: different mechanism now, even though nothing about the watch's function changed.
3. Over ten years, every part of a mechanical watch gets replaced one at a time, always with an identical part. My verdict: same mechanism the whole way through.
4. An enzyme in a cell picks up a point mutation; the protein still folds and catalyzes the same reaction, just ~5% slower. Same mechanism, mildly degraded.
5. You knock out a bacterium's main pathway for making some metabolite, and a normally dormant backup pathway gets upregulated, so the cell keeps making it at 80% of the old rate. My verdict: the cell is now running a different mechanism to make the stuff, even though the output is nearly the same.
6. A pumping station has a primary pump and a designed-in backup on a failover switch. The primary dies, the backup kicks in, output unchanged. My verdict: same mechanism, working as designed — the backup was part of the mechanism all along.
7. A backend service has its sorting routine swapped from quicksort to mergesort; same inputs, same outputs, same latency profile. If I'm on call monitoring this service I'd honestly call it the same mechanism, but if I'm profiling the code I'd say different one. This case makes me suspect "same mechanism" is relative to a level of description.

Where I think the tensions are: 5 and 6 look structurally identical (a latent route takes over, output preserved) but I gave opposite verdicts. Maybe the difference is that the backup pump was designed in while the bacterial pathway just happens to be there — but that's a wierd thing for mechanism-identity to hinge on, and the dormant pathway is as much part of the organism's inherited organization as the backup pump is of the station's. And 2 vs 3: you could plausibly get from the mechanical watch to the quartz one through a chain of small enough swaps (hybrid movements exist), and if each small swap preserves the mechanism and sameness is transitive, my verdict in 2 falls. So either same-mechanism isn't transitive (sounds bad) or one of my verdicts is wrong.

What I want: a principle, or a couple of candidates, that says when a change is a deviation *within* a mechanism versus a *replacement* of the mechanism. Stated precisely enough that I can see which of my verdicts it saves and which it overturns, and which case motivates each clause. Then tell me what it predicts for cases I didn't list — a neural net whose weights get fine-tuned, a market where trading bots gradually replace human traders, that kind of thing. And connect it back to the anomaly detection framing: if mechanism-identity turns out level-relative (case 7), does an anomaly detector just have to commit to a level, and if so, what does the committing?

Also: if you think the request itself is confused — say there's no real relation of mechanism-sameness for a principle to capture, and I'm just asking you to systematize my verbal habits — then tell me that instead of forcing a tidy answer. I'd rather hear the question's broken than get a fake-elegant solution.
