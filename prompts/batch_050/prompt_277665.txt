ok so what would a theory of when a neural network *has* a representation actually have to deliver? half of interpretability writing just assumes that if a probe reads a feature off the activations, or ablating some direction flips the output, then the network represents that feature, and the other half grumbles that this is us reading tea leaves with extra steps. I want the spec before anyone argues about particular models. give me the requirements list: the cases it must sort correctly (a probe finding a direction vs the network actually using that direction downstream; a lookup table that memorized its training set; a frozen random-init network whose activations happen to be decodable anyway; superposition where one direction carries many features; a feature a human can decode but nothing downstream ever reads), the distinctions it cant collapse (carrying information about X vs representing X; representing X vs representing X correctly -- a state has to be capable of being *wrong* about X, which a bare thermometer-style indicator never is), and the demands from practice it shouldn't contradict (interventions on the supposed representation should change behavior; representation talk should earn its keep over a purely causal-mechanistic redescription, or else the theory should just say its dispensable). for each requirement give the case or consideration behind it, and mark it non-negotiable or camp-specific prejudice -- e.g. insisting the state be consumed by some downstream mechanism smells like one camp's teleology sneaking in through the back door, but convince me either way. the part I most want: is some natural subset of the requirements jointly unsatisfiable? my hunch is that "no interpreter anywhere in the content-fixing story" + "misrepresentation must be possible" + "content fixed by actual causal covariation alone" already contradict each other (the old disjunction problem lurking in there), and if the requirements list itself is a little theorem I want the derivation, not just a gesture at it. finish by saying which single requirement knocks out the most standard contenders -- informational accounts, causal-role accounts, structural-similarity accounts, trained-for accounts -- a quick scorecard against the list is fine but keep the weight on the criteria. purpose constraint, since adequacy is relative: the theory has to be good enough to underwrite claims like "the model has an internal goal" that people want to lean on for safety arguments, not just good enough for a paper title.
