Two friends of mine — Mara, a philosophy student, and Jon, an economist — argued over dinner about what Harsanyi's aggregation theorem actually establishes. For context: the theorem says that if each individual's preferences over risky prospects satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, society's preferences do too, and society's ranking always respects unanimous individual rankings, then social utility must be representable as a weighted sum of the individuals' utility functions.

Mara opened by saying this is close to a proof of utilitarianism from thin premises: rationality constraints plus a weak requirement to respect people's preferences, and out pops the conclusion that society should aggregate individual utilities additively. Jon answered that she was conflating two things. Von Neumann–Morgenstern utility functions encode attitudes toward risk, not how well off people are, so a weighted sum of them has no automatic claim to measure total welfare; and the theorem leaves the weights unconstrained, so it doesn't even deliver equal concern for persons.

Mara replied that the weights problem dissolves under Harsanyi's impartial-observer construction: behind a veil of ignorance where you have an equal chance of turning out to be anyone, maximizing your own expected utility just is maximizing average utility, so equal weights fall out of rationality alone. Jon countered that this imports an equiprobability assumption, and worse, quietly presupposes that one person's utility numbers can be meaningfully compared with another's — each individual's function is fixed only up to positive affine transformation, so the formalism itself provides no units in which such comparisons could be made. The argument assumes the interpersonal comparisons the utilitarian needs to establish.

Mara's final move was concessive: even granting all of that, the theorem still rules out every rival view that refuses additive separability across people — prioritarianism, egalitarianism, maximin — because such views violate the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms at the social level. So the theorem at least narrows the field to additive theories. Jon's last word was that this concedes his real point: if the exclusions come from imposing those axioms on society, then the axioms themselves are carrying the controversial ethical content, and the theorem repackages the dispute rather than resolving it.

I couldn't settle it. Please go through the exchange move by move: who is right about each specific claim, what if anything both sides are missing, and who is more right overall about what the theorem shows.
