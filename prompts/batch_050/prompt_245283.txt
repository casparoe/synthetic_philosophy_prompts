At a neighborhood association meeting last week, I watched two neighbors, Dana and Marcus, get into a surprisingly heated argument about a third neighbor who wasn't there to defend himself. The facts are undisputed: this neighbor had a large, dying tree in his yard taken down not by a licensed tree service but by a friend with a chainsaw, in order to save money. The work took an entire Saturday, some of the cut limbs swung out over the adjoining yard where young children often play, and he told none of the neighbors in advance. Nothing went wrong; the tree came down safely, and everyone agrees it genuinely needed to come down eventually. Dana's position was that the outrage is irrational: blameworthiness has to be anchored in outcomes, and since the actual outcome was a necessary job completed without incident, there is nothing to condemn -- at most the neighbor was a bit impolite. Marcus replied that the moral wrong was complete the moment the neighbor unilaterally imposed an elevated risk of serious harm on everyone nearby without their consent, and that the lucky outcome does nothing to launder it retroactively; we just happen to punish lucky and unlucky recklessness differently because we can only prove the latter, not because they differ in wrongness.

Dana then pushed back with a reductio: every time Marcus drives his car he imposes a small risk of death on strangers who never consented to it, so if non-consensual risk imposition is wrong per se, we are all constantly wronging one another and civilized life is a tissue of unexpiated wrongs -- the sensible conclusion is that we tacitly accept a baseline of background risk, and the real question is whether this tree job exceeded it by enough to matter. Marcus conceded the baseline point but argued that the difference is precisely that driving is regulated, insured, and reciprocally beneficial, whereas the neighbor quietly transferred a cost (risk) onto others purely to capture a private benefit (the savings), and unilaterally raising someone else's risk above the accepted baseline for one's own profit is wrong even when it stays below the level of catastrophe. Dana's rejoinder was that this concedes her deeper point: if the wrong is really just bypassing the consent-and-compensation mechanism, then it is a procedural discourtesy rather than a grave moral offense, since the neighbors would almost certainly have agreed had they been asked, perhaps in exchange for a case of wine; wrongs that could have been dissolved by a small payment ex ante cannot be very serious. Marcus's final move was that this presumption of hypothetical consent is exactly what is objectionable: you cannot assume in advance that people would sell you permission to endanger their children at any price, some risks are not compensable at all, and treating consent as something you can infer on others' behalf whenever it is convenient for you hollows out the very idea of consent.

Neither of them convinced the other, and I genuinely could not decide who had the better of it, so I am putting it to you. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the main steps -- Dana's outcome-anchoring, Marcus's completed-wrong reply, the driving reductio, Marcus's baseline-and-benefit distinction, Dana's hypothetical-consent deflation, and Marcus's non-compensability response -- say whether it succeeds, fails, or succeeds only in a limited form, and justify the assessment. Then identify what you take to be the actual crux of the disagreement (is it about moral luck, about consent, about compensability, or something else entirely?), say what both parties may be missing, and give an overall verdict on who is more nearly right about the neighbor with the chainsaw.
