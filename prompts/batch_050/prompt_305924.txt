My housemate does philosophy of science and we've been fighting about my archaeology thesis for a week and I still can't pin down where he goes wrong. His argument:

(1) Any inference from material remains to the beliefs or institutions of the people who left them has to go through analogy with ethnographically or historically known cultures.
(2) Those known cultures are seperated from prehistoric ones by enormous gulfs in ecology, technology, and history, so every such analogy is contestable.
(3) So for any claim about prehistoric belief or social organization, there's at least one rival interpretation that fits the same remains.
(4) When rival hypotheses fit the evidence equally well, neither one is justified.
(5) So no claim about prehistoric beliefs or institutions is ever justified. (His parade case: a cache of buried figurines -- goddesses? ancestors? toys? teaching dolls? The dirt can't tell us.)

The problem: run the same argument on a soot-blackened pot and it "shows" we can't know the pot was used for cooking (maybe the soot was ritual), and run it on a flint blade and it "shows" we can't know it ever cut anything. He concedes those conclusions are absurd, he uses them in his own work. So either the argument proves too much, or there's a line between inferring function and inferring meaning that he needs but never draws. Which step do you actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Fair warning: I'd most like to keep (3), because in the figurine case it just seems flat-out true.
