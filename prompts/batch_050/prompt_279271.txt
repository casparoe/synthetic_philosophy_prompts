I've been a mod on a big nutrition discord server for like four years, and our main rule is: don't state health claims as fact unless you actually know what your talking about. Enforcing it has been a nightmare and its made me think alot about what the rule even should be. Then I fell down a wikipedia rabbit hole and found out philosophers have this whole fight about the "norm of assertion" -- is the rule for saying something that you know it, or that it's true, or just that you reasonably believe it?

So here's what I want. Restate that debate in the vocabulary of community moderation, which is the world I actually think in: what mods can and can't check, what a violation looks like from the outside, warnings, appeals, precedent, good faith vs bad faith posters. Be as precise as you can. Then give me a residue report: which parts of the debate translate cleanly into mod-speak, which parts you had to fudge, and which parts have no moderation counterpart at all.

Some cases from my actual server to test your translation on: a user posts "turmeric reduces inflammation," which turns out to be true, but they got it from a horoscope app. Another user posts something false after genuinely careful research and is devastated when corrected. A third clearly knows their stuff but can never produce sources when asked. Under each candidate norm, what do the mods do?

And the real question: the truth norm seems unenforceable since we can't check truth at post time, and the knowledge norm seems weird since you can break it without knowing you did. Does the fact that some of this debate won't survive translation mean moderation is missing something, or that the philosophers are arguing about something less real than they think?
