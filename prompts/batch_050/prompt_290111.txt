I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm currently three drafts deep into a piece about a genuinely weird corner of neurology: pain asymbolia and related conditions, where people insist they still feel pain -- same location, same burning or stabbing quality -- but that it no longer bothers them. Surgeons sometimes see this after cingulotomies and certain older brain operations, and morphine patients describe something similar. The piece is fine on the neuroscience, but I keep getting stuck on the philosophy underneath it, which is basically: what makes pain bad in the first place? I figured I'd work it out by checking my gut on cases, and now my gut seems to be contradicting itself, so I want to know what principle, if any, captures my verdicts -- and if there isn't one, which verdict you'd sacrifice.

Here are my verdicts. The asymbolia patients: I think they're genuinely better off, that something bad has been removed even though the sensation remains. Same for the morphine patient who says the pain is unchanged but she just doesn't mind it anymore -- better off, though maybe something is lost too. The masochist who enjoys the sting: nothing bad is happening to him, qua pain. But then: someone who's been conditioned over years by an abusive partner to welcome the pain of being hurt, and sincerely reports liking it -- I want to say that pain is still bad for her, which seems to clash with the masochist verdict, but I can't drop either. A dog yelping with a thorn in its paw: obviously bad for the dog, even though the dog presumably has no attitudes *about* its pain, it just has it. A meditator who's trained away all aversion and says she experiences pain as pure sensation: I think she's not suffering, though I say it with less confidence than the asymbolia case, which is odd because they look like the same case. And here's the one that breaks everything: a person in the depths of a depressive episode who reports total indifference -- the toothache doesn't bother him, but then again nothing bothers him anymore. On my asymbolia reasoning I should say the toothache has stopped being bad for him, but that seems clearly, even horribly, wrong. He's not free of suffering; he's drowning in it.

So: is there a principle about what makes pain bad that gets all of these, or close? I'd want it stated precisely enough that I can see which case motivates which clause, and I want an honest list of which of my verdicts it can't save. And tell me what your principle predicts for two cases I haven't made up my mind about: first, a pill that removes the unpleasantness of grief while leaving the sadness fully intact -- is taking it a way of suffering less, or a way of losing something that mattered? Second, whether it even makes sense to ask about the badness of pain in something -- a late-term fetus, say, or one of those brain organoids people keep writing alarmist pieces about -- that might have the sensation but couldn't possibly have attitudes about it. If your answer there is "the question doesn't make sense," explain why without making the dog case collapse too.
