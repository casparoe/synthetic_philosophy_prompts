In forecasting tournaments and on prediction platforms, participants state numerical probabilities for future events, and after each event resolves their forecasts are evaluated with a strictly proper scoring rule — typically the Brier score, which penalizes each forecast by the squared distance between the stated probability and the outcome coded as 1 or 0. Rankings, prizes, and professional reputations rest entirely on these scores: the quality of a forecaster's reasoning, the evidence available to her at the time, and whether her belief was justified play no official role, and a lucky guess is rewarded exactly as much as a carefully derived probability whenever the two happen to be equally close to the outcome.

What does this institutional practice presuppose about epistemic value? Reconstruct its implicit theory. Does it commit its designers to the claims that beliefs come in degrees, that the epistemic goodness of a degree of belief consists in its accuracy — its closeness to the truth — and that accuracy is the fundamental, perhaps the only, epistemic good, so that justification and evidential support are at most instrumentally valuable? Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits comfortably with: is a scoring-rule institution compatible with a view on which knowledge, rather than accuracy of credence, is the basic epistemic aim, or with an evidentialist view on which beliefs are to be assessed by their relation to the evidence available at the time rather than by outcomes? Does the practice presuppose anything about the nature of probability itself — does scoring a credence against a binary outcome assume that there is a fact of the matter about the event but none about the chance of the event, and would the practice still make sense on a purely objectivist interpretation of probability? Attend also to internal tensions: proper scoring rules are often chosen because they incentivize honest reporting, a pragmatic rationale that sits uneasily with the claim that scores measure epistemic value; and the dominance arguments sometimes used to justify such rules hold that any incoherent credence function is accuracy-dominated by a coherent one, which presupposes that accuracy comparisons across possible worlds are meaningful. Finally, say what would have to change in the institution if the presupposed theory were replaced — for instance, if forecasters were to be evaluated by a knowledge-first standard, or by the quality of their evidence-gathering and updating rather than by proximity to outcomes.
