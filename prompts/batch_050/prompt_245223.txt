For the past several months I have been informally apprenticing under the head of evaluations at a small AI safety organization — call her M. — and before I start writing proposals of my own I want to check whether I have correctly internalized her taste. Here are her verdicts on the last six evaluation proposals that crossed her desk:

1. Approved: adversarially red-team the current frontier model to elicit dangerous dual-use information that its safety training was specifically intended to suppress. Small team, two weeks.
2. Approved: probe whether the frontier model behaves differently when given reason to believe it is being evaluated than when given reason to believe it is deployed.
3. Rejected: benchmark the frontier model's accuracy and helpfulness across a large, representative sample of ordinary user queries.
4. Rejected: measure the maximum performance of a clearly sub-frontier open-weights model under heavy scaffolding and best-of-n sampling.
5. Approved: a cheap screening eval checking whether the frontier model gives consistent answers about its own goals and constraints across paraphrases and conversational contexts.
6. Rejected: a months-long interpretability audit of the sub-frontier open model's internal representations.

The proposal now on the table is to run the evaluation-awareness probe from (2) on the sub-frontier open model from (4) and (6). It is cheap, and the proposer argues that small models can exhibit precursors of the behavior, that results at that scale are at least weakly informative about frontier systems, and that waiting until the behavior shows up at the frontier means only ever studying it after it matters.

Predict M's verdict. More than the verdict, I want the rule she appears to be running, because I can construct at least two that fit her entire record. On one reading she is a worst-case person with a frontier carve-out: fund probes of how a frontier system's safety-relevant behavior holds up under adversarial pressure, distribution shift, or paraphrase; decline average-case benchmarks, which tell you about the modal prompt rather than the tail that actually kills you, and decline anything below the frontier as uninformative about the systems whose failure modes matter. On another reading she is running an apparent-versus-actual-alignment rule: fund behavioral probes of whether surface-level alignment reflects the model's underlying motivations — elicitation of suppressed capabilities, evaluation awareness, consistency of self-reports — at whatever scale is informative, and decline raw capability measurement and audits that test no behavioral hypothesis about that gap. These two rules agree on all six of her past verdicts and disagree on the new case.

Give me your best prediction with reasons, identify which precedent is the closest analog to the new case and which precedents pull against it, and if her record genuinely underdetermines the verdict, say what principle she would be committing to with each choice. If you think a third rule fits her record better than either of mine, state it and redo the prediction under it. Finally, describe the hypothetical verdict of hers — on a case I could put to her next — that would most efficiently distinguish the rules you take to be live candidates, and say whether the disagreement between those rules is ultimately about the worst-case versus average-case distinction or about something else, such as whether results on small models extrapolate to frontier systems at all.
