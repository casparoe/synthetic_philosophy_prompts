So I'm a junior in high school and I'm in this philosophy elective where we do a "paradox of the week" thing, and last week we did the paradox of blackmail and it completly broke our class discussion, like we spent the whole 45 minutes going in circles. For anyone who hasn't seen it: blackmail is weird because the blackmailer is basically threatening to do something they have every legal right to do anyway (like telling people your secret, which is true information and not illegal to share), but offering NOT to do it if you pay up. Either half of that seems fine on its own -- keeping quiet is fine, telling the truth is usually fine -- but putting them together into "pay me or I talk" is a serious crime. The question is why.

Here's where we got stuck, and aparently actual philosophers have been stuck on this for decades too. Some kids in my class said blackmail is wrong because it's coercion, but then someone pointed out that normal coercion involves threatening to do something you have no right to do (like hurting someone), and the blackmailer isn't doing that. Other people said it's about privacy and harm to the victim, but gossip is legal and nobody gets arrested for it, and honestly the victim might prefer the blackmail offer over the secret just getting out for free. Then someone brought up this argument they found online that it's about incentives -- if blackmail is legal, people will spend tons of effort digging up dirt on each other, which is wasteful -- but lots of wasteful stuff is perfectly legal. Our teacher said even the pros haven't settled this and there's like a whole literature of people proposing explanations and other people shooting them down.

So what I actually want to know is not which answer is right, but WHY this debate has gone nowhere for so long. Like what kind of disagreement is this even? Is it secretly a factual disagreement about what would happen if blackmail were legal? Is it a disagreement about what "coercion" means, where everyone is using the same word differently? Or is it that people just have different gut intuitions about whether two legal things can combine into an illegal thing, and nobody can argue for their gut? My own theory is that the two sides are actually asking different questions -- one side asks "what's morally wrong with this one act" and the other asks "what's a good reason for the law to ban it" -- but I might be wrong. And whatever your diagnosis is, what would actually move the debate forward? A better definition? Some kind of study? Or is the whole question maybe badly posed?
