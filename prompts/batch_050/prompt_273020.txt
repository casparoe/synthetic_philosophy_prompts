I'm drafting a short essay for the philosophy-of-language section of my personal blog, where I try to explain live theoretical problems to an audience of interested non-specialists. My current piece proposes a homegrown theory of generic sentences -- bare statements like "ducks lay eggs" and "mosquitoes carry malaria" -- and before I post it I'd like informed comments, since my linguistics background is thin and I may be misdescribing the data or reinventing known mistakes. The essay is below, followed by the specific comments I'm after.

---

**The Two-Route Theory of Generics**

Generic sentences are a standing embarrassment for anyone who thinks language reports statistics. "Ducks lay eggs" is true even though male ducks and ducklings lay nothing, so considerably fewer than half of all ducks ever lay an egg. "Mosquitoes carry malaria" is true even though only a tiny minority of mosquitoes -- females of certain species, and only some of those -- ever carry it. Yet prevalence alone can't be the criterion either: "books are paperbacks" sounds false even though the great majority of books sold are paperbacks, and "Canadians are right-handed" is false even though around ninety percent of Canadians are. No threshold, high or low, separates the good generics from the bad ones.

My proposal is that there are two independent routes to a true generic. On the first route, the characteristic route, "Ks are F" is true when being F belongs to the normal or proper functioning of the kind, so that exceptions count as abnormal instances rather than counterexamples. This handles "ducks lay eggs" (egg-laying is what reproductively normal ducks do, and males and ducklings are simply not the relevant normal cases), "dogs have four legs" (three-legged dogs are damaged, not counterevidence), and rule-governed cases like "bishops move diagonally," where nothing statistical is going on at all. On the second route, the alarm route, "Ks are F" is true when F is a striking or dangerous property and Ks are a salient practical source of it, however rare F is among Ks in absolute terms. This is what makes "mosquitoes carry malaria," "sharks attack swimmers," and "ticks carry Lyme disease" true despite minuscule prevalence: the point of the sentence is a warning, and a warning needs only that the kind be a recognizable channel of the danger. The alarm route also explains the paperback case: being a paperback is neither characteristic of bookhood nor remotely alarming, so neither route is available, and no amount of prevalence saves the generic.

The theory makes some useful predictions. It predicts that "primary-school teachers are women" stays false even where women are a large majority, because femaleness doesn't flow from the nature of the role and isn't alarming either. It predicts that "sea turtles are long-lived" is acceptable as characteristic even if most hatchlings die young, since longevity belongs to what a sea turtle that lives out its natural life achieves. And it predicts a contrast between "that dog bites" and "dogs bite": the latter is a generic warning, acceptable even though most dogs never bite anyone.

I can see two difficulties myself. First, definitional generics like "triangles have three sides" tolerate no exceptions at all, so perhaps there is a third route, or perhaps the characteristic route simply has a zero-tolerance mode. Second, negation behaves oddly: "ducks don't lay eggs" seems false even though most ducks don't lay eggs, and my two routes don't obviously predict that asymmetry.

---

Here's what I'd like comments on. First, accuracy: do I describe any of these standard cases incorrectly, or are there well-known data points in the same neighborhood (say, "lions have manes," "Germans drink beer," or generics about artifacts) that flatly contradict the two-route picture? Second, the alarm route worries me on moral as well as empirical grounds: it seems to predict that "Muslims are terrorists" could come out true for a speaker in a society where that association is salient, which is an uncomfortable consequence for a semantic theory. Is that really a prediction of the view, and if so, is it a refutation or just a cost? Third, tell me honestly whether the two-route structure does explanatory work or merely relabels the data with fancier names, and identify the single weakest paragraph. I'm more interested in being told where the essay is wrong than in encouragement.
