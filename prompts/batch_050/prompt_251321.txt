I'm a researcher at a consumer-insights firm, and much of my day-to-day work involves segmenting survey respondents and customers into generations — Boomers, Gen X, Millennials, Gen Z — and describing each segment's supposed values, habits, and attitudes. At a recent internal review, a colleague from our statistics team dismissed the entire practice as, in her words, astrology for people with spreadsheet access: you take something fixed at birth that the person had no say in, sort people into bins with fuzzy boundaries, and then circulate confident personality profiles of each bin, which readers recognize themselves in because the descriptions are vague enough to fit anyone. She intends to propose at next quarter's methodology meeting that we drop generational segmentation from our products entirely, and the analogy is doing the argumentative work. I'd like a serious audit of it before I respond.

Please lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the zodiac sign, the horoscope, the birth chart, the Barnum-style description, and the astrologer's claimed causal mechanism — and then identify where the mapping breaks. Some candidate disanalogies I can already see: cohorts, unlike star signs, share datable historical exposures (a financial crisis during one's first years in the labor market, a pandemic during one's schooling), which could in principle exert real causal influence; and there is a known statistical headache in separating the effects of getting older from the effects of living through a particular period from the effects of belonging to a particular cohort, a three-way confound with no obvious parallel in astrology. On the other side, the analogy seems to get something right about arbitrary cutoffs — I struggle to believe that someone born in December of a cutoff year and someone born the following January belong to genuinely different kinds — and about the way labels can become self-fulfilling: once a generation is widely described as anxious or entrepreneurial, its members may begin conforming to the description, so the label manufactures part of the evidence later cited in its favor, which is arguably worse than astrology, since no one becomes more stubborn because Taurus is said to be.

Tell me which of these disanalogies actually bear on the conclusion my colleague wants — that generational segmentation should be abandoned altogether — and which are pedantic. If the analogy quietly smuggles anything in (for instance, a view about what would have to be true for a grouping of people to count as a real kind), say what. If the analogy survives only in weakened form, state the weaker conclusion it would license — perhaps a conclusion about how we use the labels rather than whether we use them. Finally, if astrology is the wrong comparison class, propose a better one: are generations more like clinical diagnostic categories, marketing personas, or something else entirely, and does that comparison do better at capturing whatever is legitimate in the practice?
