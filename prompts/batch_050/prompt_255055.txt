In 2016, reporters at ProPublica published an investigation of COMPAS, a risk-assessment tool used in parts of the American criminal justice system to score defendants' likelihood of reoffending. Their central finding, drawn from Broward County, Florida data, was that among defendants who did *not* go on to reoffend, Black defendants were labeled high-risk at roughly twice the rate of white defendants (approximately 45 percent versus 24 percent) — a large disparity in false-positive rates. The company behind the tool responded that ProPublica had used the wrong yardstick: the scores, they argued, were *calibrated* — among defendants assigned any given risk score, the fraction who actually reoffended was approximately the same for Black and white defendants. Both claims appear to be true of the same data. Around the same time, several research groups published results to the effect that, when base rates differ between groups, no non-trivial predictor can simultaneously satisfy calibration and equalized error rates (false-positive and false-negative balance) across groups, except in degenerate cases.

Analyze this episode as a case study in impossibility results in algorithmic fairness. I want a disciplined mapping in both directions, not a verdict on whether COMPAS is racist. First, the theory-to-case direction: reconstruct the impossibility theorem's setup carefully — what exactly each fairness criterion demands, what the base-rate condition is, and why the theorem predicts that a calibrated score applied to populations with different underlying recidivism rates will *necessarily* produce asymmetric error rates of the kind ProPublica found. Given that, say which side of the dispute the theorem vindicates, and in what sense: does it show that Northpointe was right that ProPublica's metric was unfair to the tool, or does it show that both sides were describing the same mathematical fact from different normative standpoints? Second, the case-to-theory direction: identify the respects in which the episode strains the theorem's framing. Consider at least the following: the outcome variable the theorem treats as given (reoffending) is measured through arrests and convictions, which are partly produced by the very institutions the score informs; the theorem takes the score's inputs and the decision threshold as fixed, when both are chosen; and the theorem is silent on which fairness criterion *should* be sacrificed when a judge is setting bail, which is arguably the only question that mattered to the defendants. Finally, give an overall assessment: is the COMPAS affair a confirmation of the impossibility results (they predicted exactly this kind of public confusion), an anomaly for them, or evidence that the formal fairness framework was answering a narrower question than the public dispute was actually about? Where the theory is genuinely silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak.
