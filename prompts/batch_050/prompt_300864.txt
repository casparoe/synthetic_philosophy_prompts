I'm trying to figure out when symmetry reasoning actually explains something, as opposed to just giving you a neat derivation, so here are my gut verdicts on cases. The fact that the laws of physics don't care where you are explaining why momentum is conserved: yes, genuinely explanatory, that's why momentum is conserved and not just a coincidence. Same for rotational symmetry and angular momentum. But the gauge symmetry of electromagnetism explaining charge conservation feels like bookkeeping to me, not explanation, even though I gather the argument has the same shape as the momentum one. When a perfectly symmetric setup has to produce a symmetric outcome (like the star that's a perfect sphere collapsing into a sphere), symmetry seems to explain it. But when someone says a fair coin lands heads half the time "because of symmetry," that feels like it's really about my ignorance, not about the coin. And the fact that energy conservation fails in an expanding universe, right where time-translation symmetry fails, makes me trust the symmetry explanation more, not less. Is there one principle that sorts these the way my gut does -- some symmetry arguments explanatory, others mere derivations -- or do I have to give something up? And what does your best candidate say about a case I haven't listed: does Lorentz symmetry explain why nothing can outrun light, or is that exactly backwards?
