For a seminar on Enlightenment philosophy I recently read Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness, and afterwards me and a friend got into an arguement about it over drinks. As I understood her, she denies that happiness comes from moral perfection or ascetic self-denial: you need strong passions (she names love, study, and even gambling as candidates), pleasures of taste and the senses, freedom from prejudice, good health, and she even suggests we have to keep ourselves open to pleasant illusions rather than staring unvarnished truth in the face. My friend concluded she's basically a proto-hedonist who would rubber-stamp anything that makes people feel good. I think that's way too quick, since she also insists on cultivated taste, serious study, and virtue as conditions, but I couldn't really say why.

Here's the hypothetical we couldn't settle. Suppose du Châtelet were dropped into the present day and shown our entire mood-management apparatus: antidepressants and anti-anxiety medication prescribed at enormous scale, therapy culture, mindfulness apps, the wellness industry, and on top of that social media, where everyone curates and performs a somewhat happier, more impressive version of their life. What would she make of it? In particular: would she see something like SSRIs as restoring the capacity for passion and pleasure that she thought happiness requires, and therefore a blessing -- or as chemically flattening exactly the intense passions she prized, and therefore a threat? And would she regard Instagram-style curated self-presentation as the kind of benign, happiness-sustaining illusion she recommended, or as a counterfeit that fails for reasons internal to her own view? I want the prediction argued from her actual position, not just "she'd probably like some things and dislike others."

Give me the answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion, and tie each point to something specific in her view rather than generic Enlightenment speculation.
