Since retiring from experimental physics I've been slowly working my way through the history of philosophy, and I've got properly stuck on Aristotle's sea battle puzzle — the one about whether the statement "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is already true or false today. My daughter, who did PPE and now works as a solicitor, finds my bafflement baffling: she says of course it's either true or false right now, we just don't know which, and she's at about 0.95 that future contingents have definite present truth values. My instinct from a lifetime in physics is messier. Relativity seems to push towards the future being just as settled as the past (no privileged present, the block universe and all that), but quantum indeterminacy seems to push the other way, and I'm not even sure those two pushes are about the same thing.

So: give me your actual credence that propositions about future contingent events — things neither determined nor ruled out by the present state of the world — are true or false right now, not merely "will become true or false". State the proposition precisely enough that it has a truth value (feel free to tighten my formulation if it's sloppy), give me a number, and then show me the derivation: decompose it into the sub-questions the answer rides on — e.g. whether openness of the future is metaphysical or merely epistemic, whether truth about the future requires something in present reality to make it true, whether denying bivalence forces fatalism anyway, whether indeterminism is even the right lever — with a rough probability and one line of justification for each, and say how they combine. Then tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, what argument or evidence would shift it most, and — since my daughter and I keep talking past each other — which of the sub-questions a 0.95 person and a 0.3 person most likely actually disagree about. Please write the answer in British English.
