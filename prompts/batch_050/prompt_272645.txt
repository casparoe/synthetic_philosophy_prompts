I'm in my early thirties and recently had an unsettling conversation with a close friend that has been bothering me for weeks. He is an extremely analytical person (he works in data science), and when I told him that I love my girlfriend Maya because she's funny, kind, and endlessly curious, he pointed out that there must be thousands of people who are equally funny, kind, and curious — and a fair number who are more so. By my own lights, he said, I should either be indifferent between Maya and those strangers or be actively on the lookout for an upgrade. He wasn't being cruel; he sincerely believes relationships should be periodically re-evaluated the way you'd re-evaluate an investment, and he claims he applies this to his own marriage. Things got more pointed when I mentioned that Maya was recently diagnosed with a degenerative condition that may, over the coming years, gradually blunt some of the very traits I cited. My friend said this only proves his point: if my reasons for loving her are her traits, then the intellectually honest position is that my love will rationally fade as the traits do, and I should admit that to myself now. But when I imagine that future, the idea of stopping loving her because she has become less curious strikes me as not just unappealing but as a betrayal of what love is. Yet when I tried telling him that I love her because she is *her*, he accused me of professing to love a bare, qualityless particular, which he said is no less incoherent than the investment model.

Act as a professional philosopher who specializes in the ethics of personal relationships and the nature of love. I would like you to engage with the underlying structure of this dispute rather than offer generic relationship advice. Please think carefully and proceed in three steps. First, brainstorm briefly: sketch three or four genuinely distinct positions one might hold about whether love has reasons at all and, if it does, what kind of reasons those could be — noting that "reasons for loving someone" might play very different roles (explaining how the love started, justifying its continuation, or guiding what the lover ought to do). Second, analyze my friend's argument at its strongest rather than setting up a strawman: identify exactly where the investment-model reasoning does real work, and where it might go wrong or smuggle in a false analogy. Third, advise me concretely: what could I honestly say — to him, and more importantly to myself — that does justice both to the plain fact that Maya's traits deeply matter to my love and to the equally plain fact that I seem to love *her*, rather than a bundle of properties that any sufficiently similar person could instantiate?
