I'm a poli sci PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on performance measurement in bureaucracies, and I've been rewatching The Wire as "research" and now I actually want to use it as the centerpiece of a chapter. I want to read the whole show through Goodhart's law / Campbell's law, plus the newer specification-gaming literature from ML, but I need the reading to be rigorous and not just "haha stats bad".

The obvious reading is that the show is a five-season proof that once a measure becomes the basis for reward and punishment, the people being measured optimize the measure instead of the thing it was supposed to track. Felony stats, clearance rates, arrest numbers, then test scores in season four and journalism prizes in season five. Fine. But I think the show is doing something more specific than that and I want to pressure-test a few claims:

1. Season three's Hamsterdam is the hard case. Colvin jukes the stats by effectively decriminalizing drugs in three zones, but the drop in felonies is real, not fabricated — corners are safer, felony crime in the district genuinely falls, and the brass shut him down anyway because they can't or won't distinguish his honest gaming from the ordinary kind. So is Hamsterdam a counterexample to Goodhart (a case where optimizing the metric actually delivers the underlying good), or is it Goodhart operating exactly as predicted, just on the institutional side rather than the street side? I genuinely can't settle this and my committee will ask.

2. Season five inverts the whole thing. McNulty fabricates a serial killer — manufactures bad numbers — specifically to break the spell the stats have over the department and pry resources loose for actual police work. Meanwhile Carcetti, who ran as the reform candidate disgusted by stat-juking, ends up juking the exact same stats as the bosses he replaced once the governor's race beckons. So which is it: does the show think Goodhart failures are corrigible from inside (McNulty's scheme works, sort of), or does it think institutions just select for gamers no matter who you put in charge? Those are very different theses and the ending seems to endorse the bleaker one, but tell me if I'm wrong.

3. The alignment people draw a line between deliberate gaming by agents who know the real goal and are cheating it, versus specification gaming where an optimizer finds a shortcut nobody intended and no one is even being dishonest. The Wire's cops and principals are mostly deliberate gamers. Does the show have anything to say about the second case — any place where the measure gets corrupted without anyone deciding to corrupt it — or is the show's theory of institutions irreducibly agential in a way that limits what it can teach the ML crowd? Relatedly: is the show's pessimism actually about measurement at all, or is measurement just the symptom and the disease is something else (funding formulas, elections, career ladders)?

Then, the part I care most about: what does The Wire dramatize about Goodhart that the canonical statements of the law can't express propositionally? My hunch is something about what living inside a gamed metric does to people's self-understanding — the way Prez is the only major character who escapes the numbers and it's treated as a kind of grace — but maybe there's a better answer. And please be honest about where the show resists the Goodhart reading and I should say so in the chapter rather than forcing it.
