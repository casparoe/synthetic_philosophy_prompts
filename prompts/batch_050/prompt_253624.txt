So I'm drafting a novel where the main character, Ines, has a stroke in chapter two and comes out of it with a weird condition (it's a real thing, I checked): she still *feels* pain -- she can say where it is, how intense, sharp vs. burning, all that -- but it doesn't bother her even slightly. She'll calmly report that her hand is on a hot pan the way you'd report that it's raining out. No grimacing, no distress, no urge to pull away.

I workshopped the draft with two writer friends and they got into a proper argument about the ending, and honestly it's thrown me, because the ending hinges on whether Ines's condition is a tragedy or a kind of liberation.

(1) Dana said the injury scenes are the least sad parts of the book: what makes pain bad is that it *hurts* -- the sheer awfulness of the experience -- and Ines doesn't have that anymore. Strip out the hurting and pain is just information, like a notification popping up. She actually envies Ines a little.

(2) Marcus said she's got it backwards, and that the book is darker than I realize: pain is the body's alarm system, and alarm systems matter because of what they make you do, not because the siren sounds unpleasant. Real people who don't respond to pain end up maimed -- destroyed joints, bitten-through tongues, infections they never notice. Ines's serenity isn't liberation, it's the worst part of her condition. The badness of pain was never in the feeling; it's in what pain protects you from.

(3) Dana said Marcus just conceded her point without noticing: if the badness is in the protection, then when Ines burns her hand, nothing bad happens *in her experience at that moment* -- the bad outcomes are broken bones and infections, which are just bodily damage, the same bad thing any character could suffer in any novel. He's changed the subject from "is her pain bad for her" to "is tissue damage bad for her," and nobody ever doubted the second one.

(4) Marcus said the move in (3) only works if you can cleanly separate "the sensation" from "the caring about it," and Ines's own case shows how weird that separation is -- he half-wanted to deny that what Ines feels even counts as pain anymore, since an experience that doesn't move her to do anything is missing something essential to pain. Dana said that was obviously a cheat, redefining pain so his side wins by fiat, since Ines reports the exact same sensations as before the stroke.

They both turned to me and I realized I couldn't write the last chapter until I know who's right. Can you go through moves (1)-(4) one by one and tell me who's right at each step and why, then say what you think *both* of them are missing, and finally what it implies for whether the ending should land as tragic or triumphant?

Put the move-by-move part in a table with columns: Move (the number), Who made it, Verdict (right / wrong / half-right), and Why (one or two sentences). Then a JSON object after the table with three fields: "what_both_miss" (a string), "more_right_overall" (Dana or Marcus), and "ending_recommendation" (tragic, triumphant, or ambiguous, plus a sentence of justification).
