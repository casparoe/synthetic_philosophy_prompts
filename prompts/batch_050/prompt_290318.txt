So I write novels for a living, and a while back I fell down a rabbit hole about Kant's absolute prohibition on lying -- the whole murderer-at-the-door thing where he apparently says you can't lie even to save a life. My philosopher friend insists Kant really meant it, no exceptions, and we got into this argument over drinks that I keep thinking about because, well, my entire job is writing down things that aren't true and presenting them as if they happened. I invent narrators who deceive the reader, I write dialogue for con artists, and last year I did a book tour where I gave the same polished fake-spontaneous anecdote about my "writing process" like forty times. So here's my question: if Kant were somehow alive today and I could sit him down, how would he actually judge the stuff a modern novelist does? Does fiction even count as lying for him, given that nobody's decieved because we all know the rules of the game -- or is that too easy, and would he have some problem with, say, an unreliable narrator who's engineered to make readers believe something false until chapter 30?

Also push it into genuinely modern territory he couldn't have imagined: what would he say about a deepfake video of a politician, about someone catfishing with a fabricated identity, about an AI chatbot that smoothly pretends to be human customer support, or about authors who publish "autofiction" that blurs the line so readers genuinely can't tell what's real? I don't want a lecture on what Kant scholars have said -- I want you to actually reason it out from his principles: the bit about lying undermining the very conditions of communication, the duty to oneself, all that. Tell me where he'd be surprisingly permissive (I suspect fiction might be fine by his lights?) and where he'd be horrified in ways most modern people aren't. Bonus points if you tell me whether my book-tour anecdote makes me a moral monster or is just normal human social behavior he's too rigid to understand.
