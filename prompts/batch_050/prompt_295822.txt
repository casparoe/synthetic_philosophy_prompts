I help run a philosophy discussion night, and after our meeting on confirmation theory someone claimed that spotting a beige lampshade proves all ravens are black because Hempel. I want to turn the ensuing argument into a proper sestina for our little zine. Use exactly these six end-words: raven, black, lampshade, contrapositive, sample, evidence. Make the standard rotation do philosophical work rather than just repeat them: each stanza should recast the universal claim in another form, moving from black raven as a confirming instance, through the contrapositive about nonblack things, to the unsettling thought that indoor objects might bear on ornithology.

Please don’t let the poem settle for either easy reaction—the lampshade confirms the claim just as strongly as a black raven, or the whole thing is only an embarrassing artifact of formal logic. Around stanza four, bring in a Bayesian qualification: a randomly encountered nonblack non-raven can raise the hypothesis by a minute amount by ruling out one possible counterexample, but the size of that boost depends on the relevant background frequencies, and if the speaker deliberately sampled from things already known not to be ravens then the color tells them nothing. The three-line envoi should make explicit the difference between logically equivalent hypotheses and evidentially equivalent observations. Accuracy matters more than explaining every term; someone who knows the raven paradox should recognize the positions, while someone who doesn’t should still get a good poem out of it.
