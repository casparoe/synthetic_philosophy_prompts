So I work as the de facto data-and-models person at a small UK fintech that does consumer lending, and I keep hitting a wall I suspect the rationalist-adjacent internet has already thought about properly. Every quarter I audit our scoring models for fairness, and every quarter we end up in the same argument: risk wants calibration (a predicted 10% default should mean 10% actually default, in whatever group you slice it), compliance wants equal error rates across protected groups, and I've read enough to know those impossibility results exist showing you can't have both at once when the groups' base rates differ. But knowing the theorem exists doesn't tell me what to *do* on a Tuesday afternoon.

What I want is an actual procedure I can run each audit cycle, in words rather than maths, that takes the impossibility results seriously instead of pretending we can satisfy everyone. So: which questions do I ask first (base rates, what the score is actually *for*, who bears the cost of a false positive vs a false negative)? What counts as a sufficient answer to each? Where does the procedure force a choice between calibration and error-rate parity rather than letting us fudge it, and how should that choice get made — what's the principled tie-breaker, and when should I escalate it to the directors as a values decision rather than a stats decision? Flag the points where judgement can't be eliminated, and say honestly where the procedure will misfire (e.g. tiny groups where the numbers are noise, or proxies for protected characteristics we don't collect).

Constraints: I have maybe half a day per audit, basic stats tooling, no legal team in-house, and the FCA eventually has to sign off on whatever we do. Run the procedure on two cases for me: our main credit-scoring model, and a marketing model that decides who gets offered a promotional rate. Ideally one of them should come out with an answer I'd rather not hear. Cheers.
