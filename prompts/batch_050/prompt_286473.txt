I co-teach a philosophy of history seminar with a colleague from the actual history department, and we keep having the same fight: Hempel's old covering-law model versus the narrativist line that a well-constructed story can itself be an explanation. She's the historian, I'm the philosopher, and after two semesters of this I've started keeping score of her verdicts on student papers, because I suspect she isn't running either theory cleanly and I want to catch her out. Her calls so far: (1) "Russia had a revolution in 1917 because agrarian autocracies that lose major wars tend to have revolutions" -- she says that IS an explanation. (2) A meticulously sourced bare chronicle of 1917, dates and events and troop movements, no causal claims whatsoever -- not an explanation, she says, just material for one. (3) A step-by-step reconstruction of how the Zimmermann telegram moved Wilson and his cabinet toward war, containing not a single general law anywhere in it -- explanation, definitely. (4) "The Habsburg empire collapsed because overextended multiethnic empires collapse" -- she says NOT an explanation, she actually wrote "that's a label, not an answer" in the margin. (5) A paper that renames a stretch of the nineteenth century ("the age of capital") and shows how lots of disparate developments fit the label -- not an explanation either, though she admitted it was good history.

Next week a grad student is presenting a paper on the July crisis. It narrates the outbreak of WWI day by day -- mobilization timetables, ultimata, railway schedules, who panicked when -- and shows in detail how each step made the next one nearly inevitable. But it never once asks why war came in 1914 rather than during the earlier Moroccan or Balkan crises, and it never says what the realistic alternative to war even was at each stage. It's pure forward-driving causal narrative.

Here's what I want from you. First, reconstruct at least two different rules about what makes something a historical explanation that are each consistent with all five of her verdicts (and note which obvious rule -- say, straight Hempelian covering-law -- is already ruled out by her record and by which verdict). Second, predict her verdict on the July crisis paper under each rule, and then tell me which rule you think she's actually running and which way she'll go, with your reasons -- I've got a bottle of wine riding on this. Third, give me the follow-up case, real or hypothetical, that would seperate the two candidate rules cleanly, i.e. the case where they'd give opposite verdicts. If you think no single rule fits all five verdicts and she's just inconsistent, tell me which verdict she'd have to give up. Don't write me a bibliography, I can do that myself.
