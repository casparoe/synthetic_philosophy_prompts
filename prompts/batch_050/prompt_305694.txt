In the Prelude to Middlemarch, George Eliot writes of people with religious temperament but no religion to give it shape:

"With dim lights and tangled circumstance they tried to shape their thought and deed in noble agreement; but after all, to common eyes their struggles seemed mere inconsistency and formlessness; for these later-born Theresas were helped by no coherent social faith and order which could perform the function of knowledge for the ardently willing soul."

That last phrase has stuck with me through years of reading rationalist forums, where the default tests for belief are behavioral: does it pay rent in anticipated experience, does it constrain what you expect, would you bet on it. Eliot seems to propose a different test — a faith is doing its job when it performs the function of knowledge for someone who ardently wants to act well. I've been checking that against my own verdicts on cases, and the pattern is messier than either test suggests:

1. Miriam prays daily and attends services, but has never once asked herself whether God exists and finds the question faintly rude. Her conduct is indistinguishable from her secular sister's. My verdict: genuinely religious, whatever the rent-paying crowd says.

2. Tomas lost his belief in God at university, but his life is still organized by the moral imagination his upbringing gave him, and he calls himself religious. My verdict: admirable, but no longer religious. The word has to mean something.

3. A congregation whose doctrine is so vague no two members state it the same way, yet which reliably coordinates charity, mourning rites, and mutual aid. My verdict: religion, fully.

4. A man who professes belief in heaven, weeps at funerals as if there were none, and takes no comfort in doctrine when his own diagnosis arrives. My verdict: belief in belief, not belief.

5. A woman who follows church teaching precisely when it coincides with what she already wanted and quietly drops it otherwise, sincerely considering herself obedient. My verdict: the religion is doing no work; her conscience is.

6. A woman whose faith sends her into a fever hospital at real cost to herself. My verdict: the paradigm case of religion doing its work, whatever the doctrine's truth-value.

Cases 1 and 5 are the ones I can't reconcile. In both, conduct is fully explained without the doctrine, yet I call one religious and the other not — and I suspect Eliot's phrase convicts Miriam exactly as much as it acquits her.

So: read the passage carefully, tell me what test Eliot is actually proposing (it may not be the one I think it is), and then state the principle — hers, repaired as needed, or a better one — that captures my verdicts. Give it precisely enough that each clause is motivated by a named case; show which of my six verdicts it cannot save and must overturn; and apply it to two cases I haven't listed: a sincere believer in astrology whose entire life it organizes, and an Orthodox practitioner who describes the creeds as poetry. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, no restating of my question.
