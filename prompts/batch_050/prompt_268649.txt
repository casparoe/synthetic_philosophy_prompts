I run a small community repair workshop — people drop off broken stuff, volunteers fix it, nobody pays for anything. I've been doing it for four years and the single biggest source of drama, by far, is promises. Someone promises to cover the Saturday shift and then a paying gig comes up and they bail. Someone promises to fix a lady's radio by her birthday and then parts don't arrive. Someone promises, kind of half-joking at a barbecue, to donate his old table saw, and now people act like the workshop owns it. I keep having to decide who's actually done something wrong versus who just had life happen to them, and I've realised I have no consistent rule — I basically go on vibes, how much I like the person, how much trouble their flaking caused, which I'm pretty sure is unfair.

I was talking to a friend about this and she said philosophers have genuinely argued about why promises bind at all — like, is it because the other person is now counting on you, or because you gave them some kind of right over your actions, or because promising only works as a social practice if we treat promises as binding, so you're free-riding on everyone else keeping theirs? Apparently these theories disagree about the hard cases, which is exactly what I need. So here's what I'm asking for: turn this stuff into a procedure I can actually run. Something like an ordered set of questions or tests I can go through in my head in two minutes when someone breaks or wants out of a commitment, that tells me (a) whether a real promise even existed — because half our disputes are about whether "yeah I'll try to be there" counts, (b) how binding it is, and (c) what changes or excuses legitimately weaken or cancel it — emergencies, the other person changing their own behaviour, new information, the promise having been made under social pressure, whatever the theories say matters.

A few requirements. Keep the distinctions the theories actually fight about — if the reliance story and the rights story give different verdicts somewhere, I want to know, not have it smoothed over. But translate it so a normal person could apply it without knowing any of the names. Show me the procedure working on my three cases above (the Saturday shift, the radio, the table saw), plus one made-up case where following it honestly gives a verdict I probably won't like — like where someone I think is clearly in the wrong comes out excused, or a case where I, as the workshop, am the one breaking a commitment and have to own it. And be upfront about which steps are just judgement calls with a procedure-shaped hat on, because I'd rather know where the checklist runs out than pretend it doesn't. Oh and it can't require me to interrogate people — whatever info the tests need, I have to be able to get it from a normal conversation without sounding like a laywer.
