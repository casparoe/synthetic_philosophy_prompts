I've now watched the same pattern unfold in two organizations, and I want to know whether it has a name. A company introduces an automated system whose throughput is far beyond what any person could actually check -- say, software that pre-processes thousands of insurance claims a day, or a diagnostic tool that reads scans before a physician sees them. A human employee is then formally designated as the one who "reviews and approves" the system's output. Genuine review at that volume is impossible, so in practice the reviewer mostly confirms. When something eventually goes wrong, liability lands almost entirely on this nominal approver -- the person with the least real say over how the system was built and behaves -- while the people who chose the training data, set the thresholds, and decided to deploy at a scale that precluded meaningful oversight are treated as blameless. The whole arrangement can look suspiciously as if it were designed so that accountability flows to whoever touched the decision last and understood it least.

I know there's a literature arguing that autonomous systems open up "responsibility gaps," but that literature usually worries that *no one* ends up answerable. Here someone is very much held answerable -- plausibly the wrong someone. Is there an established concept for this specific misallocation, as opposed to the gap itself? If there are several neighboring notions, I'd like to know how the pattern I described differs from each and which fits best. And if no one has named the deliberate version -- where the oversight role is structured in advance so that blame will settle there -- I'd like a coined term with a definition and some boundary cases: for instance, is the overworked air-traffic controller supervising automation an instance, or only a cousin?
