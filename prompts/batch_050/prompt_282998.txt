Two friends of mine were arguing at a party about the endowment effect and I want an outside verdict. Maya's position is that it's just a bias: in those experiments where people get randomly handed a mug or a pen, the ones with mugs suddenly demand way more to sell than the pen-people will pay, and since assignment was random nothing real about their preferences could justify the gap, so ownership is irrationally inflating value. Dan pushed back that the gap can be perfectly sensible: once you have something you build it into your plans, and giving up something woven into your life genuinely costs you more than acquiring a new thing gains you, so the asymmetry tracks a real difference. Maya replied that the effect shows up within minutes of random assignment, before anyone's built anything into anything, so it has to be mere attachment to whatever happens to be the reference point. Dan said even that's not obviously irrational, since there's no God's-eye standpoint from which gains and losses have to count symmetrically, and maybe value is just inherently about changes from where you stand. Maya's last move was that this makes preferences incoherent, because the same person would pick differently depending on what they already hold, so their choices are hostage to accidents of history and can't all reflect what they actually want. They both seemed partially right to me and I stayed out of it. Please go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about what, and in doing so please be careful to separate what's empirically settled (like what the experiments actually show) from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present one side's interpretation as the consensus.
