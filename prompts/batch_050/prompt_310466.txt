I've been reading LessWrong and Astral Codex Ten for years, and my in-person reading group (six people, mostly with decision-theory brainworms) has recently moved from population ethics to distributive ethics. Last week we hit the standard dialectic: egalitarianism about welfare gets embarrassed by the leveling-down objection, and prioritarianism is wheeled in as the rescue, since it values only weighted absolute welfare and therefore can never prefer harming the better-off when doing so benefits no one. One member of the group is a committed prioritarian and repeated this immunity claim with great confidence. I suspect the immunity is narrower than advertised -- it seems to hold only under certainty -- and I'd like a case to bring to next week's meeting that pressures it. I've tried sketching one myself, but my versions keep either cheating (stipulating the verdict into the payoffs) or collapsing back into the standard objection against equality rather than priority.

So: construct a thought experiment in which prioritarianism itself -- the standard version with a concave weighting function over individual welfare, not egalitarianism in disguise -- delivers a verdict with the structure of leveling down: it strictly prefers a policy that every affected person, judging solely by their own prospects, has reason to reject. Constraints: (i) a realistic setting, such as a hospital allocating a scarce treatment under diagnostic uncertainty or a firm choosing between compensation schemes -- no trolleys, no demons, no predictors of any kind; (ii) uncertainty must be doing the work, specifically the gap between applying the priority weighting to realized outcomes and applying it to people's expectations; (iii) at most three affected parties, with explicit small welfare numbers and a stated weighting function (square root is fine), so that the arithmetic can be checked line by line by a skeptical reader; (iv) the prioritarian-preferred option must not secretly benefit anyone -- the embarrassment has to be generated by the theory's own machinery; (v) the scenario itself should fit in one paragraph. For context on what the case is for: I want to force the prioritarian in our group either to accept the verdict, to revise the view, or to identify a principled restriction, so a case that lets him wave it away as mere arithmetic noise would be a failure.

After the scenario, do the verification work. Show the arithmetic under prioritarianism, under plain utilitarianism, and under a simple gap-based egalitarian view, so it's visible which theories are and aren't embarrassed. Identify the load-bearing structural feature (I expect it is concave weighting applied ex post, but demonstrate this rather than asserting it), and show that removing it -- say, by applying the weighting to each person's expected welfare instead -- reverses the verdict; then say honestly what that move costs, in particular whether ex ante prioritarianism sacrifices the very intuition that motivated giving priority to the actually worse-off. Give the best prioritarian counterattack: should a sophisticated defender bite the bullet, retreat to the ex ante formulation, or deny that unanimous ex ante preference is a binding constraint on distributive principles? Finally, end with a candid verdict on whether the case shows that prioritarianism is subject to a genuine leveling-down objection of its own, or only to a distinct objection that bears a family resemblance -- I don't want to overclaim at the meeting and get caught.
