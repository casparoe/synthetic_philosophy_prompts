So I'm a software engineer (backend stuff, zero philosophy background) and over the past year I've fallen into the effective altruism rabbit hole -- partly because a coworker of mine is really into it and keeps trying to get me to donate through GiveWell, and partly because the whole idea appeals to the part of my brain that likes optimizing things. But it also bugs me in a way I can't fully articulate, so I've started a running notes doc of the standard criticisms of EA, hoping to figure out which one is poking at whatever it is that bothers me. Here's what I've got so far, roughly in my own words:

1. The institutional critique -- EA treats problems as things individuals can fix with donations, so it channels energy away from messy political/structural change (unionizing, regulation, etc.) and maybe even legitimizes the systems causing the problems.
2. Measurability bias -- EA gravitates toward interventions with clean RCT-style evidence (bednets, cash transfers) and systematically underrates stuff that's hard to quantify, like governance, research, or social movements. "Not everything that counts can be counted" territory.
3. The earning-to-give problem -- telling people to take lucrative jobs in finance or whatever and donate the proceeds creates moral offsetting, where the harm done in the career is supposedly cancelled out by donations, which seems both empirically dicey and spiritually gross.
4. The utilitarian-baggage objection -- EA inherits demandingness (why am I allowed to buy a coffee?) and rides roughshod over special obligations to family, community, etc.

What I want is one more criticism that is NOT on this list and not just a rewording, special case, or combo of 1-4 -- so e.g. don't tell me "EA neglects climate policy because it's political" because that's #1 with a hat on. Ideally it's something that would specifically resonate with someone who builds systems for a living, though that's a nice-to-have not a requirement. And then be honest with me about whether it's actually any good -- like, if my EA coworker heard it, would he have a defintely convincing comeback, and what would it be?
