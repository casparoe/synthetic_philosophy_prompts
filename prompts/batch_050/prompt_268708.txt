I am preparing an upper-level undergraduate examination on Peircean and Jamesian theories of truth. Draft one essay question that students could answer in 75 minutes, using the following case rather than asking for a generic comparison of the two theories:

A marine research institute has inconclusive but scientifically serious evidence that an engineered coral strain can survive an approaching heatwave. Publishing the forecast will probably be self-fulfilling: it will release emergency funds for shade screens and water pumps, without which the coral will almost certainly die, but with which it will probably survive. A critic argues that later survival would not show that the original forecast was true, because the intervention changed the conditions under which it was tested. A defender responds that the forecast’s successful practical guidance is precisely the relevant kind of verification. A third researcher argues that ideally continued inquiry, taking into account both the forecast and its causal effects, would eventually settle whether the strain was viable.

The question should require students to determine whether the forecast was true when made, while distinguishing truth from justified assertion, practical usefulness, and subsequent confirmation. It should also force them to address two common misunderstandings: Peircean convergence is not merely a prediction about what actual institutions will eventually agree upon, and Jamesian truth is not simply whatever produces welcome results. Students must explain how each theory handles self-fulfilling beliefs and must propose one principled refinement to whichever theory they find stronger.

Provide:

1. The examination question itself, in no more than 200 words.
2. A model answer of approximately 800 words that does not rely on quotations or bibliographic references.
3. A marking scheme totaling 30 marks, divided among six distinct components. For each component, state the required move, its mark allocation, and the characteristic mistake that should lose marks.
4. A short note explaining what the question tests that a straightforward compare-and-contrast question would not.
