Reading The Scarlet Letter with my reading group next week and I want to frame it with the guilt vs. shame debate in moral psychology. The usual story is that shame is the bad one -- it's about the whole self, it depends on being seen, it makes people hide or lash out -- while guilt is act-focused and motivates repair. But Hawthorne seems to scramble that: Hester gets maximum public shame and ends up basically fine, even strong, while Dimmesdale has pure private guilt and it eats him alive without producing a single reparative act. So read the novel against the standard guilt-good/shame-bad picture. Does Hawthorne actually complicate it, or does he just show that guilt without confession is useless, which the guilt fans would happily grant? And what about the claim that a culture can choose which emotion to run on -- the Puritan community clearly chose shame, and the novel seems to show shame failing not because it's shame but because the community never lets it end. Which scenes would you point the group to, and where does the novel resist the whole framework?
