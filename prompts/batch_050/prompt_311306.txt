So I'm TAing a seminar on scalable oversight this fall and I'm putting together a session on the debate approach -- the setup where two AIs argue opposite sides of a question in front of a (possibly weak) human judge, the hope being that it's easier to judge who won an argument than to answer the question yourself, so oversight scales past the judge's own competence.

These are the failure modes I've already got on my list, so please don't just hand me one of these back:

1. Persuasion over truth: if the judge is gulible or has exploitable biases, debaters get rewarded for rhetoric, framing, confidence, flattery etc. rather than accuracy, so training them to win debates is partly training them to mislead.
2. No truth advantage: the whole scheme leans on the assumption that the true side is easier to defend under cross-examination than a lie (the liar has to keep a consistent story, the honest debater can just point at reality). If that fails -- say the true answer is counterintuitive while the false one has a slick intuitive narrative -- debate actively selects for the wrong side.
3. Collusion: if the two debaters are copies of the same model or otherwise have aligned incentives, they can tacitly coordinate on a story that fools the judge instead of actually competing.
4. Non-decomposable questions: some things we want oversight over (moral intuitions, taste, whether a design "feels off") don't break down into sub-arguments with checkable premises, so the recursion bottoms out somewhere the judge can't verify anything anyway.
5. Burden-of-proof gaming: a debater can demand endless rigor from its opponent while handwaving its own case, and a time-limited judge can't tell whether a request for one more level of justification is legitimate scrutiny or just stalling.
6. Unverifiable leaves: even where the debate tree does bottom out in supposedly simple claims, those claims may still be beyond the judge (check this 200-line proof, confirm this paper actually says that), so the recursion never really grounds out in human-checkable facts.

What I want is one more failure mode for the list, with a genuinely different mechanism -- not a special case, rewording, or mashup of the ones above. (E.g. "the judge might be biased" is already covered by #1.) Explain how it works, why it isn't covered by anything on my list, and give me your honest take on whether it's a serious problem for the debate approach or more of a theoretical curiosity.
