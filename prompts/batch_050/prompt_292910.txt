I would like to know whether a worry I keep returning to is an established problem in the philosophy of mind and, if so, what it is called and how its boundaries are drawn. The context is the thesis that the brain is a computer. I am not asking whether that thesis is true; I am asking about a specific difficulty for it, which I will describe as carefully as I can, because I want it distinguished from two nearby difficulties I already know how to set aside.

First, the difficulty I am not raising. There is a familiar complaint that computational descriptions are cheap: with a perverse enough scheme of interpretation, one could redescribe a waterfall, a wall, or the molecular collisions in a gas as executing any program whatsoever, so that the claim that the brain computes becomes either trivial or meaningless. Grant, for the sake of argument, that this complaint can be answered — that there exist principled, non-arbitrary standards for when a physical system genuinely implements a computation, standards fixed by the causal organization of the system itself rather than by an observer's convenience.

My worry arises after that concession. It seems to me that even under perfectly principled standards, one and the same neural system admits several competing computational characterizations, each legitimate, and the standards themselves do not select among them. Three examples of what I mean. (i) A single neuron can be characterized as a threshold device computing a logic function of its inputs, or as computing a weighted sum passed through a saturating nonlinearity; the second description subsumes the first, yet attributes to the neuron a different and richer computation. (ii) A region of visual cortex can be characterized as detecting edges, as filtering spatial frequencies, or as computing prediction errors, and much the same experiments are cited in support of each characterization by different research traditions. (iii) A structure such as the cerebellum has been characterized both as a timing mechanism and as an internal forward model; these are presented as rival hypotheses about what the structure computes, yet each camp tends to regard its own favored experiments as decisive. In every case the multiplicity is not the product of interpretive laxity: the rival descriptions are each naturalistic, each causally serious, and each in some sense correct about the system.

So, my questions. Does this specific predicament have an established name, and is it treated as a problem in its own right? More particularly: (1) How does it differ, exactly, from the everything-computes objection I set aside at the outset, given that here no perverse mappings and no appeal to an observer's convenience are involved? (2) Does it threaten the claim that the brain computes at all, or only the claim that there is a determinate fact about which computation a given circuit performs — and what additional premise would be required to get from the bare multiplicity of descriptions to any genuine threat? (3) Does the standard rejoinder — that the rival characterizations are simply descriptions at different levels of abstraction and therefore compatible rather than rival — actually dissolve the difficulty or merely rename it, and what precisely would one have to show for the levels story to do real work rather than relabel the problem? (4) Are there positions in the field that embrace the multiplicity, taking computational facts about a system to be plural or interest-dependent, and if so, what do such positions concede and what do they preserve?

I am not looking for a bare label. If the predicament has no established name, I would still like it carved cleanly: a definition, the nearest concepts it must not be confused with, and cases that fall just inside and just outside its boundary.
