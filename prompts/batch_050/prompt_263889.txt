A claim I encounter constantly in technology criticism is that social media platforms are a digital panopticon, and that Foucault's analysis of disciplinary power therefore explains what these platforms do to us. The analogy is usually deployed to support a strong conclusion: users, unable to know when they are being observed, internalize the watching eye and discipline themselves -- self-censoring, conforming, and becoming docile subjects of surveillance capitalism, with resistance more or less futile because the surveillance has been absorbed into the self.

Please audit this analogy rigorously rather than accepting or rejecting it wholesale. I would like, first, an explicit mapping: in Bentham's design and in Foucault's use of it, what plays the role of the guard tower, the unverifiable gaze, the cell, and the inmate, and what plays each corresponding role on a platform -- the recommender system, the audience of other users, the metrics, the data broker? Second, identify where the mapping breaks down, and be discriminating about which disanalogies matter for the intended conclusion and which are superficial. Some candidates I have in mind but have not resolved: Bentham's trick was an economy of surveillance, in which no actual observation need occur, whereas platforms genuinely record everything and the uncertainty attaches to what will be done with the record, not to whether it exists; the panopticon's inmate is made visible but sees nothing, whereas the platform user watches as much as she is watched; the prisoner cannot leave, whereas the user's captivity is of a different and perhaps weaker kind; and the telos differs -- normalization into docility versus the extraction and shaping of behavior for commercial ends.

Third, I want a more specifically Foucauldian question answered. Foucault's central claim about modern power is that it is productive: it produces subjects, pleasures, and truths rather than merely repressing a pre-existing self. It strikes me that the technology critics who lean on the panopticon analogy usually operate with a purely repressive picture -- a pre-formed authentic self being cowed into conformity -- and thereby keep the architectural image while dropping the actual theory. Is that fair? Does the genuinely Foucauldian analysis of social media (power as constitutive of the user-subject, identity, and desire) support the critics' strong conclusion, undermine it, or transform it into a different claim entirely?

Finally: if the panopticon is the wrong model, say what would be better -- a synopticon of the many watching the few, a laboratory, a marketplace of performances -- and state precisely what conclusion about social media the surviving analogy, whichever it is, is actually entitled to license.
