I compete in my school's debate club, and I've been trying to build a personal policy out of what I've read about higher-order evidence: whenever I learn that people I regard as my intellectual equals disagree with me, I should treat their disagreement as evidence against my view and split the difference, lowering my confidence toward theirs. But applying the policy seems to produce a strange result. After I conciliate, I still think my original reasoning was sound — so I end up believing a conclusion while believing that my own evidence doesn't really support it. Since that's textbook epistemic akrasia, and akrasia is supposed to be irrational, it looks like the policy instructs me to make myself irrational on purpose. Should I conclude that the real lesson of the higher-order evidence debate is that you should stand by your own reasoning whenever you trust it, and that philosophers who recommend conciliating have simply overlooked this? If that's too quick, where exactly does my argument go wrong?
