I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am writing a long piece on how reporters and editors should weigh testimony to extraordinary events — purported anomalies, unreplicated results, one-off observations that contradict established physics. In An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section X ("Of Miracles"), Part I, Hume writes that our confidence in testimony rests not on any a priori connection between reports and reality but on experience of certain facts about human beings:

"Were not the memory tenacious to a certain degree; had not men commonly an inclination to truth and a principle of probity; were they not sensible to shame, when detected in a falsehood: Were not these, I say, discovered by experience to be qualities, inherent in human nature, we should never repose the least confidence in human testimony."

I want a thought experiment built to order, for use as the piece's central case study. Construct a contemporary scenario in which a report surfaces of a physical event that would violate a regularity supported by genuinely uniform experience — and in which each of the three grounds of testimonial credit Hume names in this passage (tenacious memory, an inclination to truth and probity, sensitivity to shame upon detection) is secured as strongly as human circumstances allow, by the structure of the situation rather than by stipulation.

Constraints on the construction:

- No gods, prophets, or supernatural agents. The reported event should be a bare physical anomaly — something of the sort a working scientist, not a pilgrim, might report.
- Do not stipulate that the witnesses are perfectly reliable or infallible. Reliability must be generated by features of the scenario: their numbers, their incentives, their independence from one another, their exposure to professional ruin if exposed as frauds or dupes, and the physical traces the event leaves behind.
- The anomaly must be a one-off that cannot be reproduced on demand, since a repeatable event would merely revise the law rather than test Hume's framework.
- The scenario should be realistic — a research institute, an observatory, an industrial facility — described in no more than two or three paragraphs.

After the scenario, do the following. First, go through Hume's three grounds clause by clause and show where and how the scenario secures each one. Second, run Hume's own weighing procedure on the case: state what counts as the uniform experience on the side of the law, what counts as the experience on the side of the testimony, and what verdict the balance of the two yields. Third, identify the single structural feature of your scenario that lets the testimony side compete with the law side at all, and say what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed. Finally, address the tension internal to Hume's position: he defines a miracle as a violation of laws established by firm and unalterable experience, which seems to guarantee that the case against the report is always maximal — so either your scenario escapes this by construction, or it shows that Hume's framework can never license believing an anomaly report no matter how the three grounds of testimony are secured. Tell me which, and how a defender of the skeptical reading of Hume would attack your scenario at its strongest point.
