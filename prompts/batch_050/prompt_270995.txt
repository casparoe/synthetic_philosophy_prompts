I run a small philosophy meetup and last week Newcomb's problem nearly ended two friendships. One guy kept repeating "the money is already in the boxes, so taking both can't cost you anything" like it was checkmate, and the other kept firing back "rationality should win — the two-boxer walks away with a thousand bucks and acts like he won." Then a third person said they were both confused because "you're not choosing between boxes, you're choosing which prediction comes true," which honestly nobody at the table understood, including maybe her.

Take those three one-liners and unpack what precise claims each could actually be making. For each one, give me the range of readings from trivial to radical, say which are plausible and which are just false, and say which reading the person probably means versus which reading their argument actually *needs* in order to work. Then stress-test the readings against three variants:

- The predictor is only 90% accurate. Walk through the expected values for both strategies. Does "taking both can't cost you anything" survive contact with actual numbers, and if not, why does it sound so convincing? (I suspect it's saying something true about each possible state of the world that just isn't the thing that matters.)
- The transparent-box version: you can see the opaque box is full before you decide. Does "rationality should win" still tell you to take just the one box, or does the slogan quietly stop applying?
- The creepy version: the prediction is based on a brain scan from last week, and your "deliberation" now is just the scan's output playing itself out. Is there even a decision left for rationality to win at?

Finally: is there a precisification where both guys are saying something true and the whole fight is really about the word "rational"? Give me all this as a menu with verdicts, not an essay that takes one side.
