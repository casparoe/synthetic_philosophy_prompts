I'm trying to work out whether there is such a thing as a rationally required prior, so I've written down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and I'd like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. A coin is about to be flipped and I know nothing about it. 1/2 on heads seems not just permitted but rationally required. Anyone who went 0.9 on heads here would be making a mistake.
2. Same coin, but now I'm asked for a prior over its long-run bias, some p in [0,1]. Uniform feels a bit arbitrary (why not the Jeffreys prior, which piles mass near 0 and 1?), but I'm inclined to say some precise prior is required, I'm just not sure which.
3. A factory produces cubes with side lengths somewhere between 1 and 3 cm, and that's all I know. Being indifferent over side length gives one distribution, being indifferent over volume gives an incompatible one, and I honestly can't see that either is rationally mandatory. My verdict: both are permissible, neither is required.
4. A die is rolled many times and I'm told the long-run average is 4.5. The maximum entropy distribution given that constraint feels like the rationally correct prior for the next roll -- going for anything else seems like smuggling in information I don't have.
5. I'm asked for my prior over the working lifetime of a lightbulb, told nothing except that it's positive. Here I think the rational response is to refuse to have a precise prior at all -- a range of credences, or no determinate credence, seems fine, and anyone with a sharp exponential distribution is pretending to know something they don't.
6. I'm told a marble is some colour and asked for the probability it's red. Indifference over red vs not-red gives 1/2, indifference over red, green, blue, yellow, etc. gives something much smaller, and my verdict is that this shows you just can't run indifference here at all -- so no prior is rationally required, contra case 1.

So: is there one coherent principle (indifference where the symmetries are real, maximum entropy where there are constraints, permissiveness elsewhere) that delivers all six verdicts? Or do I have to give one up? I notice 1 and 4 push towards objective Bayesianism while 3 and 5 push towards a permissive or imprecise-credence view, and I'm not sure these can be unified without the principle becoming so hedged it stops saying anything. If you think a verdict has to go, tell me which and why. And whatever principle you land on, tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: a sealed urn contains 100 balls, each either red or black, in some unknown proportion -- what's the rational prior over that proportion, and is it precise?
