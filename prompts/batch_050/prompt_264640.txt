I'm a high school junior, and I got into this through a conversation with my math teacher that I haven't been able to stop thinking about. She mentioned offhand that no one in history had ever added the exact pair of numbers I was working on, and I asked how she knew I'd get the same answer she would, and she said "because we're following the same rule." Later I read a summary of the rule-following paradox -- apparently from a philosopher named Kripke writing about Wittgenstein -- and I tried to reconstruct the argument as carefully as I could. The problem is that my reconstruction seems to prove that nobody ever means anything by any word, which can't be right, but I can't find the step to cut. Here it is.

(1) By the time I encountered the problem 68 + 57, I had performed only finitely many additions in my life, over a finite range of numbers.

(2) My entire history of past behavior with the plus sign -- every answer I've given, every time I've used the word "plus" -- is consistent with infinitely many different rules: ordinary addition, but also a rule that behaves exactly like addition except that it outputs 5 for 68 + 57, and infinitely many more that diverge at cases I've never reached.

(3) Therefore my past behavior cannot, by itself, determine which rule I was following all along.

(4) Nor can it be fixed by a rule I recited to myself, like "add each column and carry," since the words in that instruction are themselves symbols whose application to a never-before-seen case needs interpretation, and the interpretation would need its own interpretation, and so on without end.

(5) Nor can it be fixed by my dispositions, for two reasons: my dispositions are finite and run out at sufficiently large numbers, and more importantly, my dispositions only record what I would do, not what I should do -- if I would mis-add 68 + 57 out of carelessness, my disposition says 5, but I still meant addition, so dispositions can get the meaning wrong.

(6) So there is no fact about me -- not my history, not my inner instructions, not my dispositions -- that settles whether I meant addition rather than some deviant rule.

(7) Addition was chosen arbitrarily; the same reasoning applies to every word in every speaker, including the words I used to state this argument.

(8) Therefore no one ever means one thing rather than another by any word: the entire practice of meaning is an illusion.

Conclusion (8) is absurd -- I'm obviously meaning things right now, and my teacher and I do get the same answers. But each premise feels to me like something I'd have defended before I saw where the chain was going. If I had to pick, I'd most like to keep (3): it genuinely seems true that a finite stretch of behavior underdetermines the rule. I'd be most willing to give up (5), since it's the premise I'm least sure I understand, though the correctness-versus-what-I'd-actually-do point feels real to me.

So: which step do I reject, and why did it look innocent? I also want to know the price. If the fix is something like "meaning is just a primitive fact about a person that doesn't reduce to behavior or dispositions," what does that commit me to? If the fix is something like "what I mean is fixed by how my community would judge my answers," does that mean a lone speaker -- one person, no community -- couldn't mean addition at all? And if there's a fix I've entirely missed, I want to know what it is and what it costs too.
