Here's my partner's answer, word for word:

"An etiological account says a trait's function is the effect it was selected for in the past, so the function is the effect that explains why the trait exists in organisms today. The heart's function is to pump blood, because ancestors whose hearts pumped blood survived better; the thumping noise it makes is just a side effect, not a function. A causal-role account says a trait's function is whatever it causally does that contributes to some capacity of the bigger system it's part of, and you don't need any evolutionary history for that, which is why we can talk about the functions of parts of machines or ecosystems. So the heart's function is pumping blood because that's its contribution to the circulatory system's capacity to deliver oxygen.

So both accounts agree that the whole point of a function claim is to explain why the trait is there in the organism. They just disagree about what kind of explanation it is: the etiological account explains the trait's presence by its selection history, and the causal-role account explains the trait's presence by the role it plays in the system right now.

Example: bird wings. Etiological: wings have the function of flight because wings were selected for enabling flight. Causal-role: wings have the function of flight because flapping them contributes to the bird's capacities of escaping predators and reaching food.

Objections. Against the causal-role account: it's too permissive, because the heart also contributes to the system's capacity to make thumping sounds, so noise-making would count as one of its functions, and the account can't separate real functions from accidents. I think this objection succeeds, because when biologists talk about functions they clearly mean something non-accidental. Against the etiological account, honestly I think it holds up well. It even handles brand-new traits: if a mutation appears tomorrow and it helps the organism, the new trait's function is already whatever helpful thing it does, since that's what it's doing for the organism."
