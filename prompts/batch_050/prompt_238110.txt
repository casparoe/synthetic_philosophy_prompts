A friend of mine from grad school, Dana, treats Pascal's wager as a general life algorithm, and I want to predict how she'll rule on a case before I bring it to her. Her touchstone is a specific bit of the wager fragment in the Pensées, not the famous coin-toss part: the part near the end where Pascal answers the person who says they want to believe but genuinely can't, and advises them to copy the people who were once stuck exactly the same way and got cured of it -- go through the motions, act as if you believed, take the holy water, have masses said -- and promises that doing this will quiet the passions and belief will follow on its own. Dana brings this passage up constantly.

Her verdicts so far, on cases people have put to her:

- Pascal's original wager: bet on God. Not a hard one.
- A street preacher who invents a god on the spot and offers the same deal: decline. She says the probability is negligible even if it isn't literally zero.
- A thoughtful neighbor presenting his own long-established religion's version of the wager, with the same infinite stakes: she says the two infinities cancel each other out and you then choose between the religions on ordinary finite grounds, like which practices you can actually live with and which community you'd rather join.
- Pascal's habituation strategy itself, from that passage: she fully endorses it. Deliberately training yourself into a belief by practicing it is perfectly rational if the wager math says so.
- A stranger who promises infinite bliss in ten years if you give him $50 now (Pascal's mugger): don't pay.
- Earthquake insurance at a fair price: buy it, obviously.

The new case: our friend Nadia is secular and, after a lot of reading, honestly assigns non-negligible probability to two rival religions. Religion A she finds credible on paper but psychologically unreachable -- she spent a year attending its services and felt nothing, and is confident more practice won't fix that. Religion B she could plausibly come to believe; she grew up around it. But A's practices are far less demanding, and its promises are if anything grander. What should Nadia do, according to Dana?

Please give me: "My prediction" (~100 words, her verdict on Nadia), "The rule she's actually running" (~200 words, state it precisely and show it fits all six verdicts, and note where at least one rival rule fits them too but diverges here), "Where the passage bites" (~100 words, whether endorsing habituation commits her to telling Nadia to just try harder at Religion A), and "The tiebreaker case" (~75 words, one further verdict that would settle which rule is hers).
