Last weekend I got into a long and unresolved argument with a friend about how we ought to judge historical figures who participated in practices we now regard as monstrous. She pressed the familiar "man of his time" defence on Jefferson's behalf, and when I countered that Jefferson condemned slavery in print while continuing to enslave hundreds of people, she replied that Churchill and Columbus raise the same structure and that consistency would force me either to absolve all of them or to condemn people for failing to be moral prodigies. The exchange kept circling because we never pinned down what the "man of his time" defence actually claims. It seems to me there are at least three readings: an epistemic reading (he could not have known better, since the opposing arguments were not genuinely available to him), a motivational reading (he could not fairly have been expected to act differently, given the social and economic costs), and a standards-internal reading (we may only judge people by norms that were endogenous to their own culture). The case of Jefferson seems to cut against all three in a peculiar way, because in Query XVIII of his Notes on the State of Virginia he himself argues that slavery corrupts the character of the master, that children raised watching the daily exercise of despotism absorb and imitate it, and that a statesman who permits half the citizens to trample on the rights of the other half deserves to be loaded with execration. So Jefferson supplies, in his own voice and from within his own time, the very standard under which he fares worst.

Here is what I would like you to do, and please approach it as a professional philosopher who works on historical injustice, moral responsibility, and excusing conditions. Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. It should present two fictional protagonists in a realistic historical setting (an eighteenth-century slaveholding society, no time travel, no anachronistic abolitionist pamphlets falling from the sky, no narrator stipulating intuitions), matched on every feature you can hold fixed: era, upbringing, economic dependence on the institution, exposure to dissenting voices, intelligence, and social costs of defection. They must differ on exactly one variable, and that variable must be whether the protagonist has himself sincerely arrived at, and publicly articulated, the moral principle that condemns the practice — not merely heard the principle, but endorsed it as his own. The scenario should be engineered so that the "man of his time" excuse intuitively has real grip on one protagonist and loses it on the other, on at least one of the three readings above, without the case simply presupposing that outcome.

Structure your answer as follows. First, briefly brainstorm two or three candidate variables for the single point of divergence and say why you rejected the ones you did not use (in particular, explain why the chosen variable is not just the epistemic reading in disguise). Second, give the scenario itself, one paragraph. Third, the demonstration: state the verdict the case elicits for each protagonist under each of the three readings of the defence, and check that the specifications are jointly satisfied rather than stipulated. Fourth, identify the load-bearing feature of the construction and tell me honestly what happens to the contrast if that feature is removed. Fifth, give the strongest attack a contextualist defender of my friend's position would make against the case, and assess whether it succeeds. Finally, apply the case back to the Jefferson passage described above: if your experiment works, does Jefferson's self-condemnation aggravate his culpability, mitigate it (on the grounds that a man who diagnoses his own corruption has partially escaped it), or does it reveal that the whole "man of his time" framing was misconceived from the start?
