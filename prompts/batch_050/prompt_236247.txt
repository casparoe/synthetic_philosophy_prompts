I'm prepping a seminar on ethics and future generations and want an essay question on Interstellar (the Nolan film, 2014). Read it through Sidgwick's axiom of temporal impartiality from The Methods of Ethics -- his claim, roughly, that the mere fact of a good being later in time is no rational ground for counting it as less, so a rational agent weighs the hereafter neither more nor less than the now. My question: does the film actually believe this? The whole mission looks Sidgwickian on the surface (sacrifice your remaining years with your kids because humanity's future outweighs one family's present), but the film keeps pulling the other way: the time dilation on Miller's planet literalises a discount rate, with the crew watching Earth decades slip away as if those future years were leaking value, and the climax seems to vindicate particular love across time as the thing that saves the species rather than impartial concern for it. And then there's Plan A versus Plan B, which I think is where the film quietly breaks Sidgwick: the people Plan B would create only exist because the people alive on Earth get abandoned, so who exactly is wronged, and does the film even notice the non-identity problem it's stepped in? Give me a reading that takes Sidgwick seriously rather than using him as a stick to beat a blockbuster with -- point to specific scenes where the film resists the axiom, and say at the end whether the film adds anything to the debate about discounting future lives that the journal articles dont. British English please.
