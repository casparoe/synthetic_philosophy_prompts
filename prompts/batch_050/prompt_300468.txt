I am preparing a graduate seminar on imperfect recall and self-locating belief, and before I commit to a view in print I want to take stock of my own case-intuitions and see whether any coherent principle underlies them. I will present a base scenario and a series of variations, together with my verdict on each. What I want from you is a principle -- or, if necessary, a small set of competing principles -- that captures the pattern, stated precisely enough that I can see which of my verdicts motivates each clause, what it predicts for cases I have not listed, and which of my verdicts, if any, must be surrendered as the price of systematicity.

The base scenario: a driver travels a road at night with two consecutive intersections, each offering an exit. Exiting at the first intersection yields payoff 0; exiting at the second yields 4; driving past both yields 1. The intersections are perceptually indistinguishable, and the driver suffers from a known form of amnesia: upon reaching any intersection, he retains no memory of whether he has already passed one. He knows all of this in advance, including the payoff structure and the fact of his future amnesia.

Here are my verdicts.

(1) In the base case, the driver ought to settle on a plan in his driveway -- some fixed probability of continuing at an intersection, chosen to maximize expected payoff -- and, upon finding himself at an intersection, simply execute it. Deliberating afresh at the intersection and departing from the plan on the basis of reasoning available to him there strikes me as a mistake, not an improvement. What he knows at the intersection, he already knew he would know.

(2) Now vary the case: before the trip, a fair coin determined which of two roads the driver is on. Road A is the base case. Road B has only a single intersection; exiting there yields 4, and continuing past it yields 0. When the driver now finds himself at an intersection, I think exiting is considerably more attractive than any driveway plan covering both roads would suggest, and I think he is permitted -- indeed required -- to treat the bare fact of finding himself at an intersection as evidence that shifts his deliberation. Yet I cannot say precisely why this differs from (1), where I denied him exactly that license.

(3) A companion rides along who suffers no amnesia, but the driver has arranged in advance that the companion will call out one and the same instruction at every intersection, since the driver cannot tell them apart. I think the companion should call out whatever implements the driveway plan, and the driver should obey. Furthermore -- and here I surprise myself -- I think the companion acts rationally even when she knows they are at the first intersection and knows her instruction is therefore worse than its alternative at this instance, because her instruction-type had to be uniform across instances.

(4) The driver's amnesia is probabilistic: at the second intersection he remembers passing the first with probability one-half. If he remembers, plainly he should exit. If he does not remember, I think some adjustment away from the driveway plan is appropriate, because the absence of a memory is now informative in a way it was not in the base case, where forgetting was guaranteed. So I have, in effect, three verdicts -- (1), (2), and (4) -- that seem to demand a principled line between new self-locating evidence and the mere re-encounter of old uncertainty, and I cannot draw the line myself.

(5) Suppose the driver makes this trip every night for many years, with payoffs accumulating. Here I am confident that plan-execution is correct, and moreover that his credence at an intersection should track long-run frequencies: if his plan is to continue with probability p, his credence that he is at the first intersection should be 1/(1+p).

(6) But for the driver who makes the trip exactly once, I balk at the frequency-based credence -- I am not sure 1/(1+p) means anything for him -- while still holding that he should execute the plan. So I appear to have severed the rationality of the action from the availability of the credence that would justify it in the repeated case. I do not know whether that is a deep point or an inconsistency.

(7) Finally, suppose that before departure the driver can purchase a device that mechanically forces him to continue at the first intersection and exit at the second. I think purchasing it is not merely permissible but obviously rational, and that its value consists precisely in eliminating in-the-moment deliberation rather than improving it. Yet if the device were to malfunction at an intersection, I think the driver would then face a genuine deliberative problem rather than a non-problem -- which sits uneasily with my verdict in (1).

My questions are these. First, is there a principle governing the relation among prior plans, self-locating belief, and rational action under imperfect recall that accommodates all seven verdicts, or some principled subset of them? I suspect it will turn on a distinction between evidence that is genuinely new to the deliberator and uncertainty that was fully priced in at the planning stage, but I want that distinction stated sharply enough to sort (1), (2), and (4) without ad hoc maneuvering. Second, what does the resulting principle predict about cases I have not considered -- say, a version in which the driver finds, at each intersection, a note in his own hand reading exit here, without any memory of having written it or knowledge of which intersection he wrote it at? Third, if no reasonably simple principle saves all seven verdicts, which one or two should I give up, and why those rather than others? I would rather be told that my pattern of intuitions is incoherent in an interesting way than be offered a baroque principle that flatters all of them.
