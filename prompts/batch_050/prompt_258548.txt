Last month, near the end of our unit on Japanese American internment, my classmate Dana and my history teacher Mr. Alden got into an argument after class, and I stayed to listen. It started when Dana said she knew the internment was wrong partly because survivors had told us so — meaning the guest speaker who visited and the recorded testimonies we watched. I've since learned that philosophers call the underlying issue moral testimony — whether you can come to know that something is right or wrong by being told — but neither of them used that term, and I'd like the argument judged as they actually made it.

Dana's opening move was that moral knowledge through testimony is not only possible, it's how nearly everyone has nearly all of it. Nobody in our class derived the wrongness of slavery from first principles; we learned it from parents, teachers, and books. If testimony can't transmit moral knowledge, then almost nobody knows anything moral, which is absurd, and morality becomes the one subject where learning from other people is somehow illegitimate.

Mr. Alden replied that she was running together being told a fact and understanding a reason. He compared two students who both believe cheating is wrong: one sees that it treats classmates unfairly, the other believes it because his coach said so. If a new coach praised cheating, the second student flips — and that fragility shows he never knew it, he only had a borrowed opinion. Worse, someone whose moral views rest on authority is equipped to adopt terrible views on authority; some version of "I was told this was right" is the oldest excuse in history. Testimony can point you toward moral truth, he said, but the knowing happens when you grasp the reasons yourself.

Dana answered with two points. First, the coach case shows only that some testimony-based belief isn't knowledge, not that none is: a child taught by a decent parent that it's wrong to hurt people for fun, who holds that belief stably into adulthood, plausibly knows it, and demanding derivation from first principles sets a bar we meet for almost nothing else we claim to know. Second, his view has an ugly consequence for the testimony that started all this. When survivors tell us what was done to them and that it was wrong, saying "I can't know that from your testimony, I have to reach the conclusion myself" is not rigor — it's refusal dressed up as rigor, and an epistemology that requires discounting survivors is a bad epistemology.

Mr. Alden said he wasn't discounting survivors, he was being precise about what their testimony contains. A survivor testifies to facts: what happened, what it felt like, what it destroyed. We then judge, on those facts, that it was wrong — and we can defend that judgment with reasons, which is what makes it knowledge. His proof: if the same speaker gave the same facts but concluded "and so it was justified," we would keep the facts and reject the conclusion, which shows the moral conclusion was never part of what testimony hands us. And the child case: yes, a true, stable, valuable belief — but call it moral belief, not knowledge. The reason we teach children the reasons as they grow up is precisely that testimony alone isn't enough.

Dana's last move was that his clean split between facts and conclusions is doing all the work, and it isn't clean. "They treated us as less than human" is not a neutral fact report with the morality drained out; it's already morally loaded, and refusing the conclusion refuses part of the testimony itself. Besides, his proof proves too much: historians also give facts plus interpretations, and we sometimes reject the interpretation while keeping the facts, yet nobody concludes testimony can't transmit historical knowledge. If that move works against moral testimony, it works against testimony generally, and he's back to the absurdity she opened with.

Neither convinced the other, and when they asked me, I honestly couldn't say. Please adjudicate. Then give a real verdict on who is more right overall rather than splitting the difference, and say what, if anything, both of them are missing.
