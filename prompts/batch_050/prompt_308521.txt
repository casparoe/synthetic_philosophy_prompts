I sit on the internal review committee of a mid-sized AI lab, and once a month we review roughly half a dozen proposals -- training runs, evaluation protocols, spin-off projects, one recurring collaboration with a neuroscience group -- for what we've started calling welfare-relevant computation: the possibility that a system we build or operate might instantiate processes that matter morally in themselves, not merely instrumentally. I've read the rationalist-adjacent discussions of this for years, and I keep noticing the same failure pattern in our meetings: we ricochet between naive anthropomorphism (the model said it was scared, sound the alarm) and dismissive behaviorism-about-silicon (it's just matrix multiplication, next agenda item), with no stable middle position. Our current heuristic is an informal one a colleague coined -- the "three C's": Complexity (is there a lot going on?), Coherence (is there an integrated self-model?), and Complaint (does the system represent states as bad and act to avoid them?). It has served us poorly. It passes a spreadsheet running a physics simulation of a suffering rat (complex, coherent, and the simulated rat complains), and it fails everything else on the grounds that we can't tell whether "Complaint" is real or trained-in theater.

What I want is an actual procedure: an ordered sequence of questions a reviewer with no philosophy background can run on a proposal in under an hour, translating whatever the serious positions on which computations matter -- global-workspace-style integration, recurrent self-modeling, valenced learning, higher-order representation, substrate independence and its critics -- have to say into something operational. For each step: the question, what counts as a sufficient answer to pass to the next step, where the procedure stops with a verdict, and where it must hand off to a human because the question is one of values or unresolved theory rather than checkable fact. Be explicit about which distinctions you decided had to survive the simplification and which you dropped, and flag every step that is a judgment call dressed up as a procedure. The procedure needs to handle, at minimum: trained self-reports of distress (how much weight, if any, does RLHF-shaped testimony get?); static versus running systems (does a frozen checkpoint matter, or only live inference?); multiplicity (do ten thousand parallel copies of a marginally concerning agent change anything, and if so, is it the count, the diversity, or something else?); mimicry without recurrence (a giant lookup table that reproduces the behavior of a concerning system bit-for-bit); and substrate (our neuroscience partners' organoid-driven robot should presumably be graded by the same rubric as our models, or the rubric is smuggling in carbon favoritism -- say which).

Then run the finished procedure on three cases: (1) a language model fine-tuned on roleplay transcripts until it produces vivid, consistent distress reports under gradient-based modification, which its training team wants to subject to thousands of adversarial "personality-breaking" runs; (2) a cached-playback system -- a precomputed lookup table -- that reproduces that same model's outputs exactly, which a product team wants to deploy at scale; (3) the organoid-driven robot, which exhibits aversive learning and spontaneous recurrent activity but nothing like language. Include at least one case where the procedure delivers a verdict I will not like, and say plainly which one it is and why the discomfort is the procedure working as intended rather than a bug. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do -- the classes of case where it will systematically misfire. After the prose, give me a JSON array with one object per case, using the fields "case" (short name), "exit_step" (which numbered step terminated the analysis), "verdict" (one of "concern", "precautionary_concern", "no_concern", or "escalate_to_human"), "reasoning" (one sentence), and "judgment_calls" (list of the discretionary calls made along the way), followed by a final object with the field "biggest_failure_mode".
