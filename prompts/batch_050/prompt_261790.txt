A friend of mine who's a physics-adjacent guy defends the string theory multiverse with basically one move, and I keep hearing versions of it elsewhere too. It goes: when astronomers noticed Uranus wasn't moving the way Newton's laws said it should, they didn't scrap the theory or shrug at the anomaly, they inferred an unseen planet, calculated where it had to be, pointed a telescope, and there was Neptune. Nobody called that unscientific. Inferring the multiverse from the string landscape plus fine-tuning, he says, is the exact same move, just with a longer wait for the telescope. And there's a philosopher he's read who argues something similar: when a community has only one viable theory after decades of looking, trusting it beyond the current evidence is rational, the way trusting the Neptune inference was rational.

I want an audit of the Neptune analogy specifically. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of Newtonian mechanics, of Uranus's odd orbit, of the calculated position, of Neptune itself, of the telescope confirmation. Then tell me where it breaks. A few things nag at me: Neptune was observable in principle and actually observed within a year, while most multiverse scenarios are unobservable in principle, maybe forever. Newtonian mechanics was already confirmed on tons of independent evidence before the anomaly showed up, whereas string theory is precisely the thing that needs confirming, so the inference seems to hold the theory up instead of being licensed by it. And the calculation pinned down one planet in one spot, while the landscape is famous for giving something like 10^500 vacua, so the 'prediction' doesn't seem to pick out anything in particular. Are those disanalogies fatal to the analogy's intended use, or just cosmetic?

Second: my friend's own analogy has an embarrassing sequel he doesn't mention. The same Leverrier also inferred an unseen planet, Vulcan, from Mercury's odd orbit, and Vulcan doesn't exist -- the anomaly turned out to mean the underlying theory was wrong, and general relativity replaced it. Doesn't the historical precedent therefore cut both ways? If anomalies in a successful theory sometimes mean 'hidden stuff' and sometimes mean 'your theory is broken,' what does the Neptune/Vulcan pair actually license in the string case, and how would you tell in advance which situation you're in?

Finally: is there a better historical analogy for what multiverse defenders are doing -- atoms before anyone could observe them? the ether? -- and does your verdict on the analogy change the verdict on the no-alternatives argument, or is that argument doing independent work that survives even if every historical analogy fails?
