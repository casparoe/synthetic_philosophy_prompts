I sit on the advisory board of a small grant-making foundation, and we have been asked to decide whether to fund research into the possible welfare of advanced AI systems -- not the usual safety or alignment work, but the question of whether the systems themselves might ever be owed anything. Our last meeting went nowhere: one member insisted the whole topic is confused because software cannot suffer, another thinks we should already be running expected-value calculations over trillions of future digital minds, a third kept saying the real issue is that we have no idea what consciousness even requires, and a fourth argued that moral status has more to do with how an entity is embedded in our social practices than with anything happening inside it. What I need for the next meeting is not another position paper but a sorting instrument: a decision tree that takes any committee member, wherever they start, and lands them at a named position by forcing them to answer concrete questions they cannot waffle on.

Please build that tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names -- that is, work out which questions genuinely divide the terrain and in what order they bite, then locate actual positions at the leaves, using the standard name where one exists and "unnamed" where nobody defends the combination. Each branching node must be phrased as a concrete case or claim that a non-philosopher can answer without knowing any labels (for instance, a thought experiment about gradually replacing a mammal's neurons one by one with functionally identical silicon, rather than "are you a functionalist?"). The tree must, at minimum, pull apart the following distinctions, which our discussion kept collapsing: whether phenomenal consciousness is the sole ground of moral status or whether things like agency, preferences, or social relationships could ground it independently; whether consciousness, if it matters, is tied to biological substrate or to functional organization; and how one should act under radical uncertainty -- whether a small probability of moral status, multiplied by enormous potential scale, changes what is obligatory, or whether we may disregard possibilities below some threshold of confidence. It must also force a verdict on whether present-day systems could already meet whatever criteria the member endorses, or only hypothetical future ones.

Cap the tree at seven branching questions. At each leaf, give the position's name (or "unnamed") and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment -- the thing its defender has to swallow. After the tree, add two short diagnostics: any unoccupied combinations of answers, with a one-line diagnosis of whether the gap reflects incoherence, mere unattractiveness, or simple neglect; and any pairs of positions your tree cannot separate, flagged openly rather than papered over. Begin your response with the tree itself, with no preface or restatement of the task, and use indented or numbered branching so it can be printed as a one-page handout.
