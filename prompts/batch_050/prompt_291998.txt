I'm in a philosophy of language / AI ethics course and also volunteer with our campus writing center, and we keep getting cases where a student used an LLM and something went wrong with truthfulness. Our professer keeps saying Frankfurt's lying vs bullshit distinction should help, but it defintely doesn't map cleanly onto a model that has no beliefs, so I want to turn it into a procedure the writing center can actually use during intake.

Write me a decision procedure a staffer could run in about 15 minutes on a flagged AI-assisted submission, with no special philosophy background, that classifies the problematic output/conduct into tiers like: ordinary error, reckless error, lie by the student, bullshit by the student, "bullshit-adjacent" machine output, institutional deception, or not our jurisdiction. Put the tests in order: what evidence we need at each step, what counts as enough, where the procedure must stop and say no violation, and where it has to hand things to a human because intent or values can't be settled by checklist. Be explicit about which Frankfurt-ish distinctions have to survive the translation (indifference to truth vs intent to deceive vs falsity, speaker vs tool, assertion vs suggestion, downstream adoption of a claim) and which ones we should drop because they anthropomorphize the model too much.

Then apply it to three cases: (1) the chatbot invents a plausible-sounding source and the student pastes it without checking; (2) the chatbot gives a false claim the student honestly believes because it matches lecture notes; (3) the student asks for "a confident paragraph arguing X," gets true sentences chosen only for persuasiveness, and submits them while not caring whether X is true. Include at least one verdict I won't like, and say where this procedure will misfire.
