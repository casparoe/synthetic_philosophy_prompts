So I had a massive argument with a mate last night, fuelled by a bottle of wine, about the Libet experiments and free will, and I've realised we weren't really disagreeing about any fact -- we just kept talking past each other for three hours. His position: the science is settled, the readiness potential shows the brain "decides" before you're aware of deciding, later work even claims to predict choices several seconds out from brain scans, so free will is an illusion and philosophers are just in denial. My position: the whole experimental setup tests something no one ever meant by free will -- flexing your wrist at a random moment when you've been told to flex it sometime in the next minute is about as far from a real decision as you can get, and even Libet himself thought he'd left room for a last-minute veto. Neither of us budged, and what struck me afterwards is that this debate has been stuck in exactly this spot for decades, with both sides apparently aware of everything the other side says.

So here's what I want from you: a diagnosis of why this debate doesn't move, not another rundown of the experiments. Is this actually an empirical disagreement that better neuroscience could settle (say, if prediction accuracy got to 99% with real-world decisions), or is it a conceptual disagreement about what phenomenon the experiments are even supposed to be about -- with the neuroscientists operationalising "decision" as whatever shows up first in the brain data and the philosophers insisting that arbitrary urges to move were never the interesting case? My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the two sides are answering different questions under one banner, the stall is therefore permanent in its current form, and progress would require splitting the question rather than gathering more data. But maybe I'm being too convenient and there's a real empirical disagreement hiding in there, e.g. about whether conscious awareness is causally idle in action production, which would be testable in principle.

Please be committed rather than balanced. Tell me what kind of disagreement this really is, defend that verdict with reasons, and then say what would genuinely count as progress given your diagnosis -- a particular conceptual distinction, a particular kind of experiment, or just abandoning the framing -- rather than hedging with "it could be a bit of both". If you think the debate is stalled because the question itself is ill-posed, say that outright. I'd rather be told I'm wrong than given a menu.
