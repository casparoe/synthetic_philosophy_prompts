I sit on the professional conduct committee of the statutory body that licenses structural engineers in my country, and I have been asked to draft our decision memo in a case that has divided us. The respondent is a chartered engineer of thirty years' standing with an unblemished record. For the past five years he has published articles, a book, and newspaper interviews arguing that our national loading standard for large-span flat roofs is roughly twice as demanding as the physics and the practice of comparable countries require, and that it survives mainly because suppliers of structural material fund the committee that drafts it. He says openly that he would design to half the standard's values if the law permitted. The law does not permit it, and nobody alleges he has ever designed a non-compliant or unsafe structure: the case concerns his speech alone.

The complainants, a group of fellow engineers, charge him under the code clause prohibiting "conduct liable to bring the profession into disrepute or to prejudice public safety." Their evidence is that developers have begun citing his book in applications for dispensations from the standard, and that two building-control authorities report it is now routinely quoted at their inspectors in the field. His defence is that safety standards are revised precisely through heterodox advocacy of this kind, that everything he has said is either true or a good-faith reading of genuinely contested evidence, and that a regulator which punishes engineers for criticising the standard it administers has rendered that standard unfalsifiable.

Our powers run from reprimand to suspension. Several members keep invoking the harm principle; others reply that a licensing body is not the state and that Mill has no grip here. Draft the memo: a clear recommendation on both liability and sanction, in terms that could be entered in the published determination and answered on appeal, together with an honest account of the strongest point on the losing side. Close by stating what would change the recommendation -- the single consideration which, if it came out the other way, would flip the verdict -- and where the memo's reasoning is most likely to fail. Please write in British English.
