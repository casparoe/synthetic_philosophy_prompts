For a reading group on environmental ethics and population ethics, I've been asked to circulate a written statement of my views on wild animal welfare before we meet, so the others can attack a fixed target rather than my improvisations. Here is the set I'd like audited. (1) My axiology is broadly hedonic and total: what matters is the balance of positive and negative experience, summed across all individuals, with a life that is net-negative on balance being a bad thing to bring into existence and a net-positive life a good thing; I extend this to any being with valenced experience, weighted by my credence that it is in fact sentient, which means insects and other small invertebrates count for something but substantially less per individual than vertebrates. (2) On the empirical side, I find it more likely than not that the average wild animal life in a productive ecosystem is net-negative: most wild individuals die extremely young, juvenile mortality dominates the population count, and deaths from starvation, parasitism, and predation are plausibly among the worse experiences in nature. (3) Despite (2), I regard large-scale habitat destruction -- deforestation, wetland draining, conversion of grassland -- as among the most urgent ongoing harms, and I'd direct serious money toward preventing it. (4) I do not believe species, ecosystems, or biodiversity as such have interests or intrinsic value; they matter only insofar as they affect sentient individuals, human or otherwise. (5) Methodologically, I hold that our uncertainty about wild animal welfare counsels caution: the burden of proof lies on anyone proposing to intervene in nature, because large interventions in complex systems tend to have effects worse than expected. (6) I lean somewhat prioritarian, in that suffering at the very bottom of the welfare distribution deserves extra weight beyond what a simple sum gives it.

What I want from you is a consistency audit, not a survey of the literature. Go through the set and identify which commitments genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to; for each real tension, state precisely which commitments generate it, why the obvious escape routes are or aren't available to me given the rest of the set, and how severe the problem is. Rank the tensions rather than treating them as equally serious. Then tell me which single revision -- one commitment abandoned or weakened -- resolves the most, and what it costs me. I'd also like to know whether any of my six commitments is doing no work at all, in the sense that dropping it would leave my other five untouched. I should add that I genuinely don't know where the problem is: (3) is the commitment I'm most emotionally attached to, but I wrote the list intending to follow it wherever it leads, so please don't soften the verdict on that account.
