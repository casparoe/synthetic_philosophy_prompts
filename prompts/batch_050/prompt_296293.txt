I am a novelist at work on a historical novel in which David Hume, by a device of the plot I need not explain here, is brought to the present day and confronted with the strongest modern candidate for a publicly witnessed miracle. The case I intend to put before him is modeled on the 1917 event at Fátima: an occurrence announced in advance by visionaries, attended by a crowd estimated in the tens of thousands, reported in the secular press, and described by many present as the sun moving, spinning, or plunging toward the earth — while a significant number of people standing in the same crowd, including some skeptics, reported seeing nothing unusual at all. I may also have my characters confront him with the Lourdes model, in which a standing medical bureau of physicians examines claimed cures and certifies a small number as inexplicable by current science.

What I need from you is not a summary of the argument in Section X of the Enquiry, which I know, but a disciplined exercise in extrapolation: given Hume's actual machinery — the weighing of testimony against uniform experience, his suspicion of the love of wonder, his point that the miracle reports of rival religions cancel one another, his insistence that no testimony has ever established a miracle attested by sufficiently many men of good sense and education — what would he most plausibly conclude about a case like Fátima, which seems engineered to satisfy several of his criteria at once? In particular: does the mass-witness character of the event, with prior announcement and press coverage, force him to concede more than he conceded in any case he himself treated? Does the dissent within the crowd — those present who saw nothing — do decisive work for him, and if so, is that move available on his own principles or does it quietly assume that perception under religious expectation is unreliable in a way he never argues for? And would the Lourdes practice of institutional medical vetting strike him as meeting his demand for witnesses of education and integrity, or merely as relocating the wonder-loving disposition into credentialed hands?

Please commit. I do not want a survey of the positions a Humean might take; I want your single best reconstruction of what Hume himself would say about the Fátima-style case, defended as such, and then your own verdict — again committed — on whether his surviving reply succeeds or whether a case of this structure exposes a genuine hole in the argument that only a modern Bayesian treatment of testimony could repair. Wherever your reconstruction depends on a contested reading of Hume rather than settled text, flag it briefly and tell me why you side as you do, since I will have a skeptical Hume scholar read the manuscript and I need to know in advance which pages he will mark up.
