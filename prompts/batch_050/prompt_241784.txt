I've been persuaded by the inferentialist program in the philosophy of language -- the Brandomian idea that conceptual content is exhausted by a term's or sentence's role in the practice of giving and asking for reasons: what it can be inferred from, what can be inferred from it, what it is incompatible with, and how speakers keep score of each other's commitments and entitlements. On this view, reference and truth are downstream of inferential articulation rather than prior to it, and grasping a concept just is mastering the proprieties governing its use in that game. I want to stress-test this before I commit to it in print, so: what would actually count against it?

I don't want a list of standard objections rehearsed at me. I want a map of the view's exposure, organized as follows. First, the defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward: which observations, arguments, or results would genuinely sink inferentialism, as opposed to which would only embarrass it. Second, for each, an honest distinction between what the view's own defenders would concede as a real cost -- holism's instability of meaning, say, or the difficulty of compositional vindication -- and what they would dismiss as based on a misunderstanding of the program. Third, an assessment of collapse-risks: results or reconstructions that would show inferentialism secretly reduces to a rival -- a deflated use-theory, say, or truth-conditional semantics with extra sociological machinery -- and whether, if it collapsed that way, anything would actually be lost.

Two pressure points I'd like treated in particular. One is a dissociation worry of my own construction: imagine a subject with flawless recognitional and applicational competence with a predicate -- reliably sorting instances, deferring to experts appropriately, correcting errors in others -- whose inferential dispositions with that predicate are systematically disordered; and imagine the reverse case, a flawless inferentialist who cannot apply the predicate to the world at all. If either subject counts as fully grasping the concept, inferentialism is in trouble; if neither does, intuitions split. Which horn should the inferentialist take, and does the choice carry costs elsewhere? The other is the arrival of large language models: systems that can, to all appearances, participate extensively in the game of giving and asking for reasons, while lacking the perceptual entries and practical exits that the view's more careful versions build in. Does this constitute a natural experiment the inferentialist must welcome, a counterexample to the sufficiency of inferential mastery, or neither -- and does the answer depend on whether content requires actual scorekeepers holding the system responsible, not merely dispositions to respond to it?

Finally, I want a verdict on insulation. Inferentialism's holism gives it an unusual ability to absorb counterexamples by reindividuating meanings: any apparent inferential failure can be redescribed as a conceptual change rather than an error. State plainly whether you think this maneuver renders the view unfalsifiable in practice, whether unfalsifiability is a defect for a view of this kind -- a theory of content rather than an empirical hypothesis -- and what a good-faith inferentialist ought to hold hostage to fortune to show the position is risking something real.
