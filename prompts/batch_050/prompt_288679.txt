So last night I got into a genuinely dumb three-hour argument with my friend Danny about whether the models we ship actually understand anything or are just very elaborate sphex wasps. You probably know the digger wasp story philosophers love: the wasp drags a paralyzed cricket to its burrow entrance, pops inside to check the burrow, and if you nudge the cricket a few inches away while it's down there, it comes out, drags the cricket back to the entrance, and goes in to check again — and you can apparently do this dozens of times without it ever once just hauling the cricket straight in. Looks smart, turns out to be running a script.

Danny's position: our flagship model is the same deal, flexible-looking until something nudges it off-distribution, and the only reason nobody notices is that nobody's job is nudging. My position was that brittle and sphexish aren't the same thing and I could prove it with tests. He said fine, what tests, and I realized I didn't have a procedure, just vibes.

So write me the procedure I should've had. Context: I'm on the evals team at a small AI company, and before every release (roughly every six weeks) I sign off on whether a model is flexible enough to deploy. Black-box only, about two days of compute, I'm an engineer not a philosopher, and the VP of engineering countersigns. I want an ordered battery of "nudge the cricket" tests that seperates cached routines from genuine flexibility: which perturbations to run in which order, explicit pass/fail criteria for each, how to tell "adapted" from "restarted the script," and how to distinguish a model that can't flex from one that just never learned the thing. Say which distinctions from the philosophy have to survive translation into tests and which we can safely drop, and flag the steps where judgment can't be proceduralized away.

Then run the procedure on three hypotheticals: a support bot that's flawless on refunds until the refund policy changes in a way its templates didn't anticipate; a coding agent that handles renamed APIs fine but not a changed objective; and me, a human with ten years of engineering habits. At least one of these should come out with an answer I won't like. End with what the procedure can't settle even in principle — I suspect that's where the free will part of the argument was hiding.
