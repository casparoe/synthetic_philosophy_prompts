I'm a data scientist with roughly a decade of experience in time-series analysis and signal processing, and essentially no formal training in historiography. A reading group I'm part of recently took up Fernand Braudel's famous tripartition of historical time: the longue durée of geographical, climatic, and environmental structures; the medium-term conjonctures of economic and demographic cycles; and the rapid time of events, politics, and individuals. Throughout the discussion, the engineers among us kept translating everything Braudel said into our own idiom, and after a while I started to suspect the translation was doing more work than we realized. I'd like to test that suspicion properly.

Please restate Braudel's three-layer picture as rigorously as you can in the vocabulary I actually work in: trend-cycle decomposition, low- and high-pass filtering, frequency bands, stationarity and non-stationarity, regime changes, exogenous shocks, noise floors, and the like. Assume full fluency on my end — don't slow down to explain what a Hodrick-Prescott filter or a spectrogram is. Then report on what did not survive the translation: which of Braudel's claims map cleanly onto standard decomposition machinery, which had to be bent or weakened to fit, and which seem to have no counterpart whatsoever in how a time-series analyst carves up a signal. Two claims in particular interest me. First, Braudel seems to hold that the layers don't merely coexist at different speeds but genuinely constrain and act upon one another — the slow structures shape what the fast events can be — and I can't decide whether that's expressible in my framework or whether my framework's assumption of a single observed signal rules it out from the start. Second, his polemical insistence that historians fixated on events are studying surface agitation while missing the deep currents: is that an analytical claim that survives translation, or just a disciplinary preference for low frequencies dressed up as one?

Close with your verdict on the residue. Where the translation fails, is the failure a limitation of the signal-processing vocabulary — which treats decomposition as a descriptive choice applied to one given series, not as a causal theory of what produces the series — or does the failed translation expose places where Braudel's position was carrying rhetorical weight rather than analytical content? I have a stake in the answer: if the mapping is tighter than I suspect, my reading group can keep its shortcut; if the residue is real and important, I want to know exactly where my intuitions start misleading me.
