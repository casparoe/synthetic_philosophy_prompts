A friend of mine got really into Scanlon-style contractualism last year — the view where an act is wrong if a principle permitting it could be reasonably rejected, with everyone's complaint assessed one-to-one rather than pooled into a big total — and now she issues instant verdicts on every triage case I bring her. I want to predict her next verdict before I ask her. Her record so far:

- a million people each get a nasty headache for an afternoon vs one person dies → save the one. Trivial claims don't aggregate against a serious one, full stop.
- one person dies vs five people die, everything else equal → save the five. When the claims on each side are equally serious, numbers decide.
- one death vs one total paralysis → prevent the death. Stronger individual claim wins.
- one death vs three cases of blindness → prevent the death. When I pushed on why, she just said blindness claims can't gang up on a death claim and changed the subject.

The new case for her: one person will die vs twenty people will each be totally paralyzed, no way to help both, no other morally relevant differences between anyone. Predict her verdict, but do it properly: lay out the two or three different rules that are each consistent with all four verdicts above (I'm pretty sure her blindness answer is doing a lot of quiet work and can be read at least two ways), say what each rule predicts for the twenty-paralysis case, give your best guess with reasons, and then tell me the follow-up case I should pose to her that would actually settle which rule she's running, since the four verdicts clearly don't. If none of the standard candidate rules fit her record and she's running something stranger, say that instead and describe it.

One more thing: when you sketch the contractualist background, mark what's genuinely settled among people who work on this versus what's contested. I've gotten conflicting answers on whether Scanlon's own one-to-one machinery even delivers "save the five" or whether that's a bolt-on, and I definately doubt there's an official contractualist line on one death vs twenty paralyses. Don't present one camp's position as the consensus if there isn't one.
