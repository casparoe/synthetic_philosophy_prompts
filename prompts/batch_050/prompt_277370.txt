I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for over a decade, and one debate I keep circling back to is whether something like the Kelly criterion — or, more broadly, the claim that a rational agent should maximize geometric rather than arithmetic growth — deserves the near-axiomatic status some commenters give it. After a long argument with a friend who insists plain expected value is obviously the right criterion for any wager, iterated or not, I sat down and wrote out my actual verdicts on a series of concrete bets. I'd like you, acting as a decision theorist with genuine expertise in mathematical finance and the foundations of rational choice, to work out what principle my verdicts actually add up to. Please think carefully: begin by brainstorming at least three distinct candidate principles, test each one against all of my cases, and only then commit to a final answer.

My bankroll in every case is $100,000, and my verdicts are these. (1) A fair coin: heads pays 2.5 times my stake, tails loses it, one round only. I'd happily bet 10% of my bankroll; I would flatly refuse to bet 80%. (2) A yearly bet repeated 30 times, with the full bankroll mandatory each round: heads multiplies wealth by 1.8, tails by 0.5. Each round has positive expected value (+15% arithmetic), yet I refuse the all-in version outright; if instead I may stake a fixed 20% of current wealth per round, I accept. (3) Same payoffs as case 2 with a freely chosen fraction: if it were a single round, I'd stake about 40%; knowing it repeats annually for 30 years, I'd only stake about 15%. (4) Pay $10,000 for a 1-in-10,000 chance at $1 billion. Enormously positive expected value; I decline without much hesitation. (5) Insuring my house against a 0.5%-per-year total loss costs slightly more than the actuarially fair premium. I buy it every year and consider refusing it irrational. (6) An acquaintance offers a take-it-or-leave-it deal: my entire $100,000 for a 50% chance of receiving $400,000 in five years and nothing otherwise, no partial stakes permitted. I decline.

Now the task itself. State the most precise principle — or smallest set of principles — that reproduces as many of these verdicts as possible, in mathematical form where you can, and for every clause or qualification the principle needs, name the case that forces it. I suspect maximizing expected log wealth captures most of them, but I want that stress-tested rather than assumed: tell me plainly whether any pair of my verdicts cannot be jointly captured by any myopic, horizon-independent growth rule, and whether the right repair is to complicate the principle (a drawdown constraint, a penalty on minimum wealth along the path, an explicit survival clause, or something else) or to abandon one of my verdicts — and if the latter, which one and why. Then apply your final principle to three cases I haven't listed: (a) the case-2 bet with an option to insure each round's downside for a fixed premium, and how the optimal stake changes; (b) a pooled version of case 2 in which a thousand participants each go all-in every round and split the proceeds equally; (c) the same wagers with stakes denominated in years of healthy life rather than money. Finally, flag any verdict where your principle is silently leaning on intuition rather than delivering the answer, and be honest about which questions the principle cannot settle at all.
