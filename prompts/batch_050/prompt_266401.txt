A friend and I recently spent most of an evening arguing about Sherlock Holmes. She held that Holmes exists -- not as a man of flesh and blood, obviously, but in roughly the way a number or a symphony exists -- while I insisted that there simply is no such person and never was, and that saying otherwise confuses the stories with reality. Neither of us budged, but the argument has left me well prepared for next week's podcast: my guest is a philosopher who defends roughly my friend's position, namely that fictional characters are real abstract objects that authors genuinely bring into being when they write their stories.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that press this view where it is actually vulnerable rather than letting the guest restate it. I'd like the set to cover, at minimum: how the view handles apparently true negative claims, such as the claim that Sherlock Holmes does not exist; how an abstract object can be a pipe-smoking detective without the guest quietly relying on two different senses of "is"; what makes it true in the stories that Holmes plays the violin but not true that he has eleven fingers, given that the text is silent on most things; whether an abstract object can really be created at all, or only noticed and named; and whether the Holmes of a modern pastiche is the same object Conan Doyle made or a new one. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer my guest is most likely to give, so I can keep pressing after the first response. Please write in American English.
