I am preparing a discussion session for a small reading group working through Hume's An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, and I have run aground on a passage from Section VII ("Of the Idea of Necessary Connexion"), Part I — not the famous billiard-ball material or the two definitions of cause, but the methodological passage where Hume announces how he proposes to proceed. He has just said that the ideas of power, force, energy, and necessary connexion are the most obscure in metaphysics, and then he writes:

"It seems a proposition, which will not admit of much dispute, that all our ideas are nothing but copies of our impressions, or, in other words, that it is impossible for us to think of any thing, which we have not antecedently felt, either by our external or internal senses. [...] Produce the impressions or original sentiments, from which the ideas are copied. These impressions are all strong and sensible. They admit not of ambiguity. They are not only placed in a full light themselves, but may throw light on their correspondent ideas, which lie in obscurity. And by this means, we may, perhaps, attain a new microscope or species of optics, by which, in the moral sciences, the most minute, and most simple ideas may be so enlarged as to fall readily under our apprehension, and be equally known with the grossest and most sensible ideas, that can be the object of our enquiry."

My difficulty is not with what the passage says but with what it commits him to. As everyone knows, when Hume carries out the promised search later in the section, the impression he produces for "necessary connexion" turns out to be internal: a felt determination of the mind, arising after repeated conjunction, rather than anything perceived between the events themselves. Here are the questions I would like worked through, staying as close to the quoted passage as the argument allows.

First, the microscope metaphor seems to treat producing the impression as a way of settling meaning — of clarifying what our terms stand for. But does the method as described here settle anything about the world? If the instrument can only ever magnify what is "antecedently felt," then its findings seem confined to the contents of the mind by construction. Does that mean the later discovery that the relevant impression is internal is an artifact of the method rather than a discovery about causation — so that the passage, properly read, is silent on whether objects harbour powers we simply cannot perceive? Or does Hume's claim that it is impossible even to think of anything not copied from an impression do stronger work, ruling out so much as a legitimate thought of an unperceived objective connection?

Second, I am puzzled by the anatomy of the obscurity. The passage says the impressions are "strong and sensible" and "admit not of ambiguity," while the ideas "lie in obscurity." But if an idea is nothing but a faint copy of its impression, why would tracing the copy back to its original require a new instrument at all — in what exactly does the idea's obscurity consist, if not in the impression? Does the diagnosis here (that we mistake or conflate our ideas when "surveyed by reflection," as Hume says just above this passage) suggest that the obscurity of "necessary connexion" is a defect in us that the method removes, or a defect in the idea itself that the method exposes? These seem to support opposite conclusions about whether the term has a respectable use once the microscope has been applied.

Third, I am aware that interpreters divide over the upshot of Section VII: on one reading, Hume's conclusion is that causation in the objects is nothing over and above constant conjunction, the necessity residing only in the mind; on another, often called the New Hume reading, he means only to deny that we can know or conceive the secret powers of objects, while quietly supposing that such powers exist. My question is what this particular passage contributes to that dispute. Does the announced method favour one side — for instance, does an instrument for clarifying our ideas presuppose that there is nothing more to the matter than our ideas, or is the method precisely what a cautious realist about unknown powers would use? And does the strong statement of the copy principle quoted above ("impossible for us to think of any thing, which we have not antecedently felt") bear differently on the dispute than the weaker claim that we lack knowledge of powers?

I am not looking for a bibliography or a survey of who has held what; I would like a close reading of the passage itself — what the microscope is supposed to show, what Hume takes its limitations to be, and how much of the later conclusion about necessary connexion is already loaded into the methodology announced here, as opposed to earned by the argument that follows. If the passage is genuinely neutral between the two readings, I would like to see that demonstrated from the text rather than asserted.
