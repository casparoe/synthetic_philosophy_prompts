I'm in my final year of high school and preparing an essay for our school's philosophy competition, on the theme of whether mathematics is "really out there" in the world or just a tool we invented. My physics teacher suggested I look at the so-called indispensability argument, which I understand runs roughly as follows: we ought to believe in the existence of whatever our best scientific theories indispensably quantify over; our best science quantifies over mathematical objects like numbers and functions; so we ought to believe mathematical objects exist. I've drafted an essay that tries to test this argument against two famous examples, and I'd like comments before I expand the draft into a full text. Since I can't consult a professor directly, I'd especially appreciate remarks on whether I've gotten the science and the logic right, and on which parts of my argument you find weakest or most in need of repair.

Here is the core of my draft. I consider two cases. First, certain periodical cicadas emerge from the ground every 13 or 17 years, both prime numbers, and a leading hypothesis is that prime life cycles are advantageous because predators with their own periodic cycles (say, emerging every 4 or 6 years) will coincide with prime-cycle cicadas far less often than with composite-cycle ones. Second, honeybees build hexagonal cells, and hexagons happen to be the tiling that encloses a given area with the least perimeter, so one might say the geometry of the hexagon "explains" the honeycomb. My essay then proposes what I call the replacement test: ask whether the mathematical content of the explanation could be swapped out for purely physical vocabulary without losing explanatory power. I argue the cicada case fails this test — the explanation seems to turn on the number-theoretic property of primeness itself, since two coprime non-prime cycles would behave differently from a prime cycle interacting with arbitrary predator cycles — while the honeycomb case passes it, because what really does the work is minimization under physical constraints (wax is costly, surface tension rounds and merges cell walls), and "hexagon" is arguably just the label we put on the outcome rather than a cause of anything. Some researchers, I note in the draft, even claim bees don't actively construct hexagons at all, but that the hexagonal pattern emerges mechanically from the physics of the wax, which would make the mathematical description doubly dispensable.

My essay's conclusion is that the indispensability argument, at least in the simple form I stated, is unstable: the replacement test suggests mathematical posits are indispensable in one showcase example and dispensable in the other, and I worry that anyone who wants to believe in numbers on scientific grounds is committed to a case-by-case audit rather than a sweeping argument. My questions for you: (1) Is my replacement test actually a fair criterion of indispensability, or does it smuggle in the nominalist's assumptions by demanding that mathematics be eliminable to earn its keep? (2) Have I mischaracterized either example in a way that would embarrass me in front of judges who know biology or mathematics? (3) Does a mixed verdict — indispensable for cicadas, dispensable for honeycombs — undermine the indispensability argument, or could a defender happily accept it? (4) Finally, is there a sharper objection I should anticipate, for instance that even "purely physical" restatements covertly rely on mathematical structure the moment they involve counting, ratios, or minimization? Please be frank about where the draft is confused; I'd rather fix it now than lose points later.
