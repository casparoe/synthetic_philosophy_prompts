I am a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science writing a dissertation chapter on whether reinforcement learning agents could be subjects of valenced experience. At a reading group last week, a postdoc from the reinforcement learning lab summarized the state of this debate for me as follows:

"According to the reward hypothesis, everything an agent wants and does is just the maximization of a scalar reward signal. That means reward plays the same role in an RL agent that pleasure and pain play in us: it is the quantity the whole system is organized to get more of, and negative reward is the quantity it is organized to get less of. So a trained agent receiving a large negative reward is already in the state that, in us, suffering consists in. Whether it also feels like anything to be in that state is a separate and largely unanswerable question, and one the hypothesis itself treats as a distraction. And since the mammalian brain implements the same architecture via dopamine, which is simply the brain's reward signal, anyone who denies that RL agents can suffer owes an explanation of why rats can — otherwise they are just privileging systems that happen to be able to verbally report their pain."

For reference, here is the reward hypothesis as Richard Sutton actually states it on the Reinforcement Learning and Artificial Intelligence page he maintains:

"That all of what we mean by goals and purposes can be well thought of as maximization of the expected value of the cumulative sum of a received scalar signal (reward)."

I suspect the postdoc's summary smuggles in considerably more than the hypothesis licenses, but I cannot cleanly locate where the slippage occurs, and he is competent enough that I hesitate to dismiss him. Please audit the summary against the passage. Which parts accurately render the reward hypothesis, and which go beyond it? In particular: (1) Does the hypothesis, as stated, say anything at all about what reward is like for the agent, or only about how goals and purposes can be modeled — and does the phrase "can be well thought of as" do work the postdoc's reading ignores? (2) Is the inference from "the system is organized to maximize expected cumulative reward" to "receiving negative reward is the state suffering consists in" valid, or does it presuppose a further theory of valence (functionalist or otherwise) on which the hypothesis itself is silent? (3) How fair is the dopamine move, given the affective-neuroscience distinction between motivational wanting and hedonic liking? (4) Once the summary is corrected, does the closing charge — that skeptics are merely privileging verbal reporters — still follow, or does it depend on the earlier conflations?

Please lead with your verdict in the first sentence — roughly, which parts of the summary are fair and at which exact point its central inference fails — then give the analysis. No preamble, and do not restate my question.
