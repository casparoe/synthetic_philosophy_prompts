I'm producing a podcast episode on the Mu'tazila–Ash'arite debate, and my guest is a scholar of kalam whose new book argues that the Ash'arite position survives the Euthyphro dilemma intact — that the dilemma's force depends on assumptions about moral epistemology that classical Ash'arites were right to reject. Early in the book she anchors her reading in this passage from Plato's Euthyphro (7b–d, trans. Benjamin Jowett):

"And what sort of difference creates enmity and anger? Suppose for example that you and I, my good friend, differ about a number; do differences of this sort make us enemies and set us at variance with one another? Do we not go at once to calculation, and put an end to them by a sum? ... But what differences are there which cannot be thus decided, and which therefore make us angry and set us at enmity with one another? ... the just and unjust, good and evil, honourable and dishonourable."

Draft eight to ten interview questions. Her interpretive claim is that Socrates here concedes evaluative truths are not discoverable by any neutral procedure — there is no weighing machine for justice — and that this supports the Ash'arite denial that unaided reason ('aql) can discern husn and qubh independently of revelation. I want questions that test this rather than let her restate it: at least one on whether Socrates' point is epistemic (how we settle disputes) rather than metaphysical (what makes things just), and whether the Mu'tazilite can accept everything in the passage while insisting rational moral truths exist even when no decisive procedure for them is available; at least one pressing the standard worry that Ash'arite voluntarism makes God's commands arbitrary, with a follow-up keyed to her likeliest reply — that God's will is not constrained because nothing is prior to it — asking whether that reply concedes the arbitrariness or answers it; one on the Mu'tazilite argument from gratitude (that praising and thanking God presuppose moral standards independent of His will); and a closing question on what her reading implies for moral disagreement between communities with different revelations, given that Socrates' worry in the passage is precisely about disagreements that have no weighing machine. For each question, give the follow-up I'd want depending on whether she answers as an interpreter of the classical texts or as a defender of the view herself.
