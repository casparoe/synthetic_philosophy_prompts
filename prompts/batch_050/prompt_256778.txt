I'm a novelist — quiet literary stuff, the kind where the climax is somebody not saying the thing at dinner — and I've been stuck for months on a story about forgiveness, so I figured I'd get you to write the darn thing for me and I'll steal the skeleton. Write me a short story, somewhere between 1,500 and 2,500 words, that turns on two questions I can't stop circling: can forgiveness be *owed*, the way a debt is owed, and can it be *unconditional* without quietly turning into something else (an excuse, a shrug, a decision to stop caring)?

Here's the setup I want you to use, though you can change surface details: years after a killing, in a small town that has a whole reconciliation ritual everyone pretends is working, a man who served his sentence and has repented as completely as a person can comes to the mother of the boy he killed. Not to ask for forgiveness — he thinks asking would be one more taking — but because the town, her pastor, even her own daughter have started saying that she owes it to him now, that withholding it has become *her* wrong. And folded inside that: the dead boy left a note, written before he died (reasons are yours to invent), forgiving whoever it would turn out to be. So shes being pressed on two fronts — a forgiveness demanded from her as a duty, and a forgiveness already given on her behalf by someone who had, maybe, no right to give it. Whether she forgives at the end is up to you, but the story has to take a quiet stand on at least one of these: whether commanded or obligated forgiveness is still forgiveness or just book-keeping; whether unconditional forgiveness honors the wrong or erases it; whether anyone can forgive on behalf of the dead.

Craft rules, because I will judge this like an editor: no scene where characters debate the philosophy in dialogue — the ideas have to do their work through action, silence, and at least one physical object that carries weight. No villain; the daughter and the pastor should each have a case that's genuinely hard to wave off. First person or close third, past tense, and resist the tidy redemptive ending unless you've earned it.

Start with the story itself — a title is fine — no preamble, no note about what you're going for, just the piece.
