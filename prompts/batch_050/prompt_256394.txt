So I've been reading around on deepfakes and epistemology for a paper I'm drafting, and I've landed on a set of claims I like individually but I'm not sure they cohere. Here's where I stand: (1) the deepest harm of deepfakes isn't that people get fooled by particular fake videos -- it's that recordings lose their function as an epistemic backstop, the thing you can appeal to settle disputes when testimony and memory conflict, and that damage happens even if nobody is ever deceived by anything; (2) at the same time, I think video will remain strong evidence in practice -- detection tools improve, people get savvier, and base rates of fakes will stay low enough that seeing will mostly still be believing; (3) on the policy side, I'm convinced the right response is making platforms and the creators of generative tools liable for harmful fakes, rather than provenance-tracking or watermarking schemes, which seem brittle and easy to strip; (4) and honestly I think deepfakes pose nothing conceptually new -- we've had doctored photos since the darkroom era, this is a difference of degree not kind, so talk of a novel epistemic crisis is overblown. Before I commit to all four in the paper, audit me: which of these genuinely pull against each other and which just look like they do, rank the tensions by how severe they are, and for each real one tell me the least costly revision and what I'd have to give up. One thing I care about: if the backstop idea in (1) is doing real work, check whether my other commitments are actually compatible with taking it seriously, rather than just listing pairwise inconsistencies abstractly. And please write your answer as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, just paragraphs like an email -- I find formatted answers harder to think with.
