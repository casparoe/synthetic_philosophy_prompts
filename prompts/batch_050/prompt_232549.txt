Here's the student's answer: "Priors wash out because, as evidence accumulates, the likelihood ratio between any two hypotheses either stays bounded (if the hypotheses are empirically indistinguishable) or diverges, while the prior odds remain a fixed multiplicative factor that is eventually swamped. The central formal result here is de Finetti's merging of opinions theorem, which says that if two priors are mutually absolutely continuous -- meaning neither assigns probability zero to any event the other assigns positive probability to -- then with probability one the two agents' predictive distributions over future observations converge to each other in total variation. So disagreement is a temporary phenomenon: however far apart two agents start, agreement about the future is guaranteed. Moreover, once the posteriors merge in this way, both converge to a point mass on the true hypothesis, because Doob's theorem guarantees Bayesian consistency for every parameter value whenever the prior has full support. The slogan 'priors wash out' is therefore essentially correct, and the only genuine exception is a dogmatic prior that assigns probability zero to the true hypothesis, since no stream of evidence can then ever lift it off zero. A practical caveat is that convergence can be arbitrarily slow, so in the short run priors can still dominate an analysis even though asymptotically they don't matter."
