I'm a second-year PhD student in health economics (came in via philosopy, long story) and I wrote this argument after a methods seminar and now it's genuinely bothering me because the conclusion is insane and I can't find the break. Background: my field's whole toolkit runs on QALYs -- quality-adjusted life years -- where health states get a weight from 1 (full health) down toward 0 and below, and we rank interventions by how many QALYs they buy. The argument, as far as I can tell, shows the weights are tracking nothing:

(1) What makes a period of life better for the person living it is wholly a matter of what it's like to live through it -- the experienced quality of that stretch of time.
(2) If two health states are such that nobody could tell them apart from the inside, even living one immediately after the other, then neither contributes more wellbeing to a life than the other.
(3) Health states form a spectrum. Between full health and, say, end-stage dementia there's a chain of adjacent states -- tweak one symptom one notch at a time, slightly worse pain, slightly more fatigue, slightly foggier memory -- where each adjacent pair sits below what anyone could possibly notice. Nobody detects a 0.1% change in their daily pain level.
(4) Applying (2) at every step of the chain in (3), and chaining by transitivity: a year in full health contributes exactly as much wellbeing to your life as a year in the worst state on the scale.
(5) So QALY weights record nothing real, no treatment is better than a placebo, and there's never a reason to treat anything, since every intervention just moves you along the chain one undetectable step at a time.
(6) (5) is absurd, so one of (1)-(4) goes.

Constraints on your answer. I want to keep transitivity: drop it and I think I lose the entire aggregation framework, and worse, anyone holding intransitive weights can apparently be walked in a circle of tiny swaps and milked for money at each step. I'm also reluctant to fully sever wellbeing from what people can perceive, because half our measurement methods lean on patients and the public reporting small differences in how states feel. Given that: which premise or step do I actually reject, why did it look so innocent, and what's the honest price of rejecting it? And if the truth is that the price is higher than QALYs are worth -- i.e. the edifice really is built on a sorites-style mistake -- say so instead of rescuing it.
