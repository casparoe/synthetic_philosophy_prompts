I am a university senior enrolled in a seminar on philosophy of science and statistical reasoning, and next week I must present and defend a short paper in front of the class. My thesis: heritability estimates are not as policy-irrelevant as the standard critique suggests. I fully accept that a high heritability estimate says nothing about interventions that introduce genuinely novel environments, but I argue that it does constrain the expected effect of interventions that merely shift people along environmental dimensions already varying within the studied population. My reasoning is that if a trait is, say, 80 percent heritable in a given population, then all the environmental factors that actually vary in that population jointly account for at most 20 percent of the trait's variance, which bounds what any reshuffling of existing environments can accomplish — whereas a new drug, technology, or institution escapes the bound entirely, because it introduces variation that simply was not present when the estimate was made.

The audience is about fifteen classmates, and three of them matter most. One has fully absorbed the standard critique of behavioral genetics and will arrive armed with the familiar demonstration in which genetically variable plants are grown in near-uniform soil, so that nearly all the observed variation is heritable even though the environment is doing almost all of the causal work. One is a statistics major who will press me on the variance decomposition itself: gene–environment correlation, gene–environment interaction, and whether my "80 percent" figure survives once those are handled honestly. One is skeptical of the entire research program on political and ethical grounds and will treat any defense of heritability's usefulness as an attempt to smuggle determinism back in through a side door. The remaining students will follow whoever lands the first clean hit.

Please prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections in roughly the order I should expect them, ranked by how damaging each would be if I answer badly, and phrased the way each of these three classmates would actually phrase it. For each objection, tell me what I should concede outright, what I should hold, and a reply I can deliver in under a minute — plus a note on where that reply is weakest. Include the objection I am secretly hoping nobody raises. And where the standard critique is simply right, say so plainly; I would rather narrow my thesis on the spot than be caught defending a claim that has already been settled against me.

Begin your response with its bottom line in the first sentence — the single objection I most need to be ready for — and then give the preparation sheet, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
