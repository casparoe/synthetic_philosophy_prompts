Analyze the 1986 Challenger launch decision through the competing lenses of virtue ethics and the situationist critique of character. Take the relevant facts to be these: engineers warned that unusually cold weather could compromise the shuttle’s O-rings; the launch schedule was under considerable institutional and public pressure; the initial recommendation not to launch was reconsidered during a final conference call; and the launch proceeded with catastrophic results.

Do not reduce the case to a simple contest between bad character and bad systems. First, map the decision in virtue-ethical terms: which virtues were called for, which vices or failures of practical reasoning may have displaced them, and how the answer differs for engineers, managers, and senior officials. Then map it in situationist terms: which features of hierarchy, role division, deadline pressure, group deliberation, and prior successful launches made the disastrous choice easier to treat as reasonable.

As part of the analysis, introduce and use the concept of moral overhead: the amount of courage, independence, technical persistence, and willingness to incur immediate costs that an institutional setting demands from someone merely to make the responsible choice. Explain how the launch process increased moral overhead for the participants, whether unusually virtuous individuals should have been able to overcome it, and why an institution that routinely requires exceptional virtue may be defective even if its members are also blameworthy.

Finally, let the case test the theories as well as illuminate them. Does Challenger support the situationist claim that putatively stable traits have less explanatory power than social context, or does it instead show why courage and practical wisdom matter most precisely under adverse conditions? Identify at least one important feature of the episode that each theory explains well and one that it tends to obscure. Conclude by proposing two institutional reforms that would lower moral overhead without treating individual responsibility as irrelevant.
