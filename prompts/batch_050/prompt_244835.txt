I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in epistemic logic, 14 students, one 75-minute session. Design a structured in-class activity or game that makes Fitch's knowability paradox land before anyone hears the name. Students should first be brought to endorse the principle that every truth is knowable -- it should feel nearly trivial to them -- and then discover, through something they do rather than something I announce, that this principle commits them to every truth being known already. The activity should somehow stage the crux: a truth of the form "p, and nobody knows that p," and the strange impossibility of anyone ever coming to know it. Ideally students should have to maintain or exchange knowledge states under rules (e.g., announcements, limited communication, or turns at learning things) so that they run into the problem themselves. Give me concrete rules and materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps moments in the game onto the formal moves -- the distribution of knowledge over a conjunction, and the factivity of knowledge -- while also noting where in the derivation resistance is possible and what it costs. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (students are good at modal logic puzzles but bad at staying inside the rules), and be frank about which feature of the formal result the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply by lecture.
