So my roommate (philosophy PhD student, insufferable about it) sprung this on me over beers last week and I've been turning it over since. We're both basically Humeans about aesthetics, and he claims Hume's own commitments sink the whole idea of a standard of taste. His argument, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Beauty isn't a property of objects sitting out there in the world; it exists only in the mind that perceives the object, so an aesthetic verdict expresses a sentiment rather than reporting a mind-independent fact.

(2) A judgment can only be correct or incorrect if there's a fact independent of everyone's responses that makes it so.

(3) So strictly speaking, no judgment of taste is correct or incorrect.

(4) Hume's standard of taste says the true judges are the ones with delicacy of imagination, plenty of practice, wide experience of comparison, freedom from prejudice, and good sense, and the joint verdict of such judges fixes what's genuinely beautiful.

(5) But you can't verify whether those judges' verdicts are right, since by (3) there's no independent fact to check them against. All you can actually do is see whether other people's sentiments line up, which just restarts the question.

(6) And you can't establish the criteria for true judges without going in a circle, because the only evidence that delicacy, practice, etc. actually improve taste is that delicate, practiced critics happen to praise the "right" works -- which presupposes we already know which works are the right ones.

(7) So the standard of taste is either circular or groundless, and one person's taste is ultimately as good as anyone else's.

(8) So my niece's firm position that the Minions movie is better than Citizen Kane is, at bottom, every bit as good a verdict as the best film critic's.

I refuse to swallow (8). Worse, it seems to me that if his reasoning works here, the exact same template goes through for color, humor, and maybe even morality, and that can't be right all at once. If I'm forced to pick, I'd honestly rather give up (1) than accept (8), but I'd really rather not give up either. Which step do I reject, why did it look so plausible in the first place, and what does rejecting it actually cost me? And what's the best my roommate could say in response?
