So I run an infra team and we have a friday "paper club" where we mostly do distributed systems stuff, but I got cocky and assigned Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (the 1895 Mind piece) after it came up on Hacker News. Achilles is trying to force the Tortoise to accept the conclusion Z of a little Euclidean argument, and every time the Tortoise refuses, Achilles just writes the relevant rule down as one more premise. The bit that broke my brain:

"Do I?" said the Tortoise innocently. "Let's make that quite clear. I accept A and B and C and D. Suppose I still refused to accept Z?"

"Then Logic would take you by the throat, and force you to do it!" Achilles triumphantly replied. "Logic would tell you 'You can't help yourself. Now that you've accepted A and B and C and D, you must accept Z!' So you've no choice, you see."

Here's why I care: I maintain a little internal proof-checker-ish linter at work and modus ponens is just... hardcoded in there. Line 40, roughly. The Tortoise is basically asking what justifies line 40, and my honest answer is "it shipped and nobody's complained," which feels thin.

I want to turn this into next friday's activity: a decision tree / flowchart that walks an engineer from "modus ponens obviously works, I use it every day, what could justify trusting it?" to a named position on the justification of inference rules. Requirements:

- Max 8 questions, each a concrete scenario a programmer can answer with zero philosophy vocab. No "do you accept rule-circular justification?" — instead, code review scenarios and hypothetical coworkers.
- The root fork has to turn on the passage above: what exactly is the Tortoise doing wrong here — refusing to accept a truth that's in front of him, or refusing to perform a legal move in a game, or (plot twist) nothing at all?
- Leaves I definitely want reachable: (1) the rule is part of what "if...then" means, full stop — but this leaf has to survive the scenario where someone ships a connective called tonk whose intro/elim rules let you derive literally anything, so make that scenario a node; (2) the truth table settles it; (3) asking what justifies a rule is a category error — rules are moves, not facts, and the Tortoise's whole confusion is treating a move as a fact you can write in the notebook; (4) the justification is circular but that's fine if it's the right kind of circular; (5) nothing justifies it, deduction just floats, Achilles loses. Add leaves if the space demands.
- These cases must show up as branch nodes somewhere: the tonk connective; a reviewer who accepts all the premises, accepts the rule written down as a premise, and still won't approve the merge (the Tortoise, but in a PR); the teammate who on principle refuses proof by contradiction.
- Build the tree from the logical space first — what combos of answers are even available — then hang the actual positions on the leaves, and tell me which combos are empty and why: incoherent, or just unfashionable?
- Flag any two leaves your questions can't actually seperate.
- For each leaf, write the 2-3 sentence speech its defender would give the Tortoise at the exact moment of the passage I quoted.
- It's friday afternoon and these are engineers, so the questions should be a little fun. If some position genuinely can't be distinguished by a question I could explain to my team in under a minute, just tell me that instead of fudging it.
