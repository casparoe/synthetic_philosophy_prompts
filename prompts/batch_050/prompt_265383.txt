Pain asymbolia is usually wheeled out as a quick refutation. Patients with insular damage identify noxious stimuli as painful, localize and grade them normally, yet do not mind them: no grimacing, no avoidance, no complaint, sometimes amusement. Morphine can produce a related profile — the patient reports that the pain is still present but no longer bothers her. The standard inference is that the phenomenal character of pain and its badness come apart, and that badness must therefore be a matter of the subject's attitudes rather than of the experience itself. I think this inference is much too fast, and I want to locate where the issue actually bears weight. Consider two profiles that the standard discussion runs together:

Type S: the sensory-discriminative side of pain is intact, but the felt unpleasantness — whatever it is like for an experience to be awful while one is having it — is simply gone. The patient recognizes the sensation as pain and reports, sincerely, that it is not awful.

Type M: the patient insists that the experience is awful in the moment — phenomenal unpleasantness fully present — yet displays no aversion whatsoever: no avoidance learning, no preference against future episodes, no unpleasant memories, and calm indifference when offered the same experience again with nothing to be gained by it.

Whether anything answering to Type M occurs in the clinic is beside the point; treat it as a conceptual hypothesis.

Here is the proposition I want your credence on: it is metaphysically possible for a subject to undergo an experience with the complete phenomenal character of ordinary pain — including its phenomenal unpleasantness, if phenomenal unpleasantness is a real feature of experience — while that experience is not bad for the subject, not even pro tanto. Read badness as prudential: contribution to how well the subject's life goes for them. My own credence is about 0.25; a colleague's is about 0.8.

Give me your number, but the main deliverable is the decomposition. Break the proposition into the sub-questions it genuinely depends on — for instance, and feel free to reorganize: whether actual asymbolia patients retain the phenomenal character of pain or instead have a subtly altered sensation; whether phenomenal unpleasantness is a genuine phenomenal property distinct from, and not constituted by, occurrent aversive motivation; whether prudential badness is grounded wholly in phenomenal character or partly in the subject's conative attitudes; whether the Type M profile is coherent rather than covertly self-contradictory; and whether the dispute between those who take pain's badness to be essential to pain and those who take it to be attitudinal is substantive or partly verbal.
