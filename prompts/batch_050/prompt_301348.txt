My father-in-law, Walter, is retiring this spring after thirty-eight years as a high school rowing coach, and my wife has deputized me to give the toast at his retirement dinner. The audience will be a mix: former rowers now in their forties, current teenagers from the team, his fellow teachers, and family who know nothing about the sport. Walter is a sincere, practicing Stoic -- not the Instagram kind, but the kind who has read the Meditations annually since college and who, for as long as anyone can remember, has kept an index card taped inside his coaching clipboard with this passage from Book VII of Marcus Aurelius's Meditations, in George Long's translation: "Let there fall externally what will on the parts which can feel the effects of this fall. For those parts which have felt will complain, if they choose. But I, unless I think that what has happened is an evil, am not injured. And it is in my power not to think so."

Please write the toast, in American English, structured as exactly five numbered sections of a few sentences each, with no preamble or closing remarks outside the five. Here is the difficulty I need you to solve, and it should shape the whole speech rather than appear as decoration. Walter's teams won a great deal, but by the lights of his own passage, wins, weather, regatta officials, and the other boats were never his doing -- they "fall externally" -- so praising him for his record would, in his view, be praising him for things that were not his. I want the speech to honor him strictly for what was in his power: preparation, judgment, the character he built in kids, his habit of deciding what counted as an injury. At the same time, there is a complication: Walter was diagnosed last year with Parkinson's, which everyone in the room knows about, and the toast must acknowledge this without pitying him and without crudely lecturing a worried, loving room that his illness is only an evil if he judges it so. The passage says injury lies in the judgment -- but a speech that simply repeated that at a man with a degenerative disease would be tone-deaf. Work out, in the writing itself, what the passage actually licenses one friend to say to another on such an occasion, and end the fifth section with the raised-glass line.
