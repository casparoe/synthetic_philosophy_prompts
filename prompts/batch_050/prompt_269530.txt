So this came out of a late-night argument in a Discord full of LessWrong diaspora, and its been bugging me for a week because the conclusion is obviously crazy but every step looks fine when I stare at it individually. The claim is basically that worst-case reasoning about AI safety refutes itself. Here's the argument as clean as I can make it:

(1) If you're about to put some system in charge of something irreversible and high-stakes, you should only go ahead if you can guarantee that the outcome stays acceptable even in the worst case consistent with what you know.

(2) No system produced by gradient descent comes with such a guarantee: for any trained model there is some input or distributional shift you haven't checked where it does something catastrophically wrong, and behavioral evals plus interpretability only ever cover a tiny fraction of the behavior space.

(3) So you shouldn't deploy a trained model in any irreversible high-stakes role.

(4) But nothing in (1) restricts it to neural nets. No human operator comes with a worst-case guarantee either -- people get tired, get bribed, have strokes, turn out to be sociopaths -- so by the same principle you should never put a human in charge of anything irreversible either.

(5) Worse, your own reasoning doesn't come with a worst-case guarantee. You can't rule out that you're confused or rationalizing right now, so by (1) you shouldn't act on your own judgment, including the judgment that you shouldn't deploy AI systems.

(6) And doing nothing is also a course of action with no worst-case guarantee: refusing to build or deploy anything could be catastrophic too (someone less careful builds it, or we definately needed the tech), and you can't guarantee it isn't.

(7) So (1) demands you never act and never refrain, which is impossible, and a principle that does that should be thrown out. Hence worst-case guarantees were the wrong thing to demand in alignment all along, and we should go back to average-case reasoning like sane people.

This feels like it proves way too much -- by this logic nobody could ever be cautious about anything, and the friend who came up with it cheerfully agrees and says that's the point. But when I try to break it I keep bouncing off. My best guess is something slips between (4) and (5), like the word guarantee is doing two different jobs, but I can't pin it down precisely enough to feel like I've actually refuted anything rather than just gestured. If I have to give something up I'd rather keep (1) in some form, since worst-case thinking seems like the whole point of alignment as a field rather than just ordinary risk management, but I want to know what I'm paying for keeping it. Which step do you actually reject, why did that step look fine in the first place, and what do the different ways out each cost?
