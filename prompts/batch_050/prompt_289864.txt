I have no formal training in philosophy, but for some months I have been turning over the old puzzle about whether an all-powerful being could create a stone so heavy that it could not lift it. I believe I have dissolved the puzzle — but my dissolution appears to prove something absurd, so I would like to know where it goes wrong. Here is the argument as I have written it down.

(1) The puzzle fails. If the being really is all-powerful, then a stone it cannot lift is an impossible object, in the same family as a square circle. Being unable to create such a stone is no more a limitation than being unable to draw a square circle, and nobody's power is measured by their failures at impossible tasks.

(2) So a genuine limitation on a being's power must involve a task that is genuinely possible: something that could actually be done, which this being cannot do.

(3) But whether a task is genuinely possible has to be judged relative to the being attempting it. Lifting a bus is possible for a crane but not for me; a task that nothing of my kind could perform is, in the only sense that matters, not a possible task for me.

(4) It follows that I can do everything it is possible for me to do — and the same holds for an ant, a candle flame, and a stone.

(5) But then, in the only sense of power that survives the stone puzzle, everything whatsoever turns out to be all-powerful within its own limits, and the word "power" stops marking any distinction at all. That is absurd.

(6) So either the stone puzzle succeeds after all and (1) is wrong, or the very idea of power — mine, an ant's, or God's — is hopelessly confused.

I would much rather keep (1), since the square-circle comparison still seems exactly right to me, and I do not want to accept that the concept of power is confused. So I suspect the rot is somewhere in (2) through (4), but I cannot put my finger on it: each step reads to me like a small, careful refinement of the one before it.

Please tell me which step you would reject, why it looked innocent when I wrote it, and what rejecting it costs — including what the best version of (1)'s dissolution has to say about possibility if it does not say my (3). One further request: in answering, distinguish what is broadly agreed among people who have worked on this puzzle from what remains genuinely contested — for example, whether fixes in the family of (1) fully succeed, whether related versions of the puzzle (such as asking whether the being could bring it about that it is no longer omnipotent) are still live problems, and how much turns on exactly how "omnipotence" gets defined — and do not present a contested resolution as though it were the settled view.
