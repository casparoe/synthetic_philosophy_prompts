I'm a high school senior preparing a presentation for our philosophy club on care ethics (the tradition associated with Carol Gilligan and Nel Noddings), and I want a custom-built test case for a claim I keep running into: that care ethics amounts to "be attentive and giving toward the people close to you," and therefore can never tell you to hold back when someone you love asks for help. I suspect the view has more internal resources than that, and I'd like a scenario that forces the question.

Construct a thought experiment meeting these specifications:

1. Verdicts: an act utilitarian and a Kantian deontologist both conclude that the protagonist should provide the help being requested; a care ethicist concludes that she should refuse. All three verdicts must genuinely follow from the theories rather than being stipulated.
2. Realism: the setting must be ordinary — a family, a friendship, or a school — with no trolleys, no emergencies, no life-or-death stakes, and stakes a normal person could actually face.
3. Length: the scenario itself should be a single paragraph.
4. No smuggling: the care ethicist's refusal must arise from recognizably care-ethical notions — the difference between responsiveness and entrenching dependency, the risk of stunting the cared-for's own capacities, or the hollowing out of the relationship or the caregiver's self — and not from quietly tallying harms to third parties. If third-party welfare is doing the work, the case fails.
5. No cheap refusals: the help requested must violate no duty (so the Kantian cannot reach his verdict through a perfect duty like the prohibition on lying), and it must be clear that helping genuinely maximizes overall welfare even once long-term effects are counted (so the utilitarian cannot be answered with "but enabling is worse in the long run").
6. Real cost: the person being refused must be a specific, identifiable individual who is genuinely worse off because of the refusal — the case should not dodge what the refusal costs anyone.

After the scenario, do three things. First, walk through each theory's verdict and the reasoning that produces it, showing that specification 1 is met. Second, identify the single structural feature of the case that makes the care ethicist peel off from the other two, and say what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed — does the case collapse into one where all three theories agree that she should help? Third, give the strongest reply a utilitarian or Kantian would make against the case — for instance, that the care ethicist's refusal is either covert long-run consequentialism (which specification 5 rules out) or else a fetishizing of relationships at the expense of actual people — and assess how damaging that reply really is.
