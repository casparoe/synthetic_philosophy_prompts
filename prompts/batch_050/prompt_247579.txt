I am preparing a handout for an undergraduate philosophy of mind course and would like you to write a decision tree that sorts students into positions on narrative selfhood — the debate over whether the self is something like a story a person tells about themselves (or that gets told about them), and what that implies about what selves fundamentally are. The tree should begin from a shared starting point that nearly everyone accepts, something like: people at least seem to have selves, and the stories we tell clearly matter to us somehow. From there, build the tree out of the logical space of the debate rather than from a list of labels: each branch node should be a question or concrete case on which the positions genuinely come apart, phrased so that a student could answer it without already knowing which position their answer leads to.

Please make sure at least the following positions appear as leaves:

- the view that the self is constituted by one's self-narrative, so that losing the story is, in an important sense, losing the self;
- Dennett's picture of the self as a center of narrative gravity: a useful abstraction, comparable to a physical object's center of gravity, which we posit to make sense of a person's behavior rather than an inner thing that sits in the head;
- a realist view on which the self is an inner subject that stories merely report on, and can badly misdescribe;
- an episodic or anti-narrative view on which some people simply do not experience their lives as unfolding stories, and are no less selves for it;
- a deflationary no-self view on which neither the story nor the inner subject withstands scrutiny.

Branch questions should draw on concrete cases. Candidates include: a person with complete autobiographical amnesia who retains fluent personality and skills; a person whose autobiography turns out to be substantially confabulated, given that memory research suggests everyone's is to some degree; a person in late-stage dementia who can no longer recount anything about their life; a person who reports living wholly in the present with no felt sense of an ongoing storyline; and a fictional character endowed by their author with a perfectly coherent inner narrative. Where you use a case, state it concretely and lay out the answer options a student could give.

Beyond the tree itself, please do the following:

- Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, and explain why the questions fail to split them rather than papering over the overlap.
- Identify any coherent combination of branch answers that no named position occupies, and say briefly whether that slot is empty because the view would be incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.
- Keep the instrument to roughly six to eight branch nodes and no more than seven leaves.

Format the tree so it could be printed on a single page: nodes numbered, each branch labeled by the answer that takes you down it, and each leaf labeled with the position's name plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
