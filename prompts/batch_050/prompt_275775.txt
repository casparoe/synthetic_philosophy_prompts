So I write for a general-interest magazine, science-and-ideas desk, and my editor has greenlit a long explainer on why historical analogies keep going wrong in public debate -- every new crisis, out come Munich, Vietnam, the Thucydides trap, depending on which lesson the columnist already wanted. The brief I've set myself is: not a piece that just sneers "analogies bad," because that's obviously useless, but one that explains why analogical reasoning from history is so seductive, exactly where and why it misleads, and what a disciplined version of it would even look like.

My readers are smart non-specialists. They read the news, they know roughly who Chamberlain was, they have a fuzzy folk memory of Vietnam, they've never heard the phrase "reference class" and will bounce off anything that smells like a seminar.

Don't write the piece for me. Plan the explanation. Concretely:

1. The order of ideas. What has to be in place before a reader can even see why "this is 1938 all over again" is a bad argument? I suspect they first need to get that an analogy is a choice of which features count as salient, and that the choice is doing all the work -- but maybe something has to come before that. Give me the dependency structure and why each piece has to precede the next.

2. The carrying example. I'm torn. Munich is tempting because everyone knows it, but that might be a bug: readers arrive already knowing the lesson it's "supposed" to teach, so it can't show them the mechanism failing in real time. Vietnam is the mirror-image lesson (appeasement-thinking in reverse, basically) which is nice, but my readers' grip on it is shakier. Pick the one example you'd build the piece around, tell me what it has to have and has to lack to do the job, and why you'd reject the other one.

3. The misconception map. What will readers predictably think the piece is saying? I'm guessing at least: "so history teaches us nothing" (the overcorrection), "so the trick is just to pick the right analogy" (the overconfidence), and some readers will slot the whole thing into whichever current crisis is live and take it as ammunition for their side. For each misconception you expect, say where in the sequence it will form and where I should head it off.

4. The check. How do I know, from the finished piece, whether a reader got the actual point versus just walked away with a shinier slogan ("analogies mislead!") to deploy lazily at dinner parties -- i.e. what would a reader who understood it be able to do that a reader with the slogan couldn't?

And one last thing: tell me where this plan is most likely to be wrong. Which of your choices -- the ordering, the example, a misconception you banked on -- would you flip, and what consideration or argument would flip it? If there's a serious case that analogies from history are more reliable than my whole framing assumes, I want to hear that too rather than build the piece on a premise that collapses.
