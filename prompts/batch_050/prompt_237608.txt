You are a professional philosopher of physics with particular expertise in the foundations of quantum mechanics and the Everett (many-worlds) interpretation. I would like you to act as a critical auditor of my own position, not as a lecturer: please think carefully through what each of my commitments actually entails before passing judgement, and write your answer in British English.

For a reading group on the measurement problem, I have been asked to write down my philosophical profile on probability in the many-worlds interpretation so the others can attack it. Here is what I currently believe:

1. Everettian literalism: there is no collapse and there are no hidden variables; the many-worlds picture is just unitary quantum mechanics taken at face value, and its chief virtue over rivals is that it adds nothing to the formalism — no extra postulates, no extra ontology beyond the wavefunction.

2. Branch realism: all branches are equally real, and after a measurement every successor in every branch is a genuine continuer of me.

3. Derived, not postulated: the Born rule does not need to be added as an axiom; it can be extracted from the theory plus principles of rationality, and I take the Deutsch–Wallace decision-theoretic derivation to be essentially sound.

4. Probability as self-locating uncertainty: when I say the spin-up outcome has probability one half, what I mean is that before the measurement I am uncertain which branch I will find myself on — even though I know the universal wavefunction completely.

5. Frequentist sympathies: I am suspicious of any notion of probability that never cashes out in the statistics of repeated trials; ultimately, talk of probability has to connect to long-run frequencies, or it is not really about the world.

6. Weighted caring: a rational agent should care about her successors in proportion to the squared amplitudes of their branches, which is also why naive branch-counting (one branch, one vote) is obviously wrong.

7. A case verdict: I would refuse quantum Russian roulette at any finite stake, no matter how small the amplitude of the death branch, because it is certain in advance that some successor of mine dies.

Please audit this set rather than summarise the literature. Specifically: go through the commitments and identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection, and how severe each real tension is. For each genuine tension, state what would have to give on each side and what the cheapest fix would cost me. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most of the set, and whether any commitment is doing work it cannot do given the others. I have not indicated where I think the problems are, because I am not sure there are any — please do not assume I am right about that, but also do not manufacture tensions where the commitments are merely unfashionable.
