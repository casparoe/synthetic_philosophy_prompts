For some years I have been keeping an informal record of how a colleague of mine — an epistemologist in my department — judges cases in which a thinker acquires evidence bearing on the reliability of her own reasoning. His verdicts on three cases seem consistent enough to tempt generalisation, but I suspect they underdetermine it:

(1) Two accountants of equal and mutually recognised competence each compute a share of a bill and arrive at different figures. He holds that each ought substantially to reduce confidence in her own figure, treating the two answers as roughly equally likely to be correct.

(2) A climber is told by the expedition medic, who has just measured her blood oxygen, that she is currently hypoxic to a degree that reliably distorts spatial judgement, although she feels entirely lucid and every check she performs confirms that the route ahead is safe. He holds that she ought not to rely on her own assessment, even so.

(3) A reflective person works through a sceptical argument — a dreaming hypothesis, say — purporting to show that her perceptual faculties are unreliable, and finds the argument compelling. He holds that she may rationally retain her ordinary perceptual beliefs nonetheless.

The fourth case, on which I wish to predict his verdict before asking him: a historian reaches a heterodox conclusion through archival reasoning she has checked repeatedly and can rehearse at will, and no one has identified any flaw in it. She then encounters a large, well-conducted metastudy establishing that conclusions of this type, reached by methods of this type, are overturned within a decade in roughly seventy per cent of cases. The study in no way presupposes that her particular conclusion is false.

Which verdict best coheres with his record, and why? If more than one rule fits (1)–(3), please identify the leading candidates, state what each predicts for the historian, give your best guess at his verdict with your reasons for preferring it, and describe a further case that would discriminate between whichever rules remain in contention.
