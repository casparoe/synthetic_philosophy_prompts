So I've basically come around to Lakatos's picture from Proofs and Refutations: math isn't a body of certain truths piled up by airtight deduction, it's quasi-empirical -- conjectures get "refuted" by counterexamples, proofs get revised, concepts get stretched and monster-barred, and knowledge grows through that dialectic roughly the way science does. What would count against this view? I don't want the lazy objection ("but 2+2=4 sure feels certain"). I want to know: which actual episodes in the history of math don't fit the proof-and-refutation pattern, what the view would have to predict about modern cases like computer-assisted proofs, formal verification in proof assistants, or gigantic case-enumeration results like the classification of finite simple groups, and what it would look like for those predictions to fail. Also: has fallibilism about math been qualified into unfalsifiability? If every broken proof just counts as "the method working as intended," does the view risk anything at all -- and is that a problem for a methodological thesis as opposed to a scientific one? Rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a committed Lakatosian would concede it counts against them or wave it off.
