I am a doctoral student in philosophy of biology, and I have realized that I do not know what I actually think distinguishes a mechanistic explanation from a merely predictive or phenomenological model. Rather than argue in the abstract, I want to work from my own verdicts on cases. Here they are.

1. Hodgkin and Huxley's model of the squid action potential. I count it as a mechanistic explanation — the paradigmatic one — even though it was fitted to voltage-clamp data and its authors presented the equations as an empirical description, explicitly cautioning that the model's agreement with the data should not be read as confirming the physical picture they sketched.

2. The Jacob–Monod model of the lac operon. Clearly a mechanistic explanation; no hesitation.

3. The SIR compartmental model of epidemics. I say it genuinely explains epidemic curves and threshold phenomena, but it is not a mechanism.

4. Waddington's epigenetic landscape. A suggestive picture of canalization, but not an explanation of anything.

5. Goodwin-style negative-feedback oscillator models of circadian rhythms (a gene whose product, via an intermediate, represses its own transcription). These reproduce period and phase behavior, but I hesitate to call them mechanistic: the variables are generic rather than entities. How-possibly, at best.

6. Turing's reaction–diffusion model of morphogenesis. I say it was explanatory — a genuine, if incomplete, explanation of stripe and spot formation — even before any morphogens were identified, and became more explanatory once candidate activator–inhibitor pairs were found in systems such as zebrafish pigmentation.

7. AlphaFold. Astonishingly accurate predictions, but I deny that it explains protein structure or folding at all.

The trouble: cases 1 and 5 seem to stand or fall together, since both are differential-equation models whose variables were, at the time, only loosely tied to physical entities — yet I split them. Cases 4 and 6 likewise: both are abstract schemes of development, yet I call one explanatory and the other a mere picture.

My tentative principle, roughly in the spirit of Machamer, Darden, and Craver: a model gives a mechanistic explanation if and only if its components can be mapped onto entities and activities whose organized operation is constitutively relevant to the phenomenon, where relevance is shown by interventions on the putative components changing the phenomenon and vice versa. But case 1 worries me: the mapping there was achieved decades after the model, so either my verdict is wrong, or the principle needs a retrospective clause, or mechanistic status does not require the mapping to be in hand.

Here is what I want. First, state a principle — mine repaired, or a better one — precisely enough that each clause can be traced to the case that motivates it, and say what it implies for two cases I have not considered: a Boolean network model that reproduces cell-fate transitions in a gene regulatory network, and an attractor-based dynamical model that explains the stability of a flock's collective motion. Second, commit: do not inventory the options (phenomenological models, covering-law accounts, dynamical explanation, and so on) and leave me to choose; defend the single principle you judge correct, and where it conflicts with my verdicts, tell me plainly which verdicts I should abandon and why. Third, close with a table whose columns are: Case; My verdict; Verdict under your principle; Must I revise? (yes/no); Clause doing the work; One-sentence reason. Include all seven of my cases plus the two new ones.
