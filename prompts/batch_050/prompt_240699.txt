Okay so I write novels for a living, mostly first-person stuff, and I keep rereading The Remains of the Day because Stevens does something I can't figure out how to pull off in my own narrators. A friend with a philosophy minor once told me about Moore's paradox — a sentence like "it's raining, but I don't believe it's raining" could be true, but nobody can sensibly come out and say it. And it clicked for me: Stevens never quite utters that sentence, but he seems to *live* inside it for the whole book. He describes Miss Kenton leaving, or the business with Lord Darlington's politics, and the narration radiates that on some level he knows exactly what it all means, while the actual sentences keep calmly insisting everything was fine, professional, dignified.

So here's what I want help with. First: is there a real difference between *saying* "p, but I don't believe p" and *being in the state that sentence describes*? The paradox is about what can be asserted, but fiction seems to depict the state itself from the inside, without the narrator ever having to speak the forbidden words. Does Ishiguro show us how a person can occupy that position — compartmentalization, self-deception, whatever the right term is — or does the book quietly cheat, giving us a narrator who obviously does believe it and just won't say so?

Second, the tense thing, which I can't stop thinking about. "It was raining, but I didn't believe it" is a perfectly fine sentence. Stevens narrates retrospectively, years later, on his motoring trip. Does the past tense dissolve the paradox? Because if it does, the book should feel like a man who sees clearly now — and it definately doesn't, it feels claustrophobic to the last page. And near the end on the pier, when he gets closest to admitting to a stranger what he lost, he slides off into those big impersonal generalities about evenings and the remains of the day. Is that slide away from first-person-present itself a way of dodging the grammatical space where the paradox bites?

Third — and this matters to me, since I'm drafting an essay-ish piece on narrators who can't say what they know — where does the novel *resist* the philosophy? I don't want Stevens as a cute illustration of the self-deception literature. What does Ishiguro dramatize that the standard Moore's-paradox discussion doesn't have room for? I suspect it's something about professional identity, how a role can swallow the believer so there's no one left to do the disbelieving, but I want that tested, not just nodded along with.

Take both the book and the philosophy seriously, and be honest about where the reading strains. American English please, casual tone is fine.
