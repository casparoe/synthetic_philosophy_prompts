I teach an undergraduate seminar on formal epistemology and decision theory: 18 students, one 75-minute session. We have just spent two weeks on expected utility theory, and the students are now fluent at computing expectations but have developed a reflex I want to break — they treat "maximize expected value" as a synonym for rational choice under risk. I want them to feel, in their own play rather than in my lecture, the difference between maximizing the average outcome across parallel possibilities and maximizing what happens along a single sequence of outcomes unfolding through time.

Design an in-class betting game for them. It should have the following features:

1. Students repeatedly stake a freely chosen fraction of a play-money bankroll on a gamble with positive expected value — say, a coin they are credibly told is biased in their favor. The parameters should be set so that anyone maximizing expected value round by round should stake everything, and anyone who does stake everything will predictably bust within the session, while a modest fixed fraction steadily compounds.

2. The incentives need teeth without real money — final bankrolls should translate into something mildly desirable. But design the reward to track each student's own bankroll growth rather than their rank in the class, because I know that rank-based payoffs reward reckless betting in exactly the way I am trying to discredit. I want the debrief to be able to explain why that design choice matters, since it is itself an instance of the lesson: the right objective depends on the structure of the situation, not just on the sign of the expected value.

3. A second phase that complicates the first: for example, a gamble whose edge is announced only ambiguously, or a choice between the favorable coin and a safe asset, so that students discover that the sensible fraction depends on more than whether the bet is favorable — and that both the strategy that felt reckless and the strategy that felt timid in phase one are dominated by a proportional rule of a size they did not initially pick.

4. A debrief plan that maps moments of the session onto the formal material: ensemble averages versus time averages; why ruin is an absorbing state that no positive edge can compensate for; why a logarithmic or growth-rate objective rationalizes the proportional betting behavior they stumbled into; and — this matters to me — the difference between justifying that rule as a theorem about long-run capital growth and justifying it as expected utility maximization with a log utility function. My students will conflate these two justifications if I let them, and I want the debrief to pry them apart.

5. An honest account of what the activity cannot reproduce. The optimality of the proportional rule is an asymptotic result, and a twenty-round classroom game is precisely the regime where luck dominates — so some student who bet recklessly may well finish rich. Tell me how to run the debrief when that happens, in a way that neither concedes the point nor looks like I am explaining away an inconvenient outcome.

6. A list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: students copying the current leader's stake, a student who has done the arithmetic and announces that betting everything is "correct," students treating the chips as worthless and staking randomly once they are bored — and how to handle each.
