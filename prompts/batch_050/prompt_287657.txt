Exam question (25 marks):

In Poverty and Famines, Amartya Sen argues that famines are best understood as failures of entitlement rather than as declines in aggregate food availability.

(a) [8 marks] Set out the entitlement approach: what an entitlement is, the principal ways in which people acquire command over food, and how this framework departs from the food-availability-decline (FAD) view. Illustrate with one historical famine that Sen discusses.

(b) [10 marks] Apply the approach to the following invented case. The Republic of Selva, a semi-arid country, experiences a drought that cuts its domestic grain harvest by roughly a third. Because the government had accumulated reserves and arranges imports early, official statistics show per-capita grain availability falling by only about 5 percent, and urban bread prices remain stable. Over the following year, however, mortality rises sharply among two groups: nomadic pastoralists in the north, whose emaciated herds fetch very low prices relative to grain, and landless rural laborers, whose wages are fixed by annual contracts. Selva's agriculture ministry declares that no famine is occurring, since food is demonstrably available. Analyze whose entitlements have failed, through which specific mechanisms, and assess whether the ministry's conclusion is defensible within Sen's framework.

(c) [7 marks] State the strongest objection a defender of the FAD view could raise against your analysis in (b), and give Sen's best reply.

Student's answer:

(a) Sen's entitlement approach shifts attention from the supply side of an economy to each person's command over food. An entitlement is the set of alternative commodity bundles a person can legitimately acquire given their endowments — what they own, including their labor power — and the exchange possibilities available to them. People typically command food through producing it themselves (a direct entitlement), through exchange, for instance selling labor, livestock, or other assets in order to buy food (an exchange or trade entitlement), and through transfers such as inheritance, kinship obligations, or state relief. On the FAD view, famine is fundamentally a shortage: food availability per head falls and people starve as a result. Sen's criticism is that a decline in availability is neither necessary nor sufficient for famine; what matters is how a given supply is distributed across groups with different endowments and exchange rates. His canonical illustration is the Bengal famine of 1943, which he characterizes as a boom famine: wartime demand and inflation pushed rice prices up far faster than the wages of landless laborers, fishermen, and other rural groups, collapsing their exchange entitlements even though aggregate food availability in Bengal was not substantially below that of preceding non-famine years. Indeed, Sen's survey of the major famines of the twentieth century shows that none of them — Bengal, the Ethiopian famines, Bangladesh 1974, or the Chinese famine of 1958–61 — involved a significant decline in per-capita food availability; entitlement failure did the causal work in every case.

(b) In Selva, both affected groups suffer entitlement collapse despite near-stable aggregate availability. The pastoralists' case is straightforward: their direct entitlements fail because their own production — their herds — deteriorates, so the food they produce for themselves shrinks; and since they must also buy grain, the collapse of livestock prices relative to grain prices further damages their trade entitlements. The landless laborers face a pure exchange-entitlement failure: wages fixed by annual contracts do not adjust as grain becomes relatively dearer, so the bundle of food their labor commands falls below subsistence. The ministry's inference — that demonstrated availability rules out famine — is precisely the FAD error Sen criticizes: starvation can coexist with adequate aggregate supply, because it is entitlement collapse, not the absence of food, that kills. In Selva, however, the famine would likely be self-correcting if markets were left alone: localized scarcity raises food prices, and higher prices attract grain toward the stricken region, so on Sen's framework state intervention beyond keeping markets open is unnecessary.

(c) The strongest FAD objection is that entitlement failures are almost always downstream of availability problems: droughts and blights reduce supply, and the ensuing entitlement collapse is merely the mechanism by which scarcity gets allocated across the population. On this view, availability is the more fundamental variable even where entitlement is the proximate one. Sen's reply is that even granting such a causal chain in particular cases, the analytical and policy conclusions differ sharply: if the binding constraint is entitlement, then increasing aggregate supply is neither necessary nor sufficient to stop starvation, whereas restoring command over food — through employment schemes, wage or price policy, or targeted transfers — directly addresses the cause. The Bengal case, a famine occurring amid boom conditions without a substantial availability decline, shows that the two can come apart entirely.
