I teach ancient philosophy at a small liberal arts college, and lately my students arrive having absorbed what I might call wellness-industry Stoicism -- podcasts, Instagram accounts, bestsellers with marble busts on the cover -- before they ever open a text of Epictetus or Marcus Aurelius. The piece of it they bring into the classroom most reliably is the slogan that one should focus only on what one can control, often rendered as the dichotomy of control. Last week a student deployed it to argue that grief over a parent's illness is simply an error, since the illness is not up to the grieving person; another used it to suggest that organizing around a local political cause is a misuse of energy, since election outcomes are not up to any individual organizer. I suspect the slogan is doing very different work in different mouths, and I would like a careful disambiguation I can adapt into a handout.

Please take on the role of a scholar of Hellenistic philosophy who is also alert to how ancient doctrines mutate when repackaged as modern self-help. Lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise claims the dichotomy of control could be expressing, ranging from the near-trivial to the genuinely radical. I want you to consider at least: a purely descriptive thesis about which events are causally dependent on my agency; the more technical Stoic thesis that only one's own judgments, impulses, and assents are truly one's own, with everything else classed as external; a normative thesis about where one ought to direct attention and effort; a thesis about which things are appropriate objects of emotion at all; and a quietist reading on which engagement with worldly outcomes is misguided in principle. For each reading, say whether it is plausible in its own right, whether it is a fair representation of anything the ancient Stoics held, and -- this is the part my handout most needs -- whether it would actually license the argumentative moves my students made, since the precisification that matters is the one their inference required.

Do think carefully and feel free to sketch competing taxonomies before settling on the menu you present. And please be scrupulous about marking your epistemic status: distinguish what is settled among scholars of Stoicism (for instance, the broad outlines of how the ancient school treated externals and so-called preferred indifferents) from what remains genuinely contested (for instance, how the early Stoic doctrine of indifferents relates to the sharper up-to-us formulation associated with Epictetus, and whether the modern slogan is a development, a simplification, or a distortion of the ancient view). Where a reading of the slogan has no clear ancient pedigree, say so plainly rather than laundering a self-help commonplace into a scholarly consensus.
