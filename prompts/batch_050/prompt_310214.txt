Some background first. I'm a retired biochemist — forty years of protein crystallography — who has got embarassingly deep into political philosophy over the past year. Five of us (a retired schoolteacher, a former magistrate, a GP, an engineer who talks too much, and me) have a reading group that has degenerated into a game we call the tribunal. We put a dead or elderly regime on trial and vote on whether its record justified how it ruled. Acquitted means the performance earned the authority; convicted means it didn't.

The schoolteacher is our Mill person. She holds that chapter II of Mill's Considerations on Representative Government settles the game in advance, and reads this aloud whenever things get heated:

"Order is said to exist where the people of the country have, as a general rule, ceased to prosecute their quarrels by private force, and acquired the habit of referring the decision of their disputes and the redress of their injuries to the public authorities. But in this larger use of the term, as well as in the former narrow one, Order expresses rather one of the conditions of government, than either its purpose or the criterion of its excellence."

Our record so far:

- Lee Kuan Yew's Singapore: acquitted 4–1. Orderly, honest, rich; the harassment of opposition figures and the tame press were noted, weighed, and outweighed.
- Pinochet's Chile: convicted 5–0. Even our economic liberals kept asking "compared to what" — Chile had been a democracy, the terror was real, and the boom came late and wobbled.
- Meiji Japan: acquitted 4–1. An oligarchy, but one that built schools, a civil service, and eventually a constitution and a Diet — it looked, to most of us, like a government working itself out of a job.
- Mobutu's Zaïre: convicted 5–0, shortest deliberation on record. Looted the country and called it stability.
- Park Chung-hee's South Korea: acquitted 3–2, after our longest night. The growth and mass education were undeniable; so were the Yushin constitution and the jailed dissidents; and democracy arrived only after his death, which the two dissenters said was exactly the point.

Next term's defendant is China since 1978. To keep it simple: by most counts it lifted several hundred million people out of poverty, life expectancy and literacy are way up, and it has had four decades without famine or civil war. Throughout, though: one-party rule, no competitive national elections, censorship, the 1989 killings around Tiananmen Square, the mass internment camps in Xinjiang, and a leadership that says plainly that party rule is permanent, not a bridge to anything.

What I'd like from you:

1. Predict the verdict and the vote count, and say which earlier case is the closest analog (I keep flip-flopping between Singapore and Park) and where the analogy breaks.
2. I've fit enough curves to know five points can support several models. The ones I see: (a) pure performance — broadly shared prosperity and order justify rule, full stop; (b) a Mill-flavoured rule — what counts is what the regime does to the people, whether it builds their capacities and treats order as plumbing rather than as an achievement; (c) a necessity rule — acquit only if the good outcomes required the authoritarian means. I think all three fit our five verdicts. Do they? Are there others that fit as well?
3. The schoolteacher says the passage convicts China automatically, since Beijing offers order as its crowning achievement. I say it only demotes order to plumbing and leaves the prosperity half of the performance case untouched — and that China arguably passes Mill's real test, having educated hundreds of millions. Who is misreading Mill worse?
4. Name the case, real or invented, that would best seperate these rules. My candidate is a rich Gulf emirate — dazzling order and prosperity, but oil-funded, with no pretence of tutelage — which I think acquits under (a) and convicts under (b) and (c). Does that work, or is there a cleaner discriminator?
