I'm a high school student taking a philosophy elective, and our unit on how the sciences classify things left me with a set of intuitions I can't systematize. Below are my verdicts on particular cases, followed by a principle I drafted and no longer trust. What I want: the principle, or a small set of candidate principles stated precisely, that best captures my verdicts -- with the case that motivates each clause, what each candidate says about cases I haven't listed, and an honest account of which of my verdicts each candidate forces me to give up.

My verdicts:

1. Gold is a genuine natural kind. Anything whose atoms have 79 protons is gold -- whether mined, synthesized, or found on another planet -- and nothing without them is, however convincing the resemblance.

2. Jade is not one kind. It is two unrelated minerals, jadeite and nephrite, grouped only by appearance and use. I think the everyday category ought to be retired in favor of the two real ones.

3. "Fish" is not a natural kind either -- there is no branch of the tree of life containing everything we call fish and nothing we don't -- but I don't think marine biologists should stop using the word. It seems fine as a practical grouping even though it carves no joint.

4. Pluto's reclassification as a dwarf planet was legitimate science, not just a vote about a word: the category "planet" was revised to track a real difference between Pluto and the eight others. And yet the pre-revision category served astronomy well for decades, and I can't bring myself to call it a mistake.

5. Biological species are real kinds even though their boundaries are vague -- hybrids, ring species, asexual lineages. Fuzziness at the edges doesn't make the difference between horses and oaks a human invention.

6. A diagnosis like depression is not a natural kind. It is a cluster of symptoms grouped for clinical usefulness, with fuzzy boundaries and heavy overlap with other conditions. But I don't think it follows that depression "isn't real" or that the diagnosis should be abolished.

I can already feel the tensions. Verdicts 2 and 3 treat two practically-motivated, joint-less categories differently: jade gets retired, fish gets kept. Verdicts 5 and 6 describe two fuzzy, overlapping clusters drawn up for scientific purposes, and I call one a kind and the other not. Verdict 1 says a hidden structure settles kindhood, but species -- which I insist are real kinds -- have no shared microstructure at all, and planets may not either.

My tentative principle was: a category is a natural kind when its members share some underlying property that explains why they resemble one another. It handles gold and jade, but it seems to rule out species, and I refuse to give up species. So the principle needs repair or replacement.

Please tell me what principle, or combination of principles, best fits all six verdicts; what it implies about cases I haven't considered, such as "weed," "dinosaur," "vitamin," or "metal" as astronomers use it; and, if no clean principle saves everything, which of my verdicts is the cheapest to surrender and why.
