I've been developing the argument below for a reading group on AI safety, and I've come to distrust it because the conclusion is stronger than I can live with. It purports to show that "avoid side effects" cannot be given content short of a full specification of human value, which would dissolve a whole research program I had taken to be coherent. But it seems obvious that there is a difference between a robot that fetches coffee by crossing the room and one that fetches coffee by driving through the flowerbed, and any argument that denies a workable version of that distinction has, I assume, proved too much. I'd like to know where.

(1) To operationalize "avoid side effects," an agent's objective must include a penalty term measuring how much an action changes the world relative to some baseline, since a side effect just is an unwanted change the agent causes.

(2) The baseline must be defined by the agent's own counterfactual behavior -- doing nothing, or continuing its current policy -- because any baseline defined over what the world "should" look like already presupposes the value specification the measure was supposed to replace.

(3) Against an inaction-style baseline, an agent that acts at all is charged for everything that flows from acting, and in a chaotic world the long-run divergence between acting and not acting is enormous and roughly independent of which action was taken. The measure therefore cannot distinguish knocking over a vase from knocking over a power grid; both register as astronomical, so the penalty is blind to precisely the differences it existed to track.

(4) Restricting attention to what we care about fixes the blindness but forfeits the project: a weighting over changes by human-relevant features is itself a specification of value, and the more complete the weighting, the more "low impact" collapses into the full alignment problem it was meant to circumvent.

(5) The remaining option, a value-free measure such as change in attainable utility across arbitrary goals, is gameable in a way that reveals it never captured the notion: the agent can satisfy it by offsetting -- expending effort to restore the baseline, reassembling the vase, preserving option sets by force -- which is not what anyone meant by avoiding side effects and can be worse than the original disturbance.

(6) So the penalty either does no work -- blind to what matters, or satisfied by offsetting and stasis -- or does all the work, by quietly being the full value specification. Either way there is no substantive notion of "low impact" independent of full alignment, and the program of defining side-effect avoidance on its own terms is empty.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would like to keep (3) and (5) as genuine phenomena -- the chaos problem and the offsetting problem both seem like real obstacles any concrete proposal must answer. What I cannot accept is (6). If the argument is valid, tell me honestly and tell me which premise is cheapest to abandon; if it is not, name the step, explain why it looked sound, and price the repair. Lead with the step you reject in one sentence, and keep the whole reply under 600 words.
