I'm a novelist, halfway through a draft, and I've realized I don't actually know what I think about moral worth, or rather I think several things that might not fit together. Here's roughly where I've landed: (1) an action only has moral worth if the person did it for the right reasons, the right outcome reached by accident or for payment doesn't count; (2) the characters I find most admirable act straight out of love or loyalty without any moral deliberation at all, and would be less admirable if they'd stopped to work out their duty first, so I guess I'm with the one-thought-too-many people; (3) a character who does the right thing for selfish reasons, like the mercenary in my book who rescues a kid because it pays, deserves zero moral credit, and I write him that way; (4) nobody chooses what they care about, motives are just what you find yourself stuck with, so I don't blame anyone for their motives, only for what they actually do; (5) whether a character did well should be judgeable from what they do on the page, it shouldn't hang on hidden interior states the reader can't see. Does this hang together? I want a verdict, not a menu of options: tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it, rank the real tensions by severity, and say which single commitment you'd cut if it were your book to save. And please answer in plain continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold or tables, like a letter from one writer to another.
