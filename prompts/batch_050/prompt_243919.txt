i'm a phd student in philosophy, working on conceptual ethics / conceptual engineering, and i moderate a reading group where two people have been going at it all year. for the last session i want to force them into an adversarial collaboration and i need the document drafted.

one of them is basically an old-school conceptual analyst: before we can ask what a concept *should* be, we have to work out what our concept actually is -- intuitions about cases, paradigm and borderline instances, tacit conditions of application. otherwise "improving the concept" is just changing the subject while keeping the word. the other is an ameliorativist, roughly carnap-by-way-of-haslanger: the philosophically interesting question is never what our concept happens to be but wich concept would best serve the purposes the concept is for, and facts about current usage are at most evidence about which purposes the folk concept serves now, often badly.

their fight has converged on a concrete case, wich happens to be my third chapter: should universities treat undisclosed use of an LLM on take-home essays as plagiarism? the analyst keeps pressing that plagiarism, as we actually have the concept, seems to require passing off another person's work as your own -- that's why buying an essay from a ghostwriter counts and having a parent proofread doesn't -- and an LLM arguably isn't a person whose work you pass off, its outputs aren't authored the way a ghostwriter's pages are, so maybe the concept just doesn't apply and the policy panic is confused. the ameliorativist says this is all beside the point: ask what plagiarism is *for* in a university (keeping assessment epistemically informative, fair credit, trust in what a transcript certifies), then define whatever concept best serves those functions, and if the folk concept doesn't match, so much the worse for the folk concept -- call the new one plagiarism 2.0 if the word is doing all the fighting.

draft the joint statement they should come out with if they did this properly. i want:

1. a numbered agreed-claims section -- things both can genuinely sign: about what explication is, whether folk concepts can be dysfunctional relative to their point, the shared facts of the plagiarism case (at a level of description of LLM outputs both accept), what assessment is for, etc. nothing so vacuous that signing costs nothing.
2. a disagreements table, one residual disagreement per row, each side's position phrased so that side would actually sign it, no strawmen. my guesses at the real ones: whether changing-the-subject is a genuine objection or just begs the question; whether there are facts about "our concept" discoverable independently of normative commitments; whether the legitimacy of a purpose is itself up for grabs inside the method.
3. the cruxes -- sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side -- and for each, a case or argument both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. e.g. the ghostwriter / LLM / parent-proofreading triad: does it show the folk concept tracks *passing off another's agency*, or *unauthorized assistance* generally? both should state what the triad (or some fourth case) would have to look like to embarrass their view. another likely crux: whether identifying the legitimate purposes of a concept can be done without already doing normative theory -- i.e. whether amelioration is really a rival to analysis or just applied ethics in a trenchcoat.

don't adjudicate who wins; the deliverable is a document both would put their names to. and keep it formulation-focused, not a lit review.
