So I work on the product team at a company that makes an AI chat assistant (one of the big ones, doesn't matter which). We had a design review last week that kind of broke my brain and I want a philosophical read on it.

Here's the thing. Our assistant talks in first person constantly. It says "I think that's right," "I made a mistake," "I don't know," "I believe the answer is..." We deliberately trained it to say "I'm not sure" when it's likely wrong, because we want users to be able to trust its confidence. Our evals literally score it on things like "does the model know its own limitations" and "is the model being honest." Marketing copy says the model "understands" your question. Meanwhile, the exact same company puts out comms telling users not to anthropomorphize it, it's just a statistical text generator, it doesn't really believe anything, the "I" is a conversational convenience.

And then there's the detail that got me: we can completely flip what the model "believes" by editing one line of the system prompt. Tell it it's a cautious skeptic and it expresses doubt about everything; remove the line and it's confident again. If a person's beliefs could be swapped out that cleanly by editing a note on their desk, I don't think we'd call them beliefs at all.

So my question: what theory of belief does this whole practice actually presuppose? Like, the practice of designing, marketing, and evaluating these systems using full-on mental vocabulary. Does it only make sense if you think belief-attribution is just a useful prediction strategy that doesn't require anything going on inside (my roommate who did a phil minor keeps saying this is Dennett's "intentional stance" thing)? Or does scoring a model for "honesty" presuppose something stronger, like there's a fact of the matter about what it believes that it could misrepresent? Which rival views of belief does the practice quietly rule out? And is the "don't anthropomorphize" messaging consistent with the "it knows its limitations" evals, or are we presupposing two contradictory theories depending on which slide deck we're presenting?

Also: what would concretely have to change in the product if we adopted a strict view that only conscious systems can literally believe things? Would we have to strip all the first-person language, or does some weaker view license keeping it?

(~150 words, is the presupposition strict or would several views license the practice), "The two slide decks" (~150 words, whether the comms vs. evals tension is a real inconsistency), "What would change under the strict view" (~100 words). Then a short last section, "What's contested" (~75 words), where you separate what's more or less settled from what's genuinely disputed among people who work on this stuff — please don't present a contested position on machine belief as if it were the consensus view, I'd rather know where the real disagreements are.
