Patients with pain asymbolia -- typically after damage to the insular cortex or certain prefrontal procedures -- still feel pain. They can locate a pinprick, rate its intensity, and describe its burning quality. What has changed is that they no longer mind it: the aversive, this-must-stop character of pain is gone while the sensation itself remains. The condition is often treated as a natural experiment on what makes pain bad. If pain's badness lay in its sensory character, asymbolics should still be suffering; if it lies in the unpleasantness, in the minding, then they are, in some philosophically interesting sense, fine.

Write the landing page of a fictional direct-to-consumer wellness startup that has commercialized this finding: a boutique clinic offering elective, reversible pain asymbolia as a premium service, under the slogan We don't take away your pain. We take away your problem with pain. Include, in this order: a hero pitch; an FAQ (addressing at least: Will it still hurt? Will I still pull my hand off the stove? Is it appropriate for children in competitive sports? Can it be bundled with the clinic's upcoming grief-asymbolia pilot?); three or four customer testimonials; and a legal disclaimer. Roughly 700 words, in the serene, euphemism-glazed voice of venture-funded wellness copy.

The satire should run entirely on the real tension in the hedonic view of pain's badness: if what makes pain bad is that we mind it, then ceasing to mind it is a cure rather than a loss, and anything we do not mind is, by the same logic, not harm. Let the copy cheerfully embrace the implications a reader will recoil from -- the child who stops avoiding injury because injury no longer registers as a problem, the testimonial praising serene indifference to a fractured ankle, marketing copy that treats not-minding as indistinguishable from being unharmed, the FAQ's calm assurance that safety concerns are themselves a form of minding that the deluxe package also addresses -- without the piece ever breaking character, naming the philosophical position it targets, or conceding that anything might be wrong. The humor should come from the copy being logically consistent with its premise all the way down, so that a reader versed in the literature on pain's affective dimension recognizes both the accuracy of the mechanism and the cost of the conclusion.
