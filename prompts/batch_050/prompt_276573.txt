I'm leading my book club's session next week -- we watched The Green Mile (the movie, not the novel) and I volunteered to do something more interesting than "wasn't Tom Hanks great." Here's the angle I want to take, but I can't tell if it actually works or if I'm overthinking it.

The film basically rigs the epistemology. Thanks to John Coffey's supernatural gift, the audience knows with total certainty that he's innocent of the girls' murders, and knows with total certainty that William Wharton did it -- there's zero courtroom ambiguity, no messy evidence, we're just shown. And then the legal system goes ahead and executes Coffey anyway, while the actual guilty party gets punished completely outside the law, when Coffey makes Percy shoot him. Plus there's that horrific botched execution of Delacroix where Percy sabotages the sponge.

So what does the movie add up to as an argument about the death penalty? I ask because it seems like it might be doing something sneaky. The standard abolitionist worry is epistemic: juries are fallible, witnesses are fallible, we inevitably kill some innocent people and you can't undo it. But the film builds a world with *perfect* knowledge of guilt and innocence, courtesy of magic, and then shows the innocent man dying anyway because the institution can't access what the audience can. Is that still the innocence argument, or is it a stranger argument -- something like: even if perfect moral knowledge existed, it wouldn't be the kind of knowledge a bureaucracy could run on, so the problem isn't just that courts make mistakes but that executions require a certainty no institution is built to hold?

And the flip side bothers me too. Wharton gets what's coming to him and the film clearly wants us to feel that's justice, with Coffey as a kind of instrument of desert. Does that make the movie secretly retributivist? Could a smart retributivist watch it and say: this film agrees with me that some people genuinely deserve to die, it just doesn't trust the state to find them without divine assistance? And if so, what's the best abolitionist reply -- maybe that the fantasy of certain knowledge is exactly the fantasy that props up real-world executions, so the film ends up diagnosing the very thing it indulges?

Two more scenes. First, the Delacroix execution: is that scene doing philosophical work -- like a claim that cruelty is baked into the practice rather than being one bad apple like Percy -- or is it just there to make us hate Percy? Second, old Paul Edgecomb narrating, convinced he's cursed with long life as punishment for his role. Does his guilt amount to an argument about what executions do to the people who carry them out, or is that just pathos dressed up as one?

I'd like a reading that actually engages the retribution/deterrence/innocence debate -- what retributivists say about desert, what abolitionists say about fallibility and irrevocability -- while treating the film as a film and not a bad op-ed with miracles in it. Where does it complicate the standard arguments, where does it cheat, and does it show the debate something that philosophy papers tend to miss? Also, is it a problem for my reading that Coffey *chooses* to die rather than be exonerated? That part seems like it might undercut everything and I'm not sure what to do with it.
