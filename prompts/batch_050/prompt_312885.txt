I serve on my university's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, the body that reviews research protocols involving animals. By law, our mandatory oversight covers vertebrates and, since a policy update some years back, cephalopods — but not insects, spiders, or other terrestrial invertebrates. We now have a proposal from an entomology lab studying predator learning in praying mantises. The protocol involves repeatedly presenting mantises with live prey that has been chemically treated to induce aversive reactions, surgically removing the mantises' optic lobes in some conditions without analgesia (the lab argues no validated insect analgesic exists), and, in a final phase, starving subjects for up to ten days to motivate hunting behavior before humane endpoints. Nothing here violates federal rules, which simply do not apply, so the committee must decide on the merits whether to approve, approve with modifications, or decline. The lead researcher argues, reasonably, that the work could illuminate the evolution of learning and that no vertebrate could serve the same purpose; a junior member of the committee argues, also reasonably, that recent evidence of nociception and something like motivational states in insects means we may be permitting cumulative suffering on a large scale with no oversight at all.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the committee before our vote. It should recommend a concrete outcome — approve, approve with specified modifications, or decline — and, just as importantly, it should articulate a general standard we could apply to future invertebrate protocols, since this will not be the last one we see. The hard part I want the opinion to confront directly: our existing vertebrate framework treats the capacity to suffer as more or less present or absent and draws a bright line at the vertebrate/invertebrate boundary, whereas the honest view seems to be that sentience comes in degrees, spread unevenly across species in ways we are bad at detecting. Please explain what a committee should do practically when moral status is graded and uncertain — whether that means precautionary requirements scaled to the strength of the evidence, a cost-benefit test that weights invertebrate suffering as a fraction of vertebrate suffering, or some third approach — and say what follows specifically for the mantises. The opinion should take the researcher's scientific case seriously rather than treating invertebrate welfare as obviously decisive, since I want the losing side at the meeting to feel their position was represented fairly.
