I'm TAing a grad seminar on rational choice and for week two I want to hand out a flowchart: "where do you actually stand on what the coherence arguments prove?" By coherence arguments I mean the usual family -- the money pump for cyclic preferences, Dutch book arguments for probabilism, the vNM and Savage representation theorems. The room will contain everything from "these arguments show that any rational agent's choices can be represented as maximizing expected utility, full stop" to "they show basically nothing interesting," and I want a sheet that sorts people by what they'd say about cases, not by which camp they think they belong to.

Build me the flowchart as a decision tree. Each branch should be labeled with the claim or concrete case that splits it, phrased so someone can answer without knowing the literature. For example, the first split could be something like: can two options simply be incomparable, or does a rational person facing a choice between two careers (say philosophy and law) always have to be able to rank them or count them as exactly tied? Later splits might include: granting that preferences satisfy the ordering axioms, does the existence of a utility representation show anything more than that preferences are representable -- does the jump to cardinal utilities with the EU form add a substantive requirement or is it representational convenience? And the money pump disputes: does getting pumped require an irrational choice at some single moment, or only an irrational pattern spread over time? And does being exploitable even amount to being irrational, or is it just a practical liability like being bad at poker?

Leaves that have to appear (name the position if it has a standard name, otherwise label it "unnamed"):

- the full-strength view: coherence arguments establish EU maximization as a genuine requirement of rationality
- the deflationary/structuralist view: the theorems show representability and nothing more
- the "exploitability is not irrationality" view: money pumps show vulnerability, not a rational defect
- the diachronic-pattern view: what's irrational is the sequence, no individual choice is
- the incomparability friend who escapes the arguments before they start
- the objection that the classic money pump is incoherent as a package deal (the agent is offered trades they'd refuse given what they know about the setup)
- the asymmetric view that Dutch book arguments work for credences but the preference-based arguments for utility fail (or the reverse)

Two more things. First, work from the logical space: after the tree, list the answer-combinations that are unoccupied and say briefly why each is empty -- is that spot incoherent, just unattractive, or genuinely unexplored? For instance, is there a coherent position that accepts transitivity but rejects continuity? Or someone who thinks the money pump proves irrationality but Dutch books prove nothing? Second, if any two leaves can't be separated by answers to cases alone, flag that honestly instead of inventing a fake distinguishing question.

Constraints: 8 to 12 branch questions max, each one a concrete case or a yes/no claim; each leaf gets one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. Audience is grad students who saw the vNM axioms once a few years ago and have mostly forgoten them, so no heavy formalism in the questions themselves.
