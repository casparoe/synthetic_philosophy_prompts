A colleague and I recently spent four months deadlocked over a forecasting question at work — we had the same data, comparable track records, and full access to each other's reasoning, and neither of us budged. What unsettled me afterward was less the disagreement than my own response to it: I couldn't tell whether my sticking to my estimate was principled steadfastness or just an ego-driven failure to conciliate. Reading about the peer disagreement literature has given me names for the two poles (conciliationism on one side, various steadfast or "rational disagreement" views on the other), but every paper I've found argues for one camp, and I suspect a lot of the dispute is less clean than either side admits.

What I want is the document a conciliationist and a steadfast proponent would produce if, instead of writing rival papers, they ran an adversarial collaboration and drafted a joint statement. Please write that document, with at least these sections: first, a numbered list of claims both parties can genuinely sign — I expect this to include things like the existence of some cases where splitting the difference is clearly required (the two-people-checking-a-restaurant-bill kind of case) and some where digging in seems clearly right, so be careful that the agreed claims are substantive rather than diplomatic filler. Second, a set of precise disagreement statements, each phrased neutrally enough that each side would accept the formulation of its own position — and here I'd like you to explicitly separate disagreements that turn out to be verbal (for instance, over what "peer" or "equal weight" should mean) from those that are substantive. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side.

For each crux, give a concrete case or argument both parties agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction or verdict about that case in advance, the way a collaboration would pre-register expectations. Some candidate cruxes I'd want you to consider, refine, or replace: whether higher-order evidence (the fact that an apparent equal disagrees with me) is evidence in the same way first-order evidence is, or whether discounting my own reasoning in response to it amounts to an impermissible loss of self-trust; whether the debate about disagreement is self-undermining for conciliationism, given that conciliationists and steadfasters appear to be peers who disagree; and whether something like a uniqueness thesis about rational belief is doing hidden work on both sides. Where my candidates collapse into one another, say so. The goal is a text both camps could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact — not a verdict on who's right.
