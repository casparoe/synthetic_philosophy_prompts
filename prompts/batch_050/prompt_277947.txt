So I do entity resolution for a living -- basically record linkage: figuring out which rows across a bunch of messy databases refer to the same actual person or company. Lots of similarity scores, blocking, transitive closure over match edges, agonizing over thresholds, no real ground truth most of the time. Anyway I was at a museum with my nephew last month and ended up reading about the species problem, and it honestly sounded like my job. Biologists apparently have a pile of rival "species concepts" -- the interbreeding one, phylogenetic ones, morphological, ecological, maybe more -- and after a century they still can't agree on what a species even is. My first reaction was: this is just a clustering schema dispute that nobody has framed properly yet.

So here's what I want. Translate the species problem into the vocabulary of entity resolution and clustering, as systematically and honestly as you can. Take the main species concepts one at a time and restate each as a clustering criterion in my terms -- equivalence relations, transitive closure, similarity thresholds, graph connected components, tree-cutting rules, cluster stability under resampling, whatever fits. E.g. I assume the biological species concept becomes something like connected components under a can-produce-fertile-offspring relation, and phylogenetic concepts become rules for where to cut a tree. I'm totally fluent in that vocabulary; assume I have undergrad bio at best, so flag it when a translation step smuggles in a biological assumption I wouldn't notice.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: which pieces of the biological debate translate cleanly, which you had to fudge (my suspicion is that interbreeding isn't transitive -- ring species and all that -- so "connected components" is already a distortion, but correct me), and which have no counterpart in entity resolution at all. For each bit of residue, give me your read on whether it means my framework lacks expressive power biologists genuinely need, or whether the original concept was doing something a clustering framework would *rightly* refuse to do -- like, is "species" supposed to name a real causal unit in the world rather than just a label on a cluster, and does that even have an analogue in my world? I want to know wether my instinct that this is a schema-design argument is right or is exactly the kind of naivety domain experts hate.
