My two best friends from my philosophy PhD program are getting married in June and I'm giving the toast. Quick sketch of the situation: she wrote her disertation defending the extended mind thesis, he wrote his attacking it (he's very much a coupling-isn't-constitution guy), and they have been arguing about it since roughly their second date. Half the room will be philosophers who know this literature cold, and half will be their families, who think epistemology is a skin condition. I want to build the toast on this passage from Andy Clark and David Chalmers' 1998 paper The Extended Mind: "In these cases, the human organism is linked with an external entity in a two-way interaction, creating a coupled system that can be seen as a cognitive system in its own right. All the components in the system play an active causal role, and they jointly govern behavior in the same sort of way that cognition usually does. If we remove the external component the system's behavioral competence will drop, just as it would if we removed part of its brain. Our thesis is that this sort of coupled process counts equally well as a cognitive process, whether or not it is wholly in the head." Honestly, that describes their marriage better than it describes most notebooks. Write me a toast of about three to four minutes that works through the passage clause by clause, treating the couple as the coupled system. Two constraints that really matter: the toast must never settle whether the extended mind thesis is actually true, since nobody is winning that argument at this wedding, and the bit about removing a component has to be handled gently, turned toward taking care of each other rather than anything morbid. The philosophers should catch every allusion, and the families should just hear something warm and true about interdependence. Continuous prose I can read straight off an index card, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. American English, please.
