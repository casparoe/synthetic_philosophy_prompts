Two colleagues of mine — a field linguist and a philosopher — got into an extended argument after a workshop, and it has since consumed our department's lunch table. I'm relaying it as accurately as I can, and I have no stake in it; I'd like it settled.

The linguist had presented a lexicon for an undocumented language she studies. Her entry for one word glosses it as 'rain.' The philosopher opened by asking how she could rule out that the word actually picks out rain-events, raindrops, or rain-and-its-typical-consequences, given that her evidence — utterances made while pointing at storms, and so on — is equally compatible with all of these. (Move 1) The linguist replied that this is methodologically naive: fieldwork doesn't stop at ostension. Her consultants use the word in inference ('it rained, so the river rose'), embed it under negation and tense, distinguish it from drizzle and from hail, and apply it to rain they didn't witness. Any hypothesis that survives all of that, she said, has earned the translation. (Move 2) The philosopher responded that every such test only narrows things relative to prior translations of the surrounding vocabulary — tense markers, negation, causal connectives — and those translations face the same evidential situation, so the narrowing is holistic and the residual indeterminacy survives: a different overall assignment of meanings could fit the same total evidence while disagreeing with hers. (Move 3)

At this point the linguist changed tactics. (Move 4) She said the philosopher was conflating underdetermination with indeterminacy. All theory outruns its evidence — physics included — and no one concludes there is no fact of the matter about electrons; so unless he thinks meaning is special, his argument shows at most that translation is hard, not that there is nothing to be right or wrong about. (Move 5) The philosopher replied that meaning is special in exactly the relevant way: with electrons there is at least the physical world to be wrong about, but two translation schemes can differ in what they attribute while agreeing on every physical fact there is or could be, and where no fact at all settles a question, the question has no answer — so the rival glosses aren't unknown-but-one-of-them-true, they're both correct. (Move 6) The linguist then objected that this proves too much: by his lights the same holds between two speakers of his own language, so he must accept that nothing determines what *he* means by 'rain' either — and a thesis that dissolves its own assertion can't be a discovery, it's a reductio. (Move 7) His final move was to distinguish: from his own case he has first-person access that fixes his meanings for him; indeterminacy applies to interpreting others, and admitting that is not self-defeat but candor about what evidence about other minds can and cannot deliver.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the seven moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible-but-incomplete, with a brief justification. In particular: does the holism point in Move 3 actually answer Move 2, or merely relocate the difficulty? Is the underdetermination/indeterminacy distinction in Move 4 doing real work, and does Move 5 survive it? And is the first-person reply in Move 7 a stable resting point or an evasion? Then give an overall verdict: who is more right, and what, if anything, are both of them failing to see?
