So I'm presenting in our department's logic & language seminar series next month, and I've decided (possibly foolishly) to defend dialetheism: the claim that some contradictions are actually true. My talk focuses on the Liar paradox as the main case -- the rough line is that every classical escape route (Tarski hierarchies, Kripke's gaps, contextualist shuffling) pays some huge cost, like giving up the unrestricted T-schema or making "true" inexpressible in our own language, and that if we just switch to a paraconsistent logic like LP we can keep naive truth and let the Liar be both true and false without everything exploding, since ex contradictione quodlibet fails. I'm explicitly NOT defending trivialism or claiming contradictions are everywhere, just a small number at the edges of truth and maybe set theory.

Here's the problem: the audience is almost entirely people trained in classical logic. There's a set theorist who thinks changing logic is madness, a deflationist about truth who I suspect will say my whole setup misunderstands what "true" is for, a couple of grad students who took one course on modal logic and are ready to be hostile, and exactly one person who works on relevant logic who might be my only friend in the room. Nobody there has read Priest closely, but they've all absorbed the standard "you're just changing the meaning of negation" response secondhand.

Prep me for the Q&A. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from THIS audience (not the objections a paraconsistent logic specialist would raise), ordered by how likely they are times how much damage they'd do if I fumble them. Phrase each the way the person raising it would actually put it -- the set theorist sounds different from the deflationist. For each: a two-minute spoken reply, what I should just concede up front, and a note on where my reply is weakest. And tell me which question I'm hoping nobody asks, because I'm pretty sure there is one and I'd rather hear it from you first.
