I'm a novelist, mostly literary stuff, but my writing group (six of us, we meet once a month round someone's kitchen table) is doing a shared anthology of stories about collective creatures — hive minds, swarm intelligences, that whole territory. After the first workshop it was painfully obvious that everyone is basically writing telepathy with extra steps, so I volunteered to give a twenty-minute primer at the next meeting on the real biology and philosphy of what makes something one organism versus many: siphonophores, ant colonies, holobionts, all of that. My thinking is that if they understand where the actual conceptual pressure points are, their invented creatures will inherit some genuine tension instead of running on magic. None of them have any science background past popular science books, and neither do I beyond having gone down this rabbit hole for research.

So: don't give me the primer itself, plan how I should give it. What has to be in place before "what counts as an individual" is even an intelligible question for them — I suspect some minimal grip on natural selection has to precede any talk of evolutionary individuals, but tell me if I've got the dependency backwards — and which ONE example should carry the whole talk. I'm torn between the Portuguese man o' war and the human-plus-microbiome holobiont, so pick one, explain why it earns the slot, and explain what the runner-up would have smuggled in that I don't want. Then the misconception map: I'm expecting "one body means one organism," "a colony is just separate organisms cooperating," and the fatalist shrug of "it's all just definitions so anything goes." Tell me the order these will predictably surface in once discussion starts, and exactly where in my sequence I should head each one off, before it sets like concrete.

Two more things. My actual goal is that they leave understanding that different criteria for individuality — shared metabolism, reproduction, immune boundaries, being a unit that selection acts on — can point at different answers for the same case, and that this mismatch is where the interesting fiction lives. Should that land early as a hook or late as the payoff, and why? And give me one borderline case to throw at them at the very end (a lichen? an aspen grove? you decide) plus what their handling of it would tell me about whether the idea actually landed. Finally, tell me what I should openly refuse to cover in twenty minutes, and how to say so without it sounding like I'm dodging.
