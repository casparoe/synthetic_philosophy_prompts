I am trying to get clear on what makes consciousness unified, and I keep testing my intuitions on split-brain cases. Below are my verdicts. I would like you to find the principle, if there is one, that lies behind them — or to tell me honestly that there is not.

Case 1 — the split-screen task. A commissurotomy patient fixates on the centre of a screen; 'KEY' flashes in his left visual field and 'RING' in his right. Asked what he saw, he says 'ring'; meanwhile his left hand selects a picture of a key from an array. My verdict: at that moment there are two centres of awareness, one per hemisphere, sharing a skull.

Case 2 — the same patient at home. He cooks an omelette, plays chess with his wife, walks the dog, and shows no behavioural sign of division. My verdict: one subject, going about his life.

Case 3 — cross-cueing. After years of practice, the patient has learnt to let the speaking hemisphere and the left hand coordinate through bodily signals — a tap of the tongue against the teeth, a shift of posture — so that both sides contribute to a single spoken answer. My verdict: this is one subject who has learnt a workaround, as a deaf person learns to lip-read; not two subjects who have merged, and not one subject play-acting at being two.

Case 4 — confabulation. Shown 'WALK' in the left field only, the patient stands and walks; asked why, he says he wanted to stretch his legs. My verdict: one subject with a hole in his self-knowledge — not two subjects, one of whom is handling public relations.

Case 5 — divided attention in the intact. An ordinary driver carries on a conversation while navigating traffic he cannot afterwards recall. My verdict: one subject. Division of labour between processes does not, by itself, divide the subject.

Case 6 — partial section. In a patient whose anterior corpus callosum is cut while the splenium remains intact, simple colour information crosses between hemispheres but complex shapes do not. My verdict: one subject — though I am least confident here, and I would not stake much on it.

Two tensions bother me. Cases 1 and 2 concern the very same brain: if there are two centres of awareness in the laboratory and one in the kitchen, unity looks task-relative, and that strikes me as suspiciously convenient — as though the number of subjects present varied with the ingenuity of the experimenter. Cases 3 and 5 cut the other way: if adding a channel of communication can make one subject out of two, why doesn't the driver's division of labour make two out of one?

So my questions are these. First: is there a single principle of the unity of consciousness — some relation between experiences, or between a subject's capacities, that must obtain for experiences to belong to one subject — which delivers all six verdicts? State it precisely, and say which cases motivate which clauses. Second: what does it predict for cases I have not listed — (i) a hypothetical patient whose two hemispheres simultaneously perform two demanding tasks, neither able to monitor the other; (ii) a patient in whom the section is made millimetre by millimetre over many months; (iii) an ordinary person under dichotic listening who shadows one ear and recalls nothing of the other? Third: if no principle saves all six verdicts, tell me which you would surrender first, and what the surrender costs — in particular, whether your surviving principle still has anything non-arbitrary to say about the question everyone actually cares about: how many people are in the room when the patient signs a consent form.
