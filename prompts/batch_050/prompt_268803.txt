Bit of an odd one, bear with me. Every fall I teach a graduate seminar on self-reference and formal theories of truth, and I have a tradition of closing the last session with something that isn't a problem set. This year I want to hand them a poem about the Löbian obstacle to self-trust, and I'd like you to write it.

Quick refresher so we're on the same page: by Löb's theorem, a consistent formal system strong enough to do arithmetic can prove the conditional "if my proof predicate applies to P, then P" only when it can already prove P outright. So an idealized agent built on such a system can never derive a general policy of trusting its own future reasoning -- whatever soundness it doesn't already have, it can't earn by reflection. It can trust particular proofs it can simply carry out, but the blanket self-endorsement stays out of reach, and trying to bootstrap it level by level (trust that my trust is trustworthy...) just regenerates the same gap one rung up, forever.

I want a villanelle -- the form seems almost made for this. Let the two refrains be, roughly, the agent's aspiration and the theorem's answer: one line that keeps trying to assert self-trust, one that keeps witholding it, trading places the way the form demands. Each tercet should feel like the agent climbing one more rung (I trust my proofs; I trust my trust in my proofs; ...) with the second refrain already waiting at the top. In the closing quatrain, where the refrains finally collide, I'd like the poem to land on the actual moral rather than mere despair: that some trust has to be adopted rather than derived -- you act on the axiom, you don't get to prove it first.

Nineteen lines, proper villanelle form (aba rhyme, refrains returning where they should), and accurate enough that a logician reading over my shoulder wouldnt wince. No footnotes, no jargon-dropping; the students should be able to feel the trap closing even if they only half-remember the theorem.
