ok so I host a small philosophy podcast (nerdy audience, mostly grad students and the odd philosopher of mind who wandered in) and next week I'm interviewing the author of a new monograph defending what I'd call the "alethic" diagnosis of imaginative resistance. Roughly her view: the puzzle has been misfiled from the start. When readers balk at a story that simply states, as a fact of the fiction, that some cruel practice is morally right, the problem isn't that they can't imagine it -- it's that they can't accept it as true in the story. Moral truths, if there are any, hold necessarily, and authors have no authority to stipulate necessary falsehoods into their fictions. So resistance is resistance to a would-be truth of the fiction, not some special failure of the imagination. Imagination is off the hook.

I'd like you to act as an veteran philosophy-podcast producer who knows this literature inside out. Before drafting anything, brainstorm (and show me) the three or four places where this view is softest, and then write me a run of 8-9 interview questions, in the order you'd actually ask them, that go after those soft spots rather than letting her re-explain the view for the third time. She will happily re-explain the view for the full hour if you let her. Each question should come with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so I'm not left standing there when she pivots.

Pressure points I want covered somewhere in the sequence, though organize them however flows best on air:

1. The asymmetry problem: readers swallow outright impossibilities in fiction all the time (time travel, magic, talking animals) without a flicker of resistance. If resistance tracks the necessity of whatever truth gets violated, why do only moral violations (and a few others) bite? Anticipate that she'll reach for the arithmetic-style cases -- a story that just flatly says some even number is odd -- as evidence that non-moral necessities cause resistance too, and have a follow-up ready for that move.

2. Fictions that never assert the deviant moral claim: a story that narrates cruelty in a flatly admiring tone, or genre fiction where the heroes commit what would be atrocities and the narration treats it as fine, without any sentence actually stating the moral verdict. If nothing deviant is asserted anywhere, what exactly is supposed to be untrue-in-the-fiction on her account?

3. Authorial authority over evaluative facts generally: authors plainly can make it true in a fiction that a character is brave, or that an ending is tragic. If thick evaluative verdicts are within the author's reach, where's the principled line that keeps the thin moral verdict out of reach -- especially given that the author also controls all the underlying non-moral facts that the verdict is supposed to supervene on?

4. Gradability: resistance comes in degrees and varies between readers, genres, and re-readings. Necessity doesn't come in degrees. Square those.

5. Somewhere in the back half, a question that forces her to say what a genuine counterexample to the view would even look like. My worry is the view has a built-in escape hatch: whenever readers do accept a deviant-seeming fiction, she can always say the story must have quietly changed the non-moral facts so the morality was never deviant in-world after all. I want a question that pins down what observation would make her actually abandon the view.

House rules: no asking her to summarize the book, no "how did you get into this topic", no biography fluff. Assume she's smart and has heard every objection before -- the follow-ups matter more than the openers. Also, for two or three of the questions, flag the exact spot where shes most likely to deflect and how I should handle it.
