I'm a third-year student in a course on strategic rationality and war, and we've spent the last few weeks on the logic of deterrence: Schelling's idea that making a threat credible may require cultivating a reputation for irrationality, or binding yourself so that the choice is taken out of your hands. From this I've come to accept a fairly strong claim: between two agents with common knowledge of each other's perfect rationality, nuclear deterrence cannot be credible. If defiance has already happened, retaliation is worse for the retaliator than restraint, both sides know this in advance, and so the threat is empty no matter how loudly it is made. The doctrine of mutual assured destruction, on this view, rests on a kind of institutionalized bluff that only works because real states are not, and are known not to be, perfectly rational.

A classmate pressed me on this with a case I couldn't immediately dismiss. Imagine two artificial agents that can read each other's source code, where one visibly hard-codes a policy of retaliating if attacked. Verification here does the work that irrationality does for humans: the second agent can confirm mechanically that the commitment will be executed, so the threat is credible without any departure from rationality. If that's right, my claim is wrong -- deterrence between perfectly rational agents is possible, just by different means. I'm not sure whether to accept this (is the hard-coded policy rational at the moment of execution, or only a way of borrowing credibility from an earlier moment?), and it made me realize I don't actually know what my position is exposed to.

So: taking the claim to be that perfect, mutually known rationality makes deterrence incredible, tell me what would count against it. Not the lazy objection that real states are irrational anyway -- that concedes my point rather than refuting it. I want to know which assumptions in the setup do the load-bearing work (one-shot versus repeated interaction, complete versus incomplete information about resolve, whether commitment devices can themselves be inspected, whether rationality is defined over acts or over policies), which formal results or model constructions would refute the claim outright, and which findings would merely be awkward -- things a defender could absorb with a qualification. For each, say whether a committed defender of the claim would themselves concede it as a defeater or dismiss it as a change of subject, and rank the whole set from fatal to merely uncomfortable. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- the single most dangerous challenge to the claim -- and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
