My online reading group has been working through decision theory this fall, and our session on the Ellsberg paradox turned into an audit of my own choices, which turned out to be less coherent than I would have liked. I have read the usual discussions on rationalist blogs about expected utility and the Allais paradox, so I know the standard move of either defending one's intuitions as legitimate ambiguity aversion or biting the bullet and calling them biases. What I want instead is to know what principle, if any, my actual verdicts commit me to. Here they are. Case one: the classic urn with 30 red balls and 60 balls split between black and yellow in unknown proportion. I prefer a bet on red over a bet on black, and I prefer a bet on black-or-yellow over a bet on red-or-yellow, and I stand by both preferences on reflection. Case two: same urn, but the bet on black pays a premium. At $105 against $100 I still take red; at $150 against $100 I switch. So my aversion to ambiguity is priced, not lexical. Case three: a choice between a known 20% chance of winning and an urn whose winning proportion is unknown but somewhere between 0% and 40% -- here I prefer the ambiguous urn, the opposite of case one, apparently because the known chance is poor. Case four: I would rather bet on a football match in a league I follow than draw from the unknown urn, even when I assign both the same subjective probability; the ambiguity bothers me less in a domain where I feel competent. Case five: if the unknown composition is fixed once and I play a hundred rounds, I still take the known-risk bet; but if the composition is redrawn at random before every round, the ambiguous bet stops bothering me entirely. Case six, the awkward one: when the sure-thing principle is stated to me abstractly -- if you would prefer A to B whether or not some event obtains, prefer A before learning whether it obtains -- I endorse it without hesitation, even though I can see that my case-one verdicts violate exactly that.

What I want is a single principle that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I can apply it myself -- not a taxonomy of models from the literature. I have entertained three candidates: that ambiguity gets a flat discount against its expected value (which handles cases one and two but not three), that the discount varies with how favorable the underlying prospects are (which handles three but seems to leave four and five unexplained), and that ambiguity aversion is really a fear of playing against a better-informed counterparty (which handles four and maybe three, but I cannot see why it would produce the smooth premium in two). Please commit to one of these, or a better principle of your own, and defend it rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose. Tell me which of my six verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and which one I should give up first if forced -- I suspect the answer is either case six's endorsement or one of the case-one preferences, and I want you to pick rather than hedge. Finally, tell me what your principle predicts for two cases I have not listed: an Ellsberg choice framed over losses rather than gains, and an Ellsberg choice I make on behalf of a stranger rather than for myself.
