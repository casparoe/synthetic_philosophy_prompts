In Book XIV of Montesquieu's The Spirit of Laws (1748), "Of Laws in Relation to the Nature of the Climate," the second chapter contains the following thought experiment:

"Put a man into a close, warm place, and for the reasons above given he will feel a great faintness. If under this circumstance you propose a bold enterprise to him, I believe you will find him very little disposed towards it; his present weakness will throw him into despondency; he will be afraid of everything, being in a state of total incapacity."

I would like you to use this passage as a probe for a conceptual issue I keep stumbling over: what exactly is supposed to be wrong with "environmental determinism," and where precisely the line falls between it and the now-respectable practice of treating non-human forces as causes in history.

Please structure your answer around the following.

First, interpret the passage. Reconstruct the inference Montesquieu is inviting us to make. In particular, attend to the scale shift: the experiment concerns one overheated individual and his momentary incapacity for boldness, yet the chapter's purpose is to explain the courage, frankness, and political character of whole northern peoples. What has to be true for that inference to go through? And what role does the accompanying physiology (the contraction and relaxation of bodily fibres, the frozen sheep's tongue described in the same chapter) play -- does a stated mechanism make the claim more scientific, or merely more confident?

Second, explain the conceptual issue itself. Historians today routinely write of the Black Death reshaping labor relations in fourteenth-century Europe, of smallpox deciding the fate of empires in the Americas, of the Tambora eruption of 1815 producing the "year without a summer" and its harvest failures. In such work, germs, climate, and volcanoes are treated as genuine historical actors, and nobody calls it determinism. Meanwhile, Montesquieu-style climatology is a byword for it. So: what must a causal claim about the environment assert, or omit, to deserve the pejorative label? Is the vice logical (claiming necessity where there is only influence), epistemic (invoking mechanisms that explain everything and so nothing), aggregative (inferring from persons to peoples without justification), or moral and political (furnishing ready-made hierarchies among populations)? Or is it several of these, and do they travel together?

Third, test the passage against your account. Which of these vices, if any, does Montesquieu actually commit? Bear in mind, as a complication, that elsewhere in Book XIV he argues that good legislators should act against the vices of their climate rather than yield to them -- which suggests he did not take climate to be fate. Does that reservation rescue him from the charge, or does it merely reveal an instability in the position?

Fourth, reach a verdict. Is there a principled difference between Montesquieu's climatology and modern environmental history, or is the difference only that we possess better mechanisms and worse memories of our own overconfidence? And one further question, which I think the "historical actor" idiom smuggles in: can a bacillus or a volcanic winter be an actor in anything but a decorative metaphor, and if the metaphor is doing real work in the historiography, what work is it -- and does it conceal a confusion?

Please give an intuitive rather than technical treatment, in roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words (the quoted passage does not count toward this length).
