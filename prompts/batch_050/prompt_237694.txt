I run a grad reading group on strategy and rational choice, about 14 people, and we have one 90-minute session on commitment and threats. Design an in-class game that makes them feel how commitment races happen: the dynamic where it pays to be the first to visibly, irreversibly lock yourself into a position, so everyone scrambles to commit first, and both sides end up worse off than if they'd just bargained normally.

What I want: actual rules (roles, rounds, what information is public, what a "commitment device" in the game costs and what it buys), a payoff scheme that doesn't involve real money, and a run order -- e.g. play once with no commitment options, then add them, so they feel the difference in their gut. The escalation has to happen *to* them, not be explained to them: I don't want to mention first-mover advantage or brinkmanship until after they've raced themselves into the bad outcome.

Then a debrief that maps specific moments from the game onto the actual concepts (Schelling-style precommitment, burning bridges, credible vs. incredible threats, whatever fits), plus what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- people refusing to commit at all, or someone commiting so early the game degenerates -- and how to handle that.

Also be honest about epistemic status: which dynamics the game shows are solid, well-established theory, and which parts lean on contested empirical claims about how real actors actually behave under commitment. Don't present the contested stuff as consensus. And tell me which features of real commitment races (nuclear brinkmanship, reputation across repeated encounters) the game can't reproduce, so I know what I have to supply in the discussion afterward.
