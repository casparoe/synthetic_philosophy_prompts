So I was reading a long magazine piece about solar geoengineering (the stratospheric aerosol injection kind) and it wrapped up the case against with this summary:

"There are basically three decisive objections. First, moral hazard: behavioral research has shown that whenever people are offered a technological fix for a risk, they stop taking precautions against it, so merely discussing geoengineering has already weakened emissions policy in measurable ways. Second, termination shock: once you start, you can never stop, because if deployment ended the suppressed warming would show up in a decade or two and ecosystems couldn't adapt that fast — so it's a one-way door no responsible government could ever open. Third, the governance question is actually already settled: decisions that affect the whole planet's climate fall under the UN Security Council, and since the permanent members would never all agree, deployment is effectively prohibited, which makes further research a waste of money at best."

The article treats all three as pretty much established results. My gut says the first is doing more work than the evidence allows, the second takes a real problem and inflates it into an impossibility proof, and the third smells made up — but I've only read popular stuff on this so I can't back that up. Can you go through the three claims one by one and say what a careful person who actually works on this would accept in each, what's an overstatement or conflation of two seperate points, and what's just wrong? And once you've corrected the summary: is there anything left that still amounts to a strong argument against deployment, or even against research? Or does the corrected version mostly support a weaker conclusion like "govern it carefully" rather than "don't touch it"?
