My uncle, who died last month at eighty-six, spent his entire career as a historian arguing against the covering-law model of historical explanation. Where the Hempelian tradition holds that an event is explained by subsuming it under a general law, he insisted — in lecture halls, in print, and at the dinner table — that historians explain by telling stories: by arranging particulars into a narrative whose coherence makes the outcome intelligible without recourse to any law. He used to remark, only half-joking, that if a general law could explain the French Revolution, then some law should also have predicted his marriage, which no law would have dared.

His memorial service is next Saturday in the university chapel, and the audience is split in a difficult way. About half will be colleagues — historians and philosophers of history who debated him for decades and will catch every allusion to Hempel, to the nomological-deductive model, to the old quarrel between law and narrative. The other half will be family and neighbors: his widow, his grandchildren, people from his sailing club, who knew him as a wonderful storyteller but have never heard the phrase covering law. His widow has made one request: the eulogy must not turn into a lecture, but it also must not abandon his view at the last minute by implying that his death is explained by some larger law, plan, or cosmic regularity — that would be, she said, the one way to get him wrong.

Please write the eulogy, roughly six minutes when spoken aloud, in American English. The structural challenge I want it to meet is this: the speech should itself be an enactment of his thesis — a narrative that makes one particular life intelligible without subsuming it under any law — and it should let the family feel why that matters without needing the technical vocabulary, while letting the colleagues recognize exactly which side of an old argument is being taken. One concrete requirement: somewhere in the middle, the speech must confront the tension that a narrative view of explanation seems to have nothing to say to the family's question of why him, why now — and it must either resolve that tension honestly or admit that it cannot, rather than changing the subject.
