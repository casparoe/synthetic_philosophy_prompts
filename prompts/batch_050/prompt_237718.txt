I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece where I've basically talked myself into Dennett's position on "counterfeit people" -- roughly, that building AI systems designed to pass as human (simulating emotions, claiming inner states, presenting fake memories or a fake life story) is a serious wrong in itself, closer to counterfeiting money than to ordinary tech progress, and plausibly deserves legal restriction even if the systems turn out not to be conscious. Before I commit to that framing in print, I want to stress-test it. What would actually count against this view?

Here's what I'm after specifically:

1. The view seems to bundle at least two claims -- (a) deceptive human-passing AI causes concrete harms (manipulation, eroded trust, attachment to something that can't reciprocate), and (b) there's an independent wrong in *making* counterfeit people at all, even fully disclosed ones. Which empirical findings would sink (a)? Like, if it turned out that users reliably track that they're talking to software, or that AI companions measurably reduce loneliness rather than exploit it -- does the whole position collapse into (b), and is (b) even defensible on its own?

2. Dennett's money analogy does a lot of work. Counterfeit currency is bad because it destroys trust in a system we all depend on. What would have to be true about human social interaction for that analogy to fail? Could the presence of fake people *raise* rather than lower the epistemic floor in some settings, and would that move the argument or just complicate it?

3. A chunk of the position leans on the claim that we can't know whether AI systems are conscious, so the safe move is to not make ones that seem to suffer or care. Is that premise itself exposed to anything -- say, future theories of consciousness that give clear verdicts for transformer-based systems, in either direction? Which direction would hurt the view more: consciousness turning out cheap and probably present, or turning out to require things LLMs provably lack?

4. What would Dennett himself concede as counting against him, versus what only critics would raise? I don't want a strawman of the guy.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest if you think the position has been insulated by qualifications to the point where nothing could really touch it -- and if so, whether that's actually a problem for a view that's partly a policy proposal rather than a empirical theory.
