I am a doctoral student in philosophy working on the normative dimensions of persuasive design, and I am drafting a dissertation chapter in which I need to get clear on an analogy that does enormous amounts of unexamined work in this literature. Nearly everyone writing about the attention economy -- economists, design critics, popular authors of digital-minimalism books, and a good number of my colleagues -- treats attention as money. Attention is something we "pay" and "spend"; platforms "compete for" and "capture" it; our mental lives are a "budget" that engagement-optimised interfaces deliberately deplete; and the standard conclusion drawn is that extractive design is a kind of theft or exploitation of a resource we own, so that the rational response is to manage our attention with the same discipline a prudent person applies to their finances.

I want a rigorous audit of how far this monetary analogy actually holds. Please do the following. First, make the correspondence explicit: what in the attention case plays the role of income, prices, expenditure, savings, interest, taxation, inflation, and theft? If several of these roles turn out to have no plausible occupant, that itself is a finding and should be stated as such. Second, identify where the mapping breaks down, and distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Candidate pressure points I want addressed, at minimum: money can be stored, accumulated, and transferred whereas attention arguably cannot; money is purely instrumentally valuable whereas attention may be constitutive of experience rather than a mere means; monetary transactions are paradigm cases of voluntary exchange whereas attentional capture is typically engineered to bypass deliberation; and it is contestable whether attention is depleted like a stock of funds at all, as opposed to being reallocated, trained, or simply redirected. Third, say what the analogy quietly smuggles in -- for instance, assumptions about ownership of one's own attention, about a natural default allocation against which "extraction" is measured, and about where the value created by attention accrues. Finally, deliver the verdict: does the exploitation conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, perhaps on different grounds (autonomy, manipulation, frustration of one's ends), and does the budgeting advice survive even if the theft framing fails? If a better governing analogy is available -- attention as nourishment, as land or a commons, as gaze, as labour -- propose it and explain what it captures that the currency does not.
