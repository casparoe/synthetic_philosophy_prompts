I'm a philosophy graduate student embedded with a neuroscience lab that studies human neural mechanisms through model organisms, and we keep having the same fight in lab meeting: when does an experimental preparation count as anomalous — when has the organism stopped running the mechanism we care about and started producing the same behavior some other way? A mutant mouse recovers normal sleep-wake cycling via a compensatory pathway after the canonical clock circuit is lesioned; a cyanobacterium keeps circadian time with a three-protein post-translational oscillator that shares no components with the mammalian transcription-translation feedback loop; a collaborator builds a microfluidic device that reproduces the input-output profile of the lac operon with no DNA in it at all. Half the lab says "same mechanism, different implementation," half says "different mechanism, same output," and the disagreement stalls every paper because nobody can state the criterion they're using.

For our shared reading group, I want a decision tree that walks a researcher from a neutral starting point — two systems both reliably produce phenomenon P — to a named position on when the two count as instances of the same mechanism. Requirements: each branch node must be a question the researcher can answer without knowing any philosophical labels (for instance, whether the working parts themselves must be of the same types, or only their organization; whether sameness is relative to the phenomenon you picked as the thing to be explained; whether falling under the same mathematical model suffices regardless of parts; whether common evolutionary descent settles the matter). The leaves must include at least: the componential view that individuates mechanisms by their entities, activities, and organization; a functional-role view; a minimal-model or universality view on which sameness of mechanism is sameness of explanatory model; a phenomenon-relative view on which there is no answer until the explanatory target is fixed; and a homology-based view. If a logically available combination of branch answers has no defenders, keep it as a leaf anyway and say in one sentence whether it seems incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Flag any pair of named leaves your questions cannot actually separate rather than forcing them apart. Finally, run the lesioned-mouse case and the cyanobacterium case down the completed tree and show which leaf each lands on, since those are the cases that divide my lab.

Present the tree as an indented outline with the branch questions in italics, and keep the whole response under 800 words.
