I got into an argument with a friend after we both listened to an episode of a big history-of-ideas podacst where the host did a segment on Hegel and war. Her summary went something like this (I'm reconstructing from memory but this is the gist):

'Hegel thought war was not just inevitable but positively good for states. In the Philosophy of Right he compares peoples to the sea: just as the sea would go foul and stagnant if the winds never stirred it, nations go stale under a long calm, so war functions as a kind of moral hygiene that keeps a people ethically healthy. Because states acknowledge no higher authority above themselves, international relations remain a permanent state of nature, which is why he dismissed Kant's project of perpetual peace as a naive fantasy. And since there is no court above states, there is strictly no such thing as an unjust war between them — talk of justice only gets a grip inside a state. Popper was therefore right to see Hegel as the intellectual godfather of militarism: he made war respectable again after the Enlightenment had tried to make it look barbaric, and on his view a state that launches a war of conquest has nothing it needs to apologise for. Between states, might simply is right.'

My friend thinks this is all basically accurate and that Hegel really was a warmonger. I want to push back but I can't put my finger on exactly what's wrong, because some of it rings true — I'm fairly sure the sea-and-winds image is genuinely his, for instance. Could you go through it claim by claim and tell me which bits a scholar sympathetic to Hegel would accept, which bits are the standard hostile distortion that got recycled down from Popper, and where exactly the summary conflates things Hegel keeps apart? And does the final 'might is right' verdict actually follow from the parts that are accurate? In particular I'm confused about whether saying states sit in a state of nature with no judge above them commits you to saying there can be no injustice between states at all, or whether that's a slide. And if Hegel did think prolonged peace corrupts a nation, does that commit him to welcoming or encouraging war, or is there room to read him as describing a function war happens to serve without glorifying it?
