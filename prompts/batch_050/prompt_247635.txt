I'm a master's student in Leuven and my supervisor is writing a book on why some EU integration steps "stick" and others get clawed back or hollowed out. Her hero text is the Schuman Declaration of 9 May 1950, but the sentence she keeps circling isn't the famous one about war becoming materially impossible -- it's this one: "With this aim in view, the French Government proposes that action be taken immediately on one limited but decisive point." She insists everyone misreads it: they fixate on "limited" when the real action is in "decisive," i.e. the point of the Coal and Steel Community wasn't that it was small, it's that the High Authority's decisions actually bound governments. Everything in European integration that worked, she says, obeyed that formula; everything that failed violated one half of it or the other.

In our seminar she's given verdicts on a bunch of hypothetical reform proposals, and I'm trying to reverse-engineer her actual rule. Verdicts so far: (1) replacing national vetoes with qualified majority voting on foreign-policy sanctions against third countries -- fine, "textbook case"; (2) a permanent, binding asylum-relocation quota decided by majority vote with financial penalties for states that refuse -- no, and she got visibly annoyed at the suggestion; (3) a European public prosecutor who could indict individual citizens in their own national courts for cross-border VAT fraud, mandatory for all members with no opt-out -- no, even though I pointed out VAT fraud is about as "limited" and technical as domains get, and she just said "you're not listening to the quote"; (4) joint EU borrowing for a one-off, strictly time-limited recovery fund with the total amount fixed in advance -- yes, immediately, "that's literally what he did."

Now the case I actually need her verdict on, because a working group in Brussels is floating something like it and I want to predict where she'll land before I draft anything. Nine willing member states propose merging parts of their armed forces into standing integrated units under a shared multinational command. Participation is voluntary, any state can withdraw entirely on two years' notice, and any actual combat deployment requires the consent of every participating government. But while a state participates, command decisions inside the units are genuinely binding -- soldiers take lawful orders from officers of other nationalities, and governments can't pull individual units out of the joint structure on a case-by-case basis. My question: what does she say, and why? Tell me which reading of the Schuman sentence best fits all four earlier verdicts and what it predicts here, then give me at least one rival rule that fits those same four verdicts equally well but predicts the opposite on this one. If her track record genuinely underdetermines it, say so honestly, and describe the single hypothetical proposal whose verdict would best discriminate between the rival rules. I'm less interested in what you think of the army plan than in what *she'd* think, untill that's genuinely unknowable.
