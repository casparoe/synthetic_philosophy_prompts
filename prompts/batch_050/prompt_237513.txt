Exam question: State Sen's impossibility of a Paretian liberal theorem, illustrating it with Sen's own example, and assess what the result shows about the compatibility of individual rights with the Pareto principle. Your answer should consider one strategy for resolving the paradox.

Student's answer:

Sen's theorem (1970) shows that three plausible conditions on social choice cannot be jointly satisfied. Unrestricted domain: every logically possible profile of individual preference orderings is admissible. Weak Pareto: if everyone strictly prefers x to y, then society must rank x above y. Minimal liberalism: at least two individuals are each decisive over at least one pair of alternatives concerning their own private affairs — whichever way such a person ranks the pair, society ranks it the same way. Sen proves that the three conditions together generate a contradiction in the form of a cyclic social preference.

The example involves Prude and Lewd and a single copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover. There are three states: Prude reads the book (a), Lewd reads it (b), or nobody reads it (c). Prude ranks c above a above b: best that nobody read it, but if someone must, he would rather it be himself than watch Lewd enjoy it. Lewd ranks a above b above c: ideally Prude's horizons should be broadened; failing that, he will read it himself; worst of all is that nobody reads it. Minimal liberalism gives Prude decisiveness over {a, c}, so c is socially preferred to a, and gives Lewd decisiveness over {b, c}, so b is socially preferred to c. But both prefer a to b, so Pareto makes a socially preferred to b. The social ordering cycles: c over a, a over b, b over c.

Since Sen's theorem is essentially a corollary of Arrow's impossibility theorem — it simply substitutes a rights condition for Arrow's independence condition — the result shows that any society committed to protecting a private sphere of individual rights will sometimes be forced into Pareto-inferior outcomes. Rights and efficiency are simply in tension, and the theorem quantifies that tension.

One way out, which Sen himself proposes, is to restrict the domain. The paradox is generated by meddlesome preferences — Prude's and Lewd's concern with what the other person reads — so if we exclude preference profiles in which people hold preferences over other people's private affairs, the inconsistency disappears and both the Pareto condition and the rights condition can be retained.
