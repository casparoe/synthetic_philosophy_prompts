My political theory reading group is staging an adversarial collaboration between two members, and I need the document that a successful collaboration would produce. Both members accept the well-known impossibility result concerning the Paretian liberal: no collective decision procedure can simultaneously (i) admit every logically possible profile of individual preferences, (ii) respect the weak Pareto principle, and (iii) guarantee each individual decisiveness over at least one pair of social states differing only in that individual's purely private affairs. They disagree sharply, however, about what the result teaches.

For concreteness, anchor the discussion in the standard illustration, which I'll state fully so there's no ambiguity: two people, Prude and Lewd, and a single copy of a controversial novel. Three states matter: Prude reads it, Lewd reads it, or neither does. Prude ranks neither-reads highest, then reads-it-himself, then Lewd-reads last; Lewd ranks Prude-reads highest, then reads-it-himself, then neither-reads. Assigning each person decisiveness over the pair of states differing only in his own reading, and then applying Pareto, generates the familiar breakdown.

Camp A (call them the Restrictionists) argues that the result shows the preference-aggregation machinery must be tamed: meddlesome preferences — preferences over what others do in their private sphere — should be excluded or discounted, and once they are, the conflict disappears. Camp B (call them the Proceduralists) argues the framework itself misdescribes rights: a right is not a veto over which social states may be selected but a distribution of authority over moves and choices, so there was never anything to pit against Pareto in the first place, and the impossibility is an artifact of translating rights into outcome constraints.

Draft their joint statement with these components, in American English: a numbered list of at least six claims both camps can sign (including claims about what the formal result does and does not show, and about what a theory of rights owes us); a disagreements table with three columns — the issue, Camp A's position in wording Camp A would accept, Camp B's position in wording Camp B would accept; and a cruxes section identifying the sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one camp. For each crux, specify a concrete case both agree bears on it, with each camp's prediction recorded in advance. Please build one crux around the question of whether preferences that are meddlesome only because of how a person's environment is arranged (say, a co-op board voting on whether residents must keep their window shades raised during the day, where every vote for raised shades is motivated by disapproval of what neighbors might be doing behind them) count as private-sphere interference, and another around whether Proceduralists can give an account of cases where no one makes any impermissible move yet someone's rights seem violated anyway. If part of the disagreement turns out to be terminological — for instance, if the camps mean different things by "decisive" or "private" — identify which part and explain why it's verbal rather than substantive. Close with a short paragraph stating which camp would have to concede more if its prediction about the window-shades case proved untenable.
