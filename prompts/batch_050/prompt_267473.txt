So I mostly write novels, but somehow I agreed to lead a session at this writers retreat next month, and the theme I pitched (before fully thinking it through) was basically hinge epistemology for fiction writers. I've been reading Wittgenstein's On Certainty on the side -- the stuff about how some things aren't really beliefs we hold but the unexamined ground everything else stands on, the things you'd never think to doubt because doubting them isn't even a move available to you. My hunch is this is gold for character work: the most real-feeling characters are the ones whose deepest certainties never get stated, only enacted, and the most devastating plot turns are when a character's hinge cracks. But I have no idea how to actually teach this to a room full of novelists who haven't read any philosophy and don't need to.

So don't explain hinge epistemology to me -- plan the session. It's 90 minutes, maybe 12 people, mix of published and aspiring writers, and I get one shot at it. What I need from you:

The order of ideas. What distinction or notion has to be in place before "hinge" even makes sense to someone who's never thought about epistemology? I'm guessing they need the difference between things a character believes and things a character just... walks on, but tell me if there's something prior to that and why the sequence goes the way it goes.

The carrying example. What's the one case I should build the whole session around? It should probably be from fiction or from ordinary life rather than Wittgenstein's own examples (no Moorean hands please), and it needs to show a hinge doing work in a scene without any character naming it. Tell me what the example needs to have and what it has to avoid -- I suspect if the example's character is too self-aware about their certainty, the whole thing collapses.

The misconceptions, in the order they'll show up. My guesses: they'll think a hinge is just a strongly held belief, or that it's the same as a character's "wound" or backstory trauma from writing-craft speak, or that depicting a hinge means writing an interior monologue where the character almost-notices it. Tell me the ones I haven't guessed, and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off rather than letting it take root.

A check at the end. I want one exercise or question that tells me whether they got it -- like, could I have them sketch a short scene where a hinge is visible only in what the character never pauses over, and would that actually discriminate between people who understood and people who just found it vibes-y?

And tell me what to deliberately leave out at this level -- the riverbed stuff, the relativism questions, whatever -- and how to say "that's a real question and we're skipping it" without losing the room.
