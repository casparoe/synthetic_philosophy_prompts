so in my set theory class the lecturer introduced the axiom of choice with that old illustration from Russell: imagine infinitely many pairs of shoes, and you want a set containing one shoe from each pair. easy, take the left shoe every time, that's a rule, no axiom needed. now infinitely many pairs of socks, the two socks in each pair completely indistinguishable -- no rule available, so you have to postulate the axiom of choice to say a selection exists. his point in telling it was basically that AC is innocuous, just formalising something obviously true, and that people who get squeamish about it are confused about what it says.

but later in the same course AC turns out to give you the well-ordering theorem and gets used in the Banach-Tarski proof, neither of which strikes me as obvious or innocuous at all. so i want an audit of the socks analogy. give me the correspondence table first -- what are the pairs, what is the left/right distinction, what is the physical act of picking one sock -- and then where it breaks. a few things i suspect: (a) grabbing a sock is something you do, AC asserts a set exists, and my intuition about the first might be doing illegitimate work for the second; (b) real socks in a drawer actually are distinguishable (top one, leftmost one), so the analogy has to abstract away exactly the kind of structure that arbitrary sets of reals lack, which feels like it rigs the comparison; (c) the intuition pump only pumps for things like countable collections of finite sets, while the places AC really bites are nothing like socks. say whether i'm right about those. then the main question: does the analogy support my lecturer's conclusion, or does it actually support the opposite reading -- that AC is a substantive extra assumption precisely in the cases where no definable choice exists -- and which of those survives contact with Banach-Tarski? and if the analogy undersells AC, whats a fairer one?
