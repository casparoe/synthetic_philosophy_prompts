I work as an analyst for a county pretrial services office, and a large part of my job is attaching probability estimates to individual defendants: the chance this person fails to appear, the chance this person is rearrested before trial, the chance a diversion participant completes the program. In a statistics elective years ago I encountered what the instructor called the reference class problem -- the same individual belongs to indefinitely many groups, the groups have different observed frequencies, and there is no obvious rule for which group's frequency should govern the single-case estimate -- and I have come to realize it is the whole of my job. A 34-year-old defendant is also a first-time offender, also a renter, also employed part-time, also charged with a nonviolent offense, and each of those reference classes gives a different failure-to-appear rate. My office currently resolves this by picking whichever class the analyst finds salient, which means two analysts produce different numbers for the same file, and our estimates are increasingly challenged in court. I want a written procedure my team can actually run on a case file: an ordered sequence of questions that ends in a defensible probability estimate and a record of how it was reached, built out of whatever serious thinking exists about single-case probability, frequency, and the reference class problem, translated into steps usable by people with spreadsheets rather than philosophy degrees.

The procedure needs to address the specific moves I see go wrong. It should say how to choose among candidate reference classes of different widths (all defendants vs. defendants with this charge vs. defendants with this charge and this history), including what to do when the narrowest class has too few observations to support a frequency and the analyst is tempted to use the tiny-sample number anyway; what to do when two defensible classes genuinely conflict and neither is obviously a refinement of the other, which I gather is the genuinely hard case; whether and how the estimate should move when we have information about this defendant that no class in our data captures (a strong letter from an employer, an unstable housing situation); and what the write-up should disclose so that a judge or opposing expert can see where the number came from. It should distinguish the steps that are mechanical from the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and be honest about whether the reference class problem has an actual solution or only damage limitation -- if the literature's honest answer is that there is no uniquely correct class, the procedure should say what you do at the office on Tuesday morning anyway. Fix the constraints: analysts have about twenty minutes per file, our data go back eight years, and every estimate may have to be defended under cross-examination.

Then run the procedure on two cases and show the working. Case one: a defendant charged with a first-time nonviolent property offense, employed, housed, where the broad class gives a failure-to-appear rate around 12% but the employed-and-housed subclass (n = 61 over eight years) gives 3%. Case two: a defendant where the two most defensible classes genuinely diverge -- charge-based history suggests elevated risk, but age and prior compliance with supervision suggest low risk, and neither class is a subset of the other -- which is the case where I expect the procedure to be forced to expose its own limits rather than produce a clean number. For case two, I want the procedure's actual output, not a shrug: whatever it hands to the judge, and whatever it flags as not settleable by procedure.
