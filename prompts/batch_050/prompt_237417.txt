My brother-in-law did a masters in machine learning and now holds court at every family gathering about AI risk, which he thinks is overblown. Last week he gave me his summary of the alignment problem and I want to know whether it's actually fair, because it felt a bit too convenient. His version went roughly like this:

"The alignment problem is just the problem of getting an AI system to do what its operators intend instead of something else. People freak out about scenarios where you tell a superintelligence to make paperclips and it converts the planet into paperclips, but notice that in that story the mistake is entirely on the human side: someone wrote down a stupid objective. So alignment is at bottom a specification problem. Write the objective carefully, test the system thoroughly before deployment, and you've solved it in principle. That's engineering, not some deep philosophical mystery. The actually hard question is who gets to decide what these systems are for -- which companies, which governments, with what oversight. That's a politics and governance question, and it's where the real risk lives. Alignment researchers who treat the whole thing as an unsolved scientific puzzle are mostly overcomplicating a combination of QA and institutional design."

Some of this sounds right to me (surely the 'does what its operators intend' bit is close to the standard framing, and surely the governance stuff matters), but I have a nagging feeling the paperclip example is doing different work in the original arguments than he says it is, and that 'test it thoroughly' is handwaving past something. But I can't put my finger on what.

So: which parts of his summary would people who actually work on alignment accept as accurate, which parts are common simplifications, and which parts are misrepresentations -- e.g. is he collapsing two distinct problems into one anywhere, or assuming something in 'write the objective carefully' that's actually the whole difficulty? And once you correct the misrepresentations, does his conclusion (that it's engineering plus politics, not a deep open problem) still follow, or does it depend on the sloppy version? I'd also take a verdict on whether his overall position is at least one that serious people hold, even if his argument for it is bad.
