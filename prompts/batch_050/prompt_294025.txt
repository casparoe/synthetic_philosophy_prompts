I'm drafting a position statement for a graduate seminar on Lewis's account of convention and the game-theoretic literature that grew out of it, and before I circulate it I'd like a consistency audit. Here is where I've landed:

(1) Lewis's analysis of convention is essentially correct as a definition, and I want to keep it strict: a regularity only counts as a convention if there is common knowledge in the population of the regularity itself, of the underlying coordination problem, and of everyone's conditional preference for general conformity. Regularities lacking that epistemic backing are at best proto-conventions.

(2) The signaling systems that emerge in Lewis's sender-receiver problems, and especially in Skyrms-style evolutionary models where replicator dynamics or simple reinforcement learning drive populations of agents with no beliefs about one another to a signaling equilibrium, are genuine conventions — and the evolutionary story is the right explanation of why conventions exist at all.

(3) Conventions are self-enforcing: once established, unilateral deviation leaves the deviator worse off, which is why they persist without sanctions. This is fully compatible with many actual conventions being inefficient or unfair — people often conform while wishing things were otherwise.

(4) Conventions are normatively inert in themselves. The only force a convention has is each agent's conditional preference for conforming given that others do; nothing about a convention grounds obligation, blame, or duty.

(5) Some conventional arrangements — for instance, the convention of splitting jointly produced but contested resources equally — are genuine social norms: people experience an obligation to conform, condemn defectors, and I take that normative phenomenology to be veridical rather than a projection of mere expectations.

(6) Conventions are arbitrary: for any established convention, an alternative regularity would have served the relevant ends just as well. At the same time, I think salience (Schelling-style focal points) is an objective feature of choice situations and explains which candidate convention actually gets selected.

Which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, and which pairs only appear to? Please rank any real tensions by severity, state precisely what would have to be given up to resolve each one, and identify the single revision that restores the most coherence at the lowest cost. I don't want everything flagged equally — if an apparent conflict dissolves once the commitments are stated carefully, say so rather than manufacturing a dilemma.
