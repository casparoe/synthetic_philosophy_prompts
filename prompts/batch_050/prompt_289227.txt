I keep finding myself with strong but seemingly inconsistent intuitions about when it is acceptable to generalize about peoples, and I would like to know whether any coherent principle lies behind them — or, failing that, which of my intuitions I should abandon. Here are my verdicts on six cases:

(1) A demographer reports, on the basis of large-scale survey data, that average interpersonal trust is substantially higher in Denmark than in Brazil, and uses this in cross-country research on institutional performance. This strikes me as unobjectionable — perhaps even valuable.

(2) A travel guide advises readers that the French are aloof and that one should not expect warmth from shopkeepers. This strikes me as objectionable, even though it presumably rests, at some remove, on the same kind of aggregate evidence as (1).

(3) A political economist explains persistent differences in pension and elder-care design across Southern and Northern Europe partly by appeal to differing cultural norms of familism versus individualism. This strikes me as legitimate scholarship, despite being an explanatory use of "national character" in all but name.

(4) A manager, having read that Japanese business culture discourages open disagreement, assumes that a new Japanese hire will not push back in meetings and stops soliciting her views. This strikes me as clearly objectionable — yet if the base rates invoked in (3) are real, the manager's expectation is presumably rational in the narrow statistical sense.

(5) A historian explains Germany's role in the First World War partly by reference to features of the German national character. This strikes me as illegitimate in a way that (3) is not, though I struggle to say why: both explain collective behavior by appeal to stable dispositions of a population.

(6) A guest at a dinner party remarks, affectionately and without evidence, that the Swiss are punctual. This strikes me as harmless, even though its logical form — an unhedged disposition attributed to a people — is one I would call prejudice if the content were less flattering or the group less privileged.

My tentative principle was that generalizations about peoples are acceptable only when they are explicitly statistical, evidentially grounded, and never applied to infer anything about an identifiable individual. But this principle both condemns (6), which I don't want to condemn, and seems not to explain my discomfort with (2) and (5), which could in principle be reformulated to satisfy it.

So, my questions: Is there a principled account — perhaps turning on the direction of inference (population-level versus individual-level), the kind of predicate involved (behavioral tendency versus characterological essence), the explanatory depth claimed, or the stakes of the use — that captures most of these verdicts? Please state the principle (or two or three candidate principles) precisely, indicate which of my cases motivates each clause, and test it against two further cases I haven't resolved: an insurer pricing policies using regional-level behavioral data, and a clinician who, knowing that a patient's culture stigmatizes mental illness, adjusts how she broaches a diagnosis. Finally, if no reasonably simple principle accommodates all six verdicts, tell me plainly which verdicts you think have to go, and why those rather than others. I am not interested in a survey of the literature or in citations — I want the principle, its costs, and an honest account of where it bites.
