At a memorial dinner for our colleague Daniel, two friends of mine fell into an argument about whether anything of him genuinely survives. (1) Mara said that it does: drawing on Douglas Hofstadter's view that a self is a pattern rather than a substance, she argued that the years Daniel spent shaping his close friends copied that pattern into them, so a coarse-grained version of him literally goes on existing in their minds -- partial survival, but real survival, like software running at lower resolution on new hardware. (2) Jonas replied that this confuses information about a person with the person: a blurry copy isn't Daniel, and by Mara's standard Napoleon is partly alive in everyone who's read his biography, which trivializes survival into meaninglessness. (3) Mara answered that degree is exactly the point -- the pattern in a spouse or a closest friend is orders of magnitude richer than anything a biography transmits, so the objection proves too much; and partial persistence of the pattern is still persistence, just as a surviving fragment of a manuscript is still that manuscript. (4) Jonas's final move was to grant the pattern talk but deny that it matters: even a perfect copy instantiated in someone else's brain would have no subject, no one inside having experiences, so what survives is data, not a self -- and grieving people comforted by Mara's picture are being consoled by a category mistake. They asked me to referee and I couldn't settle it. Please go through the four moves one by one: for each, say whether it's sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and why; then say who is more right overall, and identify anything important that both of them are missing.
