Last week a friend and I got into a long, unresolved argument that began with a throwaway remark of his: that a reinforcement learning agent being penalized is "literally suffering, just a little." I pushed back, he pushed harder, and by the end we had uncovered that we didn't even agree on what the question was. He kept sliding between the scalar reward in the algorithm's update rule, the learned value function, and the reward prediction error, as though these were one thing; I kept sliding between "the agent can't suffer" and "nothing it is like to be the agent," as though those were one thing. We parted without even a map of where we disagreed. I now run a small reading group on minds and machines, and I want to turn that muddle into something useful: please write a decision tree that takes a participant from an unreflective starting point -- say, the claim that reward in RL is just a number used to adjust behavior -- to a named position on whether RL agents can suffer, i.e., whether their reward signals could carry genuine positive or negative valence.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names: first the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the actual positions located in that space. Each branch node should be a question someone could answer without knowing where it leads -- phrased, where possible, around a concrete case rather than an abstract commitment. Cases I'd like used at least somewhere on the tree: a tabular Q-learning agent with no self-model; a deep RL agent whose reward is entirely sparse and externally imposed; an agent whose "rewards" are generated by its own learned internal model; and a language model fine-tuned with RLHF that verbally reports distress. Good branch candidates include whether suffering requires consciousness at all, whether consciousness (or valence specifically) depends on a particular biological substrate or is substrate-independent, whether valence is constituted by functional role or only correlated with it, whether a persistent self-model or body is required, and whether mere verbal report of suffering is evidence for or against its presence. Sensible leaves include: functionalism about valence (reward really can be suffering, given the right functional organization); biological naturalism (only certain wetware can suffer, whatever it computes); illusionism or eliminativism (no one suffers in the relevant sense, so the question dissolves); a two-factor view separating control signals from experienced valence; and a principled agnosticism that nonetheless prescribes caution. Feel free to add leaves I haven't thought of and to rename mine.

A few format requests. Aim for eight to twelve branch nodes and six to nine leaves. Each leaf should carry the position's name (or "unnamed, but occupied" / "unnamed and unoccupied" as appropriate), one sentence on what the view is committed to that makes people uncomfortable, and -- for occupied leaves -- a gesture at the tradition it belongs to without turning the tree into a bibliography. Mark any combinations of answers that no one defends, and briefly diagnose each: incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored. Flag any pair of positions the tree cannot really separate (I suspect, for instance, that some versions of functionalism and some versions of the two-factor view will collapse into each other on any behavioral question I can pose). Finally, end with a short note -- a paragraph at most -- telling me where the tree bottoms out in empirical questions we currently cannot answer, so the reading group knows which branches are philosophy and which are waiting on neuroscience or interpretability research.
