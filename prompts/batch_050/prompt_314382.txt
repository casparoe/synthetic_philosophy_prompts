Goodman defines 'grue' as follows: an object is grue if it is examined before some future time t and is green, or examined after t and is blue. Every emerald observed so far has been examined before t and found green, so all of our evidence confirms 'all emeralds are grue' just as well as it confirms 'all emeralds are green.' Yet the two hypotheses give opposite predictions about emeralds first examined after t: the green hypothesis says they will be green, the grue hypothesis says they will have turned blue. So the problem is not whether induction is justified at all, but which hypotheses our evidence actually supports. Hume's problem was to justify the inference from observed to unobserved in the first place, and Goodman thinks that problem cannot be solved, since any justification of induction would itself have to be inductive and therefore circular. Goodman's own solution is that 'green' is projectible and 'grue' is not, because 'green' is the simpler and more natural predicate, whereas 'grue' is gerrymandered and disjunctive. I think this succeeds, since it explains why we in fact reason with 'green' rather than 'grue,' and it shows that the new riddle does not threaten induction in general but only tells us to prefer simpler hypotheses.
