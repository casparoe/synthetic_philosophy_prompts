After forty years in experimental science -- I spent most of them running drug metabolism studies for a pharmaceutical company, so I have sat on my share of protocol review meetings -- I have retired and, to the amusement of my former colleagues, developed a serious interest in philosophy. I now convene a monthly reading group of about a dozen retired researchers, physicians, and one very patient bioethicist, and we have spent the autumn on the ethics of randomized controlled trials. What has struck me, coming at this literature as an outsider, is that the participants in this debate seem to talk past one another in a particular way: everyone invokes "equipoise," but the word plainly does different work for different authors, and some (Miller and Brody come to mind) argue the whole notion should be retired in favor of something like non-exploitation, while others defend a patient-level version closer to Fried's original idea, and still others Freedman's community-level "clinical equipoise." I would like to give my group a decision tree that sorts these positions by what their defenders actually accept about cases, rather than by which slogan they prefer.

So: please write me a decision tree whose root is something like "a randomized trial is proposed" and whose leaves are the named positions on when such a trial is ethically permissible -- at minimum the strict therapeutic-equipoise view, Freedman-style clinical equipoise, the so-called uncertainty principle, the Miller-Brody rejection of equipoise as the wrong framework altogether, and whatever position governs the permissibility of placebo controls when an effective standard treatment already exists. Each branch node must be phrased as a question about a concrete trial design that a scientifically literate reader could answer without already knowing which position it funnels them toward. Please use, among your nodes, at least these cases, since they are the ones that generated the heat in our meetings: a sham-surgery control arm for a procedure already in widespread use; a placebo-controlled trial of a new antihypertensive when cheap effective drugs exist; a first-in-human Phase I dose-escalation study in oncology, where nobody pretends the doses are chosen for the subjects' benefit; and a trial conducted in a low-income country where the standard of care locally available is nothing at all. Keep the tree to at most seven or eight branching questions so it fits on a single handout page. Each leaf should carry the position's name (or "unnamed" if you judge that nobody actually defends that combination) plus one sentence stating its most awkward commitment -- the case where an adherent has to bite a bullet. Where the logical space contains combinations no one occupies, say briefly whether you take the combination to be incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored, and if two leaves turn out to be separable only by questions my tree cannot ask, flag that honestly rather than forcing a split.

Two further requests. First, I would rather you push back than comply smoothly: if my premise is confused -- for instance, if these are not really rival answers to one question but answers to different questions that a single tree will only obscure, or if one of my four cases does not actually discriminate between any of the positions -- say so plainly and tell me how you would restructure the assignment. I have been on the receiving end of too many polite protocol reviews to want another. Second, the group includes several retired clinicians who ran trials and are touchy about being told their life's work rested on a muddle, so where a leaf implies that a common historical practice was unethical, note that at the leaf; I intend to distribute the tree before the meeting, and forewarned is forearmed.
