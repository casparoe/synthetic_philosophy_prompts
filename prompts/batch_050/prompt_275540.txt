A friend who has been reading about predictive processing claims the theory refutes itself. (1) On the predictive processing picture, what we perceive is the brain's best hypothesis about the hidden causes of its sensory input; perception is a kind of controlled hallucination. (2) The only check the brain has on a hypothesis is the prediction error it generates, since there is no further access to the world against which the hypothesis could be directly compared. (3) Whenever a hypothesis generates prediction error, the brain has two options: revise the hypothesis, or explain the error away by adjusting lower-level predictions and down-weighting the precision of the error signal. (4) Nothing in the theory says the second option is ever unavailable, so any hypothesis can in principle be maintained against any sensory stream. (5) So on this picture there is no real difference between a hypothesis that gets the world right and one that is merely shielded from error; perception and hallucination differ at most in how much explaining away they require. (6) But there is a plain difference between seeing a cat in front of me and hallucinating one, and a theory of perception that cannot mark that difference is false. (7) Therefore predictive processing, at least as usually stated, cannot be right. He treats (6) as untouchable, and I agree I would rather give up almost anything else; but the theory earns its keep explaining real phenomena, so I suspect the damage is done somewhere between (2) and (5). Tell me which step you reject, why it looked fine, and what rejecting it costs the predictive processing theorist, including what account of the perception–hallucination line remains available to them.
