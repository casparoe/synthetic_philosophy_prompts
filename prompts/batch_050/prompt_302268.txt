I'm working on a piece about philosophers everyone gets wrong, and a producer sent me this gloss of Berkeley's idealism that he got from a podcast, and I want to know if its fair before I put anything like it in the article:

'Berkeley's basic move is skeptical: since all we ever have access to is our own ideas, we can never know whether a material world exists outside our minds, so for all we know there's nothing out there. His famous master argument seals the deal: try to conceive of a tree existing while nobody perceives it -- you can't, because in conceiving it you are perceiving it. This proves that matter doesn't exist. The catch is that this means objects should blink out of existence whenever we stop looking, so Berkeley has to patch the theory by bringing in God, who perceives everything constantly and keeps the world running. Critics generally regard the God bit as an ad hoc fix bolted onto an otherwise skeptical argument.'

Which parts of that would a sympathetic reader of Berkeley accept, and which would they push back on? I have a hunch the skeptical framing is off but I can't articulate why. Also, does the master argument really prove what the summary says it proves, and if not, what does it actually establish? And if the argument has a flaw, does the 'ad hoc God' conclusion in the last sentence still stand or does it fall with the rest?
