"In the Hart–Devlin debate, explain Devlin's disintegration thesis and Hart's response to it. Does Hart successfully answer Devlin's worry, and why or why not?"

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

---
The Hart–Devlin debate was sparked by the Wolfenden Report (1957), which recommended that homosexual acts between consenting adults in private be decriminalized. Devlin, in his Maccabaean lecture, pushed back on the basis of what is now called the disintegration thesis. Society, he argued, is not just a collection of individuals but a community held together by a shared morality; if that shared morality erodes, society itself starts to come apart, just as surely as if treason went unpunished. So the law is entitled to enforce society's moral code even against private acts that harm no one else. His test for what counts as society's morality was the judgement of the ordinary right-minded person, roughly the man on the Clapham omnibus.

Hart replied that Devlin had smuggled in an empirical claim without any evidence: nothing shows that private immorality, or a shift in sexual morality, actually threatens a society's existence. Drawing on Mill, Hart argued that the only legitimate reason for the state to coerce anyone is to prevent harm to others, and mere offence or disgust doesn't count as harm. He also pressed the point that Devlin's view would let a majority criminalize whatever it happens to feel strongly about, since the right-minded man standard is really just majority prejudice dressed up. Hart's conclusion follows from his legal positivism: because law and morality are conceptually separate, and the validity of a law never depends on its moral content, morality can therefore never be a legitimate ground for legislation at all. This is where he parts company most sharply with Devlin.

On balance I think Hart wins the exchange. Since feelings of disgust have historically been unreliable (they were directed at interracial couples, for example), it follows that no shared moral consensus, however deep, could ever justify a criminal law.
