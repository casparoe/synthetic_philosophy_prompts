Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about whether historians -- and the rest of us -- can legitimately judge people in the past by standards that only became widespread later. She thinks any such judgment is anachronistic projection; I think some judgments of the dead are plainly right, and I tried to defend this by running through cases, but my verdicts don't obviously cohere and I'd like help finding out what principle, if any, underlies them. Here they are. A medieval physician who bled his feverish patients, thereby hastening some deaths: no moral blame whatsoever, though what he did was harmful. An eighteenth-century plantation owner who treated the people he enslaved relatively gently by the standards of his neighbors: still blameworthy, though less so than a cruel owner, and I'd say the same even if his conscience was genuinely untroubled. A shopkeeper in 1955 who casually denied service based on a customer's ethnicity, as most of his peers did: blameworthy, and I notice I feel this more strongly than the plantation case, though I can't articulate why since both men had contemporaries who dissented. A Roman citizen of ordinary decency who attended gladiatorial games and enjoyed them: I flinch at him and want to call him blameworthy, even though virtually no one around him objected -- and this is the verdict that sits worst with my others, because if availability of dissenting views is what matters, he should be the most excused, not the most condemned. An abolitionist pamphleteer in the 1820s: deeply praiseworthy, more praiseworthy than someone holding identical views today, precisely because of what it cost her to arrive at them. And finally, a present-day friend of mine who eats factory-farmed meat without a second thought: I find myself unable to blame him even while suspecting that people in 2150 will judge him as I judge the shopkeeper.

Is there a single principle that captures this pattern, or at least most of it? My friend's candidate was that blame requires that the person could have known better given the epistemic resources of their time, but that seems to excuse the Roman, exculpate the shopkeeper more than I want, and it leaves the asymmetry between moral failings (which I do blame across centuries) and intellectual failings like the physician's (which I don't) completely unexplained. I'd like you to state a principle -- or two or three rival candidates -- precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing the work for which case, and to be explicit about which of my six verdicts each candidate forces me to abandon. Then tell me what your preferred principle predicts for two cases I haven't considered: a seventeenth-century scholar who defended a view he privately doubted because the alternative meant execution, and a person today who holds a position that is currently defended by serious, widely read dissenters but is held by a large majority of her peers. Finally, if you think one of my verdicts is simply inconsistent with the others no matter how the principle is tuned, say which one you'd discard and why.
