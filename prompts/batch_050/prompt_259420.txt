Here's the student's answer, verbatim: "Goodman defines 'grue' as follows: an object is grue iff it is examined before some fixed time t (say, the year 2100) and is green, or is not examined before t and is blue. Every emerald examined so far has been green, and therefore also grue, so the entire body of evidence we currently possess confirms both 'all emeralds are green' and 'all emeralds are grue.' Yet these hypotheses yield contradictory predictions about emeralds first examined after t. This is the new riddle: given that the evidence supports both equally, which hypothesis does it really support? Hume's problem was that no justification, deductive or inductive, can be given for expecting the future to resemble the past. Goodman's riddle arises even if we grant Hume that much: it shows that the bare idea of 'the future resembling the past' is empty until we say in what respects, since any observed sequence resembles the future in some respects and not others. The natural reply is that grue is a gerrymandered predicate, defined by reference to a particular time, whereas green is purely qualitative. Goodman himself endorses this reply: on his view, the artificial, time-indexed character of grue is exactly what disqualifies it, and that is why green is projectible and grue is not. At bottom, the example is really an instance of the underdetermination of theory by evidence: the two hypotheses are empirically equivalent, so the choice between them is pragmatic rather than evidential, much as Quine describes. In practice, of course, we project green rather than grue, and since our actual inductive practice is all we have to go on, that settles the matter."
