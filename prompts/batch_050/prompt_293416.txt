I chair the ethics review panel of a large regional hospital trust, and I need your help turning the philosophical category of supererogation into something my panel can actually use. We are a seven-person body — one clinician, one nurse, one lawyer, one patient representative, one HR officer, one administrator, and me — and none of us has philosophical training. About twice a month we receive what I've started calling "appeals to goodwill": proposals from department heads or senior clinicians that ask staff to do something beyond their contractual and professional obligations. Recent examples: asking nurses to volunteer for shifts on a new infectious-disease ward; asking a well-regarded senior physician to take on an unpaid mentoring load for struggling trainees; asking staff to donate vacation days to a colleague with a gravely ill child; and asking a particular nurse, who has informally covered gaps in the rota for over a year, to keep doing so now that we have a budget shortfall.

The problem my panel keeps hitting is this. Nobody disputes that these acts are good when freely done. The disputes are about whether asking for them is legitimate, whether repeated past willingness makes refusal blameworthy now, and whether honoring the people who do these things (awards, mentions in the newsletter) quietly converts heroism into expectation — what one of our panel members calls the ratchet problem, where each voluntary act raises the baseline for everyone else. These are exactly the issues the philosophical literature on supererogation, moral options, imperfect duties, and the praise–blame asymmetry is about, but that literature is written for philosophers and my panel meets for ninety minutes.

Please write a decision procedure the panel could run on any such proposal. Concretely, I want: (1) an ordered list of questions to ask about a proposal, where each question is answerable by a non-philosopher on the basis of facts a department head could be required to supply; (2) for each question, what counts as a sufficient answer and what answer stops the procedure with a rejection; (3) an explicit rule for how the procedure treats a request differently depending on whether the person has done the act before, since that is where the ratchet problem bites; (4) an explicit rule about publicity and honors, since my instinct is to celebrate the nurse but I suspect the procedure will tell me that's part of the problem; (5) the two or three points in the procedure where judgment cannot be eliminated and the panel must simply deliberate, flagged as such, with a sentence about why no rule can settle them.

Then run the finished procedure on three cases: the vacation-day donation request; the request to formalize the nurse's informal rota coverage; and a request to ask a young surgeon to serve as an anonymous living kidney donor for a long-time patient. At least one of these should come out in a way I'll find uncomfortable — I'd rather know that now than discover it in a meeting.

Finally, add a short section on what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of proposal it will misclassify or mishandle, and which philosophical distinctions you had to flatten to make it usable at a committee table (for instance, whether collapsing "beyond duty" into "beyond contract" loses anything that matters), so the panel knows where its decisions rest on judgment rather than on the procedure.
