Two senior devs at my company got into a real argument last week and it's been bugging me. Context: there's this weird validation check in our signup flow that's been there forever. Nobody knows what it's for, there's no comment or doc, and it rejects a small number of legit signups every month.

Dev A wants to just delete it. Her argument: code whose purpose nobody on the team can explain is tech debt, full stop. We have good test coverage and monitoring, so if it was load-bearing we'll find out fast, and we can roll back. She said keeping it is "appeasement of ghosts."

Dev B pulled out Chesterton's fence -- don't tear down a fence until you know why someone built it. He said the check was presumably added in response to *something*, maybe a fraud wave or an abuse pattern years ago, and the absence of docs means we lost the knowledge, not that there was no reason.

Dev A came back with what I thought was a strong reply: his rule is unfalsifiable. You can never *prove* a piece of code had no reason, so "figure out why it exists first" gives a permanent veto to dead engineers who left no notes. Also she said the fence analogy is rigged -- a fence on a road is a pain to rebuild, but we have version control, deletion is reversible, so the asymmetry the fence metaphor depends on isn't there.

Dev B's counter: rollback isn't actually cheap here. If that check is blocking some signup fraud that only spikes occasionally, the damage happens in the gap before monitoring flags it, and chargebacks don't un-happen when you git revert. And he said her "we can just look at git history" point is exactly the problem -- the reason was never in the repo, it was in someone's head in 2016.

Can you go through this move by move and tell me who's right at each step? Not just who wins overall -- I want to know where each of them is overreaching, and whether the fence principle even applies when the fence is software and deletion is (mostly) reversible.
