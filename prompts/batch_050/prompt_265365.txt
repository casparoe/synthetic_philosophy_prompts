I am a doctoral candidate in political theory, currently writing a dissertation chapter on the ethics of torture, and I want to settle what I actually believe by attending to my considered verdicts on particular cases rather than beginning from a theory. Below are the cases and the verdicts I find myself holding, followed by a tentative principle of my own. What I want from you is a test of that principle against the cases, a repair of it if repair is possible, and an honest account of which of my verdicts survive.

The cases:

(1) Security services have captured a man who planted a powerful conventional bomb somewhere in a city. It will detonate within hours and kill several thousand people. The interrogators are certain he planted it, certain he alone knows its location, and as certain as such things allow that he will not talk under ordinary questioning but will under torture. My verdict: torturing him is permissible. I think the official ought to do it and then accept whatever legal consequences follow.

(2) The same scenario, except the man is hardened and will not break under torture himself. He would break if his six-year-old daughter were tortured in the adjoining room where he could hear her. My verdict: impermissible, at identical stakes. I hold this nearly as firmly as (1), and I recognize that the pair is fatal to any purely aggregative principle.

(3) The bomb-maker dies in custody before talking. The only other person who knows the location is a bystander who overheard a phone call and, from a mixture of fear and scruple, refuses to say what he heard. He is innocent of everything. My verdict: this is the case I keep going back and forth on. I find myself saying that at the stakes of (1), torturing him is permissible, but I am uncomfortable with the verdict, and I cannot yet articulate what separates him from the daughter in (2).

(4) Ten detainees are held, exactly one of whom planted the bomb; the evidence does not distinguish among them, and any of them would talk under torture. Torturing all ten in sequence would certainly yield the location in time. My verdict: impermissible. I note, however, that torturing the group means torturing nine innocents in expectation, and I am unsure this verdict survives contact with whatever justifies (1).

(5) Identical to (1), except the bomb will kill exactly one person. My verdict: not permissible. So whatever I am, I am not an absolutist whose prohibition is stake-insensitive; there is some threshold at work.

(6) Identical to (2), except the device is nuclear and will kill a million people. My verdict, reported honestly: my refusal of the daughter case weakens here, and I am no longer confident it is impermissible. I record this as an instability in my own responses, not as a settled judgment.

(7) Even in the cases where I judge the act permissible, I hold that the state should neither issue torture warrants nor enact any standing ex ante justification; the official should act illegally, then face trial and plead necessity after the fact. My verdict: hold the line on ex ante prohibition, even at the price of hypocrisy the state would rather avoid.

My tentative principle: torture is permissible only when (i) the person tortured is liable to it in virtue of responsibility for the threat, and (ii) the evil averted clears a high threshold; torture of the non-liable is never permissible at any stakes; and even permissible torture may never be institutionalized in advance.

This handles (1), (2), (5), and (7), but it plainly fails my wavering verdict in (3), and (6) embarrasses the absolute clause. One repair I can see but cannot evaluate is a distinction between inflicting pain to move the will of the person tortured and inflicting pain on one person in order to move the will of another; this would separate (2) from (3) in a way my principle cannot, though I suspect it proves too much. What I want from you: either a precisely stated repaired principle, clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause; or a simpler principle plus a defense of which of my verdicts I should abandon as the price of it. Then tell me what your preferred principle predicts for three cases I have not listed: (a) torturing someone who is causally but not morally responsible for the threat, such as a courier who unknowingly transported the device and now refuses to say where he left it; (b) torture that will not itself work but whose credible threat would; and (c) torturing one person to prevent five other people from being tortured by someone else. Finally, state plainly which of my verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate, rather than complicating the principle to save them all.
