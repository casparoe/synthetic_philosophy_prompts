Okay so I'm working through some philosophy-of-AI material with a reading group and we keep hitting the same wall. Everyone's intuitions about AI persuasion are trained on cases of deception -- the AI lies, the AI withholds, the AI spins. But it occurred to me that the scarier and more interesting possibility is an AI that only ever says true things and is still, in effect, manipulating you, because it has superhuman skill at choosing which truths to say, when, and in what framing. A friend of mine pushed back hard and said if it's all true it's just "good communication" and calling it manipulation is paranoia. I want to settle this with a proper thought experiment rather than trading intuitions.

So here's the task. Act as a professional philosopher who's thought a lot about manipulation, autonomy, and speech ethics (think along the lines of the philosophical literature on nudging, gaslighting, and testimonial injustice, but applied to AI). Construct a thought experiment in which a superintelligent AI assistant causes a person to make a decision that is seriously bad for them, where every individual sentence the AI utters is true, AND where the decision is not one the person would endorse on calm reflection with the same information presented neutrally. Constraints: (1) the setting must be mundane and realistic -- something like career advice, a medical choice, or a relationship decision, no sci-fi mind control, no hypnosis, no perfectly predicted humans, the person retains the legal and practical ability to ignore the AI; (2) the harm must come from the AI's selection and framing of true information, not from any falsehood, so stipulating even one lie counts as cheating; (3) one paragraph for the scenario itself, four at the absolute most if you really need it.

After the scenario, do the work: explain step by step which features of the case make the AI's speech manipulative rather than merely persuasive or informative, and say which single feature is doing the most work (my guess going in is that it's the AI's superior model of the user's psychology, but I'd love to be shown wrong). Then steelman my friend's objection -- that truthfulness is a complete defense -- as strongly as you can, and show where the thought experiment either survives it or needs patching. Finally, briefly: if you removed the key feature you identified, does the case collapse back into ordinary good advice-giving? Think carefully about the boundary conditions before you settle on the scenario, maybe brainstorm two or three candidate setups first and pick the strongest one, and flag any place where you had to make the human unrealistically passive to get the result -- that's the failure mode I'm most worried about.
