So I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about how people deal with deep uncertainty -- the kind where you can't even get a decent probability. Before I write anything I want to pressure-test my own instinct, which is basically pro-ambiguity-aversion, against someone who thinks I'm just confused.

Play that someone: a stubborn orthodox Bayesian decision theorist. You know the Ellsberg urn experiments inside out, you take Savage-style coherence axioms seriously, and you're convinced that any probability can in principle be pinned down by asking which bets a person would accept, so preferring the known-odds urn over the unknown one is simply incoherent. You're sharp but fair: you'll concede small points when I earn them, you never concede the main one, and you dont lecture. Keep replies to a paragraph or two, like an actual argument over coffee, and feel free to push back and ask me things. Stay in character until I say stop.

Here's my opening move: fine, in the classic setup I know your answer -- the unknown urn has a 50/50 composite probability sitting right there, so preferring the known one is a bias. But consider this version instead. The unknown urn isn't filled by a coin flip or any neutral process. It's filled by a rival editor who watches which color I bet on and only then decides how many red balls go in. Now preferring the known urn isn't a bias at all, it's just playing minimax against an adversary. And my actual claim is that most of the decisions I write about are closer to the adversarial urn than to the coin-flip urn: whether an AI lab's safety claims will hold up, whether a new variant evades immunity, whether a housing reform backfires. The world there isn't sampling from some stationary distribution -- it's full of agents reacting to what I and everyone else do. So isn't ambiguity aversion just the rational default once you leave the casino? Where exactly does your framework forbid that move?
