I spent about forty years as an experimental physicist and only got into philosophy after retiring, so bear with me if this is old hat to you people. The Libet experiments have been nagging at me — the ones where brain activity supposedly predicts a "decision" to flex your wrist before you feel you've decided.

Here's a thought I can't place, maybe it's been done: in the original setup the decision is totally arbitrary. Nothing rides on when you flex. But what if freedom, if it's anywhere, lives in decisions where you actually weigh reasons? So imagine a variant: subjects have a fixed window to decide whether to accept a bet whose expected value is just barely positive — they have to genuinely crunch it. Suppose a readiness-potential-style signal still predicts their choice seconds before they report deciding. Does that threaten free will in a way the wrist-flexing never did? And suppose instead the prediction falls apart for reasoned decisions — would that show Libet told us basically nothing about the decisions we care about, like whether to take a job or leave a marriage?

Write me an essay on whether the Libet-style results actually bear on free will for decisions that matter, using this experiment-that-doesn't-exist (as far as I know) as your lens. Take a side — I've had enough of philosphers ending with "it's complicated." And since I'm new at this: also tell me what result or argument would change your verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fall apart.
