I’m secretary of the grants committee for a midsize family foundation, and we have to vote Friday on whether to fund a pilot called the Threshold Fund. A nonprofit wants to offer adults in their first year after treatment for opioid-use disorder $500 for choosing an IUD or contraceptive implant, plus $100 every three months for 18 months if they keep it. Participation would be voluntary, removal would be free at any time, and the program would also pay for counseling and transportation. The controversial part is the nonprofit’s stated rationale: it says there is a moral asymmetry between creating and declining to create a child. Starting a life likely to contain serious, uncompensated suffering can be wrong even though declining to start a happy life is not equally wrong, because in the second case no existing child is deprived of happiness. The applicant explicitly says the program should not be understood as trying to increase the number of “worthwhile” births, only as reducing predictable harm. Half our committee thinks this is respectful harm prevention for adults making their own choices; the other half thinks offering repeated cash payments to financially stressed people in recovery is coercive, stigmatizing, and uncomfortably close to deciding whose children ought not exist.

During discussion someone brought up Arthur Schopenhauer’s essay “On the Sufferings of the World,” where he says: “Evil is just what is positive; it makes its own existence felt. ... It is the good which is negative; in other words, happiness and satisfaction always imply some desire fulfilled, some state of pain brought to an end.” Please draft the committee’s decision memo recommending approval, rejection, or approval only under specified conditions. The memo should have a clear bottom line, explain whether Schopenhauer’s claim really supports the proposed reproductive asymmetry, and address whether preventing suffering can count as a benefit when the child who would otherwise exist never comes into being. It should also distinguish genuine voluntariness from pressure, consider whether the payment structure targets poverty rather than risk to a future child, and propose workable safeguards if you recommend approval. Give the strongest version of the losing side, not a caricature, and make the objections specific enough that the nonprofit could actually revise its proposal in response. No footnotes needed — this is for a committee vote, not an academic journal, and alot of us are not philosophers.
