My nephew, who is sixteen, recently announced that eating meat from factory farms is morally wrong. When I asked him for his reasons, he admitted he could no longer reproduce the argument that had convinced him: a teacher he respects walked the class through it several months ago, he found it compelling at the time, and the conclusion stuck even though the steps did not. I told him that he therefore does not actually have a reason for his belief, only someone else's conclusion on loan, and that forming moral convictions this way is just conformism. My wife thinks this is absurd. Nobody, she says, can reproduce the evidence that smoking causes cancer or that the Earth orbits the Sun, yet we do not accuse each other of conformism for believing those things. I replied that factual beliefs are different: somewhere down the line, experts actually checked, and the matter is settled, whereas on moral questions even the experts disagree. She answered that if expert disagreement disqualified secondhand belief, we would have to give up plenty of factual beliefs as well, and that a teenager trusting a thoughtful teacher is hardly an intellectual scandal. I also pointed out that in his case it is not even straightforward deference, since he once grasped the argument and has merely forgotten it; she said that makes his position better, not worse. I genuinely cannot tell whether there is a principled difference between the moral and factual cases here, or whether I am simply being hard on my nephew. Is there one? And if moral beliefs really should not rest on borrowed reasons, what should he do now: suspend judgment until he can rebuild the argument on his own?
