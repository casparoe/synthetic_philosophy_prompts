I'm putting together the final handout for a graduate seminar on moral responsibility, and the session on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument has me stuck. For completeness: the argument runs roughly that (1) what we intentionally do, we do because of the way we are, mentally speaking; (2) so if we are to be truly morally responsible for what we do, we must be truly responsible for the way we are, at least in the relevant mental respects; but (3) we cannot be truly responsible for the way we are, since that would require having intentionally brought it about, which would require a prior self whose character we were already responsible for, launching an impossible regress -- nothing can be causa sui in the required sense. My handout already catalogs the standard lines of resistance, and I tell students not to bother repeating them: the compatibilist rejection of premise 2, on which responsibility requires only that the action issue from one's own reasons-responsive mechanism, not that one be responsible for having that mechanism; Kane's libertarian proposal that undetermined self-forming actions make us partly responsible for our characters, enough to ground responsibility later; the charge that Strawson's notion of "true" responsibility builds in an impossibly strong standard of ultimate origination that no ordinary concept of responsibility ever demanded, so the argument defeats a target nobody was defending; the practice-based response, descended from P. F. Strawson, that concedes the failure of basic desert but relocates responsibility in the reactive attitudes and the practices of accountability they sustain; and the objection that the argument proves too much, since the same regress would undermine responsibility for our reasoning and belief-formation, leaving the arguer unable to claim rational credit for the argument itself.

What I want for the handout is one further objection that is genuinely distinct from these five -- not a rewording of any of them, not a special case, and not a hybrid assembled from pieces already on the list. It should engage the argument as stated rather than change the subject to whether life without basic desert would be tolerable. Please state the objection precisely, identify which premise or inferential step it puts pressure on, and then give me your honest assessment of its strength: in particular, would it move someone who has heard all five of the objections above and remained persuaded by Strawson, or does it covertly smuggle in one of the moves they have already rejected? If the best candidate you can produce turns out on inspection to be a variant of something on my list, I would rather you say that plainly than pass it off as new.
