I co-teach a second-year course on democracy and political authority, about 18 students, and I get one 75-minute session on the boundary problem -- the question of who gets to be part of the demos in the first place. The problem is that every year I explain it (you can't democratically decide who gets to vote, because you first have to decide who votes on that, and so on) and they nod along and write it down and clearly feel nothing. They treat it as a cute regress, like the liar paradox, not as something that actually bites real decisions. So design me an in-class activity or game where they run into it themselves before I name anything.

Here's what I'm picturing as raw material, but change whatever doesn't work: I'm imagining staging some kind of contested vote -- maybe the class has to decide something where it's genuinely unclear who counts as a stakeholder (e.g., a decision affecting next year's cohort, or a hypothetical merger/split where some students play residents and others play people who are affected by the outcome but not "members," like commuters or the neighboring class). The key thing I want is that at some point someone proposes expanding or restricting the franchise, and then the group has to decide THAT question by a vote, and someone should be able to ask "wait, who votes on this?" and the room should go quiet. I want them to generate the regress themselves, not have me point at it. Small stakes are good -- points toward a silly class leaderboard or chocolate or whatever -- as long as the stakes are distributed unequally enough that the franchise question actually matters to the outcome.

So: give me the full setup (roles, what the vote is about, how to rig it so the franchise dispute arises naturally rather than me forcing it, rules for what happens when they try to vote on the electorate itself), a rough timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the standard moves (all-affected vs. all-subjected principles, the regress, the "the people just decides itself" response and why it's circular). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong -- e.g., I suspect they'll try to solve it by unanimity or by appealing to my authority as the teacher -- and how to handle it. And be honest about what the activity can't capture, since a classroom game has an obvious answer to "who's in charge" (me) that real political communities don't have.
