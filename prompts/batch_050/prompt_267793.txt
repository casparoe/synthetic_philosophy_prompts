I am a PhD student in epistemology, and I help organize our department's graduate colloquium. Next month we are hosting a visiting philosopher whose new book defends a non-epistemic reading of the hinge commitments in Wittgenstein's On Certainty: on her view, the certainties that stand fast for us -- that I have two hands, that the earth existed long before I was born, that there is an external world -- are not beliefs, not knowledge, and not even propositions properly so called. They are arational, animal certainties that form the scaffolding within which doubting and justifying alone make sense; they are neither justified nor unjustified, any more than the rules of a game are moves within it. She argues further that hinges can change over time -- what was once bedrock can become an ordinary empirical claim and vice versa -- and that this explains how world-pictures evolve without anyone ever rationally revising the hinges themselves.

I need roughly ten interview questions for a forty-minute Q&A before an audience of faculty and graduate students who will have read the book's introduction. Please sequence the questions so the line of inquiry tightens rather than jumping between topics, and for each question, sketch the reply she is most likely to give, then provide my follow-up to that reply. In particular, I want the sequence to press on the following, in whatever order you judge most effective:

1. The status question: if hinges are not propositions, in what sense can they be inherited from a community, taught to children, or lost -- and does her view collapse into the epistemic readings (hinge commitments as a special kind of warranted belief or known presupposition) whenever it tries to answer this?
2. Disagreement: two communities with incompatible hinges -- say, ours and a community for whom the young age of the earth is itself bedrock. On her view, is this disagreement rationally resolvable even in principle, or are the parties merely talking past one another within different river-beds? If the latter, I want a follow-up that asks what entitles us to dismiss flat-earthers as wrong rather than simply as otherwise-hinged.
3. Hinge change: if hinges shift without being rationally revised, what does the work in a scientific revolution -- and does her account leave anything for reasons to do, or does it reduce intellectual history to a kind of drift?
4. The interpretive claim: whether this is Wittgenstein's view or a view built from his materials, and what textual or philosophical evidence could count against her reading specifically.

The questions should assume she has mastered the standard objections, so avoid anything she can answer by restating chapter one. I would like the final question to force her to identify the weakest point of her own view -- but phrased so that "I address that in chapter six" is not an available escape.
