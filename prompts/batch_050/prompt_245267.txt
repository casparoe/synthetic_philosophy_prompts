So I'm a novelist, three years and about 80,000 words into a book that I no longer believe in. The middle sags, the protagonist turned out boring, and a friend whose taste I trust read the draft and gently confirmed everything I was afraid of. I want to trunk it and start something new.

But before I do, I want you to talk me out of it. Play a character for me: a stubborn, genuinely smart defender of the idea that finishing is the right call -- not a cheerleader, someone who actually thinks the sunk cost fallacy is overapplied and that "you've put three years into it" can be a *rational* reason, not just a cognitive error. Give them real resources: stuff about self-trust and follow-through as a track record you're building, the option value of a finished (even mediocre) manuscript vs an abandoned one, what abandoning projects does to a writer's ability to finish anything, how my judgment of the draft's quality mid-despair might itself be unreliable. Let them concede points when I land a real hit -- if I show a reason is genuinely independent of the past investment, they should give it to me -- but they never fold entirely, and they don't do fake politness. First person, stay in character across turns, don't break the role unless I say "timeout."

My opening move, which your character has to actually deal with: whatever the manuscript is worth going forward, the three years are gone whether I finish or not. The only question is what the next six months buys me -- a finished bad novel or a good start on a new one. Isn't everything else just grief about the wasted time dressed up as strategy?

Also, when the character makes claims about how economists or decision theorists see this stuff, flag (briefly, in brackets is fine) what's actually settled -- like the basic fallacy -- versus what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, like whether honoring sunk costs can ever be rational. Don't let the character dress up a minority view as the consensus.
