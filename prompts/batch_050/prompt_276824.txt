I sit on the defence committee of the parliament of a small, prosperous, militarily non-aligned European state with a long tradition of armed neutrality. For over half a century, the country has maintained universal male conscription with a civilian-service alternative, on the stated rationale that a democracy's defence should be borne by its citizens rather than delegated to professionals. Two developments have unsettled this settlement. First, the armed forces now require only about a third of each age cohort, so selection for service has become de facto arbitrary: two-thirds of young men are exempted on grounds so fine-grained as to be meaningless, while the 'universality' of the system is now mostly rhetorical. Second, a recent constitutional ruling requires that any continued conscription be gender-neutral.

Three options are before the committee: (a) a gender-neutral lottery conscripting roughly one third of each cohort for full military service; (b) a universal but mostly non-military national service, in which everyone serves a short period — a small fraction in combat roles, the rest in civil protection, care work, or infrastructure; (c) abolition of conscription in favour of a professional force backed by a volunteer reserve, as most comparable states have done.

The committee has received two submissions it must reckon with. A retired chief of defence argues that the citizen-soldier link is the principal safeguard against both militarism and strategic frivolity: a society whose own children fill the ranks, he says, neither romanticises war nor treats defence as someone else's problem, and professional armies tend to drift away from the society they serve. A citizens' initiative counters that conscription is compulsory labour that a state which no longer needs it cannot justify, that a lottery merely institutionalises unequal treatment of equals, and that civic virtue produced under compulsion is not virtue at all. An academic submission adds a third idea the committee found harder to dismiss: a 'defence levy', under which those not called up pay a modest annual contribution to those who are, sharing the burden financially — but the submission's own author worries that this converts a civic relationship into a market transaction and thereby destroys precisely the good it was meant to preserve.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before our vote. It should recommend one of the three options, give the reasoning in terms a parliament can act on, and engage seriously with the strongest argument against the recommendation. In particular, it should address the question the submissions jointly raise: whether the civic goods claimed for conscription — solidarity, democratic control of the military, the seriousness that comes of having skin in the game — can survive a system in which only a minority actually serves, and whether they can be obtained without compulsion at all. Please write in formal British English.
