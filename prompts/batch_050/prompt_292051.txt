You are a philosopher of mathematics with particular expertise in the methodology of Imre Lakatos, and I want you to act as a careful case analyst rather than a textbook summariser. Think the material through before you write: first sketch for yourself the candidate mappings between theory and episode, note where the fit is strained, and only then compose the analysis.

The lens is the account of mathematical method Lakatos develops in Proofs and Refutations: the cycle of conjecture, proof, refutation and reproof; monster-barring; lemma incorporation; proof-generated concepts; and his broader claim that mathematics is a quasi-empirical discipline in which proofs are fallible instruments whose scrutiny improves the very statements they establish.

The episode is the history of the four-colour theorem. Take the salient facts to be these. The conjecture — that four colours suffice to colour any map drawn in the plane — was proposed in the mid-nineteenth century. In 1879 Alfred Kempe published a proof that was accepted by the mathematical community for over a decade, until Percy Heawood exhibited the flaw in 1890, salvaging a five-colour theorem from the wreckage of Kempe's argument. The conjecture then resisted solution for most of the twentieth century, generating substantial mathematics along the way, until Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken announced a proof in 1976 that reduced the problem to a finite but enormous set of configurations whose colourability was verified by computer — far more than any human could survey by hand. The reception was divided: some regarded the result as established, others as not proved in the only sense that matters, since no one could check the argument unaided. A fully machine-formalised version of the proof followed decades later.

Your task has three parts.

First, the mapping. Apply Lakatos's scheme to the episode with discipline: identify what plays the role of conjecture, of proof, of refutation and of monster. Where do you find monster-barring, lemma incorporation or proof-generated concepts in this history, if anywhere? Be explicit about what counts as the evidence, and do not force a fit where none exists.

Second, the test in both directions. Say what the Lakatosian account explains well here — I expect the Kempe–Heawood passage to reward the analysis — and what it misdescribes or is simply silent about. In particular, consider whether the Appel–Haken proof is the sort of object on which Lakatos's dialectic can operate at all: his machinery presupposes proofs that mathematicians can read, probe and attack with counterexamples, and it is not obvious that a machine-checked case enumeration is vulnerable to refutation in that way. Note also that the conjecture appears to have been proved exactly as stated, with no proof-generated revision of its content along the way; ask how much of an embarrassment that is for a theory on which proofs characteristically transform their theorems. Where the theory genuinely does not speak, say so rather than making it speak.

Whichever you choose, state what would have had to be different in the history for the verdict to have gone another way.
