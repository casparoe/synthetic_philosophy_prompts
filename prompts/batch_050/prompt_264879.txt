I host a small philosophy podcast and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends a strong suffering-focused view of ethics -- roughly, that preventing and relieving suffering has moral priority over creating happiness, and that at least some agonies can't be outweighed or "compensated" by any amount of pleasure. She's published on this for years and gives smooth answers, so I don't want questions that just invite her to restate the view.

Draft nine or ten interview questions that press where the position actually creaks. I want at least one question each on: the pinprick problem (does she really think one pinprick can outweigh a lifetime of bliss, and if she flinches there, what does the flinching cost the view?); the world-destruction objection (would it be good to painlessly end a flourishing world to preempt its future suffering?); whether she's committed to a lexical threshold or thinks value is all on one continuous scale, and what each horn costs her; the claim that pleasure is just the relief of a lack rather than something positive in its own right, since a lot of the view seems to rest on that picture of wellbeing; and the population-ethics fallout, i.e. whether the view implies it's wrong to have children or good to prevent happy lives from starting. For every question, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give -- I know she likes to say "I'm just taking seriously what we already believe in extreme cases," so build at least one follow-up that tests whether ordinary intuitions about extreme cases really do the work she needs.

At the end, add a short note for me as host separating what's basically settled ground in these debates from what's still genuinely contested among people who work on wellbeing and population ethics -- e.g., I don't want to accidentally treat the asymmetry intuition as consensus if it isn't, or treat the world-destruction objection as a knockdown if specialists mostly think it's answerable. Tell me where the live disagreement actually sits.
