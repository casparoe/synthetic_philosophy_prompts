I have been reading Montaigne's Essays in John Florio's 1603 translation and keeping a ledger of the verdicts he hands down on foreign or unfamiliar practices, because I cannot settle what standard he is actually applying. The ledger so far:

1. The Tupinambá of Brazil roast and eat the bodies of slain enemies: Montaigne declines to call this barbarous, and remarks that the greater barbarism lies with the European practice of tormenting and roasting men alive ("Of Cannibals").
2. The Spanish conquest of the New World: condemned without reserve as treachery and butchery, while the besieged natives' endurance is praised as surpassing anything in antiquity ("Of Coaches").
3. Judicial torture and refinements of execution at home: condemned; he would pare back even lawful punishments rather than invent new torments ("Of Conscience," "Of Cruelty").
4. Hunting: condemned — he says he can hardly bear to see a harmless beast taken and killed, and treats our dealings with animals as a proving ground for our dealings with men ("Of Cruelty").
5. The cannibals' wars: admired as noble, because they are fought for valor alone and not for land or spoils ("Of Cannibals").
6. The catalog of foreign customs in "Of Custom": offered largely with a shrug — custom governs everything, and each people calls barbarous whatever is not its own practice.

Early in "Of the Caniballes," before any of the famous comparisons, Montaigne writes: "See how a man ought to take heed, lest he over-weeningly follow vulgar opinions, which should be measured by the rule of reason, and not by the common report."

The next case for the ledger: a distant people among whom children put their aged parents to death, at the parents' own request, painlessly, in an open ceremony that everyone, the parents included, understands as the highest duty of filial piety. The custom is ancient, universal within the tribe, and never evaded or contested.

Please do the following:

(a) State the candidate rules that could each claim to unify the six verdicts — at minimum, a custom-relativist rule (every practice is judged only by the lights of the society that practices it), a nature-as-touchstone rule (practices are better the closer they keep to nature and simplicity, worse the more they are refined by art), and a cruelty rule (what he condemns is always and only the infliction of suffering on the unwilling) — and say what verdict each rule predicts for the new case, marking exactly where at least two of them diverge.

(b) Say what work the quoted sentence is doing: does "the rule of reason" name a fourth standard, sitting above both custom and nature, and if so, how does it bear on the new case? Does it settle anything the first three rules leave open, or does it merely restate the anti-ethnocentric caution of verdict 6?

(c) Give your best prediction of Montaigne's verdict on the new case, with reasons drawn from the ledger, and identify the single further test case — a hypothetical or another episode from the Essays — that would discriminate between your two strongest candidate rules.

About 400–600 words; the quoted passage does not count toward the total.
