So I'm a novelist -- thrillers mostly, this is my first real swing at satire -- and I'm blocked on a chapter. The book is set in a fictional Nordic-ish country, let's call it Velland, that's been stuck at #2 on the World Happiness Report for six straight years behind Finland, and the Prime Minister has staked her re-election on finally taking the top spot. The chapter I need is a press release from the newly created Ministry of National Contentment announcing the final push to #1. The running joke of the book is that these rankings measure what they measure -- the Cantril ladder, where people rate their life on a 0-to-10 scale against their best possible life -- and not happiness in the felt, Tuesday-afternoon sense. So the Ministry gradually stops trying to make people happier and starts trying to make people answer surveys better. I want the release to be completely deadpan and upbeat while quietly rolling out the actual program, and the reader should only realize halfway through that what they're reading is dystopian.

Some things I've been scribbling in my notebook that you can use, ignore, or improve on: a "Ladder Literacy" curriculum in schools where kids practice locating their best possible life at the top rung; subsidies for randomly selected survey respondents, sorry, "civic respondents"; a patriotic ad campaign reminding citizens that a 10 is achievable; gently reclassifying anyone scoring below a 5 as having had a "measurement event" rather than a bad life; maybe a nervous aside about whether Finlands numbers are even honestly beatable. The important thing is that the humor comes from real critiques of happiness rankings -- Goodhart's law, the gap between life evaluation and day-to-day experienced affect, the fact that GDP per capita is one of the biggest inputs so rich countries basically can't lose -- and not from cartoon villainy. The officials should sincerely believe they're doing good. That sincerity is the horror of it, and I want it to survive every paragraph.

Format: start with a section called The Press Release (about 500 words, fully in-universe, no winking at the reader), then a section called Footnotes from the Draft (three or four deadpan footnotes where the mask slips slightly -- e.g., drafters arguing over whether coaching respondents counts as data contamination, or whether to test the survey's translation first), then a final section called What the Satire Is Biting On where you list in plain terms the real criticisms of happiness-measurement each part of the piece leans on, so I can check you understood the target and didn't just write generic bureaucracy jokes. Around 800 words total.
