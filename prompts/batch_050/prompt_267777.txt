I am a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course in moral and political philosophy, and I am grading short essays (about 600 words, graded out of 20). Please grade the essay below against the prompt, giving a numerical grade plus the specific comments that justify it, organized point by point rather than as a general impression.

ESSAY PROMPT GIVEN TO STUDENTS: Philosophers commonly distinguish three ways one person can change another's behavior: coercion, legitimate persuasion, and manipulation. State the distinction as you understand it. Then present two different accounts of what makes an influence attempt count as manipulation, and test each account against at least one hard case -- an influence attempt that the account classifies in a counterintuitive or contested way. Conclude by saying which account you find more defensible and why.

STUDENT'S ESSAY:

Coercion, persuasion, and manipulation form a natural triad. Coercion works by threats or force: it removes options from the victim's choice set, as when a mugger takes your wallet. Persuasion works at the opposite extreme: it gives the other person reasons and leaves them free to weigh those reasons and decide. Manipulation sits between the two: it influences someone while neither removing their options nor straightforwardly engaging their judgment.

A first account of manipulation defines it by deception. On this view, manipulation always involves some element of untruthfulness -- a lie, a half-truth, or at least the concealment of what the influencer is actually doing. What makes manipulation objectionable, on the deception account, is that the target is kept in the dark about the real state of affairs or about the influencer's methods.

A second account defines manipulation by its mechanism rather than by any falsehood. On this view, an influence is manipulative when it bypasses or short-circuits the target's rational capacities: instead of offering reasons the person can assess, it plays on emotions, habits, social pressure, or psychological vulnerabilities. Guilt-tripping is the paradigm case: when a mother says to her adult son, after all I have sacrificed for you, you would really leave me alone at Christmas, nothing she says is false, yet the son is being steered by guilt rather than by a fair presentation of considerations.

The deception account handles obvious cases well, such as a salesperson who hides a product's known defects. But it struggles with the guilt-trip case and with other influences -- flattery, emotional appeals, staged environments -- where nothing false is asserted. The bypassing account handles these better, but it faces hard cases of its own. Consider nudges: a cafeteria that places fruit at eye level and cake at the back influences choices through the design of the environment rather than through reasons, so the bypassing account classifies it as manipulation, even though many people find such nudges benign or even desirable.

On the deeper question of why manipulation is wrong, both accounts can appeal to Kant, who held that we must never treat other people as a means to our own ends. Since manipulation treats the target as an instrument of the manipulator's purposes, it follows that manipulation is always wrong. Admittedly, this creates some tension with the earlier point that benign nudges seem permissible, but the Kantian conclusion stands: where an influence is manipulative, it is impermissible.

On balance, I find the bypassing account more defensible, because it explains why truthful emotional pressure can still be manipulative, which the deception account cannot.
