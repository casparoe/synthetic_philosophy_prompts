I am a PhD candidate in political philosophy, and my dissertation concerns the epistemic justification of democratic institutions. One chapter defends futarchy -- the proposal, associated with Robin Hanson, of settling collective values by vote while delegating beliefs about the consequences of policies to prediction markets -- against the charge that it is epistemically unstable. At a recent works-in-progress seminar, a colleague who is broadly hostile to market-based governance pressed an argument that, if sound, would not merely refute futarchy but would discredit forecast-guided decision-making in general. That conclusion strikes me as proving far too much, since it would equally condemn central banks that react to inflation expectations and governments that order evacuations on hurricane forecasts. Yet when I tried to locate the flaw in the seminar, I could not. Here is the argument as faithfully as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Futarchy can work only if prediction markets produce reliable estimates of how the chosen welfare metric would fare under each candidate policy, so that the policies can be compared.
(2) A market yields a reliable estimate of what a policy would do only if the policy has a genuine chance of being adopted; otherwise contracts conditional on its adoption attract no informed traders, and the market never resolves against observed outcomes that could discipline or reward accuracy.
(3) Under futarchy, a policy is adopted precisely when the market rates it best; hence traders know in advance that any policy the market disfavors will not be adopted.
(4) Therefore the markets corresponding to disfavored policies attract no informed trading, never resolve against outcomes, and their prices carry no information about those policies' consequences.
(5) So the comparison required by (1) is a comparison among markets of which at most one -- the market for the policy already expected to win -- is informative.
(6) Therefore futarchy cannot reliably identify the best policy; its selection mechanism destroys the very information it needs.
(7) Nothing in this reasoning is specific to futarchy: any institution that lets a forecast determine an action thereby makes the forecast's accuracy uncheckable in the cases where the forecast's advice is followed, since the world in which the advice was ignored never occurs.
(8) Therefore no institution can reliably act on forecasts -- which is absurd, so something has gone wrong, but every premise seems defensible.

I would like you to respond as a political philosopher with particular expertise in epistemic institutional design, decision theory, and information aggregation in markets. Please think carefully and proceed slowly: begin by brainstorming every juncture in the argument where the reasoning might break down, including steps whose failure is not obvious, and only then settle on a diagnosis. In your diagnosis, identify the earliest step you take to fail, explain why it looked compelling (my colleague defends (2) by appeal to the well-known refund problem for conditional markets, and I confess I find that defense plausible), and state what it would cost to reject each of the neighboring premises instead -- I am most reluctant to surrender (2) and (7), and would like (6) to be the casualty. Then address the generalization separately: does (8) fail for the same reason as the futarchy-specific conclusion, or does the inference at (7) involve an additional slide of its own? Finally, tell me the strongest conclusion a repaired version of my colleague's argument could legitimately establish, since I suspect something true about thin conditional markets survives even if the sweeping conclusion does not.
