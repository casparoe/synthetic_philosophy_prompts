I tutor physics undergrads and last week I used an analogy I've heard a bunch of times to explain quantum statistics: electrons are like money in a bank account, not like coins in your pocket. Two coins are each somewhere, you can track which is which, so swapping them gives you a genuinely different arrangement. But if two people each put $100 in a shared account, the account just has $200 -- there is no fact of the matter about which dollar belongs to whom, so "swapping" them isn't a different state of anything. I used it to explain why quantum particles get counted differently (why there's no factor of 2 for the swapped arrangement, the whole Bose/Fermi vs. classical counting thing), and I also said, maybe overconfidently, that this is why identity itself doesn't really apply to electrons -- it's not that we can't tell them apart, it's that there's nothing to tell apart.

One of my students pushed back and I didn't have a great answer. She said bank balances are just bookkeeping -- the dollars "in" the account are an abstraction, there's no little dollar-stuff in the vault -- but electrons are actual things in the world, so the analogy proves too much: it would make electrons fictional. She also asked how entanglement fits, since in entangled states it seems like the particles have something like individual roles in a joint story, and my analogy has no room for that.

So: audit the analogy for me. Give me the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the coin, the dollar, the account balance, the swap -- then where the mapping breaks, and whether the breaks are fatal for the two uses I put it to (explaining the counting, and the stronger claim that identity doesn't apply). Also, is there a better analogy I should switch to for the counting part at least? And please separate what's settled physics/statistics from what's genuinely contested metaphysics among philosophers of physics -- I get the sense the "no identity at the quantum level" claim is more disputed than the counting rule it was supposed to explain, and I dont want to present a contested reading as textbook fact.
