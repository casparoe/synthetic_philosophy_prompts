I’m giving a seven-minute speech at the reopening of our town museum, and I’m stuck on the philosophy of it. For eighty years, local memory has said that during the 1946 mill strike the shopkeepers fed the strikers for free, and that this act of solidarity forced the mill owners to settle. It’s the town’s favorite story about itself. The museum’s new research shows that several shopkeepers actually extended credit on harsh terms and later took strikers to court when they couldn’t repay it. At the reopening they’re unveiling a revised exhibit, and half the audience will be local historians who want the record corrected plainly, while the other half will be older residents and descendants who experience the old story as part of their family inheritance.

Please write the speech around this distinction, which I want to treat seriously rather than just name-drop: historical records govern claims about what happened, but collective memory also carries a kind of practical knowledge about what a community has needed, valued, and promised itself. That doesn’t mean a false story becomes true because it was useful, but it may mean that correcting it requires more than replacing one sentence on a plaque with another. The speech should say what has to give way to the evidence, what can legitimately be preserved from the remembered version, and where the line sits between honoring a myth’s moral function and laundering a falsehood.

Make it genuinely speakable, not like an academic paper, and don’t resolve things with the easy line that memory is emotional and history is factual. It should acknowledge that archives also get curated, neglected, and selectively preserved, while still insisting that the ledgers matter. Ideally it should leave the room feeling that revising the exhibit is an act of loyalty to the town rather than an attack on it.
