My roommate is doing his comps on Heidegger and he walked me into this argument last week, and I genuinely can't find the exit. Quick gloss so we're on the same page: readiness-to-hand is the mode things have when we're absorbed in using them (the hammer while hammering), presence-at-hand is the mode of things just sitting there as objects with properties, and breakdown is when the hammer snaps or goes missing and suddenly you see it as a thing. Okay, his argument:

(1) Something counts as equipment only insofar as it's absorbed into a task, and to the degree it's really absorbed, it withdraws — you attend to the nail and the shelf, not the hammer.
(2) But to describe something, attribute properties to it, or even pick it out as "a hammer," you have to direct attention at it rather than through it at the job — and that shift of attention is just what breakdown, obstinacy, or stepping back to inspect consists in.
(3) So any encounter in which something shows up as a describable thing with properties is, by definition, an encounter with a present-at-hand object, not with equipment as equipment.
(4) Obviously we do describe hammers all the time and say true things about them.
(5) So every actual encounter anyone ever has is an encounter with the present-at-hand; the ready-to-hand is at best something we reconstruct or infer afterwards, never anything experienced.
(6) But the whole analytic is supposed to rest on readiness-to-hand being the more primordial mode of being, the one presence-at-hand is derived from.
(7) So the priority claim undermines itself: the phenomenon that's supposed to ground the ontology is one that, by the theory's own lights, nobody can ever actually undergo.

His verdict: Being and Time's foundational chapter is an elaborate theory of something with zero instances. I'm not ready to pay that. If I had to choose I'd rather keep (1) and (4) than the conclusion, and I suspect the trouble is somewhere around (2) or (3), but when I push there it looks fine to me — you really can't stare at the hammer and swing it fluently at the same time. Which step do you reject, why did it look plausible, and what does rejecting it cost? Bonus points if your fix doesn't just redefine "encounter" to make the problem dissapear.
