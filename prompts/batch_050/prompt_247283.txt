I'm presenting a paper at a workshop on moral psychology and the philosophy of emotion, defending an unfashionable pair of claims: first, that shame is, on balance, a better moral emotion than guilt — guilt, I argue, privatizes wrongdoing by letting the agent discharge it through inward self-punishment without repairing anything, whereas shame lodges the failure in the agent's standing identity and social relationships, which is where repair actually has to happen; second, that cultures can deliberately shift which moral emotions they cultivate, so that a community choosing between shame-centered and guilt-centered norms is making a real, steerable decision rather than undergoing blind drift. The audience is roughly a dozen empirical psychologists who study the self-conscious emotions — most work in the research tradition that treats shame-proneness as a risk factor for aggression, concealment, and externalizing blame, and guilt-proneness as tied to reparative action — plus several virtue ethicists and one anthropologist who doubts emotional norms are steerable at all. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the seven or eight objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage each would do, phrased the way this audience would phrase them (in their vocabulary — proneness, attribution style, approach vs. avoidance motivation, habituation), with a two-minute reply for each, a flag where I should concede part of the objection rather than fight it, and a note on where each reply is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Open directly with the most dangerous objection and work down from there — no preamble, no summary of my thesis.
