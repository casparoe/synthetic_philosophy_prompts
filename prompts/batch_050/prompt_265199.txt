I am a PhD student in applied animal ethics sitting on the scientific advisory panel of a mid-sized seafood certification scheme. The scheme is deciding whether its next welfare standard should require effective electrical stunning before slaughter for farmed shrimp, and whether it should phase out two widespread practices: eyestalk ablation in hatchery broodstock and slaughter by chilling in ice slurry without prior stunning. Two panel members have been arguing past each other for a year, and I want to move them from debating to drafting. I would like you to write the joint statement that I hope both could sign after a serious adversarial collaboration.

The two positions, in what I take to be their strongest forms, are these. The precautionary reformer holds that decapod crustaceans display behaviour that is difficult to explain without valenced experience — avoidance learning that persists over time, wound-directed attention, and motivational trade-offs in which an animal appears to weigh exposure to a noxious stimulus against access to shelter or food — and that, given the sheer number of shrimp slaughtered each year (plausibly more individual animals than all farmed land vertebrates combined), even a modest credence in crustacean sentience makes inexpensive welfare measures obligatory and costly ones defensible; on this view, refusing to regulate until sentience is proven is itself a moral choice that bets against the animals. The evidential sceptic holds that none of the behavioural evidence distinguishes valenced experience from nociceptive reflexes coupled to simple learning; that crustacean nervous systems are small, decentralised, and organised so differently from our own that projecting felt experience onto them is anthropomorphism; that stunning and slaughter methods assumed to be humane may perform poorly or inconsistently in aquatic species, so mandates could impose real costs on farmers, many of them smallholders in low-income countries, while doing nothing for the animals; and that the honest position is agnosticism pending better evidence.

Please write the statement as continuous prose, the way one would write a long formal letter — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — and in British English. It should contain, in this order: the claims both parties can sign, covering the behavioural evidence, the neuroanatomy, the scale of the industry, the limits of current knowledge, and what stunning can and cannot realistically be expected to achieve; the residual disagreements, each phrased carefully enough that the person who actually holds the view would accept the wording of their own side; the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party — I suspect one crux is whether motivational trade-off behaviour requires some common internal currency that amounts to something like felt badness, another is how much weight arguments from neural architecture should carry against behavioural evidence, and a third is the decision-theoretic question of what exchange rate between credence in sentience and compliance cost each side is actually willing to state; and, for each crux, the experiment, observation, or argument both agree would bear on it, with each party's prediction recorded in advance so the statement could be returned to later. If any part of their dispute strikes you as verbal rather than substantive, the statement should say which part and why. Finally, because this document will be read by people who will act on it, please end with a candid passage identifying where your rendering of either position is most likely to have been unfair or mistaken, and what argument or evidence would have led you to draft the balance of the statement differently.
