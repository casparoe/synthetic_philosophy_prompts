My roommate and I have a long-running game we call "refereeing": whenever one of us abandons or keeps a personal resolution, the other issues a verdict on whether it was weakness of will or a rational change of plan. Over the years she has been the more consistent referee, and I've kept a record of her rulings. She ruled that my quitting a gym membership after discovering the only bus there had been cancelled was a sensible revision, not weakness. She ruled that my eating a donut an hour after pledging to cut sugar, on the grounds that "one won't matter," was textbook weakness. She ruled that my staying up to finish a show and telling myself I'd start my thesis "first thing tomorrow" was weakness, and she noted that I'd made the same promise the three previous nights. She ruled that skipping a planned morning run because of an unforecasted cold rain was a legitimate revision. And she ruled that my declining a second slice of cake at a wedding, even while openly saying I badly wanted it, was a clean case of strength of will.

Here is the new case I want a prediction on. Last Sunday I had firmly resolved to spend the morning drafting a report. When the morning came, no external fact had changed -- no new deadlines, no new information about the report or the alternatives -- but the task suddenly struck me as tedious and the couch as unusually appealing, and I told myself, with what felt like genuine conviction at the time, that I do my best writing in the evenings anyway, so moving the work was really an optimization. I spent the morning on the couch. (That evening I did not write.) When I put the case to her, she hesitated for the first time in years, and we've been arguing about what her own track record commits her to.

Please tell me: what verdict should she give if she wants to be consistent with her own prior rulings? In answering, identify at least two distinct principles that each fit all five of her past verdicts -- for example, principles differ on whether weakness requires acting against a plan without any new information, whether it consists in a preference reversing merely because a temptation has become temporally nearer, and whether it requires that she still endorse the resolution at the moment of action. Show that these principles diverge on my Sunday case, say which ruling best coheres with her record overall and which earlier verdict is the closest analog, and describe one further hypothetical case that would empirically distinguish the leading candidate principles from each other.
