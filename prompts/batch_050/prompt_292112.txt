So I wrote this short essay for my blog and I want comments before I post it. I don't have any academic background, I've just been chewing on this for a while. What I mostly want: (1) where does the argument actually go wrong, logically, (2) is anything factually off about how guilt and shame work, and (3) is the ending any good or is it cheesy. Be blunt.

Essay draft, "In defense of shame":

Everyone in pop psychology seems to agree guilt is the good emotion and shame is the bad one. Guilt says "I did a bad thing," shame says "I am a bad person," and the second is supposedly toxic — it makes you hide and lash out — while guilt makes you apologize and fix things. I think this is backwards, or at best half the story.

First: guilt only works if you already accept the standard you violated. Guilt is a private conversation between you and your own values. Shame is about how you appear to others, and that sounds superficial, but its actually how standards get enforced in the first place. A society with only guilt and no shame is one where morality only binds people who already agree with it. Shame is the community saying "we're serious about this one."

Second: the "I am bad" vs. "I did bad" line is overstated. When you feel guilty for lying to a friend, part of what stings is that you were being a liar — the self is always involved. And when shame hits, most people don't conclude they're irredeemable; they conclude they fell short of a kind of person they want to be seen as. Shame has a built-in aspiration that guilt lacks.

Third, my main claim: a culture can partly choose its dominant moral emotion, and ours is mid-choose right now. Therapy culture and social media pile-ons are both attempts to engineer the shame/guilt balance — one trying to abolish shame, the other weaponizing it. Neither asks the real question: what do we want shame for? I'd say shame is like fire — you don't abolish it, you decide what it's allowed to burn.

I end by saying the real test of a moral emotion isn't whether it feels good or produces tidy behavior in lab studies, but whether a person governed by it becomes someone you'd want as a neighbor.

Also — does the fire metaphor in part three earn its keep, or am I just reaching for something that sounds nice?
