I am a professor of philosophy preparing to apply for a large, five-year collaborative grant — the kind that funds a small research group with two postdocs and regular visitors, jointly housed between a philosophy department and a microbiology/immunology lab. The funding theme I want to target is biological individuality: the question of whether holobionts, colonies, and so-called superorganisms count as organisms, and what follows if they do or do not.

You are a senior philosopher of biology who has advised on grant panels and knows this literature cold. I want you to help me design the research agenda for the proposal. Think carefully and work through this in stages: first brainstorm the landscape of open questions, then narrow to a prioritized list, then defend the prioritization. Do not give me a literature survey or a reading list — I need a defended agenda.

Here is what I already take for granted, so you don't waste space on it. I know the standard repertoire of candidate criteria for individuality — functional integration, germ-soma separation, policing of cheaters, bottlenecks and vertical transmission, immunological self-recognition, continuity through reproduction — and I know the standard zoo: lichens, siphonophores like the Portuguese man o' war, eusocial insect colonies, biofilms, the squid-vibrio symbiosis, aphids and their endosymbionts. I am also aware of the two broad meta-positions: that "organism" picks out a natural kind with a fact of the matter about its boundaries, versus that individuality comes in degrees along several dimensions and the interesting work is tracking the dimensions rather than crowning a winner.

What I need from you is a ranking of the open questions in this area, specifically the three or four most worth a five-year group's attention, with the ranking defended. Some candidate directions to consider (feel free to reject, merge, or replace any of them):

1. The meta-question of whether the pluralist, "it comes in degrees" response dissolves the debate or merely postpones it — i.e., whether there remains a substantive disagreement once all the dimensions of individuality are disentangled, and if so, what it is about.
2. The relationship between individuality and levels of selection: whether something can be a unit of selection without being an organism, and whether the holobiont debate is really just the levels-of-selection debate wearing new clothes.
3. The immunological criterion and its tension with holobiont claims: the immune system both excludes and tolerates microbes, and whether tolerance mechanisms give us an empirically tractable, non-circular way to draw organismal boundaries.
4. The downstream question: if holobionts or colonies are organisms, what actually has to change — in evolutionary theory, in conservation practice, in how we think about biological health and disease — and whether anything substantive turns on the classification at all.
5. The question of whether evolutionary transitions in individuality are a completed historical process or ongoing, and whether we can identify transitions in progress rather than only in retrospect.

For each question you rank, give me: why it matters (what depends on it — how many other questions, inside and outside philosophy, are hostage to it), how tractable it is for a group mixing conceptual and empirical work over five years, how neglected it is relative to its importance, and what would have to be true for you to move it up or down the ranking. Be explicit about the trade-offs — for instance, if your top-ranked question is less tractable than your second, say why importance wins, or why it doesn't. And identify the single crux you think the whole proposal would implicitly rest on: the claim such that, if it turned out false, the agenda would need to be redesigned.
