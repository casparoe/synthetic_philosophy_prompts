So in about three weeks I have to give a talk at my department's annual historiography workshop, and I've decided (maybe against my better judgement) to defend a version of the "volcano helped cause the French Revolution" thesis. Not the whole revolution, obviously — my claim is narrower: that the Laki fissure eruption in Iceland (started June 1783, ran about eight months, pumped a huge sulfur load into the atmosphere, cold winter of 83-84 across Europe) belongs on the list of causal actors in the Revolution's origins, and not just as background weather. My rough chain: volcanic forcing hits mid-1780s French agriculture and kills livestock → rural debt, depleted herds, no buffer left → so when the 1788 harvest fails and the July 1788 hailstorm lands, the subsistence crisis is far worse than it "should" have been → the crown's fiscal crisis becomes unmanageable → Estates-General. I know where the soft spot is: the direct aerosol effects fade by about 1785, so I need this "lagged damage, no resilience" story to carry me to 1788-89, and that's a bridge not a fact. On top of that I want to make the more provocative claim that Laki acted — that it did something in history in the same sense Necker or the parlements did something, and that historians' allergy to that phrasing is a prejudice about agency, not a real methodological position.

The audience: maybe thirty people, mostly French Revolution specialists. The core of the room is senior social and political historians who came up fighting monocausal explanations of the Revolution, were shaped by the class-conflict wars and then the political-culture turn, and are openly suspicious of environmental framings — two of them have said within my hearing that climate talk is how non-historians launder determinism into respectability. There's a younger material-history contingent who might be friendly, and one actual climate historian who is definitely on my side but whose endorsement might cost me with the others. Nobody's a philosopher of history, but the senior folks have strong, untheorized views about what counts as a cause.

Give me my Q&A prep sheet: the objections this audience will actually raise — not the ones a geology department would raise — roughly ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way a skeptical social historian of the Revolution would phrase them. For each: what I should concede, what I should hold, a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, and where that reply is weakest. Also tell me what to concede up front in the talk itself to disarm the room, and what the question is that I'm praying nobody asks.
