So I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, and I keep ending up in free will arguments at work (long story, started as a joke about whether our PM "could have done otherwise" than ship a broken release). From the outside the compatibilist vs incompatibilist debate looks to me like two teams arguing about a function signature: one side defines "free" as roughly "does what it wants without a gun to its head," the other defines it as "could have output a different result given the exact same inputs," and then they argue past each other for decades.

Turns out Hume made basically this exact complaint in 1748. In Section VIII of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding he writes:

"This has been the case in the long disputed question concerning liberty and necessity; and to so remarkable a degree, that, if I be not much mistaken, we shall find, that all mankind, both learned and ignorant, have always been of the same opinion with regard to this subject, and that a few intelligible definitions would immediately have put an end to the whole controversy... I hope, therefore, to make it appear, that all men have ever agreed in the doctrine both of necessity and of liberty, according to any reasonable sense, which can be put on these terms; and that the whole controversy has hitherto turned merely upon words."

So Hume's diagnosis is: if a dispute survives centuries of smart people on both sides, that's itself evidence the terms are ambiguous and the disagreement is verbal. My question is whether he's right about the modern version of this debate. Is compatibilism vs incompatibilism actually just a definitional stalemate -- two legitimate notions of freedom that were never in competition -- or is there a substantive residue that survives once everyone fixes their definitions, like whether praise, blame, and desert still make sense in a determined world? Basically: is this a bug in the spec or a real disagreement about the spec? And given your answer, what would actually count as progress here -- a cleaner definition, a new distinction, some empirical result from neuroscience or physics, or just everyone agreeing to deprecate the term "free will" and split it into two? I'd rather have your honest diagnosis than a balanced survey of both sides.
