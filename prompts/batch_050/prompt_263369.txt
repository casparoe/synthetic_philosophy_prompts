I'm a PhD student in political science (development / political economy side) and I'm TAing a grad seminar this semester on the political economy of disasters. Next week I'm running the famine session, and the reading list runs from Malthus through Sen's entitlement stuff to more recent work (de Waal and co.) arguing that mass starvation is basically always made, not born. The problem: my students keep flattening the whole debate into a single "nature vs. politics" slider, and then arguing past each other, because someone saying the Great Leap Forward was a policy catastrophe and someone saying Bengal 1943 was a market failure are actually making pretty different claims even though both count as "anti-natural" on the slider. I was complaining about this to my advisor and she suggested I make them a handout that forces the distinctions apart. So that's what I want from you.

Please write a decision tree that sorts positions on the question "what kind of event is a famine, causally and morally speaking?" Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: figure out which questions actually divide people, then locate real positions at the leaves. Each branch node should be a question phrased so a student could answer it without knowing which camp it lands them in -- e.g. something like "can mass starvation happen in a country where aggregate food supply never dropped?" rather than "do you agree with Sen?" The splits I suspect matter (but correct me / restructure if I'm wrong): whether food availability decline is even necessary as a background condition; whether entitlement collapse is something markets and weather can produce on their own or whether it takes deliberate or reckless state action; whether intent matters for the moral verdict (is a foreseeable-but-unwanted famine a crime the way a deliberately engineered one is?); whether Sen's claim about democracies and a free press preventing famine is doing explanatory work or is empirically fragile; and whether war, rather than distribution failures in peacetime, is the real main driver in the modern cases.

Leaves should be named positions where they exist (Malthusian naturalism, Sen-style entitlement analysis, the strong famine-as-atrocity-crime view, a war-centered view in de Waal's neighborhood, whatever else you think belongs) and "unnamed" with a one-sentence diagnosis where a coherent combination of answers has no defender (incoherent? unattractive? just unexplored?). Roughly 8-12 nodes, aimed at grad students who've done the week's reading. Two more constraints: the tree has to be usable as a classification tool for actual cases, so after the tree show where Bengal 1943, the Irish famine, and the Great Leap Forward each land and note any case that falls awkwardly between leaves; and flag explicitly any pair of positions your tree can't separate, rather than papering over it.
