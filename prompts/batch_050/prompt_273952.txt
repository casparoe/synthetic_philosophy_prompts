Philosophers have been arguing for decades about whether the so-called money pump argument succeeds in showing that rationality requires transitive preferences. The setup is familiar: an agent prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A; a clever trader offers her a sequence of small swaps, each of which she accepts by her own lights, until she has paid repeatedly to end up where she started, minus the fees. On one side of the debate stand those who take this to demonstrate that cyclic preferences are irrational, since they reliably lead an agent to act against her own ends. On the other side stand critics who deny that any requirement of diachronic consistency follows. Their objections come in several forms: that the argument smuggles in extra premises (the agent must foresee the whole sequence yet still trade stepwise; she must retain her preferences throughout; she must be incapable of adopting a policy of refusing further trades); that rationality governs only preferences at a time, so that an agent whose preferences at each moment are locally in order violates no requirement, however the sequence unfolds; and that what the pump really exploits is not intransitivity but the absence of forward-looking choice dispositions, which is a separate defect. Defenders reply that this fragments the agent into time-slices and cannot explain why the pumped agent's predicament is even regrettable, let alone irrational.

The literature on this has been mature for some time, and as far as I can tell no new argument has moved anyone in years; each side keeps restating its case in terms the other side already rejects. My working hypothesis is that the dispute has stalled because it is really two disagreements wearing one coat: a substantive disagreement about whether rationality can assess patterns extended across time, or only states and choices at times, and a largely unnoticed verbal disagreement about what the money pump argument is even supposed to be an argument for — a norm of preference, a norm of choice, or a merely prudential warning about exploitable dispositions.

Write an essay diagnosing the structure of this impasse. Is it substantive, verbal, or a case of the two sides answering different questions under a single heading? Where exactly does the disagreement bottom out — in rival starting intuitions about agency over time, in different views about what counts as a requirement of rationality, or in a hidden premise (about foresight, preference stability, or the nature of choice) that neither side has made fully explicit? Engage with what each side actually argues rather than with caricatures, and say what, given your diagnosis, would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction that dissolves the dispute, a formal result that settles it, an agreed test case that would force one side to concede, or an argument that no such resolution is available and the debate should be reframed or abandoned.
