After forty years in experimental science I have recently been reading epistemology, and I would like a check on something. A popular book on theories of knowledge summarizes Robert Nozick's tracking theory as follows: "Nozick held that knowledge is true belief that 'tracks the truth': you know that p when, if p were false, you would not believe it, and if p were true, you would believe it. Because your experiences would be exactly the same if you were a brain in a vat, your belief that you are not one fails the first condition — so on Nozick's account you do know that you are not a brain in a vat, and the skeptic is refuted. The cost is that knowledge is not closed under deduction: you can know you have hands, and know that having hands entails that you are not a handless brain in a vat, while not knowing that you are not a brain in a vat. Most philosophers have regarded this closure failure as a decisive refutation, which is why tracking theories are now only of historical interest." Two things strike me as suspicious — the inference in the second sentence seems to go the wrong way, and I recall reading that Nozick embraced the closure point rather than being embarrassed by it. But the author is a professional philosopher, so I hesitate to trust my instincts. Please tell me which parts of this summary are accurate, which are common simplifications a specialist would tolerate, and which are misrepresentations — and whether the summary's verdict that the theory refutes skepticism, and its verdict that the theory has itself been refuted, survive correction. Please answer in American English.
