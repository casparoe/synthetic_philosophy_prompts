I run the standards desk of a mid-sized news wire. About once a week we handle a photo or video that, if authentic, is strong enough to carry a story by itself, and we typically have half an hour before the item either runs or dies. I want a decision procedure my editors can follow in that half hour, grounded in what philosophy of testimony and applied epistemology actually have to say about recordings as evidence, but usable by people who have read none of it. Describe the ideas generically; I will add any citations myself.

The procedure should take seriously a specific diagnosis I want built into its design. Recordings have served as an epistemic backstop: sources and witnesses are believed partly because what they say could, in principle, be checked against a record, and records settle disputes that testimony alone cannot. My worry is that the main damage from synthetic media is not that fakes get believed but that the backstop collapses -- genuine footage loses its settling power (denials become cheap), and testimony loses the credit it had been borrowing from the availability of records. Call that borrowed credit "backstop debt." The desk's job is then not only to avoid publishing fakes but to avoid spending down a commons we depend on.

The procedure should give, for any piece of footage, one of three verdicts: run it as evidence, run it only as a claim attributed to the footage, or hold. Specify the steps in order (provenance, independent corroboration, automated detection, source incentives, and an explicit test for how cheap a denial would be), what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, what to do when no number or tool output is available, and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three cases: an anonymously supplied video of a mayor apparently accepting cash, two weeks before an election, with the detector flagging nothing; shaky phone footage of police violence from a source who has been right twice before, with the detector returning an inconclusive result; and one case you construct yourself where the procedure refuses to clear footage that is very likely genuine. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do -- in particular, whether any desk-level procedure can protect the audience's trust rather than merely the desk's own accuracy, and what the answer implies about whether we should publish our confidence alongside the footage.
