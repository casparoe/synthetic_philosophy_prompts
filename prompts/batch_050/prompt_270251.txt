Last weekend I got into a long, somewhat heated argument with a friend about whether I would survive teleportation, and we ended up talking past each other because we kept sliding between different thought experiments without ever agreeing on what question we were answering. Since then I've tried to write down, case by case, what my gut actually says, and I'd like help figuring out whether there is a single coherent principle behind these verdicts or whether I have to give some of them up. Here are the cases and my verdicts. Case one: the standard teletransporter -- my body is scanned, destroyed, and a perfect replica is assembled on Mars from new matter, with all my memories and character. I'd step in without much hesitation; the person who walks out on Mars is me. Case two: the same machine, but the original is not destroyed, and I get to chat with my replica on a video link. Then the replica is obviously not me; he's a stranger who thinks like me, and if the machine were now set to vaporize me on Earth that would be my death, full stop. Case three: my neurons are replaced one by one with functionally identical silicon chips over the course of a year. I'm confident I'd survive this -- at no point does anyone die. Case four: my brain is divided and each half is placed in a different body, both waking up fully psychologically continuous with me. Here I freeze. It seems wrong to say I become both, arbitrary to say I become one, and yet depressing to say I become neither, since surviving with half a brain in one body would have been survival. Case five: cryonic freezing and revival centuries later. Survival, I think, even granting the total interruption of consciousness. Case six: a teleporter variant where the original is destroyed not instantly but five minutes after the replica is confirmed on Mars. Suddenly it feels like murder again, which is strange given verdict one.

My friend claims these verdicts are a mess, and I suspect she's right -- in particular, verdicts one and six look hard to reconcile, since the only difference between them is a five-minute delay, and verdict one also sits oddly with four, since if psychological continuity is enough for survival on Mars it seems it should be enough even when it branches. What I'd like from you: first, a precise statement of one principle -- or, if you think it's needed, a small set of competing principles -- about what matters in survival that captures as many of my six verdicts as possible, with a note about which specific case motivates each clause of the principle. Second, an honest accounting of which of my verdicts your favored principle cannot accommodate, and whether you'd recommend overturning those verdicts or complicating the principle. Third, tell me what your principle predicts for two cases I haven't settled: a teleporter that malfunctions and produces a replica who shares my memories only up to my twentieth birthday, and a machine that offers to copy me nightly so that a fresh replica can be printed if I die unexpectedly -- would taking out that 'insurance policy' mean I never really die?
