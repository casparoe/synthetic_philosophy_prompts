I'm developing a framework for what I'll call moral trade: arrangements in which two agents holding different moral views each redirect some of their effort toward what the other values, so that both end up with more of what they care about than either could get alone. A simple case: a donor who prioritizes global health and an activist who prioritizes animal welfare agree that the donor funds some animal charities while the activist does advocacy for bednets, when each would otherwise have worked exclusively on their own cause. The arrangement is supposed to be attractive without either party conceding that the other's values are correct, and without requiring any shared measure of moral value across the two views.

Before I compare any particular accounts, I want the specification written out: the desiderata a theory of moral trade must meet to count as adequate. Give me the full requirements list, with the motivating case or consideration behind each. In particular, I'd like the list to cover at least the following pressure points, though you should add any I've missed:

- A theory should not quietly presuppose intertheoretic comparisons of value — a common currency in which both parties' moral views can be priced — since the whole appeal of trade is that it seems to work without one. If some comparison turns out to be smuggled in anyway, that's a defect to be flagged.
- It must handle asymmetries: the parties will typically differ in how confident they are in their own moral views and in how much evidence supports each. Say what the theory should do when one party is nearly certain and the other is deeply uncertain, and whether "better by one's own lights" should be cashed out in actual values, expected value under moral uncertainty, or something else.
- It must distinguish genuine trade from capitulation. If one party agrees to the arrangement only because the other credibly threatens to do something the first regards as terrible, is that a trade? The theory needs a principled line here, not just a stipulation.
- It must confront the integrity problem: some trades ask each party to do things they independently regard as wrong, not merely less valuable. State whether a theory may license this, forbid it, or must treat it case by case, and what follows either way.
- It must say what happens when the parties' views are such that trade produces a Pareto improvement in each party's expected moral value while making the world worse according to any plausible objective ranking — if one allows that moral views can be objectively right or wrong, can trade still be endorsed, or does adequacy require neutrality on that question?
- It should extend to many-party trades and to trades with parties who cannot reciprocate or verify compliance (future people, the dead, strangers with no enforcement mechanism), or else state honestly that it does not.

For each requirement, mark whether it is non-negotiable or a camp-specific preference, and give the case for the marking. Then the two things I most want: first, whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, whether respecting both parties' integrity constraints can conflict with securing mutual expected-value gains in cases where one party's view is extremely demanding — since if the list is inconsistent, that itself is the interesting result; and second, which single requirement does the most work in excluding popular informal ways of thinking about moral cooperation (parliamentary models, compromise-as-splitting-the-difference, simple expected-value maximization under moral uncertainty). Score existing proposals briefly if you like, but keep the weight on the criteria. A numbered list with short paragraphs is fine; no bibliography needed.
