A friend who has been reading Japanese Buddhist philosophy summarized Dōgen's teaching of "being-time" (uji) for me like this: "Dōgen's point is that time is ultimately unreal. Because every moment fully contains its own time — being and time are one thing, so that mountains are time and seas are time — nothing genuinely comes into being or passes away. Firewood and ash each occupy their fixed place forever, which shows that change is an illusion we project onto an eternal now. It follows that the usual picture of time flying past us is wrong, and the practical upshot is that we should stop striving toward future enlightenment and simply rest in the timeless present, where life and death are both already complete."

I know just enough to be suspicious of parts of this — particularly the claim that Dōgen denies passage and impermanence, which seems to clash with his reputation as a philosopher of radical impermanence — but I can't articulate where the summary goes wrong. Please audit it: which parts fairly represent what Dōgen claims in the being-time teaching, which parts import ideas or imagery from elsewhere in his work and treat them as belonging here, and which he would reject outright? Does the concluding advice about practice actually follow from the accurate parts, or does it rest on precisely the misreading?
