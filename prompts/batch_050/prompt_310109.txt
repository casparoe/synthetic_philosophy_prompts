I run the consensus-statement program for a mid-sized medical specialty society. Once a year, a twelve-member panel of clinicians and methodologists reviews forty or so contested claims in our field and decides which of them may be promoted into our practice guidelines as flat statements of fact ("screening with X reduces mortality from Y"), which must stay in the guidelines only as hedged statements of uncertainty, and which get dropped entirely. Each claim gets about ninety minutes of panel time. The decisions matter: a promoted claim changes what thousands of physicians tell patients, and a wrongly shelved claim can cost lives just as a wrongly promoted one can.

In preparing this year's panel I have been reading the pragmatists on truth, and I want their ideas turned into a procedure my panel can actually follow. Two strands interest me. The first is Peirce's. In "The Fixation of Belief" (Popular Science Monthly, 1877), he writes:

"Kepler undertook to draw a curve through the places of Mars; and to state the times occupied by the planet in describing the different parts of that curve; but perhaps his greatest service to science was in impressing on men's minds that this was the thing to be done if they wished to improve astronomy; that they were not to content themselves with inquiring whether one system of epicycles was better than another but that they were to sit down to the figures and find out what the curve, in truth, was."

As I read it, Peirce's point is that what made Kepler's work scientific was not the conclusion he reached but his submission to a tribunal that would have delivered the same verdict to anyone: the figures, not the inquirer's taste, fix the belief. This connects to Peirce's broader view that the method of science settles belief by reference to something independent of what any of us wants or thinks, and that truth is what unlimited inquiry would converge on. The second strand is James's: roughly, that a belief's truth shows itself in its practical fruits, and that a claim proves itself good in the way of belief by successfully guiding action in a world that will not wait for inquiry to finish. My panel cannot wait either. Patients are being screened or not screened this year.

Write me the procedure. Specifically:

1. An ordered set of tests the panel runs on each claim within its ninety minutes, translating the two strands into checks a clinician with no philosophy can perform. For the Peircean side, I want the Kepler passage to do real work: the tests should ask, in effect, whether this claim's fate is in the hands of "the figures" — evidence that would push any competent investigator the same way regardless of prior preference — or whether it is still being settled by comparing epicycle systems, i.e., by taste, authority, or disciplinary fashion. For the Jamesian side, the tests should ask what follows practically from treating the claim as settled versus unsettled, given that withholding a decision is itself a decision with consequences.

2. An explicit rule for what the panel does when the two strands diverge: a claim the evidence would not force on a neutral investigator but which it would be very useful to treat as true (say, a cheap screening rule of thumb with weak but suggestive support and strong demand from members), and the reverse — a claim the evidence forces but whose promotion would change little or do harm.

3. Three worked applications: (a) a well-replicated finding with a persistent, competent minority of dissenters; (b) a claim where the underlying research community is small, clubby, and shares the same funding sources, so that apparent consensus may be tenacity or authority wearing the costume of convergence; and (c) at least one case where the procedure returns an answer I will not like — preferably one where it blocks promotion of something our membership badly wants.

4. A statement of the procedure's limits: which classes of claims it cannot settle (value-laden recommendations, one-off historical questions, questions further inquiry cannot in practice reach), which of its steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of method, and where the ninety-minute constraint forces the panel into Jamesian territory whether it likes it or not.

Finally, be straight with me about the load-bearing assumption. I am treating two theories of truth as if they yielded a decision procedure for what a committee should certify, and I know that may be a category error — Peirce's convergence at the limit of inquiry is a regulative ideal, not a test you can run in ninety minutes, and James did not think committees could vote beliefs true because believing them is convenient. If the request as framed rests on a confusion, or if one of the two strands simply will not survive translation into a checklist and I should drop it rather than keep it as decoration, say so plainly and tell me what the honest version of the procedure looks like.
