I am a PhD student in philosophy with a sideline in welfare economics, and I help run an interdisciplinary reading group (about 12 people, mostly graduate students) that is currently working through the ethics of discounting. For our next 75-minute session I want to design an activity, not a lecture, that makes participants feel the distinction between discounting future goods because the future is uncertain and discounting them merely because they are later -- pure time preference. My rough idea is a two-stage allocation game: in one stage participants divide a real small stake (say, a pot of good chocolate or café vouchers) between themselves now and the group next week, with various probabilities of the later payout actually arriving; in the other they allocate resources across successive cohorts of a simulated society without knowing which cohort they themselves will turn out to belong to. I want the rules and incentives arranged so that participants end up discounting far more steeply in the first stage than in the second, notice the inconsistency themselves, and only afterwards have the concepts named. Please give me concrete rules, numbers, and materials; a debrief mapping what happened onto the distinction and onto the standard worry that pure time preference is morally indefensible while uncertainty-based discounting is not; an account of what will predictably go wrong the first time (for instance, participants treating the second stage as a puzzle about risk rather than time); and a frank note on which feature of the idea the game cannot reproduce. Write in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if writing me a letter, and use British English.
