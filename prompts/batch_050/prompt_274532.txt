My reading group has been stuck on the nonidentity problem for like three sessions now, and we keep talking past each other, so I want something more constructive than another "here are both sides" rundown. Write the joint statement that a committed person-affecting theorist (an act is wrong only if there's someone who ends up worse off than they otherwise would have been) and a committed impersonal theorist (outcomes are better or worse in virtue of the wellbeing they contain, whoever's wellbeing it is) could both actually sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Ground the whole thing in these three cases, which both sides have to rule on:

1. A couple can conceive now and have a child with a mild but real lifelong condition, or wait three months and have a different, healthy child. They conceive now.
2. A city picks between two 40-year transit plans. Plan B leaves the residents of 2065 noticeably worse off than Plan A would have, but the construction disruption differs enough that almost nobody alive in 2065 would have existed under the other plan.
3. Same setup as 2, except the plans don't change who gets born — they just expose the same fixed future population to a 1% risk of a serious harm that may or may not materialize.

Deliverables: a numbered list of claims both sides can sign (real claims — "the cases are puzzling" doesn't count); a table of the disagreements that survive, each one worded carefully enough that the person holding that view would accept the wording; and then the cruxes — the specific sub-questions where settling one would actually move at least one side — with, for each crux, a case or argument both parties agree bears on it and each side's prediction about what it shows, written down in advance. If you think one of the apparent disagreements is really just about words (say, what "harm" means), name it and dissolve it rather than listing it.

Case 3 is in there on purpose btw. I have a hunch it forces the person-affecting side into ex ante vs ex post territory and I want to see how they handle it.

Also, don't be wishy-washy about any of this. Actually commit: if you think one side has an easier time with these cases, or that one crux is load-bearing and the others are downstream of it, say so and argue for it. I'd rather get a joint statement with a definite editorial point of view than a perfectly neutral one.
