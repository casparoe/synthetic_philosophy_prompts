Schopenhauer held that the will-to-live is the inner nature of all things, that willing generates an endless oscillation between suffering and boredom, and that the only genuine release lies in the denial of the will through ascetic resignation, compassion, and — temporarily — aesthetic contemplation. He also insisted that suicide is not a denial of the will at all but a disguised affirmation of it: the suicide wills life and rejects only its conditions.

Now imagine a society in which a safe, widely available medication suppresses craving at its physiological root. Those who take it report that hunger, appetite, and the compulsive pull of various pleasures simply quiet down; the restless striving Schopenhauer described falls silent without effort, discipline, or insight. Later versions dampen other cravings as well — for sex, for gambling, for distraction itself.

Reconstruct, as carefully as you can, what Schopenhauer would make of this. Would he regard the pharmacological suppression of desire as a genuine instance of the denial of the will-to-live — asceticism achieved by other means — or as something categorically different from the resignation he prized, and on what grounds? How much work does his treatment of suicide do in settling the answer? Would he predict that chemically quieted willing actually reduces suffering, or merely relocates it, perhaps into the very boredom he took to be the other pole of human misery? And would he draw distinctions among the cravings suppressed, treating some as more essential to the will's dominion than others? Ground your answer in his metaphysics of the will and his ethics, mark where his position genuinely underdetermines the verdict, and identify the single point at which his likely judgment would most surprise his present-day admirers.
