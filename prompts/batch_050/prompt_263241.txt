So I've been voluntold to run the friday discussion section for a second-year phil of mind course, and the topic is Dennett's case against qualia — specifically the two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn. For anyone rusty: Chase insists the coffee tastes different to him now than it used to (same coffee, same brewing, it just tastes worse), while Sanborn insists the coffee tastes exactly the same as it always did and what's changed is him — his palate, his standards, whatever. Both are perfectly confident, and Dennett's move is to argue there's no way, even in principle, for either of them to settle which description fits his own inner life, and that this is meant to break the philosophers' notion of a quale (private, intrinsic, ineffable, immediately known).

Fifty minutes, about fifteen students. They've technically done the reading but I'd bet half of them skimmed it on the bus. They've already seen inverted spectra and the knowledge argument in lecture, so they'll arrive primed to defend qualia to the death.

Plan the section for me — don't just re-explain the paper at me. I want:

- the order the pieces have to go in, and what depends on what. E.g. do you nail down the defining features qualia are supposed to have before touching the coffee, or after? Does the distinction between "the taste itself changed" and "my reaction to the taste changed" need to be in place before Chase and Sanborn are even intelligible as two different claims?
- the example that carries the hour. Is it the tasters themselves, or should I warm them up with a simpler everyday case first (a song you loved at fifteen that's unlistenable now — did the song change or did you?) so Chase and Sanborn land as the payoff? Argue for your pick, including what the warm-up case must NOT accidentally smuggle in.
- the misconceptions they'll walk in with and the exact point in the sequence to kill each one. My guesses: that Dennett is denying taste is like anything at all, i.e. denying consciousness exists; that this is just memory skepticism and Chase could settle it by digging up an old diary entry; and that one taster is obviously right and the other is just confused, so the argument never gets off the ground. Where does each of those get headed off rather than festering till the end?
- one closing question that seperates "they can parrot the thought experiment" from "they see why Dennett thinks the quale concept is broken, not merely undetectable."
- what to openly skip at this level and say we're skipping (heterophenomenology? the rest of the intuition pumps?).

Also the prof wants pushback time, so flag the spot in the plan where the smart qualia-defender objection will surface, and what I should concede there vs where I hold the line.
