I am taking a seminar this term on the ethics of artificial minds, and our final assignment asks each of us to evaluate some theory of moral status for digital minds. Before I can evaluate anything, though, I realized I do not actually have a standard in hand: the readings all argue for or against particular theories, but nobody states what a theory in this area is even supposed to accomplish.

So: please write out the specification. What would a theory of the moral status of digital minds have to do to count as adequate? I want the full requirements list, with each requirement justified by the case or consideration that motivates it, and a judgment about which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are the preferences of one particular camp.

Here are the things I think the list has to cover, but feel free to add, cut, or reorganize:

- The phenomena the theory must explain: e.g., why pain and pleasure seem central to our ordinary judgments, why we extend some concern to entities that merely behave as if distressed, and whether the theory has to explain that pattern or can simply overrule it.
- The cases it must deliver a verdict on, including the hard ones: a digital system that is behaviorally sophisticated but arguably has no inner experience at all; a system that is a bit-for-bit copy of another (does making a thousand copies create a thousand times the moral weight?); a digital mind running at very slow speeds, or in discrete steps, or intermittently paused; a system whose suffering is easy to switch on and off; and the limiting case of simple systems like thermostats, where the theory should say *something* principled rather than shrug.
- The distinctions it must not collapse: for instance, between having moral status at all and having a great deal of it; between being a moral patient and being a moral agent; between a system that matters morally and a system that merely matters to someone; and between uncertainty about whether a system is sentient and the conclusion that it therefore does not matter.
- The constraints from outside philosophy: what the theory must be consistent with from cognitive science and neuroscience (can it assume substrate-independence as settled, or must it remain neutral?), and whether it may rest on contested theories of consciousness or must work across several of them.
- The meta-level requirements: whether the theory must be implementable in principle (could we ever actually test for what it says matters?), whether it must avoid collapsing into our anthropomorphic biases, and how it should handle the fact that digital minds could be radically unlike anything in our moral training data.

Two specific requests on top of the list itself. First, if some subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — say, a requirement of empirical testability combined with a requirement to respect theories of consciousness that make untestable claims — then identify the conflict explicitly, since that seems like a result in its own right. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the currently popular approaches (roughly: the view that the right functional organization is sufficient, the view that biological embodiment is necessary, and the view that we simply cannot know). I will do the actual scoring of contenders against the list myself; the deliverable I need is the standard of evaluation, not the verdicts.
