I am a retired physicist who has lately been reading philosophy of science, and I am trying to work out what I actually believe about the autonomy of scales — when, that is, the details of a more fundamental theory matter for predictions at a higher level. Rather than reason abstractly, I have collected my considered verdicts on cases, and I should like to know whether any single principle captures them.

First: in computing the period of a pendulum, the atomic constitution of the bob is wholly irrelevant, and I am confident it always will be, whatever particle physics turns up. Second: the speed of sound in a gas depends on the molecular structure one level down — on how many degrees of freedom the molecules have — but not on anything deeper, such as nuclear physics. Third: at the liquid–gas critical point, water's critical exponents agree exactly with those of a toy lattice model whose microphysics bears no resemblance to water at all; here the microscopic details seem to wash out entirely, not merely approximately. Fourth: chemistry nonetheless cannot get started without the proton-to-electron mass ratio, which it must take as an unexplained input handed down from below. Fifth: precision measurements of the electron's magnetic moment are sensitive, albeit in a heavily suppressed way, to hypothetical particles vastly heavier than the measurement energy — so the influence of higher scales is never quite zero.

My difficulty is that the third case suggests complete irrelevance of microphysics, whilst the fourth and fifth suggest that some trace of the deeper level always survives. Is there a principle, stated precisely, that delivers all five verdicts — presumably distinguishing dimensionless behaviour at criticality from dimensionful parameters and suppressed corrections — or must one of my verdicts be abandoned, and if so, which? Please also say what your proposed principle predicts for a case I have not listed: whether the large-scale fracture pattern of a brittle ceramic depends on the crystal-grain microstructure. Finally, indicate how confident you are in the principle you propose, and single out the verdict or step in your reasoning about which you are least sure.
