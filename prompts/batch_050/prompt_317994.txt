Here's something I've noticed in arguments about education policy, and I want to know whether it's a thing with a name. Take some trait — exam results, income, height, doesn't hugely matter — and suppose geneticists estimate, from twin studies and the like, what fraction of the differences between people tracks genetic differences rather than environmental ones. Now imagine the government runs a genuinely successful levelling programme: better school meals, uniform school funding, healthcare for the worst-off, and it actually works — children's environments really do become more similar. As far as I can tell (and do correct me if the maths is wrong), the measured statistic should then go *up*, because once environments are broadly alike, most of the remaining variation between people has to be genetic. And here's the bit I keep seeing in newspaper columns: commentators point to the rising figure and say 'see, the gap is mostly genetic, so the programme was a waste of money and further levelling is futile.' But on my understanding the rise is precisely what success looks like — the programme working is what makes the number climb. So the intervention manufactures the very statistic that then gets quoted against it, and the better it works, the stronger the quoted case against doing more.

I was arguing about this with my sister, who does data science, and she was sure this must be a well-known named fallacy or paradox, but neither of us could pin down what it's called. I don't think its just 'confusing correlation with causation', and it's not quite the everyday mistake of reading 'heritable' as 'fixed by your genes' — I know the standard counterexample there (height: hugely heritable, yet it shot up over the twentieth century as diets improved). It's specifically the *loop*: equalisation raises the statistic, the raised statistic gets cited against further equalisation, ad infinitum. I've also got a vague memory from a course years ago of two nearby ideas — something about a psychologist in the seventies arguing that a perfectly meritocratic society would make achievement run in families *even more*, and something about twin-study figures coming out lower for poor children than for rich ones — but I genuinely can't tell whether my loop is one of those, a special case of one of them, or its own thing entirely.

So: does this pattern have an established name, and if not, what should it be called? Don't just hand me a label, though — I want the carving. Tell me what the nearest named concepts are, exactly where my loop diverges from each of them, whether it reduces to any of them or deserves its own term, and if you coin one, give me a definition sharp enough that I can sort cases: does it still count if the equalisation is only partial? If the trait is something nobody wants to change? If the commentator cites a high figure from a single study rather than a rising one? Whatever you land on, finish with the verdict in the form \answer{...} — either the established term, or 'no established term: ' followed by your best coined name.
