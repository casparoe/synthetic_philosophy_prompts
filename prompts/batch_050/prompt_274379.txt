I recently joined the steering committee of a regional community land trust whose chapters are debating whether one valley chapter should become an independent organization. The dispute has made the problem of self-determination feel less abstract to me: before anyone can decide whether a group may govern itself, someone must first decide who counts as that group and which boundaries define the relevant electorate.

I facilitate a monthly political-philosophy workshop for 18 adults, with 90 minutes available. Design a complete classroom activity that makes participants feel the force of this boundary problem. Use a fictional federation containing several regions, districts, and smaller enclaves, with preference cards arranged so that the same population produces different outcomes depending on whether the vote is held federation-wide, regionally, or within a smaller proposed unit. At least one minority should become a majority after a boundary is redrawn, and at least one successful secession should leave behind a new minority that can plausibly demand secession in turn.

Participants should discover the problem through decisions and votes, not through an introductory lecture. Provide the setup, roles, maps or materials, eligibility rules, petition procedure, voting sequence, and a non-monetary payoff system that gives participants reason to care about both autonomy and continued access to shared institutions. The procedure should allow some strategic boundary-drawing, but not so much freedom that the activity collapses into chaos.

End with a structured debrief that connects what happened to Scotland, Catalonia, and Quebec at a structural level without assuming detailed knowledge of those cases or relitigating their current politics. Explain where participants could resist the apparent conclusions: by privileging the existing state, the local majority, affected parties, constitutional rules, or negotiated consent—and what each response costs. Also identify what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run and which features of real secession disputes it cannot reproduce.
