I'm in a grad seminar on epistemic logic and I keep ending up defending a position that my classmates think is hopeless, so I want to stress-test it properly. The position: the problem of logical omniscience is overblown, because possible-worlds models of knowledge and belief were never meant to describe actual cognizers. They're idealizations, like frictionless planes in physics or perfectly competitive markets in econ, and complaining that the model's agents know all logical truths and all the consequences of what they know is like complaining that the inclined-plane model doesn't predict where the box actually stops. The right response to closure failures in real reasoners, on my view, is to keep the idealized model for the questions where the idealization is harmless and reach for something else when it isn't -- no need for impossible worlds or awareness filters or any of the patchwork fixes.

So: what would count against this? I want the defeaters, not just the standard textbook complaint that real people aren't deductively closed, since that's exactly what my position shrugs off. Some things I suspect belong on the list but I can't rank them myself: the point that a good idealization comes with a story about what error it introduces and when the error is small (physicists can say when friction matters), and it's not clear the epistemic logician has anything comparable; the worry that the model was actually sold as doing normative or explanatory work, not just descriptive work, and the idealization defense might concede ground I don't want to concede; and the possibility that the closure failures aren't noise around an idealized core but are systematic in a way that suggests the core itself is wrong. What else belongs on the list? For each defeater, tell me whether a sophisticated defender of my position would themselves concede it counts against the view or would wave it away, and rank the whole thing from fatal to merely awkward.

Also worth addressing: is my frictionless-plane analogy even apt, or is there a disanalogy doing hidden work -- e.g., that physics idealizations idealize the *system*, whereas epistemic logic arguably idealizes the *subject matter itself* (what belief is), which might be a different and shakier move? And if you conclude the position is well insulated precisely because it can always retreat to "it's just an idealization for some purposes," say so, and tell me whether that's a strength or whether it's the kind of unfalsifiability that should make me less confident rather than more. I'd rather be talked out of this before I put it in a term paper.
