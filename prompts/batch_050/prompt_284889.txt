I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind and cognitive psychiatry, and I am writing the final exam. I want one essay question on the two-factor theory of delusion that students can answer in about ninety minutes, and I need your help designing it. You should take on the role of a philosopher of mind with real expertise in the literature on delusions — someone who knows the standard terrain (Maher's one-factor rationality account, the Davies-and-Coltheart two-factor framework as applied to Capgras and kindred monothematic delusions, and the ongoing dispute about whether delusions are genuine beliefs) well enough to know which parts of it students merely recite and which parts they actually have to think through. Please think carefully about where the genuine crux lies before drafting anything, and write everything in American English.

Here is the problem with most exam questions on this material: a student can get full marks by reciting that the first factor is an anomalous experience explaining the delusion's content and the second factor is a reasoning or belief-evaluation deficit explaining why the hypothesis is not rejected, plus one memorized application to Capgras via face processing and familiarity. I want a question that makes that recitation insufficient. My current idea is to build the question around a constructed case of mirrored-self misidentification — a patient who insists the person in the mirror is a stranger who mimics his movements, yet continues to use the mirror unproblematically when shaving and shows no tendency to address or avoid the supposed stranger. I like this case because the behavioral disconnect forces the doxastic question (is this state even a belief, and by what criteria?) in a way Capgras usually doesn't, and because the doxastic question in turn threatens to change the second factor's job description: if the state is not a belief, it is unclear that a belief-evaluation deficit is what's needed to explain its persistence. I want students to have to construct the best two-factor explanation of the case — specifying what each factor would have to be and what evidence would tell them apart — and then to assess whether the case's behavioral profile undermines, or merely complicates, that explanation, and what a one-factor theorist would make of the same data.

Please deliver three things:

1. The exam question itself, phrased as students would see it, with any sub-parts and an indication of how the ninety minutes should be divided.

2. A model answer of roughly 600 words, written at the level of an excellent student in the course. It should take a defensible position rather than survey every option, but it should show awareness of the main competing moves — for instance, treating the state as an imagining or some other non-doxastic attitude, or defending belief status on functional grounds despite the behavioral evidence.

3. A marking scheme out of 25, built around five must-hit points with the marks each carries, stating explicitly what distinguishes an A answer from a B answer in terms of specific argumentative moves (not vague gestures like depth or clarity), and noting for each point the most common way students go wrong — for example, treating the two factors as sequential stages rather than distinct explanatory roles, or assuming the second factor must be a bias rather than a deficit.

One request about your stance: please be genuinely self-critical rather than accommodating. If my case is a poor probe — for instance, if the behavioral details I have stipulated don't actually bear on the doxastic question the way I assume, or if mirrored-self misidentification is the wrong vehicle because the two-factor literature already handles it in a way that defuses my intended difficulty — say so plainly and either fix the case or tell me to pick a different one, rather than politely building an exam on a confused premise.
