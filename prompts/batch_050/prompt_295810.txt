A friend of mine from a philosophy of physics reading group put an argument to me last week that he says shows ordinary logic breaks down at the quantum level, and I've been turning it over without finding the step I want to refuse. Here it is as he gave it. (1) If two things are genuinely distinct, there must be some property one has and the other lacks; distinctness cannot be a bare, unexplained fact about the world. (2) Electrons, being all of one species, share every intrinsic property: the same mass, the same charge, the same spin magnitude, and nothing else intrinsic to distinguish them. (3) The remaining candidate properties are relational or state-dependent -- position relative to something, momentum, spin along an axis -- and in an entangled two-electron state, such as the spin singlet, neither electron has its own definite profile of these: each is in exactly the same reduced state, and every relation one bears to anything, the other bears as well. (4) So any two electrons have all the same properties there are. (5) By (1) and (4), there are never two electrons; there is at most one. (6) But chemistry, spectroscopy, and the Pauli exclusion principle all presuppose enormous numbers of electrons, and quantum statistics only makes sense given particle counts. (7) Therefore the principle in (1) -- and with it the classical logic of identity, since (1) is just half of what identity means -- fails for the quantum world, and we need a non-classical logic in which things can be distinct without differing in any way.

The conclusion seems to prove too much to me. I don't want to give up on many-electron physics, and I'm also reluctant to accept that the world simply contains distinctions with no basis whatsoever, since that seems to make numerical difference a kind of miracle. My friend insists that every premise is either a truism about identity or a textbook claim about entangled states, so the only exits are revising logic or revising physics. I suspect the real action is somewhere in (3) -- in particular, I have a nagging feeling that the two electrons in the singlet do stand in a relation to one another that each fails to stand in to itself -- but I can't tell whether that's a genuine difference-making property or just a verbal trick, and I don't know what I'd be conceding if I built an exit on it.

So: which step should I reject, and why did it look innocent? If the answer is that I have to pick a price to pay, please lay out and cost the main exits I've seen gestured at -- granting particles a primitive thisness that grounds their distinctness without any qualitative difference; allowing irreflexive relations to count as weakly discerning, so that (4) fails even when no monadic property differs; or taking the argument at face value and concluding that particles just aren't individuals to which identity applies. For each, tell me what it commits me to elsewhere, and whether it leaves (1) standing in any form worth keeping.
