I am a teaching assistant for an undergraduate philosophy of language course, and I am grading responses to the following exam question (worth 20 points):

"Explain Grice's distinction between what is said and what is conversationally implicated. Your answer should state the Cooperative Principle and the maxims, give one example of a conversational implicature generated by exploiting a maxim, and identify two features that distinguish conversational implicatures from the literal content of what is said."

Here is the student's answer, reproduced in full:

"Grice distinguishes what a speaker says from what the speaker merely implicates. What is said corresponds closely to the conventional meaning of the sentence uttered: fixing the reference of indexicals and resolving ambiguities is required before we have a complete what-is-said. What is conversationally implicated, by contrast, is no part of the proposition expressed; it is conveyed because the hearer assumes the speaker is observing the Cooperative Principle, roughly the requirement that one make one's conversational contribution fit the accepted purpose and current stage of the exchange. From this principle Grice derives four maxims: Quantity (be as informative as the exchange requires), Quality (do not say what you believe false or lack evidence for), Relation (be relevant), and Manner (be perspicuous, orderly, brief).

Implicatures arise when a speaker flouts a maxim. To flout a maxim is to violate the Cooperative Principle: the speaker is being uncooperative in an overt way, and the hearer, noticing this, casts about for some further meaning the speaker must have intended. For example, a professor asked to write a recommendation letter for a candidate for a philosophy lectureship writes only that the candidate's command of English is excellent and that his attendance has been regular. By giving far less information than the situation demands, the professor flouts Quantity, and the reader works out the implicature that the candidate is weak at philosophy.

Two features distinguish conversational implicature from what is said. First, implicatures are cancellable: the professor could add that the candidate is, for all that, a superb philosopher, without contradicting anything actually said. Second, implicatures are detachable: the same literal content expressed in different words need not carry the same implicature, so the professor could have conveyed the identical content in a different form of words without implying anything negative about the candidate. Implicature thus lets speakers communicate more than they strictly say."
