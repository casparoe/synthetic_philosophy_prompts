I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics working on the foundations of quantum mechanics, and I'm trying to pin down the exact role of the Born rule in the Everett interpretation. As I understand it, decoherence solves the preferred-basis problem by dynamically selecting a quasiclassical basis, and once branching is well-defined, every outcome of a measurement occurs in some branch. Since all outcomes therefore occur with certainty, it seems to me that the only remaining quantitative question is how often each outcome type occurs across the multiverse, which should be settled simply by counting branches. On that picture, the Born rule looks like a purely phenomenological summary of branch statistics rather than anything that needs to be derived or postulated. So why do Everettians like Deutsch, Wallace, and Saunders treat the justification of the Born rule as a substantive open problem requiring decision-theoretic or envariance-based arguments? What work is a probability rule even supposed to do in a theory where nothing is left to chance?
