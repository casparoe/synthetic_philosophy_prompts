ok so I'm a novelist working on a book where one of the POV chapters is written by a Tupinambá man who's been brought to France around 1562, the same crowd Montaigne ran into at Rouen and wrote about in "Of Cannibals." I need the actual chapter and I want it to work as a satire, so I figured I'd get a draft of it from you and then mess with it.

Write it as his letter home, maybe 600-700 words, deadpan the whole way through. He's genuinely reporting his travels to his people the way a serious traveler would, and the comedy has to come entirely from the inversion: Montaigne's whole move in the essay is to point out that we call them barbarians for eating their dead enemies while we do things that are arguably worse and just don't see them because they're OUR customs. So his letter should flip that. He marvels at stone buildings and writing, sure, but the stuff he finds truly savage should be things like: grown bearded men kneeling to a child because of who the child's father was, beggars starving at the gates of men who are literally too full to finish their dinners, and a justice system that torments living bodies slowly in public -- compared to which, from where he stands, eating a brave enemy after death as an honor looks downright reasonable.

The key thing for my book: his judgments all have to follow from HIS values (valor in war, generosity, sharing, keeping your word) and stay internally consistent. He can't suddenly talk like a Frenchman. No winking at the reader, no moment where the mask slips and you can tell the author thinks cannibalism is gross actually. And don't put any quotes from Montaigne in it or any made-up quotes from real people -- paraphrase everything, I don't want to deal with fact-checking citations, this is fiction.

Also, and this is the part I care most about: by the last paragraph the reader shouldn't be totally sure anymore who's being judged, him or us. Montaigne does this thing where the mirror ends up pointed at you. If the letter lands there, I've got my chapter.
