I'm in a philosophy of death seminar this term and we've spent like six weeks on the Epicurean argument (roughly: death can't be bad for you, because when you're alive death hasn't happened yet, and once it has happened there's no "you" around for it to be bad for) versus the standard deprivationist reply (death is bad for you because it cuts you off from goods you would otherwise have had). We keep going in circles, so for my final paper I want to draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a proper adversarial collaboration.

Here's what I need in it:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. I suspect both sides can sign more than they admit — e.g. that being dead isn't painful or unpleasant for the dead person, that something can be bad for a person without being experienced by them is the actual contested premise (or is it?), that a reversible coma case and death aren't obviously analogous, stuff like that. Only include things a thoughtful Epicurean and a thoughtful deprivationist would genuinely both accept, not strawman concessions.

2. A disagreements table with the exact points where they split, each phrased so that side would accept the wording of its own position. I especially want the timing problem in here: the Epicurean presses "at what time is death bad for the person who dies — not before, not after, so when?" and the deprivationist has to say whether harms can be untimed or comparative-between-lives, and whether that's coherent or just dodging.

3. The symmetry argument as a named crux: Lucretius-style, why is the nothingness after death worse than the nothingness before you were born? Write down what the deprivationist's response commits them to (I think something about it being rational to care about the future differently than the past?) and whether the Epicurean can accept that response without giving up their whole position.

4. For each crux, one concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance — like, what would the Epicurean have to say about a 20-year-old dying versus an 80-year-old dying, and would the deprivationist prediction actually differ there or do they converge in practice.

End with a short section saying whether any of the disagreement turned out to be verbal once the formulations were pinned down, and which part. Don't adjudicate who's right — I want the document, not a verdict.
