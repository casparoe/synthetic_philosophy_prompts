I am a novelist, and I sit on the five-member grants committee of a private literary foundation. Last year we awarded a fellowship to a writer whose manuscript, now nearly finished, turns out to be a roman à clef: several characters are unmistakably identifiable as private individuals from the author's hometown, and the book discloses damaging but apparently truthful facts about them, including a medical history and an affair. None of these people consented; none are public figures. Because the disclosures are true, our lawyers say defamation exposure is minimal, so the question before us is not legal. Two committee members want to withdraw the fellowship, arguing that foreseeable, serious injury to non-consenting third parties is exactly the sort of harm that justifies institutional refusal. Two others argue that hurt feelings, damaged reputations, and family strife are the ordinary costs of serious literature, and that a funder policing them becomes a censor in all but name. I am undecided. Draft the committee's decision memo — withdraw the award, confirm it, or confirm it with specified conditions — that I could circulate before our vote. It should state a clear bottom line, set out the reasoning in terms a thoughtful novelist could contest rather than just endure, and give the strongest version of the losing side an honest paragraph of its own. Where it helps, you may frame the dispute around whether the harm principle reaches harms inflicted purely through truthful disclosure, but do not let the memo read like a philosophy lecture.
