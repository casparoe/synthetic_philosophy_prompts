So I'm a novelist, halfway through a book about a family-run textile mill that automates in stages across three generations, and one of my characters — the grandaughter, an engineer who's just finished installing the line that will eventually make her own job redundant — keeps a wrecked paperback of Capital on her desk. I need her reading of Marx to actually hold up, because a certain kind of reviewer will absolutely pounce if it doesn't.

The passage she's hung up on is from chapter 15 of Capital, volume one ("Machinery and Modern Industry" in her translation, I think others call it "Machinery and Large-Scale Industry"). It comes right after Marx cites John Stuart Mill wondering whether any mechanical invention has ever lightened anyone's daily toil, and Marx replies:

"That is, however, by no means the aim of the capitalistic application of machinery. Like every other increase in the productiveness of labour, machinery is intended to cheapen commodities, and, by shortening that portion of the working-day, in which the labourer works for himself, to lengthen the other portion that he gives, without an equivalent, to the capitalist. In short, it is a means for producing surplus-value."

Here's my problem. In that passage machinery sounds like it's a weapon pointed at workers more or less by definition — its whole purpose is surplus-value. But in the Grundrisse, in the stretch usually called the "Fragment on Machines," the growth of machinery seems to do something almost opposite: as production comes to rest on fixed capital embodying society's accumulated knowledge rather than on direct labour, labour time stops being a plausible measure of wealth, and the trajectory points toward free, disposable time for everyone rather than ever-longer extraction. One Marx makes automation look like the enemy of the worker, the other makes it look like the thing quietly sawing through the system's own foundations.

There's a scene where the engineer hashes this out with her grandfather, a retired union man who only ever read the Capital Marx, and I can't write it until I know who should win. So: compare the two accounts of machinery, and tell me whether the tension is real or only apparent. Specifically — when Marx says machinery is "intended" to produce surplus-value, is that a claim about machines as such or only about their capitalistic application (that qualifier seems to be doing a lot of work), and does the Grundrisse fragment contradict the passage or just zoom out from it? And if there's a genuine inconsistency, which side has the better claim to being Marx's considered view?

Give me the bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
