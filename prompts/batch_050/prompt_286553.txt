My friend and I have been arguing for months about what she calls Ulysses contracts — like when Odysseus had himself tied to the mast so he could hear the sirens without sailing into the rocks. She thinks locking your future self in is one of the smartest things a person, or a whole country, can do. I think it's basically your past self bossing around a future self who might know better by then. We keep applying it to everything: apps that charge you money if you skip the gym, telling the bartender not to serve you no matter what you say later, constitutions with clauses that can never be amended.

Instead of going in circles again we want to write up a joint statement, adversarial-collaboration style. Draft it for us:

1. The claims we can both sign. I suspect these include: willpower genuinely fades in the moment of temptation; a commitment is only doing work if the later self would actually want to back out (otherwise theres nothing to bind); and reversibility matters — a commitment you can undo with a phone call is a different beast from one you can't undo at all.

2. The exact points where we still disagree, each phrased so the person holding that view would accept the wording. I think one is: is the tempted future self the same person in the sense that matters, or is the precommitting self protecting someone who sort of no longer exists? Another: does it matter whether the future self's new preference is a considered change of mind versus a moment of weakness, and can you ever reliably tell the difference from the inside?

3. The cruxes — the subquestions where getting an answer would actually move one of us. For each, give a concrete case we both agree bears on it and write down what each of us predicts about it. Please use these two cases rather than the tired diet examples: (a) someone signs up for a service that emails their embarrassing search history to their mother if they ever gamble again, and ten years later, genuinely reformed and totally calm about it, they want out of the contract; (b) a country whose constitution permanently forbids one specific kind of law, and two generations later most citizens have thoughtful, non-panicky reasons for wanting to at least debate it.

Keep it neutral, don't sneak a verdict into the framing. And if part of our fight turns out to be about words rather than substance, say which part and why.
