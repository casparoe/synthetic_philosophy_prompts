I am taking an undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of language, and we have just begun Frege's 1892 essay "On Sense and Reference," which opens with his famous puzzle about identity statements. Our assigned course reader (an anthology with editorial introductions written by the editors) reproduces the following passage, in which Frege has just introduced the sense/reference distinction using the example of the morning star and the evening star:

"The sense of a proper name is grasped by everybody who is sufficiently familiar with the language or totality of designations to which it belongs; but this serves to illuminate only a single aspect of the reference, supposing it to have one. Comprehensive knowledge of the reference would require us to say immediately whether any given sense belongs to it. To such knowledge we never attain."

Frege attaches a footnote to this remark in which he discusses the name Aristotle and concedes that different speakers may take its sense differently, but I will not quote the footnote here because it is exactly what is at issue below. The reader's editorial introduction glosses the whole passage as follows:

"Frege's position here is that the sense of a proper name is the description a speaker happens to associate with it: one speaker's sense for a name might be one identifying description, another speaker's quite a different one. Since sense determines reference, two speakers who associate different senses with the same name will, strictly speaking, be referring to different things, and Frege therefore regards this variability of sense as a standing defect of natural language that makes everyday communication about a person only approximate. He also claims that complete knowledge of a thing would consist in knowing which senses belong to it, and since he says we never attain this, our knowledge of any object is confined to a single aspect of it. Frege introduces the sense/reference distinction in order to explain how a true identity statement can still be informative: the two names flanking the identity sign share a reference but differ in sense."

The final sentence matches what our lecturer said about the identity puzzle, so I am inclined to trust it; it is the middle of the gloss where I lose the thread, since it seems to sit badly with Frege's own remark, in the quoted sentence, that the sense of a proper name is grasped by everyone sufficiently familiar with the language. Is the gloss a fair summary of the passage? Finally, does the gloss's closing explanation of why identity statements can be informative still stand after your corrections, and if you were writing a one-paragraph replacement introduction for a student meeting Frege for the first time, what would it say instead?
