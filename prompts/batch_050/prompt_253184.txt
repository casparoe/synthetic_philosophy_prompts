So my phil of mind reading group did Malcolm and Dennett on dreams last week and someone (fine, it was me) produced the following argument, which pretty clearly proves too much, but I cant find the weak joint. Help me take it apart.

Quick background so we're on the same page: Malcolm's line is roughly that a dream report isn't a report of an experience you had while asleep -- your tendency to tell the story on waking is all the "dream" ever was. Dennett's cassette theory makes this vivid: maybe the whole dream narrative gets composed at the moment of waking and loaded straight into memory, so nothing was experienced during the night at all. My argument tries to run their engine and ends up running off the road:

(1) A report counts as describing a genuine experience only if there could in principle be some evidence bearing on its accuracy beyond the reporter's own disposition to produce the report. If the disposition to say it is the whole story, there's nothing for the report to be right about.

(2) For dreams, no such independent evidence is even possible. However good sleep science gets, a complete neural record of the night couldn't distinguish "the experience happened during sleep" from "the memory was written at waking," since both would leave the same traces by morning. (This is just Dennett's point generalized.)

(3) So dream reports don't describe experiences; "I dreamt that p" really just means "on waking I was disposed to tell the p-story." Malcolm wins.

(4) But (1) is perfectly general -- it applies to any memory-based report of a past experience, dreamt or waking.

(5) Take any fleeting private experience that leaves no external traces and that you mention to no one at the time: a tune running through your head on a walk, a passing flash of irritation at a stranger on the bus. Later on, the only evidence bearing on your report of it is your disposition to produce the report -- exactly the situation as in the dream case.

(6) So those reports don't describe experiences either. "The tune was going through my head" means only "I'm now disposed to say it was."

(7) But that's absurd. Either the tune was going through your head or it wasn't, and your later say-so has nothing to do with it. So something above is wrong.

Three things I want:

First, name the premise or step you'd reject and explain why it looked fine in the first place. I want a diagnosis, not a vote -- the argument survives a casual once-over, and I want to know exactly where it stops doing so.

Second, price the rejection. If you go after (1), doesn't that take Malcolm's and Dennett's whole anti-dream-experience project down with it, since (1) is basically their engine? And if you go after (2), you owe me a story about what possible evidence could settle what happened during a night's sleep -- what would that even look like?

Third, a wrinkle I can't stop thinking about: lucid dreamers in sleep labs have apparently signaled with pre-agreed eye movements from inside REM sleep, which sure looks like exactly the kind of independent evidence (2) declares impossible. Suppose that rescues dream reports. Does it do anything for the tune in (5), or are all my traceless waking experiences still condemned, and is that now just a cost we swallow?

For the record: I'd much rather keep the tune than keep the argument, but if there's an honest way to hang on to (1) I'd like it.
