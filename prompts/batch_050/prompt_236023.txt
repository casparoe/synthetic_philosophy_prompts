I've been mulling over Thomson's lamp for weeks, and I've reached the point where I can recite the standard reply — the switching schedule only describes moments before the two minutes are up, so it simply doesn't say whether the lamp is on or off at the mark — but I can't feel why that's right, and I want to understand it rather than recite it. What keeps undoing me is a variant I came up with: instead of a lamp, a machine flips a coin at half a minute, then at three-quarters, then at seven-eighths, and so on — each flip a perfect half-turn, but taking half as long as the flip before. By the two-minute mark the flips have shrunk to nothing and the coin is lying still on the table showing something; the world doesn't permit it to end in a blur, and yet the schedule seems to underwrite heads and tails equally well. Please explain, as concretely as you can, how a process can plainly end somewhere while everything that happens before the end genuinely fails to determine where it ends; what sort of further fact would have to be added to close the gap; and whether the coin is really a different case from the lamp, or just the same case in a form that's harder to be glib about.
