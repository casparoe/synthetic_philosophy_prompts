I'm writing a philosophy of language exam and I want one question that's a specification question rather than a defend-a-view question. Help me draft it by writing the desiderata list a unified theory of indicative vs counterfactual conditionals has to meet.

Context for what I mean: every intro course teaches the Oswald/Kennedy pair -- "if Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did" (plausibly true) vs "if Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have" (probably false) -- and then the literature splits over whether the difference is two different conditional connectives, one connective plus epistemic context, or one connective plus tense/mood doing the work. Before my students score any of those views I want them to have the full job description.

So: write out what an adequate theory of the divide must deliver. The cases it has to get the right verdicts on (the Adams-style minimal pairs, Sobel sequences like the "if Alice hadn't come it would have been fine, but if Bob hadn't come it would've been a disaster" reversal pattern, Gibbard-style standoff cases where an indicative and a counterfactual seem to diverge between two well-placed observers, future-directed indicatives vs their counterfactual correlates). The distinctions it can't collapse (truth vs warranted assertability, temporal backtracking vs the metaphysical reading, the mood marking vs the modal claim). The cross-linguistic fact it has to at least be consistent with -- that many languages mark the counterfactual with the same morphology they use for past + imperfective + modal, i.e. the "fake past" pattern, so any theory that makes the divide purely about English "would" is in trouble. For each desideratum, say what phenomenon motivates it and who'd reject it as a mere prejudice of their camp.

Two things I especially want:

1. Flag any pair of desiderata that look jointly unsatisfiable or at least in real tension. My suspicion is that "give one semantics covering both" and "respect the intuition that Gibbard observers can both speak truly" can't both be met without going relativist somewhere, but you tell me.

2. Here's a test case I made up that I haven't seen discussed, and I want your verdict on whether it belongs on the requirements list or is a dud. Suppose a detective concludes, on solid evidence, "if the butler didn't do it, it was the gardener." The scene is then faked and reopened years later, and the same detective says "if the butler hadn't done it, it would've been the gardener" -- which now sounds like a confession that the frame-up would have pointed at the gardener. Same speaker, same suspects, and the two conditionals diverge in what they commit him to about evidence vs about the crime. Does a theory need a desideratum covering diachronic pairs like this (call it the reopened-case constraint), or does it reduce to something already on the list?

Keep it as a numbered requirements list with a sentence or two of motivation each, mark non-negotiables vs camp preferences, and end with one paragraph on which single requirement you think knocks out the most standard theories. No scoring the big views against it, that's the students job. Casual tone fine, this is going in an exam doc not a journal.
