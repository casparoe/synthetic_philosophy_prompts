So I got into an argument at a pub night with some friends from my stats program about the St. Petersburg paradox -- you know, the coin-flip game where the payout doubles each round so the expected value is infinite and nobody will pay more than twenty bucks to play. I thought I was being clever and invented a variant: same setup, flip until the first heads, but the payout on round n is 2^n/n dollars, AND it alternates sign -- if the first heads lands on an odd-numbered flip you win that amount, if it lands on an even-numbered flip you *owe* that amount. When I sat down to compute the expected value, each round contributes 1/n with alternating plus and minus, and here's the thing that broke my brain: depending on how I group or order the terms when I add them up, I get different answers. One grouping gives me about 0.69, my friend reordered the same terms and got a different finite number, and another arrangement apparently blows up to infinity. So it's not that the expected value is huge or infinite like in St. Petersburg -- it's like the expected value just... isn't a thing? There is no number. Meanwhile "maximize expected value" is supposed to be the foundation of rational choice, and here it doesn't even output an instruction. Should I pay a dollar to play this game? Refuse to play it at any price? The framework gives me silence.

Then I realized I'd seen what might be the same shape of problem in a totally different place. People arguing about infinite universes in ethics point out that if there are infinitely many happy people and infinitely many miserable people, the "total amount of good" depends on how you march through them and add things up -- pair them off one-to-one and it cancels, group the happy ones first and it's paradise. Same vibe: no well-defined total, so no verdict. But maybe that's a different beast, since there it's about aggregating welfare and not about probabilities at all.

So my actual questions: does the phenomenon in my coin game -- expected value failing to exist not by being infinite but by depending on summation order -- have an established name, and is there a literature on it? How exactly does it differ from St. Petersburg, where the EV is infinite but at least *determinate*? And is the infinite-ethics case the same problem in different clothes or a genuinely distinct one? I'm not just fishing for a label -- I want to know where the edges of the phenomenon are (is it about unbounded payoffs? alternating signs? infinity per se?) and what, if anything, decision theorists say an agent should actually *do* when the expected value is undefined, since "refuse to compute" isn't a policy. If there's no settled answer to that last part, tell me what the live options are.
