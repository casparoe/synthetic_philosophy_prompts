I'm a software engineer at a company that builds dispatch software for regional emergency services. Our triage module ranks calls when resources are scarce, and right now it applies a simple rule: when crews can't reach everyone, send them where they'll save the most lives. Our ethics advisory panel has to re-approve that rule next month, and a dispute has broken out that I've been asked to write up.

The trigger was a concrete scenario from our testing: one crew, two simultaneous incidents — a collapsed scaffold with five trapped workers in one direction, a house fire with one trapped resident in the other. The current rule sends the crew to the five every time. One panel member objects that this makes the lone resident's survival depend entirely on arithmetic over which she had no control; her life matters as much as anyone's, and a fairer design would give each person an equal chance — say, a weighted or even coin-flip lottery — rather than letting her be automatically sacrificed whenever a larger group exists somewhere. The reply is that a lottery knowingly lets more people die in expectation, and that any individual in the larger group would reasonably protest being passed over just to make things feel fair to someone elsewhere.

Draft the memo I'll present to the panel recommending which tie-breaking rule the software should ship with: strict save-the-most, a proportional lottery, an equal-chance lottery, or some hybrid. Give a clear bottom-line recommendation with reasoning the panel can act on, and engage the strongest argument on the losing side honestly rather than waving it away. If you think the framing above rests on a confused premise — for instance, if the dispute is really about something other than what the panel thinks it is, or if no software rule can settle it — say so plainly rather than writing around the problem. Please write in American English.
