Ok so I run a little blog for people in tech (I'm a backend dev, no philosophy background, fair warning) and I keep getting into the same argument in my comments section about the so-called gender-equality paradox. That's the finding that countries which score *highest* on the usual gender-equality indexes -- the Nordics especially -- tend to have a *smaller* share of women in STEM degrees and some other fields, while less egalitarian countries often have more women in engineering. Everyone seems to read the same data as proof of whatever they already believed.

I want to make a "what do you actually think the paradox means" quiz, but done properly: sorted by reactions to cases, not by which label people find flattering. Here's what I need:

- 8 to 10 questions. Each one is a concrete hypothetical or follow-up observation, with 2 or 3 answer options. For example (just to show the flavor, don't reuse this one verbatim): a country keeps every formal barrier removed for 30 years, both parents take parental leave, toy aisles get aggressively de-gendered, and the engineer ratio still drifts *more* male over time -- what do you conclude from that?
- The quiz should be able to land people on at least these positions (feel free to rename them, my names are placeholder-y):
  1. socialization just isn't finished -- even egalitarian countries have subtler stereotype channels, so the paradox shows how deep it goes, not that it failed
  2. the paradox is basically what you'd expect if there are average differences in interests that only fully express themselves once people are rich, safe, and free to pick what they enjoy
  3. it's largely a measurement artifact -- the effect depends on how you compute the share of women in STEM and which equality index you use, so the phenomenon itself is shakier than the headlines
  4. it's about economic necessity, not preferences -- in poorer countries STEM is one of the few reliable paths to security, so women's choices there are instrumental, and rich countries just reveal what unconstrained-but-still-socialized choice looks like
  5. the paradox doesn't matter for justice at all -- justice is about removing barriers and discrimination, not about equal outcomes, so a 70/30 split under genuinely free choice is fine
- A scoring key that maps answer patterns onto those positions. I know it won't be one-answer-one-position; some answers should give partial credit to two positions, and the key should say which questions do the heavy lifting of separating, e.g., position 2 from position 4, which I suspect are easy to confuse.
- Flag any pair of positions your quiz genuinely *cannot* separate with 10 questions, and say what extra question (or what kind of real-world evidence) would be needed to split them.
- Also: if you think there's a coherent sixth position that people in these debates actually hold and my list misses it, add it as a leaf -- but only if the quiz can actually distinguish it from the others.

Hard constraint: no question may ask anything like "do you think differences are biological or cultural?" or "do you think outcomes should be equal?" Nobody answers those honestly, they answer tribally. Every question has to be a scenario with a fact pattern and a forced choice about what follows from it. The ideal quiz taker shouldn't be able to tell which position a given answer feeds into.

One more thing -- I want a short note at the end for me (not the quiz takers) on the biggest design risk: i.e., which position's supporters are most likely to take the quiz, get sorted somewhere they hate, and claim the quiz is rigged, and whether thats a flaw in the quiz or just inevitable.
