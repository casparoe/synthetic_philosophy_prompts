I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for going on fifteen years, and few moves in that ecosystem are as reliably rewarded — or as rarely cashed out — as invoking Chesterton's fence. The instance that prompts this question: in a comment thread about a proposal that my professional society abolish its rule requiring unanimous committee consent before any standing policy can be changed (a rule whose origins nobody present could recall), someone replied, in full, "Chesterton's fence," and the reply sat at the top of the thread by a wide margin. The reform was dropped from the agenda shortly after.

For the avoidance of doubt, the principle as usually glossed: do not take down a rule, norm, or institution whose purpose you cannot state; before removing it, first understand why it was put there, and remove it only once you know what it was doing. That gloss is doing a lot of work in a lot of different directions, and I want it disassembled.

Here is what I'd like. Turn the slogan into a menu of distinct, precisely stated theses it could be expressing — each one something a person could actually assert or deny. I'm especially interested in your separating at least the following families, and in adding any I've missed:

- epistemic readings, where the claim concerns what you must know before acting, and what counts as "knowing the purpose" — being able to articulate some plausible function? knowing the actual historical origin? possessing a model that would predict what breaks?
- evidential-selection readings, where the claim is that a rule's having survived is itself evidence of function, and where the strength of that evidence presumably depends on whether the selecting environment resembles the current one;
- procedural readings, where the claim concerns burden of proof and default policies rather than knowledge — for instance, that reform must beat the status quo by a margin, or that reversibility changes the calculus entirely;
- decision-theoretic readings, where the fence is really about option value, hysteresis, or the asymmetry between the cost of destroying an institution and the cost of rebuilding one.

For each reading: classify it as platitude, substantive-but-defensible, or false; and say whether, if true, it would justify what happened in my thread — that is, whether it supports deploying the slogan to end a discussion rather than to inform one. Note that the stronger readings bite hard in this case: nobody could recall the rule's origin, so if knowing why it was erected is a necessary condition for removal, the rule is effectively immortal. Say whether you take that to be a feature or a reductio.

Then two harder questions. First, the regress: fence-removal is itself an ancient and venerable practice, and so is the practice of objecting to reforms on grounds of principle. Does the fence principle apply to its own wielding, and is there a coherent way to restrict its scope so that it neither paralyzes everything nor licenses anything? Second: which reading do the people who actually deploy the slogan most likely have in mind, and is it a reading they would endorse if it were stated in full? My suspicion, after years of watching this move, is that the slogan's usefulness depends on the gap between a weak reading everyone accepts and a strong reading that does the actual argumentative work. Tell me whether you think that diagnosis is right.

A numbered list I can adapt into a comment of my own would be ideal.
