So my buddy and I rewatched A Clockwork Orange (the Kubrick one) last weekend and then got into a huge argument about it that basically lasted until 2am and we never resolved it, so I want an outside opinion.

Background: I'd just finished reading Discipline and Punish by Foucault. It opens with this absolutely grisly account of the public execution of Damiens the regicide in 1757, where the Gazette d'Amsterdam reports: "This last operation was very long, because the horses used were not accustomed to drawing; consequently, instead of four, six were needed; and when that did not suffice, they were forced, in order to cut off the wretch's thighs, to sever the sinews and hack at the joints." Foucault's point, as I understand it, is that this is how old-school sovereign power punished: on the body, in public, as a spectacle for the crowd, and kind of sloppy and theatrical about it. And then modern "discipline" works totally differently -- not by wounding bodies in public but by training them quietly, timetables, surveillance, producing "docile bodies," all that.

Here's the argument. I said the Ludovico technique in the film is basically a perfect illustration of Foucault's contrast: the state gives up on the old model (prison, the chaplain, punishment as something done TO Alex) and instead has technicians condition a reflex into him, eyes clamped open, drugged, Beethoven playing, so that his own body betrays him without anyone touching him. No spectacle, no wounds, just training. And the bit where his old droog Dim and the rival gang leader show up as POLICEMEN proves the system just absorbs everybody. My friend said I'm forcing it. His view: the film is straightforwardly on the prison chaplain's side, i.e. the humanist "a man who can't choose isn't a man anymore" thing, the state is just evil in a cartoonish way, and Foucault is the wrong lens because Foucault would say there never was some free inner Alex to violate in the first place -- the delinquent is a product of the system, so the whole free-will framing of the movie is exactly the kind of humanism Foucault is suspicious of.
