I've been reading on my own about Darwin and Turing, and about the claim that both pulled off a "strange inversion": Darwin showed how nature can produce staggering competence (navigation, camouflage, immune defense) without any comprehending designer, and Turing showed how machines can execute any effective procedure by following rules they don't comprehend. A friend and I tried to push the idea to its limit and ended up with an argument that seems to prove that nothing ever understands anything, which we can't accept. Here it is. (1) Natural selection can produce arbitrarily sophisticated behavioral competence without comprehension occurring anywhere in the process. (2) Human brains are products of natural selection, and their parts are neurons, each of which merely responds to inputs -- a Turing-style competence with no comprehension. (3) Whatever understanding is, it must be realized entirely in the causal activity of such parts, since nothing extra is added. (4) But comprehension cannot arise from parts and processes that contain none of it; competence without comprehension, stacked however deep, remains competence without comprehension. (5) Therefore no human ever genuinely understands anything. (6) Then there is no fact of the matter in virtue of which a human understands and a giant lookup table or a chatbot does not. We each have different attachments: I'd rather keep (4), since emergence of a wholly new property from its absence seems like magic to me, while my friend would sacrifice anything except (6), which she finds absurd. Which step do you reject, why did it look harmless, and what does rejecting it cost? Please keep your answer under 400 words.
