I run a reading group for graduate students split between philosophy and computer science, and our next session is on Buridan's ass — not as a historical curiosity but as the general problem of rational choice without preference: what an agent can or should do when two or more options are exactly tied by every consideration it has. The group keeps conflating distinct positions (one week someone says "just pick arbitrarily," the next someone says "real ties never occur," and both think they're agreeing), so I want a taxonomy they can use to locate themselves before the discussion starts.

Please build the taxonomy as a tree whose branches are labeled by the substantive claim that splits them, not by names of philosophers or positions. Build it from the logical space rather than from a roster of views: identify the questions on which positions genuinely come apart, then locate the actual positions within the space those questions define. The questions I suspect matter, though you should revise or extend this list if you think it's wrong: whether a perfectly balanced case is psychologically or physically possible at all; whether, granting the case, inaction is irrational or merely causally unavailable; whether arbitrarily selecting a tied option is a distinct act from choosing it (the picking/choosing distinction), and if so what makes it rational; whether a reason to take one of the tied options is itself a reason that breaks the tie (the "second-order reason" move); and whether invoking genuine randomness — a coin flip, a quantum event, a jitter in the algorithm — solves the problem or merely relocates it.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position where one exists (or "unnamed" with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether the slot is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored), plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot in principle separate by answers to the branching questions.

Two further constraints. First, phrase every branching question so that a reader can answer it without knowing which position it leads to — concrete cases, not labels. Please include among your cases at least these: the medieval ass between identical haystacks; a person choosing between two job offers identical in every respect she cares about; and a chess engine that has evaluated two moves as exactly equal and must output one within a time limit. The chess engine is there deliberately: my computer science students tend to think symmetry-breaking algorithms dissolve the philosophical problem, and I want the tree to force the question of whether they do. Second, somewhere in the tree there must be a branch addressing the libertarian or agent-causal response — the view that the agent simply originates a choice uncaused by the balance of reasons — since half my group will want that escape hatch and I want them to see precisely what it costs.

Aim for six to nine leaves. No readings or citations; the instrument itself is the deliverable.
