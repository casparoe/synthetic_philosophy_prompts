I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I lead a monthly film-and-discussion night for a few coworkers. This month we're watching Planet of the Apes (the 1968 original), and I want to frame the discussion using Kant's ethics concerning animals, as I understand it from secondary reading: that because animals lack rational self-consciousness we owe them no direct duties, yet cruelty toward them is still wrong, because abusing a creature trains habits of callousness that then leak into how we treat persons. The wrong runs through our own character, not through any claim the animal has on us.

The reason this grabbed me is a parallel from work. On my team we say you can read an engineer's real habits from how they treat the things that don't count: throwaway scripts written carelessly, test fixtures mocked up sloppily, staging environments left broken. The fixture doesn't matter in itself; what matters is that carelessness there migrates into production code. That sounds to me almost exactly like Kant's indirection thesis, and I want the reading to test whether the analogy holds or whether it's a superficial resemblance that breaks down somewhere important.

Read the film through this lens and answer three things. First, does the film actually dramatize Kant's thesis? The apes hunt, cage, and experiment on mute humans the way we treat animals; does the film show that treatment deforming how apes deal with one another — I'm thinking of Zaius's suppression of evidence and the heresy proceedings against Zira and Cornelius — in the way the indirection thesis would predict? Second, does the film covertly presuppose the opposite? Our horror at Taylor's treatment arguably only lands because we take Taylor to be directly owed something, which would make the film an argument for direct duties dressed as the inversion of our own practices. Third, the Statue of Liberty ending: once the reveal collapses the distance between the ones in the cages and the ones holding the keys, does the clean separation Kant needs between the animal case and the human case survive?

Please don't quote dialogue from memory, and don't force the fit. Where the film resists the Kantian reading, say so plainly. Close by telling me what, if anything, the film adds that Kant's argument alone doesn't: does dramatizing the inversion — putting the rational being in the animal's position — expose something about who gets counted as rational that the armchair version of the thesis lets you ignore?
