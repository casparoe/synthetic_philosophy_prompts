I am a retired experimental physicist who has, rather late in life, developed a serious interest in philosophy of religion. The argument I keep returning to is the kalam cosmological argument, which I take to run: whatever begins to exist has a cause of its existence; the universe began to exist; therefore the universe has a cause. I am not asking you to settle whether the argument is sound. I am auditing my own list of objections to it, which I believe covers the standard repertoire, because I want to know whether I have missed a line of attack. The objections I already know, and do not need repeated, are these:

1. The special-pleading retort: what caused the cause? I am aware of the standard reply — the first premise is restricted to things that begin to exist, and the proposed cause is alleged never to have begun — so please do not rehearse that exchange.
2. Physical counterexamples to the causal premise: quantum vacuum fluctuations and the timing of individual radioactive decays are said to be events without causes.
3. The B-theory objection: if time is tenseless, the universe has a first temporal boundary rather than a beginning in any metaphysically weighty sense, and nothing begins to exist in the way the argument requires.
4. Doubts about the a priori case against an infinite past: Hilbert's Hotel shows at most that actual infinities are strange, not impossible, and the impossibility of forming an infinite collection by successive addition does not touch a past that was never so formed because it had no starting point.
5. The equivocation charge: our evidence for the causal premise concerns rearrangements of pre-existing matter, never origination out of nothing, so the induction supporting the premise fails at exactly the point where the conclusion needs it.
6. The inapplicability objection: causation as we understand it is a relation within the temporal order, so extending it to the origin of time itself may be a category mistake; there is no moment before the universe at which a cause could operate.
7. The gap objection: even granting a cause, the argument by itself does not establish that the cause is one, personal, timeless, or worthy of worship; the distance from a cause of the universe to God must be covered by further argument.

What I want is an objection to the kalam argument that is not on this list — and not a rewording, a special case, or a conjunction of items already on it. Three conditions. First, the objection must bear on the kalam specifically, not on theism generally: the problem of evil, for instance, is no answer to this argument and does not count. Second, it must not be an objection to the Leibnizian contingency argument in disguise; I know the two arguments are cousins, but I am asking about this one. Third, if your objection leans on contested physics or cosmology, keep the physics and the philosophy separate: state the empirical assumption it requires and say whether the objection survives if that assumption is denied.

Then give me your honest assessment of whether the objection is any good — which premise it puts under pressure, whether a defender of the argument has a ready answer, and how it stands in relation to my list: does it subsume one of the seven, presuppose one of them, or stand on its own? If you conclude that my list is in fact exhaustive, so that every possible objection is a variant of one of these seven, then argue for that claim instead, and tell me which item on the list does the most work in the argument's defense.
