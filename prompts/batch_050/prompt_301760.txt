so im TAing an intro phil of language section this semester and the textbook my prof assigned keeps leaning on this analogy: language is conventional the way driving on the right side of the road is conventional. words mean what they mean because we've all tacitly agreed to use them that way, it could just as easily have gone the other way, nothing in the word "dog" ties it to dogs. the book uses this to argue that meaning is arbitrary and that people who get precious about "correct" usage are confused -- there's no fact of the matter beyond what the convention happens to be, same as there's no fact of the matter that right is better than left.

my undergrads love this and honestly it does feel compelling, but i've been reading Lewis on convention on the side and i suspect the analogy is doing some sleight of hand. can you audit it properly for me?

what i want:

1. the correspondence table first -- what plays the role of the road rule, the drivers, the tacit agreement, the arbitrariness, the mutual expectations that keep everyone conforming. be explicit about what in Lewis's actual account (regularities in behaviour sustained because everyone prefers to conform given that others do, as solutions to coordination problems) is supposed to map onto language.

2. then where the mapping breaks or strains. some things that occur to me but i want you to find others: road conventions have basically two options and total symmetry, a language has astronomically many pairings of sound and meaning; nobody ever agreed to english the way a legislature could just pick a side of the road; people coin words and get understood without any prior regularity at all; and understanding a novel sentence seems nothing like coordinating at an intersection. which of these disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion the textbook wants, and which are just cosmetic?

3. the payoff question: does the "meaning is arbitrary, usage pedantry is confused" conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the road analogy carrying it? if there's a better analogy for whatever is genuinely conventional about language, propose one -- or tell me why the conventionalist picture itself is the problem.

one thing i care about: please separate what's basically settled among people who work on this (e.g. that meaning isn't fixed by intrinsic connection between sound and thing) from what's genuinely contested (i gather Lewis's specific account of convention, and whether convention is even the right framework versus Gricean inference or norm-based views, is disputed). don't present a contested position as the consensus view -- i'll be teaching this and i don't want to hand my students one philosopher's take dressed up as received wisdom.
