Wikipedia-style encyclopedias have a wierd division of epistemic labor: eyewitness footage, leaked documents, datasets, and a person’s own statements usually do not establish much by themselves, while established newspapers, textbooks, and official reference works can settle what the article says. Editors may not combine several reliable claims into a conclusion unless some published source has already drawn it. Yet these same reference works are treated as fallible, and major outlets can be deprecated after enough failures. What epistemology does this practice presuppose? Is it a theory of testimony, institutional trust, evidential sufficiency, or just a practical way of preventing motivated reasoning? Reconstruct the strongest implicit account, say whether it commits encyclopedias to treating social recognition as evidence of truth, and explain what would have to change if the goal were instead to help readers judge contested claims—including conspiracy claims—for themselves.
