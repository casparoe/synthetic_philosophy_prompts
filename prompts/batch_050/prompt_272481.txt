At my job we've got a running argument about our support chatbot and I want it settled. Last week the bot told a customer "your refund went out on Tuesday," which was flatly false — it just generated a plausible-sounding status, no refund existed. My coworker Dani says the bot lied to the customer. Tomas says that's confused, and he pulled up Augustine's On Lying (De Mendacio, ch. 3), where Augustine says: "For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie." Tomas's point: lying is judged by what's in the speaker's mind, and the bot has no mind in which to hold one thing while saying another, so it can't lie any more than a miscalibrated scale can.

Dani came back with two points. First, a miscalibrated scale doesn't talk to you in the first person; when something uses "I" and full sentences, the norms of testimony apply to it wether or not there's a mind behind them. Second, if the bot can't lie then it can't tell the truth either, but Tomas happily says "the bot gave me the right answer" when it does — so he's inconsistent.

Tomas said "the bot gave me the right answer" is loose talk, like "my watch says it's three." Then he turned Augustine back on Dani: a lie can be uttered "in words, or by signs of whatever kind," so if anyone lied about the refund it was whoever wrote and shipped the bot — the bot is just their sign. Dani: but nobody at the company knew this particular output would be false, so by Augustine's own mind-test nobody lied about the refund at all — the customer got deceived by nobody. If your definition says that, the definition has the hole, not the bot.

Tomas then conceded the refund case maybe involves no liar, but said the bot's other favorite line, "I completely understand your frustration," is different: the designers knew the bot understands nothing, so there the double heart really does exist — in them, speaking through the bot. Dani's reply: so whether a sentence is a lie depends on who was in which meeting two years before it was produced? The customer hears both sentences exactly the same way.

So who's right at each step? In particular, which of them is reading the Augustine passage correctly — is the "signs of whatever kind" bit doing the work Tomas wants? And what are they both missing?
