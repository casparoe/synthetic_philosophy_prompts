I am a PhD student in philosophy, working on comparative moral status, and I sit on my university's animal ethics committee as its designated ethics member. The committee reviews roughly a dozen research protocols a month, and I have noticed a recurring rhetorical pattern I want to be able to handle systematically: applicants cite some measure of brain size — absolute brain mass, brain-to-body ratio, or occasionally the encephalisation quotient — as grounds for treating their chosen species as requiring less stringent justification. In the last two cycles alone, one group argued that switching from mice to fruit flies removed most welfare concerns because flies have 'minuscule brains', while another proposed octopus work and downplayed the ethical burden on the grounds that cephalopods have small brain-to-body ratios relative to the vertebrates we already license. My fellow committee members are veterinarians and bench scientists rather than philosophers, each protocol gets perhaps fifteen minutes of scrutiny, and I have about five minutes in which to intervene, so I cannot give them a seminar on theories of welfare. I need something they can run themselves.

Please write a screening procedure I can hand to the committee for assessing any claim of the form 'this species' neural endowment reduces the moral weight of the proposed harms'. The procedure should distinguish, step by step, between the legitimate use of neuroanatomy as defeasible evidence about morally relevant capacities (sentience, nociception, the capacity for frustration or boredom) and the illegitimate inference from sheer size or ratio to moral status, and it must specify: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points at which the committee should stop and demand more from the applicant; and the points at which the procedure must hand the decision back to a person because the question is one of values rather than of fact. The procedure should be robust to at least three complications I worry about: neurons doing the relevant work may not be in the brain at all (cephalopod arms contain most of an octopus's neurons); total neuron counts and size measures can diverge sharply; and uncertainty about a species' capacities is itself something the procedure must convert into a decision rule rather than silently resolving in the applicant's favour.

After stating the procedure, please run it in full on two recent-style cases: the fruit-fly substitution described above, and a proposal to use crows in a food-deprivation study justified partly by the birds' small brains — noting explicitly where each case exits the procedure and why, and including one point where the procedure yields a verdict the applicant, or the committee, will find unwelcome. Please also say candidly what the procedure cannot do and where it will misfire. Lead with the procedure itself — its first question or its governing rule — in the opening sentence, with no preamble, no restating of my request, and no commentary before the procedure begins; aim for something a non-philosopher could apply unaided after one careful reading.
