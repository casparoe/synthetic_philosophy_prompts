Write a villanelle about Harsanyi's social aggregation theorem — the result that if each individual's preferences and society's preferences both satisfy the expected-utility axioms, and society is indifferent between two prospects whenever every individual is, then social welfare must take the form of a weighted sum of individual utilities. Let the two refrains carry the two halves of this result: one should insist on the linearity of the conclusion (nothing survives but a sum), the other on what the theorem leaves open (the weights, and who gets to choose them). The villanelle's repetitions should do argumentative work: each return of a refrain should feel like the axioms closing in, the way Pareto indifference and independence together squeeze out any separable concern for how utility is distributed, until only the weighted total remains. In one of the middle tercets, acknowledge what this costs — that an outcome shared evenly and an outcome concentrated in one person can come out equal if the weights decree it. A reader who knows the theorem should find the poem correct rather than merely allusive; a reader who does not should still find it a good villanelle.
