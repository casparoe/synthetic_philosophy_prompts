A friend and I have been arguing about the philosophy of mind for weeks, and the argument keeps deadlocking on a single line he deploys whenever he is cornered: conceivability is a guide to possibility. His move is that since he can vividly imagine a world physically identical to ours but in which nobody is conscious, such a world is possible, and physicalism is therefore false. My instinct is that the slogan smuggles in far more than it appears to say, but when I press him on it he retreats to something so weak it proves nothing, and then quietly re-inflates it when he needs the conclusion. I am sure the slogan hides several distinct principles that differ enormously in strength, but I cannot get him to separate them.

Could you lay out, as a menu, the different precise claims that slogan might be making? For each reading, please specify what kind of conceivability is doing the work (mere ability to picture something, absence of detectable contradiction, full rational coherence on ideal reflection, or whatever other notions are available) and what kind of possibility it is meant to indicate (logical, metaphysical, epistemic, or something else), since I suspect the slogan trades on sliding between these. Then, for each candidate principle, say whether it is trivially true, substantive but defensible, or simply false, and where the well-known trouble cases bite: things conceivable but impossible, things inconceivable but possible, and necessities we can only discover empirically. Finally, work backwards from my friend's actual use: which of these readings would a physicalist happily grant, which would genuinely entail that a zombie world is possible, and whether any single reading both is plausible on its own merits and gets him his conclusion, or whether the argument only works by equivocating between a modest reading he can defend and an ambitious one he needs.
