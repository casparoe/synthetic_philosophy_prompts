So I recently got voluntold into running comms for our tenants union (we're in the middle of a dispute with the landlord's management company, hence me thinking about this at 1am), and someone in the group chat sent round Orwell's "Politics and the English Language" as a kind of joke about how the management company writes. I reread it properly for the first time in years and now I want to actually use it for how we write our own statements. One passage keeps bugging me:

"prose consists less and less of words chosen for the sake of their meaning, and more and more of phrases tacked together like the sections of a prefabricated hen-house"

Here's roughly where I've landed, and before I inflict this on the committee I want to know if it hangs together:

1. Orwell's six rules at the end of the essay should be treated as hard house rules for everything we publish. No exceptions, no case-by-case judgement calls, because judgement calls are how the rules get eroded in the first place.
2. The hen-house passage is the real heart of the essay, and the six rules are just heuristics that push against the deeper vice he describes there -- assembling ready-made phrases instead of choosing words for their meaning.
3. When we have to respond to the management company's lawyer within the hour, it's fine to lean on stock phrases ("we categorically condemn", "this leaves much to be desired", that kind of thing), because in a fast-moving exchange nobody is reading closely anyway and speed beats freshness.
4. Clear prose in our public statements is a political duty, not an aesthetic preference -- vague language just lets the other side fill in the gaps with their own interpretation.
5. Two of our committee members write in a flowery, very Latinate style, and that's fine for the longer internal pieces where they genuinely do think through every sentence -- style discipline only really matters for the punchy public stuff.

I have a nagging feeling that a couple of these pull against each other but I can't pin down where. Which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like it? Rank the real tensions by how bad they are, and for each one say what would have to give. Also: given what the passage is actually saying, does enforcing the six rules as exceptionless rules even make sense on my own reading of the essay, or am I missing that Orwell builds in an escape hatch himself? I'd rather an honest audit than a pat on the head -- if one of these has to go, tell me which and what it costs.
