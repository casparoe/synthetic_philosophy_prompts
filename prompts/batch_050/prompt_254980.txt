I'm putting together the unit on social construction for my philosophy of social science course, and I realized while prepping that my own intuitions are a mess and I can't figure out what I actually think. So let me just lay out my verdicts on a bunch of cases and you tell me what principle, if any, I'm committed to. Case one: a perfect counterfeit twenty-dollar bill that passes every test and circulates for years with nobody the wiser -- my verdict is it was never money, it's a very good fake. Case two: a genuine banknote from a defunct currency that no shop will take and no state honors -- my verdict is it's not money anymore, it's a collectible, just fancy paper. Case three: Pluto. I think the demotion shows that our *definition* of planet is up to us, but not that planets are socially constructed -- the thing out there doesn't care what we call it, and astronomy answers to the world. Case four: weeds. Obviously constructed, there's no botanical kind there, a weed is just a plant a gardener doesn't want. Case five: race -- constructed through and through, but I want to insist this doesn't make it unreal or harmless, it still structures who gets hired and who gets pulled over. Case six: gender -- I have the intuition that it's constructed in something like the way money is, not merely in the thin way "planet" has a conventional definition, though I admit I couldn't currently defend the asymmetry with case three if pressed.

The pair that's clearly causing trouble is one and two: whatever principle says money is just whatever we collectively treat as money gets case two right but case one badly wrong, since everyone treated the counterfeit as money. And three versus four/six is almost as bad, since I seem to be using "the definition is conventional" as proof of construction in some cases and as irrelevant to it in others. Maybe the difference is that some kinds exist only because of our practices while others merely get *carved up* by them, but I'm not sure that survives the counterfeit case, or the defunct note for that matter.

Give me your answer as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the single most plausible principle that captures as many of my six verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that it could in principle be applied mechanically; (2) which cases motivate each clause of it, if it needs more than one clause; (3) which of my verdicts it has to overturn, if any, and whether I should actually give that verdict up or complicate the principle instead; (4) what the principle predicts about a case I didn't list, namely citizenship -- say, a child born abroad to citizen parents who was never registered with any consulate; (5) the strongest single objection to the principle, stated the way a sharp undergrad in the back row would put it.
