I've been persuaded by the Lakatosian picture of mathematics: theorems are conjectures, proofs are thought-experiments that invite counterexamples, and the real action is the dialectic of proof-generated refutations, monster-barring, and lemma incorporation, with formalized proof as just the fossil record of that process. Now I want to know what would count against this view, because I'm worried I've fallen for a good story. Some candidate stress tests, but feel free to find better ones: the rise of proof assistants and automated theorem proving, where verification seems to come first and counterexample-hunting plays almost no role, so the quasi-empirical machinery looks idle exactly where modern rigor lives; routine areas like finite combinatorics or computer-checked case enumeration, where results seem to get settled without any dialectic at all; and the charge that Lakatos's narrative only really fits a few favorite 19th-century episodes (Euler's polyhedra, the rigorization of analysis) and fails for, say, arithmetic or set theory. Which of these would actually damage quasi-empiricism rather than just sit awkwardly next to it? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely inconvenient, and for each say whether a committed Lakatosian would concede it or absorb it by stretching the notion of refutation (counterexamples to lemmas, hidden assumptions, shifts in what counts as a proof). And be honest about the meta-question: can any piece of mathematical practice be redescribed as conjecture-and-refutation, and if so has the view been qualified into something no evidence could touch? Write your answer as continuous prose, like a letter -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
