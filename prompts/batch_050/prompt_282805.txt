I have no formal training in philosophy, but I've been reading on my own about how a brain state can be about something at all, and I've arrived at a set of commitments I'd like stress-tested. Please respond as a careful philosopher of mind would: take each commitment seriously, trace what it actually entails, and think slowly before judging.

My commitments:

1. Mental content must be fully naturalizable. Whatever makes a neural state be about flies, rather than about nothing or about something else, must be explainable without appealing to any irreducibly mental or semantic notions.

2. The teleological approach is the right one: a state represents what its underlying mechanism was selected, by evolution or by learning, to detect or to bring about. The frog's prey-catching circuit represents flies because detecting flies is what it is for.

3. A molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person, assembled by sheer accident (lightning striking a swamp, say), would have thoughts with content just as the original does. What a state is about cannot depend on where the system came from.

4. Misrepresentation must be possible. The circuit can fire and simply be wrong, as when the frog snaps at a pellet, and any theory that cannot allow genuine error is a non-starter.

5. On this approach, content comes out determinate for free: the circuit represents fly, not fly-or-small-dark-moving-thing, and the selection story by itself secures this.

I'm not asking for a survey of the literature. I want an audit of this set: identify which pairs of commitments genuinely conflict and explain what forces the conflict; identify any pair that appears to conflict but dissolves once the view is stated precisely; rank the genuine tensions by severity; and close by naming the single revision that restores the most coherence at the lowest cost.
