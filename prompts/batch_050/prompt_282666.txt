So there's a line I keep seeing in arguments about AI stuff, on LessWrong comment threads and also from a couple of my coworkers who build on top of these models: "LLMs don't lie, they just hallucinate." People deploy it in really different ways though. Sometimes it's exculpatory, like, don't anthropomorphize, the model isn't doing anything morally loaded, it's just wrong the way a buggy calculator is wrong. Sometimes it's almost the opposite, like, hallucination is *worse* than lying because at least a liar is tracking the truth. And sometimes it's used to shrug off safety concerns entirely, since if there's no intent there's no deception so what's the big deal.

Here's the thing that got me stuck: I read Frankfurt's On Bullshit ages ago and his whole point (as I remember it) is that the bullshitter is neither lying nor telling the truth because he's indifferent to how things actually stand, and that Frankfurt thinks this indifference makes bullshit a greater enemy of truth than lying is. Which makes me think there are actually like four or five distinct precise claims crammed into that one sentence -- "the model lacks the intent to deceive that lying requires" is not the same claim as "the model is merely making errors" is not the same as "the model is bullshitting in Frankfurt's sense" is not the same as "the correct analogy is a broken instrument." Lay out the different readings for me as a list: what each one is actually claiming about the model's relationship to truth, which are trivially true, which are substantive but defensible, and which are just false or confused. Then for each reading, say whether it would actually support the use people put the slogan to -- I especially want to know whether "it's bullshit, not lying" should make us *more* worried, given Frankfurt's own argument, rather than less.

One caveat I want flagged: Frankfurt's analysis is about an agent with attitudes, so there's a real question of whether any of these categories apply to a system that maybe has no attitudes at all, or whether the whole lying/bullshit/error taxonomy only gets traction at the level of the company deploying the model or the discourse around it. If a reading only works under some contested picture of what LLMs are doing internally, say so. And at the end, tell me how confident you are in each of your verdicts and single out the single point in your answer you're least sure of -- I have a feeling the weakest link is going to be somewhere in the "does the model have the relevant attitudes" step, but maybe I'm wrong about that.
