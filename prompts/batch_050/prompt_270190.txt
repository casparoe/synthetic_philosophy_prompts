I'm a grad student TAing a third-year philosophy of mind course, and I've got one 80-minute seminar to teach Dennett's case against qualia, built around the Chase and Sanborn coffee-tasters story. The students have read the Nagel bat paper already and most of them arrived on campus basically convinced that "what it's like" is the one thing you can't be wrong about, so I'm expecting resistance.

Don't explain the argument to me, plan how I should teach it. Specifically:

- The order the pieces have to come in. My sense is I can't open with the coffee case at all, because unless they've first committed out loud to some picture of qualia as ineffable, intrinsic, private, and directly known, the case just reads as a cute story about changing tastes. But tell me what the actual dependency structure is: what has to be in place before the case does any work, and what has to come after it before the "there's no fact of the matter" move lands rather than just sounding like a dodge.
- Why Chase and Sanborn should carry the session instead of the obvious alternatives. Students always want to run it as an inverted spectrum case, and I could also just do the beer example with one person. Say why the two-tasters setup is doing something those can't, i.e. what the symmetry between two equally authoritative verdicts buys you that a single-subject case doesn't.
- The misconceptions I'll meet, in the order I'll meet them, and where in the sequence to defuse each. My predictions: (1) "Dennett is denying that anything happens when you taste coffee," which will come up in the first ten minutes; (2) "this is just behaviorism rebranded"; (3) "the story only shows memory is fallible, which everyone already knew"; (4) some student will try to settle Chase vs Sanborn by introspecting their own relationship to coffee right there in class, and I'm honestly not sure whether to let them or shut it down. Tell me if I'm missing one and where each should be headed off rather than answered after the fact.
- One question I can put to them at the end that separates "got it" from "repeating it." I was thinking of asking whether Dennett thinks Chase or Sanborn is right, since the correct answer is that the question is malformed, but maybe there's something better.
- What to openly bracket at this level: I assume the full heterophenomenology apparatus and the later back-and-forth with Chalmers stay off the table, but confirm or correct me.

Keep in mind these are smart students but half of them did the reading at 2am, so the plan should survive that.
