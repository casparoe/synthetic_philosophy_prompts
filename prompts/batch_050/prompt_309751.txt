I've been going to my university's effective altruism chapter this semester, mostly out of curiosity and partly because they have good snacks. Last week we got into it over my roommate, who's training to be a primary school teacher, and one of the regulars basically said look, the whole point is to do the most good you can, and by that standard she'd do more good as a quant trader donating half her salary, since those donations could save way more lives than one classroom of kids ever gets. The phrase did all the work in that argument and nobody questioned it, because it sounds totally innocuous, almost like a platitude your gran would agree with. But the more I turn it over the less I know what it actually says.

Can you lay out the different precise claims 'do the most good you can' could be making? I suspect it's smuggling in a bunch of separable commitments: something about whose good counts (everyone's equally? including people who don't exist yet? animals?), something about what 'most' means (best actual outcome vs highest expected value, and expected by whose lights), something about what 'can' means (physically possible? psychologically sustainable? consistent with ordinary moral constraints?), and maybe something about whether this is a strict obligation that binds at every choice or more of an aspirational ideal. For each of those, give me the menu of readings with verdicts -- which are trivial, which are substantive but defensible, which are probably false -- and say which readings are actually load-bearing for the trader argument. I also have a nagging worry that any version precise enough to rank careers like that quietly favors the kinds of good you can count, like lives saved by bednets, over the kinds you can't, like what a good teacher does over thirty years, and I'd like to know which readings that worry actually bites against.

Not looking for a reading list or anything, just the disambiguation done properly so next week I can push back with something sharper than 'this feels off somehow.'
