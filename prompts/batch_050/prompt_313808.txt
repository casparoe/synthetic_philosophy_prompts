Since retiring from a career in chemistry, I have been reading the early moderns, and I keep returning to a passage from Book II, Chapter XXI of John Locke's An Essay Concerning Human Understanding:

"Thus we say, Fire has a power to melt gold, i.e. to destroy the consistency of its insensible parts, and consequently its hardness, and make it fluid; and gold has a power to be melted; that the sun has a power to blanch wax, and wax a power to be blanched by the sun, whereby the yellowness is destroyed, and whiteness made to exist in its room. In which, and the like cases, the power we consider is in reference to the change of perceivable ideas. For we cannot observe any alteration to be made in, or operation upon anything, but by the observable change of its sensible ideas; nor conceive any alteration to be made, but by conceiving a change of some of its ideas."

Here is my difficulty, and my request. Every working scientist says that a stoppered reagent remains corrosive and that an unlit match is still flammable, yet Locke's final sentence appears to bind our very idea of a power to observable change. Construct two thought experiments to test how far that binding can be pushed. Constraints: each scenario must fit in a single paragraph, must take place in an ordinary laboratory with no idealizations beyond normal bench practice, and must generate its philosophical verdicts from your reading of the passage rather than stipulating them.

First, a case in which a substance retains the relevant power throughout its entire career, yet every time the appropriate triggering conditions are realized, some further standing feature of the setup counteracts the manifestation itself, so the power is present, is triggered, and is never once displayed. Second, a case in which a substance genuinely possesses the power up to the very moment the triggering conditions obtain, but the obtaining of those conditions is itself what strips the power away, so that nothing is displayed for an entirely different reason.

After presenting both cases, tell me what Locke can and cannot say about each. Does his insistence that power is understood in reference to the change of perceivable ideas force him to treat the two cases alike, both being situations in which we can form no idea of the power at all? Or does the passage contain resources for distinguishing the substance that keeps its power under suppression from the substance that loses its power on cue? If the latter, show me exactly where in the quoted text the distinction gets its grip; if the former, say whether the fault lies with Locke's account or with my cases. Identify which feature of each scenario is doing the philosophical work, and close with the strongest reply available to a reader who holds that neither case poses Locke any difficulty whatsoever.
