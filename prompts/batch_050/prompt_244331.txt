I am a novelist at work on a book set in the Warring States period, and one of my central characters is a itinerant sophist of the School of Names, loosely modeled on Gongsun Long. In the pivotal scene he must defend, before a duke's assembled court, the thesis that a white horse is not a horse. I have drafted his argument, and my worry is not that it is too weak but that it is too strong: if it works, it seems to prove far more than my character needs, indeed something absurd. Here it is as I have written it for him.

(1) A statement of the form "A is B" asserts an identity: it says that what "A" names and what "B" names are one and the same thing, as when we say that the evening star is the morning star.
(2) "Horse" names the kind horse. "White horse" does not name the color, nor the shape, but a kind of its own: the kind white horse, which has only white members.
(3) The kind white horse is not identical with the kind horse, for the latter includes horses of every color and the former does not; two kinds with different members are two kinds, not one.
(4) Therefore "a white horse is a horse" is false: a white horse is not a horse.
(5) Nothing in the reasoning so far is particular to horses. For any kind K and any modifier M that excludes some members of K, an M-K is not a K.
(6) So a tall man is not a man, a slow train is not a train, and -- comfortably, I think -- a counterfeit coin is not a coin and a toy gun is not a gun.
(7) But every kind has members distinguishable from one another by some modifier that excludes the rest. Hence nothing whatsoever falls under any general kind term, and names can never classify anything.

The conclusion at (7) would destroy my character rather than crown him; a sophist who proves that language cannot sort the world at all has talked himself out of his own profession. Yet when I reread the scene I cannot locate the step where the argument overreaches, and the counterfeit coin and toy gun at (6) genuinely do strike me as not being coins and guns, which makes me doubt that the schema at (5) is where the trouble lies. If something must be sacrificed, I would rather give up (1) than (3), since (3) seems to me a plain truth about kinds, but I want to know what that sacrifice costs the rest of my character's philosophy of naming.

Tell me which step or premise I should have him pressed on by his interlocutor, why that step looked innocent, and what each of the available repairs would commit him to -- so that I can write the refutation he half-deserves and the rejoinder he would actually give.
