So I'm writing a novel set about twenty years from now in a Midlands town where the distribution depot, the call centre and most of the routine office work have all been automated away, and everyone's on a perfectly adequate citizens' income plus free access to the fabrication sheds. Two of my main characters have been rowing about this the whole book and it's time they actually got somewhere.

Marta, late fifties, ex-depot manager, thinks something essential has been amputated: work gave people structure, status, a reason to be needed, the daily texture of being good at something someone else relied on, and she watches her neighbours drift into what she calls "the long weekend with no Monday". Danny, early thirties, thinks she's romanticising what was mostly toil -- that people were always going to build meaning from care, craft, allotments, voluntary projects, looking after each other's kids, and the town is slowly proving it, just slower than the optimists predicted.

Neither of them is stupid and neither is a strawman, that's the whole point of the book. Marta concedes the depot destroyed knees and Sundays. Danny concedes that his uncle has not, in fact, taken up woodwork, and mostly watches telly and gets heavier.

Write the joint statement the two of them could actually sign by the final chapter, after doing an adversarial collaboration (their therapist daughter makes them, long story). I want:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims both would put their names to -- about what work genuinely provided beyond money, about what automation has and hasn't taken, about how unevenly people are coping.
2. A disagreements table where each row states one residual disagreement, phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own position. No slanted framing. If any of their row turns out on inspection to be verbal -- them using "meaning" or "dignity" in different senses -- mark it as such and say so.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions where settling them would actually move one of them. I suspect one crux is whether the status and structure work provided can be replaced by anything non-compulsory, and another is whether the uncle-types are the median case or a minority, but find your own if those aren't the real ones.
4. For each crux, something both would accept as bearing on it -- an observed case in the town, a comparison (retirement, aristocrats who never worked, the already-unemployed, parents of small kids), or a prediction about how the next generation turns out -- with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Keep it in the register of a document two ordinary, sharp people could write, not an academic paper, and don't make one side the obvious winner -- my editor will smell it.
