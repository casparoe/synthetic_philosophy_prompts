I am developing a novel set in a prosperous city built around a network of movable bridges. For generations, merchants have resolved potentially dangerous encounters on the bridges through an unwritten practice: the cart carrying the lighter load yields, unless a funeral procession, imperial messenger, or fire brigade is involved. The system has survived without legislation, but a recent change in transport technology is destabilizing it. New enclosed cargo wagons make loads difficult to estimate; meanwhile, migrants from nearby provinces have brought competing customs, and the city council is considering whether to impose a simpler rule such as always keeping left. I want this social transition to feel intellectually credible rather than merely serve as a convenient source of disorder.

Using David Lewis’s account of convention, please identify the most important implications for how this fictional society would function and change. I am less interested in a textbook summary than in applying the theory carefully: determine what must be true for the yielding practice to count as a convention; explain the roles of coordination problems, mutually recognized expectations, precedent, salience, and arbitrary alternatives; and distinguish convention from habit, law, morality, and coerced compliance. Then consider how the practice might fragment, evolve, or suddenly tip toward a new equilibrium when concealment, migration, unequal enforcement, and official intervention enter the system. Finally, draw out the narrative consequences: what sorts of misunderstandings, strategic manipulations, status advantages, accidents, reform campaigns, and pockets of local resistance should plausibly arise? Please also note where Lewis’s framework might fail to capture the messier moral or emotional significance the custom has acquired for the city’s inhabitants.
