Here's a stress test for the zombie argument, in the form of a construction exercise. Build a conceivability argument with exactly the same shape as the standard one: (1) situation S is coherently conceivable, on ideal reflection; (2) if S is ideally conceivable, it's metaphsyically possible; (3) if S is possible, then facts of kind K don't supervene on the physical facts. But K has to be some domain other than phenomenal consciousness, and the conclusion should be one most philosophers would badly want to reject. The point is an overgeneration test: if your argument is genuinly as good as the zombie argument, premise for premise, then either we accept a weird dualism about K or the original machinery proves too much. So no stipulating -- each of your three premises gets whatever motivation the corresponding zombie premise has, no more and no less.

Constraints, because the easy targets are boring:
- Nothing whose concept is transparently functional or organizational, like life or being a thermostat. That's the standard reply in this game (once all the functions are physically in place there's nothing left for the K-zombie to lack), and I want cases where that reply isn't available.
- Nothing where the dualist-sounding conclusion is already a respectable position, so no morality and no math.
- Conceivability means the demanding kind the zombie argument itself needs -- ideally conceivable, no contradiction on full reflection -- not "I can sort of picture it."

Then answer three things. Where would the zombie argument's defender try to break the symmetry between your K-argument and his? Does that move actually succeed, or does it succeed for his argument too only by assuming consciousness is special in precisely the way the argument was supposed to establish? And stepping back: which premise of the original zombie argument does your exercise suggest is really load-bearing?
