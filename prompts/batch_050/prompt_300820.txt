A friend and I spent most of last weekend arguing about Julian Savulescu's principle of procreative beneficence -- the claim that prospective parents, when choosing among possible future children (say, at the embryo selection stage of IVF), have a significant moral reason to select the child expected to have the best life, given the available information. My friend finds the principle close to obvious once you accept that some lives go better than others; I kept pressing objections, and by the end of the evening I realized we had simply recycled the standard ones without getting anywhere. I would like to come back to her with something she will not have heard before.

Here are the objections I already have on my list, so please do not repeat or repackage any of them. First, the expressivist objection: selecting against embryos with disabling traits sends a demeaning message to existing people who have those traits. Second, the non-identity problem: choosing a different embryo does not make the child you would otherwise have had worse off, since that particular child would not have existed at all, so it is unclear whom the principle is protecting. Third, the procreative liberty objection: the principle converts a permissible parental preference into an obligation and thereby intrudes on reproductive autonomy. Fourth, the reductio sometimes pressed by Robert Sparrow and others: taken seriously, the principle seems to demand the maximally enhanced child, generating absurdly demanding obligations to pursue whatever biotechnology can offer. Fifth, the unconditional acceptance objection: a parent who approaches reproduction as quality control adopts a stance toward the child that is conditional on its traits, which sits badly with the kind of love parenting is supposed to involve. Sixth, the distributive objection: in any realistic market for selection or editing, the principle entrenches and legitimizes genetic inequality along class lines.

What I want is one further objection that is genuinely different in structure from these six -- not a rewording, special case, or combination of them, and in particular not another appeal to messages sent, to identity, to parental rights, to demandingness, to the quality of the parent-child relationship, or to inequality. State the objection precisely, say how it differs from the ones on my list, and then give me your honest assessment of whether it actually has force against the principle as Savulescu states it, or only against a caricature. Where the scholarly debate has reached something like a stable verdict on any of the moves involved, please mark that as settled, and where informed philosophers genuinely disagree, say so rather than presenting one side as the consensus. Finally, please write your whole answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
