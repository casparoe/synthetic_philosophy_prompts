I work as an analyst in a national statistics office (small European country, won't say which one), and last month our ministry started floating the idea that the country should aim to reach the top of the World Happiness Report within ten years -- basically treat it like an export target or the Olympic medal table. I raised some doubts at the internal workshop and the senior advisor shut me down with "what gets measured gets managed." His point, as far as I could tell, was that putting the life-satisfaction score on the goverment dashboard is exactly how you force policy to actually care about people's happiness, and that my nitpicks about the metric were beside the point.

I've been turning that sentence over since, and it seems to dissolve into a bunch of very different claims depending on how you push on it. Can you lay out, as a list I could literally print and bring to the next workshop, the distinct precise theses "what gets measured gets managed" might be making -- from the near-tautological end (you can't track progress on something without numbers) through a moderate managerial claim (organizations optimize whatever shows up in performance reviews) up to something almost metaphysical (unmeasured goods effectively don't exist politically, so measuring happiness is what makes it real as a policy goal)? For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive-but-defensible claim, or actually false, and what mechanism or evidence would have to hold for it to be true.

Then the part I actually need: which of these readings, if any, can bear the weight he put on it? Some wrinkles specific to happiness measures that you should engage rather than hand-wave: the headline number comes from a single self-report ladder question; the measure is plausibly reactive (respondents can learn what the "right" answer is supposed to sound like); there's Goodhart-style pressure once civil servants' careers and budgets depend on the score; and there's a real difference between a country trying to raise its measured score and trying to climb the ranking, since the second is zero-sum in a way the first isn't. For each reading of the slogan, tell me whether it survives these wrinkles and whether it supports the ten-years-to-number-one plan, or only some much weaker policy like "collect the data at all" or "run pilot programs and evaluate them with wellbeing outcomes." If your verdict is that no reading supports the strong plan, tell me what the strongest honest case for the plan would look like without leaning on the slogan.
