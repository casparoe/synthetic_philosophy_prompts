So I gave my grad seminar Scheffler's Death and the Afterlife this term, and I've mostly talked myself into the afterlife conjecture -- the claim that a great deal of what we value depends, in a way we don't notice, on the background assumption that humanity will continue after we're gone. Not our personal immortality, just the collective afterlife: people, projects, practices outliving us. The infertility scenario and the asteroid scenario do a lot of work convincing me that without that assumption, things like conservation, scholarship, even some personal relationships would lose their point or at least their grip.

But I'm worried I've been snowed by a couple of very vivid thought experiments, so I want you to map the view's exposure for me. What would actually count against the conjecture? Specifically:

- Which pieces of real-world evidence, if they held up, would genuinely trouble it? I'm thinking of stuff like: how do people who already expect no long human future (deep pessimists about climate, certain religious eschatologists, some effective altruists who put real probability on extinction this century) actually behave with respect to the things Scheffler says should lose their value? What would we have to find there for the conjecture to be in trouble, versus just mildly embarrassed?
- Which thought experiments cut the other way? Are there cases where the afterlife assumption is removed and valuing clearly survives -- and do those cases show the conjecture is false, or just that it's about fewer things than Scheffler lets on?
- Is the dependence claim even stated precisely enough to be false? It slides around between a claim about motivation, a claim about justification, and a claim about what our values commit us to. Say which reading is strongest and which reading is the one the scenarios actually support.
- Where has the position been quietly qualified -- "much of what we value," "would lose some of its hold on us" -- into something close to unfalsifiable? And honestly, is that a defect for a view of this kind, or is a soft conjecture in this neighborhood the best we can hope for?
- What does it stand or fall with elsewhere? E.g. if it turns out people readily value things they know are transient and terminal (last days, doomed relationships, ephemeral art), does the conjecture survive that, or does it need a contrast with personal death that the transient-cases undermine?

Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a sympathetic reader of Scheffler would concede it counts against him or brush it off. I'd also take a view on what the strongest version of the conjecture is -- the one that survives the most -- since I suspect its defenders retreat to it under pressure without saying so. And one more thing: does the conjecture predict anything its denial doesn't? If it's just a redescription of valuing that no observation could disturb, I want to know that before I build a paper around it.
