I'm the analytics person at a mid-sized software company (~800 employees), and I keep getting pulled into the same fight. It happened again this week: we ran an A/B test on a new onboarding flow, the new flow won overall, but when our growth team sliced the data by platform it lost on iOS *and* on Android. Half the room said "obviously trust the platform splits, that's Simpson's paradox," the other half said "obviously trust the topline, that's more data," and honestly I couldn't give a principled reason for either side. This comes up maybe once or twice a month for me, across product experiments, hiring funnel reviews (where Legal occasionally asks whether our numbers show a pattern by gender), and churn analysis, and the stakes range from "we ship a slightly worse button" to "someone writes a memo to a regulator."

What I want is a reusable procedure I can run in about 20 minutes whenever I see a reversal -- i.e., X beats Y in aggregate but loses in every subgroup (or the reverse). Walk me through the questions to ask in order, and be concrete about what distinguishes the cases where disaggregating is the right move from the cases where the aggregate is actually the number to trust -- my vague understanding is that it depends on how the subgroup variable is causally related to what we're measuring (whether it's a common cause, something downstream of the thing we're testing, or something we conditioned on in a weird way like a collider), but I've never seen that turned into steps a non-statistician can follow. Tell me what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure bottoms out in "you cannot settle this from the data alone and need to argue about the causal story," and what to do when I don't know the causal structure and can't run another experiment to find out. Also say how to handle the annoying cases I actually hit: subgroups with tiny sample sizes, subgroupings somebody invented after looking at the results, and two different ways of slicing that each reverse the aggregate but in different directions.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (a) the classic medical one where a treatment looks worse overall but better within both severe and mild patients, where severity drives both who gets treated and outcomes; (b) my onboarding A/B test, where the platform split is real but it turns out the new flow also *caused* some Android users to be misclassified as iOS via a shared-device quirk we introduced; and (c) a hiring funnel that looks neutral overall but shows a gap within every department, where candidates choose which department to apply to and that choice is itself influenced by our employer branding. I'm hoping at least one of these lands somewhere unintuitive -- e.g. the aggregate being the right call even though every subgroup reverses it -- because that's exactly the result I'll have to defend to a skeptical room, so please spell out how I'd justify it. And be upfront about what the procedure *can't* do, i.e. which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.
