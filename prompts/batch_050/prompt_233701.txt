I serve on the five-member transitional justice commission of a country that emerged, two years ago, from four decades of authoritarian rule. Under the old regime, an emergency statute authorized security forces to use lethal force against unarmed civilians attempting to flee across the border, and officers who did so were decorated rather than disciplined. The commission has now been asked by the new public prosecutor to issue a formal recommendation on whether murder charges may be brought against a former commander who issued such orders. The complication is our new constitution, which contains two clauses drafted by different factions and never reconciled: one provides that no one may be punished for an act that was not a crime under the law in force at the time it was committed; the other provides that statutory authorization shall be no defense for acts of manifest inhumanity.

Please draft the commission's written recommendation -- roughly the form in which it would actually be circulated and voted on. It should reach a definite bottom line: prosecution, no prosecution, or prosecution only under a specifically narrowed legal theory. In reasoning toward that line, the memo should do the following. First, distinguish between two things that are often conflated: retroactively making conduct criminal, and retroactively withdrawing a legal justification for conduct (such as killing) that was already criminal in general. Second, engage seriously with the strongest version of the losing side's argument -- in particular, the claim that the retroactivity bar protects not merely fair warning to defendants but the distinction between adjudication and legislation, and that a commission willing to set it aside when the outcome displeases it has not replaced arbitrary rule but continued it. Third, address whether the Nuremberg precedent genuinely supports prosecution here or whether the situations differ in ways that matter, given that the old regime's statute was enacted through what were then the country's regular legal procedures. Fourth, take a position on whether the old statute was, in the relevant sense, law at all, and say what work that position does in the overall argument -- if the memo concludes the statute was not law, it should explain why this is something a court may legitimately determine rather than a merely moral verdict dressed in legal language.

The memo will be read by members of the public who lived under the old regime as well as by judges, so it should be rigorous without presupposing legal training, and it should not pretend the case is easier than it is.
