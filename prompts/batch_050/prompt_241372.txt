So I cover science and ideas for a magazine and I've been interviewing the same animal-welfare philosopher for a few years now about which invertebrates deserve moral weight. Her calls so far: octopuses, strong priority even though relatively few of them exist; honeybees, priority, and she stressed the staggering numbers; lab fruit flies, basically no, even with billions used in research every year; farmed shrimp, leaning yes after the recent nociception studies, with the sheer scale of the industry helping her case. Now there's a push in effective-altruist circles to start caring about springtails and soil mites -- quadrillions of them, brains of a few hundred neurons, evidence for anything like felt experience basically nil. Predict her verdict on the mites, tell me which rule she seems to be running (a probability-of-sentience threshold applied per individual, an expected-total-sentience calc where the numbers swamp everything, or something about evidence quality), which of her earlier calls does the most work in telling those rules apart, and what new case would actually settle it if her track record underdetermines the answer.
