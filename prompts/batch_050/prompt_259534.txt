I run the incident review process at a mid-sized regional hospital -- every time there's a serious adverse event (a missed diagnosis, a med error, a fall), a team writes up a root-cause analysis and I have to sign off on the causal story before it goes to the quality committee. That's maybe 15-20 reports a year, and honestly the stakes feel high because whatever cause we land on becomes policy: new checklists, new staffing rules, sometimes disciplinary stuff. Lately I've gotten obsessed with the worry that these reports are basically all contaminated by hindsight bias -- once you know the patient died, every slightly-off thing in the chart looks like an obvious red flag that "should have been caught." And the narrative fallacy on top of that: the report always ends up as one clean story with a single cause, when my gut says most of these events are messy piles of small things that happened to line up.

What I want is an actual procedure -- like a checklist or a sequence of tests my review teams can run on their draft causal claims before I see them -- that operationalizes whatever we know about hindsight bias and narrative fallacy into concrete steps. For example, is there some version of "would this factor have seemed significant if you didn't know the outcome?" that can be turned into an actual test people can apply to a chart note, rather than a vague aspiration? I'd like the steps in order, with clear instructions for what to do when a causal claim fails a step (rewrite it? downgrade it? drop it?), and I need it usable by smart clinicians who have zero background in psychology or philosophy and about 90 minutes per report. Please mark explicitly the points where the procedure can't make the call and someone just has to exercise judgment, because I don't want teams treating this like a flowchart that absolves them.

Also walk me through the procedure applied to two or three realistic example findings (you can invent them, e.g. "nurse failed to recheck the allergy band" or "the overnight CT was read as normal"). Include at least one case where the procedure spits out an answer I won't like -- like concluding we can't actually blame anyone or any single process, when the committee is going to really want a culprit -- and tell me how to defend that answer. And be upfront about what this whole approach can't do, so I know what I'm still exposed to.
