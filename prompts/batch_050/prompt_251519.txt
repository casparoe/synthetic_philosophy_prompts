I teach a final-year political philosophy module, seminar group of about 18, and I get one 75-minute session on secession and self-determination. Every year the discussion goes the same way: everyone says "let the people vote" and then someone mentions Crimea or whatever and it all gets mushy.

Design me an in-class activity (simulation, game, whatever works) that makes them run into the *recursive* problem of secession themselves, before I name it. By which I mean: if a region of a state gets to secede because its people want to, what about a region of *that* new state whose people (a minority within the seceding territory) want to stay with the old state, or secede in turn? Think the Catalonia question turning into a question about some comarca inside Catalonia. I want the students who played the secessionists in round one to find themselves in round two arguing, with dawning horror, exactly the unionist arguments they just demolished -- the indivisibility of the territory, the unfairness to the minority, "you can't just carve off the bits you like". That's the moment I'm after.

Practical stuff I need:
- Actual mechanics: how the room gets divided into territories/factions, what the factions can do (vote? negotiate? block?), how secession claims get made and adjudicated, and how many rounds it needs for the recursion to bite. Nothing that requires more than paper, pens and maybe some counters.
- Some way of making the stakes feel real-ish without money -- points, tokens, whatever -- but the point system shouldn't swamp the philosophy, so keep it simple.
- How to set it up so the seceding group *doesn't see round two coming*. Ideally the rules allow for counter-secession from the start but nobody reads that bit carefully.
- A debrief plan (last 15-20 mins) mapping what happened onto the actual literature-ish questions: who is "the people" that gets to decide, whether self-determination claims are symmetric, remedial vs. primary-right theories of secession. But keep it in plain language, they won't have done the reading.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to rescue it. I'm guessing at least one group will try to secede immediately just for laughs and break the pacing.
- One honest paragraph on what the activity *can't* capture about real secession disputes -- the history, the identity stuff, the actual grievances -- so I know what I have to supply by talking.

Answer in British English please, and keep the tone fairly practical -- I need to actually run this, not admire it.
