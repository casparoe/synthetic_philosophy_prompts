I am a retired molecular biologist who, after forty years in the laboratory, has spent the last two years reading philosophy, and I have decided to spend my retirement doing a serious year-long project rather than merely attending reading groups. My former university runs a small fellowship for independent scholars -- one year, no teaching, a modest stipend, no laboratory access, nothing but a library card and time -- and I need a two-page proposal. My half-formed idea concerns the paradox of fiction: whether, and in what sense, we can genuinely fear for, pity, or grieve over characters we know perfectly well do not exist. But what strikes me as underexplored, coming at this as an empiricist, is a tension internal to the phenomenon itself. On the one hand, emotions toward fiction are strangely dissoluble: reminding oneself that it is only a story, or noticing a camera crew in the frame, can deflate fear almost instantly in a way that reminding oneself of statistics rarely deflates fear of flying. On the other hand, they are strangely persistent: suspense survives re-reading and re-watching, when ignorance of the outcome -- and hence any plausibly belief-like state about it -- is entirely gone. My suspicion is that these two facts pull against the standard taxonomy of positions (roughly: illusion or belief theories, make-believe or simulation accounts, and thought-based accounts) in ways the literature has not exploited, and that an account of the attentional and regulative character of these emotions, rather than of their cognitive content, could explain both facts at once.

Please turn this into a proper research proposal. It should state the question precisely -- I suspect my two facts above need sharpening before they can do any work, and part of the proposal's job is to say exactly what would have to be true of each phenomenon for my argument to go through. It should explain why the existing positions, considered as I have described them without names or citations (I will supply the references myself; I would rather you misattribute nothing), leave this question open, and why approaching the paradox through dissolubility and persistence is better than simply relitigating the old question of whether fear of fiction is irrational. It should describe the method concretely and month by month: this is a conceptual and phenomenological project, not an empirical one, but I want it to remain answerable to what psychology knows about emotional regulation and attention, so the plan should include systematic first-person case studies (mine and collected reports), analysis of the conditions under which fiction-reminders succeed and fail, and engagement with whatever empirical findings bear on these, all achievable by one person with a library card. And it should say what a deliverable looks like at year's end, and -- this matters to me as a former bench scientist -- what would count as the project failing, including a plausible result in which my two phenomena turn out to be independent and the whole framing collapses.

Finally, since the proposal will have to defend a direction of argument rather than merely survey options, I want it to be explicit about its own vulnerability. Please include a short section stating which consideration or argument, if it turned out to hold, would lead to the opposite verdict from the one the proposal anticipates -- for instance, some fact about the re-reading phenomenon that would make the attentional account otiose -- and identify the single point in the projected reasoning where the argument is most likely to break. I would rather submit a proposal that knows where it can be killed than one that pretends to be bulletproof.
