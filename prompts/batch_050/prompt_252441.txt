I work as a statistician on problems in causal inference and predictive modeling, and I've been reading philosophy of biology on the side. I keep running into the propensity interpretation of fitness -- the idea, as I understand it, that an organism's fitness is not its actual number of offspring but a probabilistic disposition, something like its expected reproductive success given its traits and environment -- and the old objection that "survival of the fittest" threatens to be a tautology if the fittest are simply defined as those who survive. I find myself automatically translating this debate into the vocabulary I actually think in -- estimands versus estimates, expectations versus realized draws, identifiable versus unidentifiable parameters, model specification, prediction versus retrodiction -- and I'd like you to do that translation systematically and carefully.

So: restate the propensity interpretation and the main moves in the tautology debate in statistical vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. For example, is "fitness" best understood as an estimand (a feature of the data-generating process) rather than an estimate, and does that already dissolve the circularity worry, the way it's not circular to say a coin has a bias even though we can only learn the bias from observed flips? Does the objection resurface as a point about identifiability -- that if the propensity can only ever be inferred from the very reproductive outcomes it's supposed to explain, then the model is underdetermined no matter how much data we have? Where do the defenders' distinctions -- between token and type organisms, between actual and expected offspring number, between fitness as a cause and fitness as a summary -- land in the translation?

Then, and this is the part I care most about, give me a residue report. Which claims in the debate translate cleanly into statistical terms, which ones you had to distort to fit, and which have no expression in that vocabulary at all? I suspect the residue involves something about dispositions being real features of individual organisms rather than parameters of a model a scientist writes down, and maybe something about what evolutionary theory is for that isn't captured by prediction or causal estimation. Tell me whether the residue shows that my statistical vocabulary is missing something the biologists and philosophers need, or whether it shows that part of the original debate was less substantive than it looks. Be honest in the translation: don't smuggle statistical assumptions into the restatement and then call the mismatch a defect of the philosophy.
