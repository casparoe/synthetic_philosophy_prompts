I'm part of a philosophy reading group, and last week a friend who is very enthusiastic about whole-brain emulation walked us through an argument that has been bothering me since, because it seems to prove too much. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher who specializes in personal identity and the ethics of mind uploading, and to think carefully and slowly about this — first reconstruct the strongest version of each step before diagnosing anything, and only then tell me where the argument goes wrong. Here is the argument, as I wrote it down after the meeting. (1) You would survive the replacement of a single neuron by a silicon chip that does exactly what that neuron did, moment to moment. After all, your brain's matter turns over constantly anyway, and no one thinks a person is constituted by one particular neuron. (2) If you would survive one such replacement, you would survive any sequence of them carried out gradually, because there is no principled place to draw a line at which swapping one more neuron suddenly kills you — any such line would be arbitrary. (3) So you would survive having your entire brain gradually replaced, neuron by neuron, with functionally equivalent silicon: an in-place upload. (4) What the gradual-replacement argument shows is that what makes a future being you is that it carries your psychology — your memories, character, and intentions, realized with the right causal organization — rather than what it is made of. (5) But a being can carry your psychology and organization without being made of your matter or being causally continuous with your body in the gradual way: your brain could be scanned and a silicon copy assembled elsewhere from entirely new materials. (6) So you would also survive scan-and-copy uploading — your body is painlessly destroyed while the copy wakes up on a server and greets your family. (7) And since the reasoning at no point depends on how many copies are made, if two copies were made from the same scan, you would survive as each of them. (8) But the two copies, once their experiences diverge, are plainly not identical to each other, and nothing can be numerically identical to each of two non-identical things. Contradiction — so something in (1)–(7) has to go.

My friend's own verdict was that the error must be in (1)–(3): gradual replacement is a slow, dignified death, and the intuition in premise (1) is just a sorites fallacy applied to persons. I find that conclusion very hard to swallow — it gives up the whole appeal of the uploading picture — but I can't accept (7) either, and I notice that nobody at the meeting could agree on which step to blame. If I have to choose, I would most like to keep (1) and (2), and I'd at least like to keep (3). What I want from you is a genuine diagnosis, not a survey: name the specific step you reject, explain why it looked innocent (especially if the trouble hides in the transitions — between (3) and (4), (4) and (6), or (6) and (7) — rather than inside any numbered premise), and show that your diagnosis actually dissolves the contradiction rather than merely refusing to state it.

Then price the alternatives I didn't pick, so I know what each costs: the animalist who rejects (1) on the grounds that you are a biological organism and no replacement of your parts preserves you; the psychological-continuity theorist who accepts (1)–(6) but adds a no-branching clause to block (7), and who owes us an account of why the existence of a second copy could make a difference to whether the first one is you; and the view that concedes the contradiction by denying that numerical identity is what matters in survival at all, treating branching cases as ones where you get everything that matters twice over. Finally, answer the follow-up that worries me most: if the flaw lies somewhere after (3), can one consistently hold that gradual in-place replacement is survival while scan-and-copy is death-by-duplication, without the difference between the two cases collapsing into a mere preference for one delivery mechanism over another? If yes, say what does the philosophical work; if no, say which of the two I should give up.
