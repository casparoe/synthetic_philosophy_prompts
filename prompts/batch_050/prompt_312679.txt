I'm a PhD student in bioethics (my dissertation is on population ethics, but I've gotten sucked into the applied side this semester) and my reading group is going through the literature on procreative beneficence. Last week I tried to reconstruct what I take to be the strongest version of the critics' "this proves too much" objection, and the annoying thing is I can't break it. Here's my reconstruction. Say a couple -- call them the Reyeses -- are deciding whether to conceive this year. (1) Procreative beneficence: among the children a couple could have, they should select the one whose life can be expected, on the available evidence, to go best. (2) Which children a couple "could have" is fixed by what they could bring about through their choices, since conceiving at a different time or with different gametes produces a numerically different child (this is just the non-identity point that defenders of the principle themselves rely on). (3) But then the children the Reyeses could have in five or ten years -- with better polygenic screening, better embryo selection, maybe safe gene editing, plus their own improved finances -- are just as much "children they could have" as any child conceived tonight. (4) For almost any child they could have now, they could have a predictably better-off child later, since the tech keeps improving and the relevant traits are substantially heritable. (5) So the principle requires the Reyeses to postpone, and requires the same of their later selves, and so on -- meaning almost no one alive today is permitted to conceive now, full stop. (6) That conclusion is absurd, so something above has to go.

What bugs me is that each premise looks like something the principle's own defenders already accept, and if the argument is valid then the view isn't a moderate thesis about embryo selection but a near-universal moratorium on reproduction. Tell me which step you think fails and why it was tempting, and -- just as important -- what it costs to reject each of the alternatives. For instance, if you reject (2) or (3) I'd like to know where the boundary of "possible children" is supposed to fall that doesn't also wreck the standard non-identity treatment of the original embryo selection cases; if you reject (1), what weaker principle survives and does it still do the work procreative beneficence was introduced to do; and if you bite the bullet on (5), explain why indefinite postponement isn't actually a reductio. Please answer in American English, and feel free to be blunt if you think I've equivocated somewhere -- I half suspect there's a slide between steps but can't pin it down.
