Between October 2018 and March 2019, two Boeing 737 MAX airliners crashed shortly after takeoff — Lion Air Flight 610 and Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 — killing 346 people. The salient facts, which you may take as given, are these. A flight-control subsystem called MCAS could command repeated nose-down trim in response to readings from a single angle-of-attack sensor; on both flights, faulty sensor data triggered it, and the crews could not diagnose or counter it in time. MCAS had been deliberately engineered to be invisible to pilots, so that the MAX would handle like earlier 737s and airlines could avoid simulator retraining; it was absent from the flight crew manual. Each aircraft in fact carried two angle-of-attack sensors, and an alert that would have told the crew when the two disagreed was inoperative on most delivered airframes because Boeing had wired it to an optional display feature. The aircraft, in a defensible sense, possessed information that would have reorganized the crew's entire understanding of their situation; no channel existed by which that information could reach them, and this was the result of design and commercial decisions, not accident.

Analyze this episode through the framework of eliciting latent knowledge (ELK). The framework, in outline: an AI system whose internal computations encode information its overseers cannot directly verify is paired with a "reporter" meant to state, in terms humans can check, what the system has in effect concluded. The framework's central pathology is that a reporter optimized for answers humans find plausible converges toward being a human simulator — reporting what the human would conclude given the human's evidence — rather than a direct translator of what the system's internal state actually tracks, and the two coincide exactly in the cases where the human's judgment matches the system's, which are precisely the cases where the reporter is least needed.

Do the analysis in both directions. First, map the machinery: what plays the role of the model, the reporter, and the overseer, and is the human-simulator pathology the right description of an alerting architecture whose outputs were optimized, by explicit design, to be unsurprising to a crew trained on an earlier airframe? Second, say where the mapping strains: the framework presupposes an overseer posing questions and locates the failure in the reporter's training signal, whereas here the crew could pose no question they had not been told existed, and the one channel that would have answered the question they did face had been severed for commercial reasons. Does the framework even distinguish a reporter that distorts from one that was never connected, and should it? Third, run the case back against the theory: does the episode vindicate the ELK diagnosis (the cockpit was organized so that pilots saw roughly what prior experience taught them to expect, until the divergence was fatal), or does it reveal that the framework has misidentified the binding failure mode — that the practical problem is not deceptive reporters but institutional decisions about what gets reported at all, and to whom, before any question of faithful translation arises? Finally, consider the remedies actually imposed after the grounding — mandatory disagree alerts, dual-sensor inputs, crew disclosure — and ask whether the framework would have prescribed them, or whether its remedies (better training signals for the reporter) address a different problem than the one this case exhibits. Where the framework is simply silent about the episode, say so rather than forcing it to speak.
