I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine and I'm writing a piece on the "taxation is theft" slogan, which is everywhere again online. What I keep noticing is that both sides are actually running an analogy, not an argument: the libertarian side says taxation is like a mugger taking your wallet at gunpoint (the state has a nicer costume but the structure is identical: hand over your money or else), and the defending side says taxation is like membership dues at a club you chose to join, or like the bill at the end of a restaurant meal you ordered knowing the prices. Each side then treats its analogy as settling the matter.

Can you audit both analogies for me? Specifically:

1. The correspondence table for each: in the mugging version, what plays the role of the threat, the wallet, the absence of consent? In the club-dues version, what plays the role of joining, of the membership benefits, of the option to resign?
2. Where each mapping breaks. The obvious pressure points I can see are: you never actually signed up to the club (you were born in it and leaving costs you your language, family and career, unlike cancelling a gym membership); but equally the mugger doesn't fix the road outside your house or inoculate your kids, so the wallet-story ignores everything you get back. Work through these and any others systematically rather than just listing them.
3. What each analogy quietly smuggles in. For example I suspect the mugging picture presupposes that your pre-tax income was fully and legitimately yours in the first place, which is arguably the very thing in dispute -- and the club picture presupposes that political obligation works like a contract, which is also in dispute. Check my hunches here.
4. Whether the slogans' conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether there's a better analogy that captures whatever's genuinely load-bearing in each (someone suggested to me taxation is like being billed for a meal you were served at a restaurant you never chose but also can't easily leave -- is that better or worse?).

Please keep it accessible -- my readers are smart but not philosophers -- and write in British English. Also be fair: I don't want a piece that pretends one analogy is perfect, and I don't want a mushy both-sides conclusion if one of them genuinely collapses under scrutiny.
