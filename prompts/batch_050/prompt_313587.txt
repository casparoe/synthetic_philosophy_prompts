While reading a comparative essay on national myths, I kept encountering the slogan 'Masada shall not fall again' — invoked, at one point, not as a claim about archaeology or the events of the siege itself, but as an argument-clinching reason why the state in question must never depend on outside powers for its security. I realised I could not say precisely what the slogan asserts, and I suspect its argumentative force depends on leaving that unclear.

Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses this one sentence might be expressing? For each reading, please identify: (i) whether it is a descriptive claim about history, a prediction about the future, a prescriptive principle of statecraft, a piece of identity-constituting myth, or some hybrid; (ii) whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or straightforwardly false; and (iii) whether that reading is actually capable of supporting the use I saw it put to — namely, justifying a doctrine of military self-reliance and pre-emption — or whether that use requires sliding between two readings, one plausible and one strong enough to carry the conclusion. I would also welcome your judgement on which reading the people who repeat the slogan most likely intend, and whether the slogan's power as a national myth depends on the ambiguity rather than surviving it.

Please write in British English. Where your answer commits to a claim — particularly in (iii) and in your reconstruction of what slogan-users intend — please say how confident you are in it, and single out explicitly the one point in your analysis you are least sure of.
