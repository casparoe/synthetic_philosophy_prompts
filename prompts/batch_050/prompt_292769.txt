A philosophy reading group is preparing a session on the grandfather paradox, and I want a decision tree that sorts the main positions on it by what their proponents actually believe rather than by the names they give their views. Take as the fixed starting commitment that travel to the past has occurred: a time traveler steps out of a machine fifty years before their own birth, stands beside their grandfather's house, armed and resolved to prevent their own conception. The tree should begin from this scenario and consist of four to six diagnostic questions, each phrased as a concrete question a thoughtful person could answer without knowing which position the answer commits them to. Good raw material includes questions about whether the past can be changed or only affected, whether there is one timeline or many, and whether whatever prevents the killing does so as a matter of logic, of physical law, or of contingent fact, but you should select and order the questions yourself, working from the logical space of possible answers rather than from a list of named views.

Each leaf should land on a named position: a self-consistency view of the Novikov type, on which the traveler was always part of a single fixed past and so necessarily fails; a branching or many-worlds resolution, on which the killing succeeds on some branch while the original history remains intact; a chronology-protection view, on which physics conspires to prevent closed timelike curves from ever being exploited in this way at all; and at least one further leaf of your choosing, whether or not any real position occupies it. Wherever a combination of answers corresponds to a view nobody holds, mark it and diagnose why: is that region empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? Flag explicitly any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate; in particular, say whether a branching view in which killing one's grandfather on another branch counts as changing the past genuinely diverges from the self-consistency view on the question of whether the past can be changed, or only redescribes it, and identify the further diagnostic question that would be needed to pull the two apart.

Present the entire tree in continuous prose, the way one would explain it in an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, no diagram. Narrate each question and each branch in sentences, so that the reader follows the tree by reading statements of the form: if you hold such-and-such, the next question to ask is this, and a no answer there leads to that position. Close with a candid paragraph on where the tree is most likely to mislead: which of its distinctions you expect to blur or dissolve under pressure, and which single argument or consideration, if it turned out to be sound, would most change the way you had drawn the whole thing.
