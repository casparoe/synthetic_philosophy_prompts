I am drafting the survey chapter of a doctoral thesis on the rule of law as an ideal of legality, and before I can argue that the standard inventories are incomplete I need to be certain that they are. The desiderata I have catalogued, drawn from Fuller, Raz, and Waldron, are as follows. From Fuller, the eight principles of legality, failure to satisfy which counts, on his account, as a failure to make law at all:

1. Generality: governance by standing rules rather than ad hoc commands.
2. Promulgation: rules must be made available to those they bind.
3. Prospectivity: no retroactive imposition of duties.
4. Clarity: rules must be intelligible.
5. Consistency: rules must not contradict one another.
6. Possibility of compliance: rules must not demand what cannot be done.
7. Constancy: rules must not change so frequently that subjects cannot orient their conduct by them.
8. Congruence: official action must match the rules as declared.

From Raz, the further requirements that the law be capable of guiding conduct in practice: an independent judiciary; fair and open hearings in adjudication; judicial review of conformity with the other principles; accessibility of the courts, without prohibitive cost or delay; and constraints on police and prosecutorial discretion such that it cannot pervert the law's purposes. From Waldron, the insistence that the rule of law comprises not only rules but institutions and procedures of argument — legislatures and courts in which people can be heard and can contest the law's application to them.

Take this as a fair and complete summary of the canonical positions. My question is whether the canonical positions are themselves complete. Is there a further desideratum — a genuine failure mode of legal systems qua legal systems — that is captured by no item above, nor by any combination of them? If so, give it a short name, state it as a principle, and show that it earns its place: describe a legal system, realistic rather than fanciful, that satisfies every item on the list, or at least violates none of them, while failing yours. The candidate must clear two further hurdles. First, it must not be a rewording, a degree-variant, or a special case of anything already listed — check it in particular against clarity, promulgation, and congruence, which are the items most often stretched to absorb newcomers. Second, it must plausibly belong to the ideal of legality rather than to substantive justice or democracy: on Raz's well-known knife analogy, the rule of law is a virtue of law as such, whatever its content, so the candidate must not smuggle in rights or outcomes under another name. If the best available candidate fails one of these hurdles, say so, and say which existing item it collapses into. Finally, tell me whether the exercise leaves you more or less confident that the traditional inventories are exhaustive.
