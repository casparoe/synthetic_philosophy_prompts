I am a retired biologist -- forty years in ecology and physiology -- and have recently taken up philosophy, at present Zhuangzi, especially the material on death: his equanimity when his wife died (he drummed on a tub when Huizi came to offer condolences), the butterfly dream and the "transformation of things," and his request to be left unburied for the birds rather than fed to the worms. Please restate his view of death in the vocabulary I actually command: conservation of mass and energy, trophic cycling and decomposition, the organism as a metastable eddy maintained far from equilibrium, ecological succession, and the distinction between an individual and a lineage. I want precision, not analogy for its own sake; a partial translation is worth more to me than a fluent-sounding loose one. Then give me the residue report: which elements translate cleanly; which translate only with distortion (I suspect the equanimity is one of these, since biology describes and his stance recommends); and which have no counterpart at all in this vocabulary -- perhaps the question of from whose point of view death counts as a loss, or the skepticism about whether we know life is preferable to death. Finally, your judgment: is the residue genuine philosophical content my framework cannot express, or confusion that the exercise of translation exposes?
