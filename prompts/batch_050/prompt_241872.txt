I'm a retired chemist who spent forty years in a materials science lab, and since retiring I've joined a philosophy reading group at the local library (long story, my wife talked me into it). Last week the group read something about the value of philosophy and two regulars, Geoff and Marta, got into a real argument that ran the whole session, and I've been turning it over since. Neither of them would give an inch, and I'd like an outside referee.

Geoff's position, as I understood it: philosophy obviously makes progress, but the progress mostly shows up as spin-offs. Natural philosophy became physics, the philosophy of mind birthed psychology and then cognitive science, logic went from Aristotle to Frege to computer science. His line was that philosophy is the discipline that works on questions until they're clear enough to hand over to a method that actually settles them, and that's a perfectly respectable kind of progress. When Marta said that sounded like conceding philosophy only advances by ceasing to exist, Geoff said no -- plenty of progress happens *inside* philosophy too, it just looks like elimination. Logical positivism is dead and everyone knows why it's dead, naive versions of various theories get killed off for good, and we now know exactly which assumptions generate which paradoxes. A discipline that reliably falsifies its own bad options is making progress even if it never crowns a winner.

Marta wasn't having it. Her counter was roughly this: eliminating options isn't progress toward truth unless the surviving options are closer to the truth than what you started with, and there's no evidence of that in philosophy. In science, she said (looking at me a bit pointedly), wrong theories die because the world kills them, and the replacements predict things the old ones couldn't. In philosophy, positions die of fashion and then get resurrected -- she claimed behaviourism was supposedly refuted in the sixties and yet its basic instincts are alive and well, and that surveys of professional philosophers show the big questions (free will, moral realism, dualism vs physicalism) split the field roughly the same way decade after decade, which you'd never see in a mature science. Geoff replied that persistent disagreement just shows the questions are hard, and that by her standard mathematics made no progress before anyone had proof machinery because people disagreed -- plus, he said, the PhilPapers-type surveys actually do show movement on some questions. Marta's final move, which Geoff didn't really answer, was: even granting all the eliminations, philosophy never *compounds*. Scientists build on settled results; philosophers have to re-litigate everything from the ground up every generation, which is the signature of a field spinning its wheels rather than climbing.

So -- who's right about what? I'd like a point-by-point verdict: is progress-by-spinoff a real kind of progress or a concession in disguise, does eliminating positions count as advancing toward truth, does persistent expert disagreement show no progress is happening, and does Marta's "no compounding" point actually land? And if both of them are missing something, tell me what it is. Overall, who comes out ahead?
