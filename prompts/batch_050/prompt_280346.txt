Before I pick a side in the grounding literature, I want to know what I should even be demanding of a theory of fundamentality. Write me the desiderata: what any adequate account of the grounding relation, and of what counts as fundamental, has to deliver. It obviously needs to handle the toolkit cases (wholes grounded in parts, the moral grounded in the natural, sets grounded in members), but also stress-test the list on weirder scenarios, including one I made up: a world where every fact is grounded in strictly more fundamental facts, the descent never bottoms out and never repeats a level (call it a gunk chain), yet the world is perfectly determinate at every stage — should a good theory say this world is impossible, possible but nothing in it is fundamental, or possible and unproblematic? Your list should at least let a theorist state the question. Also cover cyclic grounding (a grounds b and b grounds a — ever coherent?), whether grounding is non-monotonic and whether demanding that is a real constraint or just a camp prejudice, and whether grounding facts themselves need grounds (and if so, what grounds them). For each desideratum give the motivating case or consideration and mark it non-negotiable or camp-specific. Then flag any subset of desiderata you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable — that's the part I care about most, since then the list is itself a result. You can briefly score the main contenders (Fine-style operationalism, grounding as metaphysical causation, primitivism about the relation) against the list, but keep that part short. Answer in American English.
