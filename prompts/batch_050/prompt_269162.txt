I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and a pattern in my reporting keeps nagging at me. Let me describe three instances, all drawn from real assignments, and then put my question.

First, a state court uses a risk-assessment tool that classifies a defendant as high risk because people with his record reoffend at a given rate. His attorney counters that among people with his record who also have a job lined up, the rate is far lower. The state's expert replies that among people with his record, a job, and one prior failed diversion program, the rate is higher than the original figure. Nobody disputes anyone's arithmetic. The entire dispute is over which group he really belongs to.

Second, a physician tells a patient that a drug cuts cardiac events by some percentage based on a large trial, then concedes that the patient differs from the average trial participant in several ways — age, weight, a prior condition — and that figures exist for some of those subgroups but not all, and that each further subdivision rests on fewer and fewer people, so the more finely she tailors the estimate, the shakier it becomes.

Third, a climate researcher describes a flood to me as a one-in-a-hundred-year event, and when I ask one hundred years relative to what, the answer depends on whether the comparison set is storms in that river basin, storms in the wider region, or storms under present climate conditions rather than historical ones, each choice yielding a different number.

What strikes me is that in each case there is an obvious temptation to settle the matter by picking whichever classification yields the answer one already wants, and nothing in the data itself seems to forbid this. Is there an established name for the general problem — the fact that any individual case falls under indefinitely many groups with different statistical profiles, so that the prediction you get depends on a choice of group that the facts alone do not dictate? And is there a separate name, or should there be, for the specific move of selecting the group only after seeing which answer it produces? I am after more than a label. I want to know how this general problem relates to, and differs from, things I half-recognize — Simpson's paradox, cherry-picking, base-rate neglect — and, if the second, more deliberate move has no established name, what you would call it, with a definition precise enough to show where it ends and ordinary statistical judgment begins, including some boundary cases. Please write in American English, and in continuous prose only: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. I plan to work passages of your answer into an email to a researcher I hope to interview, so it needs to read like a letter, not a memo.
