I'm a high school student writing an essay for my school's philosophy seminar, and I'd like your help producing a strong model essay on a question I've been circling around.

The topic is what AI-generated fake video and audio do to our ability to know things. Here are two ideas I want the essay to engage with carefully; please treat my definitions as the working ones rather than assuming prior familiarity with the debate. First, the epistemic backstop: before convincing fakes existed, the mere possibility of recordings disciplined public speech. A politician or executive who lied knew that a tape or video might surface and expose them, so recordings didn't just give us evidence after the fact; they kept people more honest in the first place. Second, the liar's dividend: once fakes are cheap and good, wrongdoers caught on genuine recordings can dismiss them as fabricated, collecting a benefit from the very existence of forgery without forging anything themselves.

I think these ideas create a real puzzle when set against an older view of testimony. In his Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man, Thomas Reid argued, in effect, that we are built with a natural propensity to speak the truth and a matching natural disposition to believe what others tell us, and that this trust is a basic starting point of knowledge rather than something we earn through reasoning or experience. A sympathetic reader might say: if deepfakes destroy the backstop, we simply fall back to Reid's default, the same position humans were in before photography, and we managed fine then. A pessimist might reply that we fall somewhere worse than Reid's baseline, because the liar's dividend corrodes the standing disposition to trust itself, which is exactly the thing Reid thought could be taken for granted.

Please write an essay of about 800 to 1,000 words (excluding any material I quoted above) that interprets Reid's idea, applies it to the case of deepfakes, and adjudicates between these two reactions. The essay should weigh the strongest version of each side, address the most serious objection to its own position, and reach a definite verdict rather than ending on both-sides agnosticism. Finally, in a short closing paragraph, the essay should state what would change its mind: which argument or empirical consideration would most plausibly overturn its verdict, or where its reasoning is most vulnerable.
