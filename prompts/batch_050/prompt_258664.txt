A colleague argues that the distinction between "tool" and "agent" AI systems is empty, and that proposals to build advanced systems as tools rather than agents therefore buy no safety. (1) A system counts as a tool only if it produces useful outputs without itself pursuing outcomes in the world. (2) Any system that reliably performs open-ended cognitive tasks -- proving theorems, writing working code, answering novel questions -- must internally represent the world, generate candidate plans, and evaluate them against an objective; that is, it must contain a planner. (3) A planner just is an agent in the only sense that matters for safety: its behavior is explained and predicted by its objective, not by its operators' intentions. (4) So every sufficiently capable tool contains an agent, and the tool/agent distinction is a difference in packaging, not in kind. (5) Packaging -- an API instead of actuators, a query-answer format instead of autonomy -- does not change what the planner inside is optimizing for, and so cannot change the system's safety-relevant properties. (6) Therefore "build tools, not agents" offers nothing: there are only agents, some aligned and some not.

This seems to prove too much. Taken at face value, it implies that a chess engine, a search engine, and a hypothetical superintelligent assistant are all the same kind of thing, differing only in how they are wrapped, and that no design choice short of alignment itself could ever reduce risk. Which step goes wrong, if any does? Explain why the failing step looked plausible, and then price the three main rival diagnoses: rejecting (2), rejecting (3), and rejecting (5). For each, say what criterion or claim you would have to defend to make the rejection stick. If forced to keep one step intact, I would keep (5), but I want to know what that commitment costs me elsewhere in the argument.
