I organize a monthly rationality reading group (about 12 regulars, mostly software engineers and grad students) and our next session is on the reliability of introspection -- specifically the claim, familiar from the social-psychology literature on confabulation, that people's verbal reports about the causes of their own judgments and choices are often confident, fluent, and wrong. Everyone in the group has read the standard summaries and nods along; nobody actually believes it applies to them. I want to fix that.

You are an experienced facilitator of experimental-philosophy workshops with a background in cognitive psychology and a track record of designing activities for skeptical, quantitatively literate adults. Think carefully about this request. Before settling on a design, briefly brainstorm at least three distinct activity structures (one paragraph each), note the main weakness of each, and then commit to the strongest one and develop it in full. Feel free to combine elements across candidates if that wins.

The final design should run about 90 minutes and meet these requirements:

1. Participants must generate their own evidence of confabulation before the concept is named. No lecture up front. They should make real choices, give real reasons for them, and only afterward discover that the causes of those choices were (at least partly) something else -- manipulated by the procedure, or predictable from aggregate patterns they themselves contributed to. The moment of recognition has to come from their own data, not from my authority.

2. Include at least one round where participants are explicitly forewarned about the manipulation and asked to guard against it -- I want the group to find out, in their own case, whether knowing about confabulation protects you from it. Build in a way to compare the warned and unwarned rounds (or warned and unwarned subgroups), and tell me in advance what result you expect and what each possible result would mean for the debrief.

3. Include a written prediction-and-logging component: participants privately record predictions about their own and others' susceptibility before seeing any results, so that overconfidence becomes part of the exhibit.

4. Give me concrete materials: the choice tasks themselves (or precise templates for constructing them), the manipulation mechanism, scripts for transitions, and a timeline. No sleight of hand requiring skills I don't have; everything must be executable by one facilitator with paper, pens, and a whiteboard.

5. A debrief plan that maps specific moments of the session onto the underlying claim, including how to handle the predictable move of "I really did choose it for that reason" -- which is itself the phenomenon and should be treated as such rather than argued down.

Also: an honest section on what this activity cannot demonstrate (e.g., it shows something about post-hoc verbal reports of causes, not necessarily about the accuracy of all introspection, such as reports of current experience), and a list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, with mitigations.
