My philosophy-of-mind professor has been ruling, case by case, on whether people who say strange things genuinely believe what they say. Her verdicts so far: a patient with erotomania who has insisted for years that a television host is secretly in love with her, moved cities to be near him, and organizes her days around decoding his messages -- a genuine belief. A committed conspiracy theorist who sincerely maintains that the moon landings were faked, reasons carefully within that conviction, and spends real money and time on it -- a genuine belief. A brain-injured patient who confidently confabulates an explanation for why he is in the hospital, but abandons it the moment it is gently questioned and never acts on it -- not a belief. A man who for years has loudly credited his lucky cap for his team's wins, but by his own cheerful admission is joking and never lets it influence a single decision -- not a belief. When asked, she also endorses a two-factor account of how convictions like the first one arise (an anomalous experience, plus a failure to reject the hypothesis that would best explain it), but insists that etiology is one question and doxastic status is another.

The next case: a Capgras patient who, for months and under patient cross-examination, unwaveringly maintains that his wife has been replaced by an impostor -- yet lives with her exactly as before, nursing her through an illness, deferring to her judgment, and managing their finances jointly.

Predict her verdict on this case. Then work out at least two rules about what makes an avowal a genuine belief that each fit her entire track record, show where and why they diverge on this case, say which rule she is more likely running and why, and describe one further case that would settle between them. Lead with your predicted verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
