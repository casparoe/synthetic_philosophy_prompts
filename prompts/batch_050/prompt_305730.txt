I am a novelist, and I am trying to work out what I actually believe about time by taking stock of my gut verdicts on particular cases. I would like to know what single view, if any, these verdicts amount to. Here they are.

First: my mother died six years ago. If someone asks whether I have a living mother, I say no, and I mean it without reservation. Yet when I say that I miss my mother, it does not feel as though I am speaking about nothing -- the way I would be if I claimed to miss my sister, since I never had a sister. The dead woman seems to retain a standing that the never-existent sibling lacks.

Second: the grandchildren I may one day have seem to have no standing whatsoever. There is nothing there to refer to, pity, or hope for. The asymmetry between this case and the first strikes me as completely compelling; I do not think I could give it up.

Third: in a finished manuscript of mine, I feel no asymmetry between the first chapter and the last. The ending is exactly as settled as the opening. A reader who has only reached chapter three is merely ignorant of what happens; he is not confronted with something that is not yet there.

Fourth: while I am drafting, by contrast, the unwritten chapters feel genuinely open in a way the completed ones do not. There is, as yet, no fact of the matter about whether my protagonist survives the fire in chapter nineteen. I will decide, and until I do it is unsettled -- not unknown, unsettled.

Fifth: in my historical fiction I feel morally constrained by the real dead in a way I am not constrained by invented characters. I will not slander an actual eighteenth-century apothecary for a plot convenience, though my invented apothecary may be as wicked as the story requires. The real dead are owed something; the invented, nothing.

Sixth: once, on deadline, I deleted an entire subplot and restored it from a backup a week later. I cannot decide whether its events went out of existence and then returned, or persisted quietly in a folder. Both descriptions feel wrong, though for different reasons.

Seventh: my own nonexistence before my birth does not disturb me in the least; my nonexistence after my death fills me with dread. Rationally I suspect these should feel symmetrical, and they simply do not.

Is there one coherent account of what exists in time that delivers all of these verdicts, or nearly all? Please state the principle precisely, clause by clause, and tell me which verdict motivates each clause. If a simpler account requires me to abandon one or two of my verdicts, name them and explain why they are the ones that should go. Finally, tell me what the resulting account predicts for two cases I have not listed: my relationship to my ten-year-old self, who in some sense no longer exists either, and the status of an abandoned manuscript I will never finish.
