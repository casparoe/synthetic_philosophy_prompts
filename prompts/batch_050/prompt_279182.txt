I teach a second-year decision theory course, about 24 undergrads, 75 minute seminar slot. We're about to do fanaticism — the claim that expected value reasoning commits you to trading away any certain good for a sufficently tiny chance of a big enough payoff. I've lectured this before and it always lands as a curio, something they nod at and forget. This time I want them to actually feel the pull in their stomachs before anyone names the principle.

Design me a classroom activity with real but small stakes (points that convert into something minor, like snacks or a participation bump). The spine I'm imagining, improve freely:

1. A probability ladder. Before anything happens, each student privately writes down their cutoff: the smallest probability at which they'd still pay a point for a shot at a payoff large enough to keep the expected value positive. Then we run actual rounds, halving the probability each time with coins and then dice, and they find out whether they can hold their own line — and what it costs them when they can't.

2. A bank, run by two students, which must genuinely be able to pay out whatever it promises. The moment promised payoffs go astronomical the game should break in an instructive way, because nobody can credibly offer that much of anything. I want that to be a discovery they make, not a point I make.

3. One rigged round they must not know about in advance: a long-shot bet actually pays out. Then in the debrief, before I reveal the rig, we argue about whether winning retroactively vindicated taking the bet. Include how to handle the ethics of the reveal.

Give me: full rules and payoffs, a minute-by-minute timeline, a debrief that maps specific moments onto the philosophy (the arbitrariness of any cutoff, the mugging dynamic, the hidden work done by the fact that you will never collect), the failure modes you predict when this is actually run (students treating points as meaningless, collusion, boredom when nothing pays out — which is sort of the point, but still) and how to handle each, and an honest note on which feature of fanaticism the activity structurally can't reproduce. My guess is it's the part where the stakes are real, but push back if it's something else.

They should leave genuinely unsure whether their cutoff was a principle or a mood.
