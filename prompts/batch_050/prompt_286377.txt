Since retiring from a career in experimental chemistry, I have been reading in philosophical methodology, and I keep running up against a dispute I would like to see mapped rather than summarised: whether philosophers should be analysing the concepts we actually have or engineering better ones. Please construct a decision tree that takes a reader from the broad commitment that part of philosophy's job is to get our concepts right to a named position, with each branch node phrased as a question about a concrete case or claim rather than a slogan -- for instance, whether a term like 'planet' ever tracked a natural kind that revision could simply get wrong, or whether a theorist who stipulates a new meaning for an old word has thereby changed the subject rather than answered the question. Include as leaves at least conservative conceptual analysis, Carnapian explication, ameliorative analysis in Haslanger's sense, and thoroughgoing conceptual eliminativism; place each by what its defender believes, not by what it calls itself. Note any combination of answers that nobody occupies and diagnose briefly whether the gap is incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect, and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate. Aim for five to seven diagnostic questions, pitched at a reading group of scientists new to philosophy, and please answer in British English.
