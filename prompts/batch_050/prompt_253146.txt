My friend and I have this running argument about which events are "already settled" versus still open. Her verdicts so far: a coin flipped an hour ago in the next room, unobserved -- settled, it landed some way. A coin being flipped right now in Tokyo -- settled, there's a fact of the matter even though we can't know it yet. Whether a star 1000 light-years away exploded 999 years ago, so the light reaches us next year -- settled, it either did or it didn't. The numbers in next year's lottery, which is drawn by a genuinely quantum machine -- not settled. And whether a probe launched yesterday on a perfectly Newtonian trajectory will hit its target -- settled, "that's already fixed, we just haven't done the math." Now the new case: on a planet in Andromeda, about 2.5 million light-years away, someone is right this moment (in our frame) deciding whether to launch a ship, and their decision hinges on the outcome of a quantum measurement happening at that moment. Is their decision settled from our perspective right now? I can see at least two rules that fit all her earlier verdicts and diverge here -- one that cares about what's determined by what's already in our past, one that cares about what lies on or behind our present moment -- and maybe a third that says the question itself is missing something about reference frames. Which rule do you think she's actually running, what does each candidate predict for the Andromeda case, and what case would you put to her next to tell the rules apart?
