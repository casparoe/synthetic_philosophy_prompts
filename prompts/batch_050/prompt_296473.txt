So I was re-reading Anselm's Proslogion after an argument with a friend (long story, it started as a conversation about whether LessWrong-style "map and territory" talk would have helped medieval philosophers) and I got stuck on something that all the explainers I remember seem to gloss over. In chapter 2 Anselm moves from the fool understanding the phrase "that than which nothing greater can be conceived" to the claim that it would be a contradiction for this thing to exist only in the understanding and not in reality, because a version existing in reality would be greater. In chapter 3 he then adds what looks like a second, modal move: something that cannot be conceived not to exist is greater than something that can. Everyone treats these as one argument, but the logic seems genuinely different -- the first turns on comparing the same object across two "modes of existence" (in intellectu vs in re), the second turns on a modal property (necessary vs contingent existence). So my question: are these actually two separate arguments, or is chapter 3 just a corollary of chapter 2?

The thing I really want explained is what has to be true about intentionality and about "greatness" for the chapter 2 inference to even get off the ground. Like, for the comparison to work, the very same thing has to exist-in-the-understanding and then either exist or fail to exist in reality, with existence-in-reality counting as a respect in which one is greater than the other. But that seems to require treating "the thing the fool understands" as a genuine object that can bear properties -- which sounds like it commits Anselm to something Meinong-flavored, objects that have some features without existing. Meanwhile Kant's famous line of objection (existence is not a real predicate, it adds no new property to a concept) attacks exactly that comparison. Does Anselm have the resources to reply? I've seen it suggested that his own distinction between grasping the words and genuinely conceiving the thing (he draws it when he responds to the fool) does some quiet work here, maybe even undercutting the premise that the fool successfully has this being "in the understanding" at all -- which would be weird, since that premise is doing the loading in chapter 2.

So basically: walk me through the actual structure of Proslogion 2-4 as carefully as you can. What exactly is the inference in each chapter, what does it presuppose about objects and about existence as a greatness-making feature, where does the Kant-style objection bite hardest, and does the chapter 3 modal version survive objections that kill the chapter 2 version (or vice versa)? I'm not asking for a survey of every philosopher's take or a reading list -- I want the mechanics laid out clearly enough that I could reconstruct the argument myself and see precisely which premise a skeptic should deny.
