Now that I am retired from a career in experimental physics, I have been reading in the philosophy of history, and I keep noticing a pattern in arguments that I cannot find a name for, though I suspect one exists. It goes like this. Someone claims that some episode in history was contingent and therefore mattered: had things gone slightly differently, the outcome would have been different. The reply, almost always, is that the outcome would have arrived anyway by another route. The lightbulb would have been commercialized without Edison, since Swan and others were there at nearly the same moment; calculus would have existed without Newton, since Leibniz produced it independently; the theory of evolution by natural selection would have reached us without Darwin, since Wallace was converging on it; Bell and Gray filed their telephone patents on the same day. From the existence of these near-substitutes, the conclusion is drawn that the particular person or event did not really matter. What strikes me is that this inference is treated as complete, when it seems to me to contain a gap.

Here is the gap, stated by analogy with my old field. When we say a system reaches the same equilibrium regardless of initial conditions, we mean the relevant state variables end up the same; we do not mean every detail of the final state is identical. The historical version seems to conflate the two. Suppose Edison had never lived and the incandescent lamp had been commercialized by someone else two years later, with a different filament, a different socket, a different voltage convention, and a different company setting the early standards. At the coarse level, we still get electric light; the thesis that the lightbulb was inevitable survives. But at the fine level, all sorts of downstream arrangements -- which technical conventions got frozen in, which firms and institutions accrued the early advantage, perhaps even the layout of electrical infrastructure -- would presumably differ, and once such things are established they are notoriously hard to dislodge, as the persistence of keyboard layouts and railway gauges is supposed to show. So the reply that the outcome would have happened anyway seems to establish inevitability at one level of description while leaving contingency untouched at another, and the arguer slides between the two levels without noticing. The same move appears in the opposite direction too: defenders of contingency point to trivial differences in the replacement outcome as though any difference at all refuted the inevitability claim, when the interesting question is whether the differences are of the kind that later amplify rather than wash out.

My question is whether this pattern -- this sliding between "an outcome of that kind was bound to occur" and "the outcome as it actually occurred was bound to occur," or the mirror-image slide between "the details would have differed" and "things would have gone differently" -- has an established name, and whether the literature has a principled way of marking the two levels apart. I am aware of adjacent notions: the sociological observation that discoveries are often made independently and near-simultaneously, the debate between those who find history contingent and those who find it inevitable, and the idea that early accidents can lock in later arrangements. But none of these is quite the thing I mean, which is a specific failure of inference that trades on the level of description. If it has no name, what would you call it, and where exactly would you draw its edges -- what counts as the same outcome, for these purposes, and what counts as a difference that matters? I should say plainly that I will not be satisfied with a bare label; what I want is the carving.
