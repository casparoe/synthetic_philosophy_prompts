So some background: I spent about 35 years as an experimental physicist (condensed matter), retired a few years back, and somewhere in there I fell down the philosophy of statistics rabbit hole. I ran my whole career on frequentist machinery — p-values, confidence intervals, the works — and now I'm reading about subjective Bayesianism and I honestly can't tell whether these two camps actually disagree about anything or whether they just mean different things by the word probability.

Here's what I want: draft the joint statement that would come out of an adversarial collaboration between a strict frequentist and a subjective Bayesian (the de Finetti flavor, where probabilities are nothing more than an agent's degrees of belief). Imagine both are smart, charitable, and stuck in a room together until they produce something both will sign. The format I'm after:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. The boring ones don't count — sure, both accept the Kolmogorov axioms and Bayes' theorem as math. I want the substantive ones: can both sign, say, that observed frequencies are evidence that should constrain credences? That long-run calibration is a contraint on rational belief even if it isn't what probability *is*? Push past the trivial stuff.

2. A disagreements table. Each row: the exact sentence each side would put its own name to, worded so the other side reads it and says "yes, that is indeed what you think" rather than complaining about strawmen. Cover at least these: single-case events (my favorite example — a coin that gets flipped once and then melted down; the frequentist seems to have no probability for it at all, while the Bayesian produces one instantly); what a weather forecaster's 70% chance of rain actually means, and how you'd check afterward whether it was *right*; and whether probability lives in the world or in the head.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions where, if you could somehow settle them, at least one camp would have to give ground. For each one, record in advance what each side predicts or what it concedes would follow. And be honest — if it turns out neither side can name anything that would actually move them, that itself is a finding worth stating plainly.

4. Last: is any of this dispute purely verbal? My suspicion after a year of reading is that both sides could sign a truce where the word probability gets split into two concepts (roughly, credence and something like limiting frequency or objective chance) and the whole fight evaporates. But every time I try to write down what the frequentist means by chance for a single case, it slips through my fingers. If the truce works, draft it. If it breaks somewhere, show me exactly where.

One constraint: both parties are allowed to acknowledge that the propensity interpretation exists as a rival, but don't let it into the room. Two camps is complicated enough for one document.
