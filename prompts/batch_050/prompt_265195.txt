I teach an introductory comparative ethics course at a small university (about 20 students, mostly first-years, one 75-minute session), and we're about to start a unit on classical Chinese ethics. During a recent department lunch, a colleague half-jokingly claimed that modern effective altruism is just Mohism rediscovered, and a sinologist colleague pushed back hard, saying that gets Mozi wrong and ignores why Confucians found him so threatening. The argument stayed with me, and I want my students to feel what was actually at stake in it before they read a word of either text. So: design a structured activity or game that makes participants feel the genuine pull of both impartial care and graded love — the Mohist idea that each person's welfare should weigh the same regardless of their relationship to you, and the Confucian idea that love is properly strongest toward family and attenuates outward in circles. No lecture beforehand; students should not hear the names Mozi or Confucius, or the phrases "impartial care" and "graded love," until the debrief.

The hard part, and the thing I most want help with, is making sure the activity doesn't rig the contest. Every version I've sketched collapses into a morality play for one side. I want a scenario (I'm imagining something like distributing limited resources — medicine, sandbags, boat seats — across a community where each participant has designated kin and neighbors of varying closeness, but take or leave that) with explicit rules, roles, and payoffs such that: (a) there is a round or mechanism where partiality toward one's own looks not merely natural but morally admirable — where a player who abandons their own child or parent for a stranger seems cold, even monstrous, to the table; and (b) a separate round or mechanism where the same partiality, played out by everyone at once, scales into something ugly — hoarding, the neglected orphan, the village-level coordination failure, something that rhymes with the Mohist diagnosis that the world's harms flow from people caring only for their own. Crucially, I also want a rule variant that forces students to see that impartial care is not the same thing as giving everyone an equal share — that weighting interests equally can mean giving more to the worse-off stranger than to your comfortable cousin — because my students reliably conflate the two.

Please give me: the full setup and materials; the rules round by round, including what players know and don't know and how payoffs or outcomes are scored; a rough timeline for 75 minutes; and then a debrief plan that names the doctrines only at the end and maps specific, predictable moments from the game onto the two positions — including where each position bites back (the Confucian charge that impartiality erases the relationships through which care is learned and constituted, and the Mohist reply that graded affection is nepotism with a pedigree). Finally, tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong when I run this — students gaming the impartial round, quiet students being steamrolled, the group treating it as pure game theory — and how to handle it, plus a frank note on which feature of the dispute the game cannot reproduce (I suspect something about cultivation over a lifetime, or the statecraft context) and what I'll need to supply in discussion myself.
