I'm a program officer at a mid-sized charitable foundation. We fund anti-poverty stuff -- job training, reentry programs for people leaving prison, financial counseling, that kind of thing. I review maybe 40 proposals a year, each asking for somewhere between 50k and 500k, and I spend a couple hours on each before it goes to our board. No formal training in any of this, I came up through nonprofit admin work.

Here's my problem. In review meetings we keep getting stuck on the same fight. Every proposal explains why some problem happens. One will say recidivism happens because of "prison culture" or because "the system is designed to fail people." Another will say a neighborhood stays poor because of "the local labor market" or "structural racism" or "the culture of poverty." And then half the room nods along and the other half says these aren't explanations at all, just big vague words, and they want to know what actual individual people are doing -- which landlords, which hiring managers, which case workers -- before we fund anything based on it. Meanwhile the first half says if you only ever look at individuals you miss the entire point, that the whole reason these problems exist is stuff that's bigger than any one person.

I've been chewing on this for months and I honestly think both sides are partly right, but we have no consistent way of deciding when a big-picture explanation is doing real work and when it's just a smoke machine. Last month we funded a program whose entire theory of change rested on "changing the culture of the school," whatever that means, and rejected one that seemed solid to me because its explanation was too "holistic," according to one board member. It feels arbitrary.

So what I want is a procedure I can actually run on a proposal. Like: the questions to ask, in order, about any explanation that appeals to something bigger than individuals (a culture, an institution, a market, a norm, a structure). What would count as a good enough answer to each question, at the level of detail I can realistically demand from a two-page concept note, not a dissertation. Where the procedure should just stop and say no. And where it should stop and say "this is a judgment call, escalate to the board," because I definately don't want a checklist pretending it can settle everything. Please be honest about which distinctions the procedure has to keep even if they're fiddly, and which ones I can safely ignore at my level.

Also, is there anything to the worry that the opposite mistake exists -- that some proposals reduce everything to individual choices in a way that's just as hand-wavy? Like "people stay poor because they make bad financial decisions," full stop. If so I want tests for that direction too.

Then run the procedure on these two, walking through each step:

1. A reentry nonprofit says recidivism in our county is high because of "the stigma of a record" -- employers won't hire, landlords won't rent, and the person ends up back inside. They're asking us to fund employer outreach.
2. A jobs program says its last cohort had poor placement rates because "the regional labor market was soft," and wants funding to try again.

And then, if you don't mind, tell me what happens when I run it on my own favorite theory -- the "changing the culture of the school" one we funded. I have a bad feeling it fails, and I'd rather hear that than not.

Finally: where does this whole procedure break down? What kinds of proposals will it judge wrong, in either direction?
