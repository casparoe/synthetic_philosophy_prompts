I've been reading about Galen Strawson's Basic Argument (the one where being responsible for what you do requires being responsible for how you are, which requires already having responsibly made yourself that way, regress, so nobody is truly morally responsible). I'm not sure what I actually think, so let me just give you my gut verdicts on some cases and you tell me what principle I'm operating on, if there is one.

Case 1: ordinary guy, stable upbringing, deliberates carefully and cheats on his taxes anyway. Fully responsible, no question.

Case 2: a man raised from infancy by a violent criminal family, taught that outsiders are prey, becomes a hitman. I want to say less responsible than the tax cheat, but not zero. Something like 40%?

Case 3: same hitman, but at age 17 he had one clear moment where a mentor offered him a legit way out and he shrugged it off. Suddenly I want to push the number up a lot, like the rest of the bad stuff got retroactively his fault.

Case 4: a woman with a brain tumor pressing on the relevant regions who becomes impulsive and shoplifts. Basically not responsible. But here's the thing: I know plenty of impulsive shoplifters have nothing wrong with their brains, just bad genes and worse childhoods, and I blame them. I can't articulate why the tumor matters so much when "caused by stuff outside your control" is true of both.

Case 5: a guy who knowingly lets himself become callous -- stops visiting his sick mom, tells himself he'll feel bad later, doesn't -- and years later doesn't even feel the pull anymore. I blame him for the current coldness even though he can't switch it on now.

Case 6: two identical twins raised identically except one coin-flip event at age 12 sends one into a good crowd and one into a bad one. Both end up where their paths lead. Punishing the unlucky one feels like punishing luck, and yet in practice I totally would.

So: is there one principle behind all this? I suspect my verdicts commit me to something like "you're responsible for an act only if the character it flowed from traces back to some genuinely free self-shaping choice you made" -- but isn't that just Strawson's premise, which makes cases 1, 3, and 5 collapse too, since the tax cheat never chose the character that produced his choice either? If the principle that saves my verdicts ends up implying nobody is responsible, tell me which verdict you'd throw out instead. Also tell me what the principle says about a case I havent listed: someone raised to be a hitman who sincerely, reflectively endorses it -- says he'd choose his life again. More or less responsible than case 2?
