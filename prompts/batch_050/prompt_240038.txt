In most legal systems I know of, you can be convicted as an accomplice for helping someone's crime even when your help made no causal difference at all -- the classic case is the lookout at a robbery that nobody was ever going to interupt, or the person who sells a crowbar to a burglar who had three other crowbars lined up. Meanwhile the same legal systems are absolutly insistent in tort law that you can't be liable for a harm you didn't cause. What theory of moral responsibility does accomplice liability presuppose, if any? Does it assume that wrongfully aligning yourself with someone's wrongdoing is blameworthy in itself, regardless of outcomes, or can a purely causal view make sense of it (maybe via overdetermination, or risk of contributing)? And is the doctrine consistent with how the law treats bystanders, who are usually off the hook entirely, and with tort's causation requirement -- or is the criminal law quietly commited to a view of responsibility that the civil law rejects?
