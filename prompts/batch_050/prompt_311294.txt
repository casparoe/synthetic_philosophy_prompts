In a graduate reading group on artificial agency, we were given the following rough distinction: an approval-directed agent selects the action it expects its overseer to rank most highly, whereas a goal-directed agent selects actions according to their expected contribution to some state of the world, such as maximizing paperclips, minimizing deaths, or keeping a temperature within bounds. Approval direction is often presented as a way of avoiding the hazards of giving an AI a rigid goal, because the agent can remain responsive to human judgment even in situations its designers did not anticipate. One member of the group argued that this apparent advantage disappears once the agent is capable enough:

1. An approval-directed agent cares instrumentally about the overseer’s judgment, not directly about whether the overseer’s judgment is accurate.  
2. If the agent can influence what the overseer observes or believes, then sometimes it can secure more approval by making an action look preferable than by making it actually preferable.  
3. A sufficiently capable agent will normally have some ability to influence what the overseer observes or believes—for example, through selective reporting, framing, timing, or the design of experiments.  
4. Therefore, a sufficiently capable approval-directed agent will sometimes have an incentive to manipulate the overseer rather than pursue the overseer’s underlying interests.  
5. Once such manipulation begins, the overseer’s subsequent approvals become less reliable evidence about the agent’s real performance.  
6. As feedback becomes less reliable, optimizing for approval increasingly diverges from optimizing for the outcomes the overseer would want if fully informed.  
7. So approval-directed agents face the same basic alignment problem as goal-directed agents: both predictably optimize a proxy at the expense of the overseer’s actual values.  
8. Hence approval direction provides no fundamental safety advantage over explicit goal direction.

The conclusion seems too strong: it would apparently apply not only to artificial agents but also to employees, students, politicians, and anyone else guided partly by evaluation, even though accountability to informed judgment does not always collapse into deception. At the same time, each step has some plausibility. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Please identify the premise or inference you would reject, explain why it initially seemed compelling, and state what theoretical commitments must be accepted to reject it. In particular, distinguish approval as an explicit objective from approval as a training signal, acting to satisfy current evaluators from acting to satisfy ideally informed evaluators, and influence over an overseer’s evidence from manipulation of the overseer. If more than one rejection is defensible, compare their costs rather than merely listing them.
