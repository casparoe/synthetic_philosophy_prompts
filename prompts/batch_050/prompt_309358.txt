So I wrote a load balancer that scores candidate servers and then picks uniformly at random among the top scorers, and a code review comment about the random tie-break sent me down a rabbit hole. I ended up with an argument that seems to prove something absurd, and I can't spot where it goes wierd.

(1) A choice is rational only if the chooser has a reason that favours the option chosen over the options not chosen.
(2) When two options are exactly equally good, nothing favours one over the other -- that's just what "exactly equal" means.
(3) So no choice between exactly equal options is rational.
(4) But faced with a forced choice between equals, people just pick one and get on with it; nobody actually starves between two identical bales of hay.
(5) So what they do there isn't really a choice, it's a coin flip wearing a choice's clothes.
(6) A reason only counts for the chooser if the chooser could in principle detect the difference it makes -- a reason you couldn't possibly notice can't do any work.
(7) Loads of everyday cases are near-ties whose difference is far below anything I could detect (grabbing this tin of beans off the shelf rather than the identical one behind it).
(8) So those are coin flips too, and any time I spend deliberating over them is strictly wasted -- I'd do exactly as well delegating to rand().

(8) is obviously the bit that proves too much: it can't really be that half my decisions are no better than a PRNG. But every step looks fine to me on its own. Which step do I bin, why did it look fine, and what does binning it cost? If it matters, I'm most attached to (2) and least sure about (6), though I can't articulate why.
