Construct a thought experiment in which two members of the same biological population leave exactly the same number of viable offspring, yet the propensity interpretation of fitness assigns them unequal fitness. The case should be realistic rather than idealized: no infinite populations, no stipulated miracles, just ordinary organisms, ordinary luck, and one carefully isolated difference between the two individuals, with everything downstream of that difference allowed to wash out by chance so that actual reproductive outputs coincide. State explicitly what the isolated variable is and why it bears on each organism's probabilistic disposition to reproduce rather than on its actual tally. Then demonstrate that the case meets its specifications: give the verdict of the propensity interpretation and the reasoning behind it, give the verdict of any account that defines fitness in terms of actual reproductive success, and explain why the case matters for the tautology objection — specifically, whether it shows that fitness is explanatory and predictive rather than definable as whatever survives. Close with two honest diagnostics: which single feature of the scenario does all the work (and what happens to the verdicts if you remove it), and how a defender of the tautology objection would attack the case, for instance by denying that propensities can be empirically estimated independently of observed outcomes.
