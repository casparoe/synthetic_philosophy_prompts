You're an expert in philosophy of religion who's worked on the foreknowledge and free will debate for years, and I want you to think this through carefully -- brainstorm the key moves and the likely sticking points before you write the final document. Don't rush into drafting.

Here's the situation. A small theology department is running an "adversarial collaboration" workshop between two of its faculty: one a committed Boethian (God exists outside time altogether, so what we call divine foreknowledge is really God's eternal knowledge of all events as present to him, the way all of a landscape is visible from a mountaintop), the other a committed open theist (the future is genuinely open; God knows all possibilities and their probabilities perfectly, but there is no settled future fact available for anyone, even God, to know). They've been told to co-author a joint statement and they're completely stuck. I've been hired to ghost a first draft for them, and that's your job.

The draft needs four parts:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims -- things both would actually sign. This should cover, at minimum: how both cash out "God is omniscient" in wording neither can object to; what both agree "free" has to mean for this debate to matter at all; and what both take Boethius's distinction between simple and conditional necessity (the move he makes at the end of the Consolation) to accomplish, since both sides appeal to it.

2. A disagreements section where each side's view is stated in wording that side would accept for itself -- no strawmen, no slanted adjectives, nothing either one would want to strike out. For each disagreement, name the crux: the sub-question such that if it got settled, at least one side would have to move.

3. For at least two of the cruxes, propose something both sides would accept as bearing on it -- an argument, a thought experiment, a case analysis (say: a prophecy that turns out false, or a person who does otherwise than what God eternally knows them to do) -- and record each side's prediction about how the case goes, in advance. Where both sides give the same prediction but explain it differently, flag that explicitly, because it's usually a symptom that part of the dispute is verbal rather than real.

4. A short verdict section: which parts of the Boethian/open-theist dispute, if any, dissolve into merely verbal disagreement on close inspection, and which are genuine and load-bearing.

A few traps I want you to explicitly avoid, because these are where drafts like this die:

- Don't let the Boethian treat timelessness as if it dissolves the problem on its own. The open theist will immediately say that God eternally "seeing" my future choice fixes that choice just as much as foreknowledge does -- the draft has to engage with whether that rejoinder works, not pretend it wasn't made.
- Don't let the open theist accuse Boethians of making God unresponsive to prayer without noting how Boethians actually handle it (God eternally knows the prayer and the answer together, in one eternal act).
- The simple vs. conditional necessity distinction is the point where the two sides typically agree far more than they realize -- use it as scaffolding for the agreed-claims section rather than wasting it.
- Don't let the whole thing collapse into "both sides just mean different things by 'knows'" unless you can show that's true. If you conclude that, argue for it; don't just assert it.

Keep it around 1000-1200 words. The test of success is that both faculty members could sign the thing without feeling misrepresented -- if either would want to red-pen a single sentence in the other side's section, the draft has failed. If you find yourself unable to formulate some crux neutrally, say so and explain why the disagreement resists neutral wording rather than papering over it to accomodate the format.
