At a reading group I attend -- mostly people who came up through LessWrong and the adjacent blogosphere, so the conversation had that flavor of everyone hunting for cruxes -- two regulars, Tomás and Ingrid, got into an extended argument about the "wrong kind of reasons" problem for fitting-attitude accounts of value. I stayed out of it, but I keep turning the exchange over, and I'd like an outside adjudication. Here is the exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

Background they both accepted: a fitting-attitude account says, roughly, that something is valuable just in case it is fitting (appropriate, merited) to hold some pro-attitude toward it -- admirable things merit admiration, desirable things merit desire, and so on. The standard trouble case they started from: a powerful and vindictive demon credibly threatens to torture your loved ones unless you genuinely admire him. There is clearly some sense in which you have a strong reason to admire him, yet he is in no way admirable.

Tomás opened by saying the problem is real but tractable: we just distinguish object-given from state-given reasons. Object-given reasons are provided by features of the attitude's object (the demon's cruelty); state-given reasons are provided by features of the state of having the attitude (that admiring him averts the torture). Wrong-kind reasons are exactly the state-given ones, right-kind reasons exactly the object-given ones, and the fitting-attitude analysis should be run only with the latter.

Ingrid replied that this relabels the problem rather than solving it, because the object-given/state-given line doesn't track the right/wrong line. Her example: the fact that the demon will be pleased if you admire him is a fact about the demon -- his psychology, his dispositions -- so by any natural criterion it is object-given, yet it's still obviously the wrong kind of reason. Conversely, she said, suppose admiring a particular mentor would make you more attentive to their genuinely excellent qualities; that's arguably state-given (it's about what the admiring does for you) yet feels connected to their admirableness in a way the demon's threat never does. So the proposed criterion misclassifies in both directions.

Tomás then refined: the right kind of reasons aren't just "about the object" -- they're the reasons that figure in the content of the attitude itself, the ones the attitude presents its object as having. Admiration presents its object as admirable; so the right-kind reasons for admiration are exactly those that bear on admirableness. The demon's threat bears on nothing the admiration is about, which is why it slides off. This, he said, is a principled criterion, not a label.

Ingrid countered that this works only for attitudes with tidy propositional contents. Belief, yes: reasons of the right kind bear on truth. But the analysis is supposed to cover love, grief, amusement, respect -- and it's notoriously unclear what, say, grief "presents" its object as. Either you credit these attitudes with contents rich enough to do the work (which looks like a projection of the theorist's taxonomy onto the phenomena) or the criterion goes silent exactly where the account is most needed. She also added a pressure point: on some substantive theories of value, the good consequences of your admiring something can partly constitute its value -- and then the line between "bears on admirableness" and "bears on the consequences of admiring" gets blurry from inside the theory, not just from edge cases.

Tomás's last move was a retreat-and-counterattack: fine, maybe the criterion needs work, but Ingrid owes an alternative, and the obvious one -- deny there are any wrong-kind reasons for attitudes at all, by insisting the demon's threat is only ever a reason to bring it about that you admire him, never a reason to admire him -- just relocates the puzzle. Now you need a principled story about why reasons for attitudes can never be generated by the attitude's consequences, while reasons for actions routinely are, and telling that story is doing the same work under a different name.

Ingrid said she'd take that trade: relocating a problem into the philosophy of normativity generally is progress if the original framing was generating pseudo-paradoxes, and she thinks "reasons to feel" was always a confused category that borrows its intuitive pull from "reasons to make yourself feel."
