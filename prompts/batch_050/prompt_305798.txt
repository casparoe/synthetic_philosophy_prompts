I'm TAing an intro moral philosophy course this semester (about 20 undergrads, one 75-minute discussion section a week) and we're about to hit the consequentialism unit. Every year the same thing happens: within ten minutes someone says utilitarianism is "too demanding," everyone nods, and thats the end of the conversation. They treat it as obviously decisive without being able to articulate what exactly is wrong with a morality asking a lot of you, or why "a lot" rather than "some" is supposed to be the dealbreaker. I want to blow up that complacency before they get to settle into it, but I don't want to just argue at them.

Design me a classroom activity or game for this group that makes them feel the ratchet of the demandingness objection in their own choices before they ever hear the phrase. Ideally it involves real (small) stakes and iterated decisions, where the rules or the facilitator keep confronting them with what they could have done instead with whatever they kept for themselves, escalating until "always do the most good" visibly leaves them with nothing -- not even a private life. I'd love a mechanic where the game's needs somehow scale with what the players hold back, so the line they instinctively draw keeps getting undercut from inside their own reasoning. The pressure has to come from the mechanics, not from me lecturing. Bonus if at some point it forces a choice between impartial good and something that feels non-negotiable to them personally (a promise to a friend in the game, a project they've invested in), because I want the integrity/partiality corner of the objection to show up on its own without me flagging it. Give me concrete rules, setup, materials, and a rough timeline for the 75 minutes.

Then a debrief plan: map the specific rounds and mechanics onto the actual moves in the debate (the collapse of the doing/allowing distinction, "why is my current budget the morally special line," satisficing and rule-flavored escape hatches, the reply that demandingness is a feature not a bug) -- but only after they've lived it, and without front-loading any of the vocabulary. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it (e.g., one kid donating everything in round one for the laugh, or the group quietly colluding to ignore the ratchet) and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the objection a game with snack-sized stakes just can't reproduce, so I know what I still have to supply by talking afterwards.
