I've come to accept the Strawsonian account of moral responsibility: that to be responsible just is to be an appropriate target of the reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, indignation, and the rest), that these attitudes get their justification from their constitutive role in human interpersonal relationships rather than from any external metaphysical fact about freedom or determinism, and that the question of whether they would be rationally abandoned under determinism is therefore misconceived, since no theoretical argument could or should dislodge a practice that is part of the fabric of human life as we live it. I want to know what would count against this account.

Do not just recite the standard worry that it is too permissive toward our emotions. I want a map of the view's genuine exposure. Consider, at minimum: (a) empirical findings about people who seem to lack the standard reactive-attitude repertoire -- psychopaths, very young children, possibly members of cultures in which something like resentment plays a thinner role -- and whether such cases show that responsibility outruns the reactive attitudes or merely that our practice has fuzzy edges; (b) the objection that the view makes the appropriateness of resentment hostage to nothing beyond the fact that we do resent, and so collapses into a conservatism that could have ratified slaveholding-era attitudes toward slaves; (c) the question of what the Strawsonian says about cases where our reactive attitudes turn out to rest on systematic factual error, e.g. widespread implicit bias about who is blameworthy; (d) whether the "no external standpoint" move actually forecloses revision, given that Strawson himself allows for internal modification of the practice; and (e) whether the account has a principled way of handling entities toward whom we can feel reactive attitudes but arguably shouldn't (pets, sophisticated AI systems, the dead).

For each candidate defeater, I want: what would have to be true, empirically or conceptually, for it to land; how decisive it would be (fatal, serious, merely awkward); whether a fair-minded Strawsonian would concede it counts against the view or dismiss it as attacking a straw man; and what repair or qualification, if any, the view would need to absorb it. Then give me an overall verdict on whether the position is dangerously insulated against counterevidence -- and if it is, whether that is a defect for a view of this kind or just what it means for a theory to be about our practices rather than the furniture of the universe.
