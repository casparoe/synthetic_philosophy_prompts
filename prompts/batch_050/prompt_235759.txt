I'm a junior on my high school's Ethics Bowl team, and after practice last week two of my teammates, Priya and Daniel, argued for nearly an hour about whether genuine moral dilemmas — situations in which a person does wrong no matter what they choose — actually exist. Their example was a hospital during a blackout: one working backup generator, two patients on ventilators in different wings, and the generator can power only one wing, so the doctor on duty saves one patient and the other dies.

(1) Priya argued this cannot be a real dilemma. Morality cannot require the impossible ("ought implies can"), and the doctor cannot save both, so at most one patient had a genuine claim on her. Hard choices feel like dilemmas, but feeling torn is not the same as being trapped.

(2) Daniel replied with what he called the residue argument. Suppose the doctor draws lots, saves patient A, and patient B dies. Surely she should grieve, and perhaps even apologize to B's family — but nobody owes an apology for simply doing the right thing. The leftover guilt shows that an obligation to B existed and went unmet.

(3) Priya answered that guilt and apology do not track actual wrongdoing. A surgeon apologizes to a family after a flawless operation that the patient does not survive; survivors of disasters feel guilty for living. These are understandable reactions to terrible outcomes, not evidence of violated duties. So the residue argument proves something about psychology, not about obligations.

(4) Daniel's last move: even so, B's family has a complaint, and complaints have to be directed at someone. The family cannot lodge a complaint against bad luck — luck is not an agent and owes no one anything. So their complaint is against the doctor, which means she wronged B even while doing the best thing available to her. That, he said, is exactly what a genuine dilemma is.

(5) Priya's response: complaints can be misdirected. The family's grievance belongs to the situation, or to whoever was responsible for the blackout — not to a doctor who made the only decision open to her.

I stayed out of the argument and honestly cannot tell who won. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move — say who is right on each of the five points and briefly why — and then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing about what a genuine dilemma would even amount to. Please write your answer in American English.
