I'm a second-year philosophy student currently enrolled in a seminar on philosophy of mind, and our departmental student society runs a small interview series with visiting speakers. Next month I'm interviewing a guest whose book defends the view, developed along the lines laid out in Gödel, Escher, Bach and its successor works, that human selfhood is a strange loop: the brain, like a sufficiently expressive formal system, builds symbols that represent its own states, and the "I" is what happens when such a system's self-model becomes a stable, causally active, self-referential pattern — an emergent level at which the system perceives and describes itself without being able to see the neural machinery underneath.

I'd like you to draft eight to ten interview questions, ordered as a sequence with a deliberate arc, that do more than invite the guest to restate the analogy. Concretely, I want the sequence to press on the following points, in roughly this order:

1. The load-bearing step of the analogy: in Gödel's construction, self-reference is achieved by arithmetization, a fixed coding scheme imposed from outside by the mathematician. In the brain, no one assigns the Gödel numbering. What plays that role, and does the analogy survive if nothing does?
2. The static/dynamic disanalogy: formal systems are fixed axiom sets, whereas brains constantly rewire. Does self-reference in a system whose "axioms" change over time produce a self, or a succession of selves?
3. Whether the strange loop explains anything beyond the bare fact of self-representation. Thermostats and error messages in a compiler are, in a thin sense, self-involving; what has to be added for a self-model to constitute a self, and is that addition doing the real explanatory work rather than the loop itself?
4. The competition: higher-order and self-representational theories of consciousness offer a similar structure with fewer exotic commitments. What does the strange-loop view predict or explain that they don't — or is the disagreement partly terminological?
5. The level-of-description question: the "I" is supposed to be real at the symbolic level even though it's causally inert relative to the neurons. Is this epiphenomenalism about the self in disguise, and if not, what work is the claim of downward causation doing?
6. A closing question about exposure: what observation or argument would the guest himself count as evidence against the view, and is there a risk that the framework is flexible enough to absorb anything?

For each question, please add a follow-up keyed to the reply the guest is most likely to give — she defends this view professionally, so expect polished answers — and note briefly what the follow-up is meant to establish. A sentence or two on the intended arc of the sequence as a whole would also help.

Two requests about how to answer. First, if any of the questions above rest on a misunderstanding of the position or of the underlying logic — for instance, if I've mischaracterized what the analogy claims — please correct that in passing rather than building the question on the error. Second, if you think part of this task can't be done well as posed — say, because the view is too loosely specified for question 6 to have a determinate answer — tell me plainly instead of smoothing it over.
