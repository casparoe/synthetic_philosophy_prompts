The question: What is the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism in metaethics? State the problem clearly and illustrate it with an example. Some expressivists respond by adopting a minimal or deflationary notion of truth for moral sentences. Does this solve the problem?

The student's answer:

Expressivism holds that moral utterances do not state facts or express beliefs but instead express non-cognitive attitudes such as disapproval. On this view, "Stealing is wrong" functions like "Boo to stealing": it gives voice to the speaker's disapproval rather than describing stealing. Crucially, this is not the claim that the speaker is reporting her own disapproval; the utterance expresses the attitude rather than stating that the speaker has it.

The Frege–Geach problem, first pressed by Gottlob Frege against the emotivists, arises because moral sentences can occur in unasserted contexts. When I say "Stealing is wrong," I express disapproval. But consider: "If stealing is wrong, then getting your little brother to steal is wrong too." Here I have not asserted that stealing is wrong — the sentence occurs as the antecedent of a conditional, and I express no disapproval of stealing in uttering it. So whatever the embedded sentence contributes, it cannot be the expression of an attitude, since no attitude is being expressed. The same goes for negation: "It's not the case that stealing is wrong" does not express disapproval of stealing.

Geach's sharp version of the point concerns inference. The argument: (1) If tormenting the cat is wrong, then getting your little brother to torment the cat is wrong. (2) Tormenting the cat is wrong. (3) So, getting your little brother to torment the cat is wrong. This is valid by modus ponens. But validity requires that "tormenting the cat is wrong" mean the same thing in premise 1 and premise 2 — otherwise the argument equivocates. If in premise 2 the sentence expresses disapproval while in premise 1 it does something else (since nothing is asserted there), the inference is a mere equivocation, and expressivists cannot explain why it is valid.

Quasi-realists such as Simon Blackburn respond by allowing that moral sentences are truth-apt in a minimal, deflationary sense: to say "It is true that stealing is wrong" is just to say that stealing is wrong. Once we have minimal truth, we get everything Frege–Geach demands: moral sentences can be true or false, they embed in conditionals and negations, and they figure in valid inferences. So the problem dissolves — the expressivist can say everything the realist says about the logical behavior of moral language while denying the metaphysics of moral facts. The residual disagreement is then not about logic but about what moral thought is like from the inside, and expressivism survives. Since deflationism about truth is independently motivated, the expressivist is entitled to it, and the Frege–Geach problem should be filed as solved.
