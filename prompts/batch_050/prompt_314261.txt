I share an office with two people working on a robotics demo for a warehouse client -- a mobile arm that's meant to fetch and stack crates. Last week they got into a proper row about the safety spec and it's still going, so I want an outside referee. The client asked for the robot to "do its job without knocking anything else about", and my colleague Dev said they should just write that as a penalty term: penalise the robot for changing anything in the warehouse that isn't the crates. His pitch was that you can define "low impact" without ever saying what's good or bad -- you just measure how much the world changes relative to how it would have gone without the robot, and keep that number small. Priya said this smuggles in exactly what it claims to avoid: "how much the world changed" isn't a number until you pick which features of the world to track and how to weight them, and whether you count a scuffed floor as more or less change than a shifted pallet *is* the value-specification problem, just wearing a fake moustache.

Dev's counter was that there's a value-neutral fix: instead of tracking features, penalise the robot for reducing the *humans' ability to get things done* -- keep their options open. You don't need to know what the workers want in order to avoid wrecking their capacity to want it. Priya gave it a round and then produced her best objection: run it forward and the incentive goes strange. If change itself is the penalty, the safest thing for the robot is a warehouse where nothing ever changes, which means it has an incentive to stop the *workers* from moving things, or to freeze a process mid-way, and it will also "offset" -- knock a fire extinguisher over and then stand it back up and call it zero impact, even though the interval mattered. Dev conceded the offsetting thing needs patching but said she's proving too much: humans tell side effects from main effects constantly -- the surgeon's scar is a side effect of the operation, the cure isn't -- and we do that without comparing whole life-plans. So there's a pre-theoretical notion of causal footprint that the penalty just needs to approximate, and the evaluation of good vs bad can stay with the task reward. Priya's last move was that we only call the scar "small" *because* we care about it less than health -- the footprint intuition is value-laden all the way down, and a robot without those weights will happily trade a scar for a severed artery if both count as "one change".

Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about which specific point? I want a verdict on each: (a) whether any change-metric really avoids value judgements or just defers them, (b) whether the options-open fix escapes that, (c) whether the weird-incentive examples are fatal or patchable, and (d) whether the surgeon/scar point shows there's a value-free notion of footprint or shows the opposite. And if both of them are missing something, say what it is -- I genuinely can't tell and they're making me pick a side at our next meeting.
