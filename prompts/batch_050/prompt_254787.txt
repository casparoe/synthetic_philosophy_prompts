The sunk cost fallacy is close to foundational doctrine in the rationalist-blogs canon I grew up reading: don't finish the bad meal because you paid for it, don't stay in the career because you spent a decade entering it, money already spent is gone and decision-irrelevant. That doctrine is mostly right, and I have no interest in rehabilitating loss-chasing. But the boundary cases have bothered me for years, because there are regimes where what looks like honoring a sunk cost is actually sound reasoning: past investment can be evidence about the venture's quality (you didn't burn the money randomly, you burned it on something that keeps surviving scrutiny), a reputation for finishing what you start is an asset, and a habit of abandoning any endeavor the moment it disappoints is exploitable -- by counterparties, and by one's own future selves, who learn that no commitment is binding. I want to run a workshop that lets people feel both sides of this rather than just reciting the fallacy.

Design a two-hour activity for a rationality meetup of about 15 adults, most of whom have already internalized the standard doctrine and will need to be surprised out of it. The core should be a multi-round game in which participants act as fund managers deciding whether to keep funding projects whose quality is hidden, with a points-based payoff scheme (no money) and the following structure: in some rounds the world is a pure loss-chasing trap where past spend carries zero information and the only winning move is to abandon, and in other rounds the world is one where past investment is genuinely informative and the too-eager abandoner bleeds points to a stubborn opponent. The facilitator should control which regime is active without announcing it, and the game should be tuned so that participants predictably commit both errors -- throwing good points after bad in the trap regime, and abandoning valuable projects in the informative regime -- before either error is named. Tell me: the full rules and materials, how the facilitator seeds and conceals the regime, the timeline, how to keep participants from gaming things once they suspect two regimes exist, a debrief that maps specific moments in play onto the distinction between decision-relevant and decision-irrelevant past costs (and onto the commitment and reputation points above), what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and an honest note on what a points game cannot reproduce -- I'm guessing the emotional texture of real sunk costs, where identity and pride are part of what's spent -- and how I should say so without deflating the exercise.
