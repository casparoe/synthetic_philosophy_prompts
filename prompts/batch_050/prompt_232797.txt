Last year, a driver in my town glanced at his phone, rolled through a red light, and struck and killed a cyclist. He is now in prison, and nearly everyone I know speaks of him as a monster. Around the same time, an old friend confessed to me that he has done essentially the same thing — phone in hand, through the light — several times, and simply never happened to hit anyone. He feels bad about it, but he still has his job, his family, and his clean conscience, and nobody thinks the worse of him. I have been turning this over ever since. The two men made the same choice, with the same carelessness and the same character; the only difference is what happened next, which neither of them controlled. And yet I cannot shake the feeling that the first man genuinely deserves more blame — not merely harsher treatment, not merely worse evidence about what kind of person he is, but more actual blameworthiness. My friend, when I pressed him, said the opposite: since the outcome was luck, their moral standing is identical, and our urge to condemn the killer more is just our confused response to tragedy. I gather philosophers call this puzzle moral luck, though I came to it before I ever heard the term.

So here is what I would like. My own credence that unlucky outcomes can genuinely make someone more blameworthy — that the killer is worse, morally, than my lucky friend, even holding fixed everything about their choices and character — is about 0.7. My friend's credence is about 0.1. Please give me your own credence in that proposition, stated as precisely as I have stated it, and decompose the reasoning: assign a rough probability to each sub-question you take the answer to depend on — for instance, whether a person's blameworthiness can only ever turn on what was up to them at the moment of choice; whether our stronger condemnation of the unlucky killer survives careful reflection or is really just anger at the outcome plus the fact that we now know what he is capable of; whether the difference can be explained away as a difference in what we learn about the two men rather than in what they deserve; and whether refusing to let outcomes matter would force us to give up blaming people for almost anything, since what we even attempt is itself soaked in luck. Tell me how those probabilities combine into your final number, which sub-question the number is most sensitive to, and what argument or thought experiment would shift that term most. Finally, tell me which part of the decomposition you think my friend and I actually disagree about — because I suspect our 0.7 and 0.1 come apart at one specific joint rather than everywhere at once.
