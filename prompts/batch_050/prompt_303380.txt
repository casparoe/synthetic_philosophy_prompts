I recently joined a working group that is formalizing a body of results in a proof assistant, and the experience has unsettled me in a way I did not expect. Checking a proof line by line against a kernel feels nothing like the proofs I was trained on, and it has made me wonder what a proof even is once a machine is doing the certifying. This has sent me back to Lakatos's account in Proofs and Refutations -- mathematics as a quasi-empirical enterprise in which concepts, lemmas, and theorems are revised dialectically through proofs and refutations rather than established once and for all -- and I would like you to test that account against the episode that seems most designed to strain it: the four-color theorem.

Take the episode as commonly known: the 1976 Appel-Haken proof, which reduced the theorem to a large set of configurations checked for reducibility by computer; the ensuing controversy over whether a proof no human can survey counts as a proof at all; the later simplification of the argument by Robertson and others; and the eventual full formal verification of the theorem in a proof assistant in the mid-2000s. Analyze this episode through Lakatos's framework. Do the mapping carefully: in his scheme, who or what plays the role of the refutation when no counterexample is on offer, what corresponds to monster-barring and lemma incorporation when the object under revision is a method of proof rather than a conjecture, and does the community's gradual acceptance of computer assistance look like the dialectical concept-stretching he describes -- or like something else, perhaps a Kuhnian shift in standards, or mere sociological accommodation? Then run the analysis in the other direction: what does the episode show about the quasi-empirical thesis? Lakatos's model was built on cases where proofs change the conjecture; here the conjecture stayed fixed for over a century while the epistemics of proof shifted underneath it. Say plainly whether that is a confirmation of the model, an anomaly it can absorb, or a case it never anticipated, and whether a verified-in-Coq proof vindicates the traditionalist's demand for certainty, the quasi-empiricist's picture of fallible methods, or neither.

I am not looking for a verdict on whether computers can prove theorems -- that debate is stale. I want the disciplined two-way case analysis. Where Lakatos's apparatus is silent about some feature of the episode, say it is silent rather than forcing it to speak. And because I will rely on this in deciding how much weight to put on our formalization work: state explicitly what would change your conclusion. Which argument about the Appel-Haken reception, or which feature of Lakatos's framework, would have to come out differently for the episode to count as a refutation of quasi-empiricism rather than an extension of it, and where in your analysis is the reasoning most likely to fail?
