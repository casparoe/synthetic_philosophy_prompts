My grad decision theory seminar keeps ending up in the same place, so let me try to get unstuck by asking about the literature rather than the case. Since Gibbard and Harper gave us the Death in Damascus puzzle, there's been a stable standoff: one camp says the case, and the Newcomb-style cases behind it, show that causal decision theory recommends acts an agent is guaranteed to regret, and that any adequate theory has to build in stability or ratifiability along the lines Jeffrey, Skyrms, and later writers developed; the other camp, in Lewis's spirit, says CDT gives the right verdict and that the puzzle only shows there is no deliberatively stable act, which is a fact about the situation, not a defect of the theory. Everyone knows all the arguments, nobody has switched sides in decades. My suspicion is that this isn't really a disagreement about the case at all but about what a decision theory is for -- evaluating acts relative to information, versus guiding an agent through deliberation -- and that the two camps are answering different questions under one label.
