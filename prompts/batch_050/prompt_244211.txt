I'm drafting an essay for a philosophy working-paper series (and eventually a shorter version for a blog read by people who know the cluelessness debate), and I want comments of the kind a hostile-but-fair referee would give — specifically a consequentialist referee who thinks the standard epistemic framing of cluelessness is basically fine. Below is the current draft, with paragraphs numbered so you can key comments to them. After it I've listed the four things I most want pressure on; please give marginal comments keyed to paragraph numbers, then a ranked list of the three most serious problems with a sentence or two each on what a fix would require. I don't want line edits or style feedback.

Draft essay, working title "The Oracle Test: Cluelessness as a Problem of Credit, Not of Knowledge":

P1. The cluelessness objection to consequentialism runs roughly as follows. Nearly every act has remote effects that are vast and unpredictable — which people come to exist, which accidents occur, which meetings happen — and these plausibly swamp the near-term effects we actually cite when justifying decisions. Taking the train rather than the bus changes whom you sit next to, which may change which child is conceived decades from now. Consequentialists nonetheless reason about daily life as though the remote ledger could be set aside. The objection says it cannot, and that consequentialist decision-making about ordinary life is therefore unsupported by the reasons we actually give for it.

P2. The replies I see in the vicinity are two. The wash-out reply: in expectation, remote effects are symmetric across options, so they cancel, and expected-value comparisons may proceed on near-term terms. The proxy reply: near-term effects are our best available evidence about long-run value, so acting on them just is acting on expected long-run value rather than ignoring it. Both replies treat cluelessness as a knowledge problem: we don't know the remote effects, and here is why that is manageable.

P3. I think this framing misses the harder problem, which is not knowledge but credit. Suppose, per impossibile, that we had the full causal map of the future conditional on each available act. A question would remain that the map does not answer: which remote outcomes count as consequences of my act? My act changes which other people exist, and those people — who exist only because of what I did — go on to make the choices that dominate the far future. Attributing that future to my act double-counts the choices of agents my act brought into being; attributing it to them leaves my act nearly innocent. Call the first problem deliberative cluelessness (I cannot now compute which act is best) and the second credit cluelessness (even given all the facts, the ledger lacks an agreed accounting rule). My thesis: deliberative cluelessness is ordinary uncertainty and was never philosophically interesting; credit cluelessness is the real residue, and it belongs with responsibility and the non-identity problem, not with prediction.

P4. The oracle test. Imagine an oracle that reports, for each act in your choice set, the total value of the long-run future conditional on that act, computed by the objectively correct credit rule, whatever it is. Bracket how it knows the rule. If you would act on the oracle's answers, deliberative cluelessness reduces to mundane empirical ignorance, and the live question is which credit rule the oracle should use — the philosophy is in the accounting, not the forecasting. If you would not act on the answers, then in the disputed cases you were never really maximizing long-run value; your near-term focus rested on something else, and cluelessness is a cover story that misdescribes your own view. Either way, cluelessness as standardly posed is not the problem.

P5. A wrinkle I find telling. Most of us would consult the oracle for big decisions — careers, large donations, policy — but not for small ones, like the train or the bus. That asymmetry is hard to explain if cluelessness is really about uncertainty, which applies equally to both. It is easily explained if near-term focus is doing the work of a discount rate: we weight the near-term because it is near, or because it is ours, and cluelessness supplies a respectable-sounding rationale. But a discount rate needs a defense in its own right; smuggling it in under the label of epistemic humility lets it off the hook.

P6. The constructive upshot: a theory of credit for far-future consequences would have to say at least whether credit is plain counterfactual difference-making, whether effects mediated by other agents' choices — especially agents who exist because of the act — count differently, and whether effects on who exists count at all. I do not have that theory. I claim the literature has been answering the epistemic question so as to avoid this one.

The four things I want pressed:

(a) Whether the central distinction survives the obvious objection. My own worry: given any consequentialist decision procedure, credit cluelessness collapses back into deliberative cluelessness, because the procedure needs expected values, not attributions — the "credit rule" is just part of the axiology, so stipulating that the oracle knows the correct one smuggles in exactly what the cluelessness proponent denies exists. Have I relocated the problem or merely relabeled it?

(b) Whether the stipulation in P4 ("bracket how it knows the rule") rigs the test by assuming away the interesting half of the dispute, and if so whether a version of the test survives without that stipulation.

(c) Whether the big/small asymmetry in P5 has an innocent explanation — decision costs, robustness of near-term proxies, option value — that commits no one to tacit discounting. If the asymmetry is just economics of attention, P5 should come out.

(d) Accuracy: have I misstated the objection or the two standard replies in P2, and is there a reply in the neighborhood — say, views on which the options are incomparable or the relevant credences are imprecise — that dissolves the framing entirely rather than losing to it?

If you think the essay's thesis is wrong, say so at the top rather than softening it into the comments.
