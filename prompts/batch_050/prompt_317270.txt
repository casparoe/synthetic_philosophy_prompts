I teach a first-year university seminar on personal identity, and the unit that reliably generates the best discussion is mind uploading. This year I framed the whole unit around a thought experiment of my own that my students have started calling the drip-feed: instead of scanning a brain in an instant, surgeons replace a person's neurons one at a time with silicon prostheses that do exactly the same functional work, a few hundred per day, over about a decade. The subject never notices anything; there is no morning on which she wakes up feeling like a copy; and yet at the end of the process no biological tissue remains. Most of the class decides the drip-feed preserves the person, and then, when I confront them with the standard instantaneous scan-and-destroy cases, they balk without being able to articulate what the drip-feed has that the scanner lacks. Working with them, we've settled on a rough vocabulary for the distinction they keep groping toward: a procedure might preserve the unbroken stream of experience, as the drip-feed arguably does, or it might preserve only the pattern, the information, which is all a scanner keeps. For the end-of-unit screening I've chosen Christopher Nolan's The Prestige, because I think the film is secretly about exactly this distinction, and I'd like your help reading it that way.

Here is what I want from the reading. Tesla's machine produces a second Angier at a distance from the stage; the man who stepped into it drops through a trapdoor into a locked water tank and drowns; the duplicate appears at the back of the hall and takes the bow. Does the film treat the man under the stage as dying, or does it remain metaphysically neutral — and does its horror, above all the eventual reveal of a cellar of tanks each holding a drowned double, depend on the audience instinctively taking the stream-of-experience view rather than the pattern view? Relatedly: the first time Angier uses the machine, one copy shoots the other within moments of his appearance, and Angier's closing admission is that night after night he could not know in advance whether he would be the man in the tank or the man receiving the applause. Is that uncertainty coherent on any account of personal identity, and if it isn't, what confusion does the film need Angier to be in for the line to land as it does? And consider a variant my students will certainly propose: suppose Tesla's machine worked gradually, replacing Angier cell by cell across a hundred performances rather than duplicating him in an instant. If that drains the horror, does it show the film's entire emotional architecture rests on the drip-feed distinction?

Two further things. First, Borden's secret is the mirror image of Angier's: two streams of experience jointly maintaining one continuing public person, one life lived in alternation. Does the film notice that its two plots are inverse thought experiments about what makes a person persist, and does it take the same metaphysical side in both, or does it ask us to feel differently about Borden's arrangement than about Angier's, and on what grounds? Second, I want you to push back where the reading strains: the drowned men are murdered, whatever the metaphysics, and the film is plainly also about obsession, sacrifice, and what performers owe their audiences — so tell me where the personal-identity lens stops illuminating the film and starts distorting it. Finally, the question I most want answered for my own teaching: what, if anything, does the film show about uploading and gradual replacement that the seminar-room thought experiments, with their clean stipulations, cannot?
