A colleague of mine has spent years developing a Kantian account of deception — the line of thought running from Kant's 1797 essay against Benjamin Constant, in which Kant insists that the duty of truthfulness does not bend even for a murderer asking after his intended victim. Over the years I have watched her deliver verdicts on a series of cases, and a new case has now arisen in her own life. I want to predict what she will say, and to understand what her track record actually commits her to.

Her verdicts so far:

1. A graduate student falsely stated on a fellowship application that he had completed a language requirement. She condemned this without hesitation: a false statement made to an institution entitled to an honest answer.

2. A man sheltering a friend from the friend's violent ex-partner was asked point-blank whether the friend was in his house. He answered, truthfully but misleadingly, "She told me she never wants to see you again," correctly expecting the ex-partner to infer she was elsewhere. My colleague judged this permissible — uncomfortable, but no lie: no falsehood was asserted, and this questioner had no claim on the truth anyway.

3. In a charity poker tournament she bluffed aggressively, representing hands she did not hold, and she regards this as wholly unobjectionable: the conventions of the game mean that no one at the table is owed the truth about their cards.

4. A nurse on a dementia ward, asked daily by a patient where her long-deceased husband is, had taken to answering, "He's not here today, but I'll tell him you asked." My colleague said the nurse should not do this — better to redirect, or find something true to say — since the patient, whatever her condition, is no wrongdoer, and a flat falsehood told to her is still a lie.

The new case: the friend from case 2 is now staying in my colleague's own home, hiding from the same ex-partner, whose behavior has escalated to stalking. A neighbor — kind, entirely innocent of the situation, but a compulsive gossip who chats daily with everyone on the street, including the ex-partner, whom she believes to be an old family friend — knocks and asks directly: "Is Elena staying with you?" Any visibly evasive or merely deflecting answer will sharpen the neighbor's curiosity rather than satisfy it; a refusal to answer will be taken as confirmation; and whatever the neighbor comes to believe, she will repeat it on the street within the day. My colleague can assert a falsehood ("No, she's abroad this month"), or she can answer in a way that lets the truth reach the stalker.

Please do the following. First, set out the two or three distinct principles, each consistent with all four of her prior verdicts, that could be generating the track record — I expect candidates will differ over whether the wrong lies in the false assertion itself, in deceiving someone entitled to the truth, or in knowingly enabling the harm the truth would serve. Second, state what each candidate principle predicts about the new case, noting where they converge and where they diverge. Third, give your best prediction of her actual verdict, with reasons drawn from the shape of the record and from her professed Kantianism — including whether Kant's own refusal to make exceptions for consequences should settle it, given that Kant's case involved a questioner with murderous intent and hers does not. Fourth, if the record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say so plainly, state what principle she would be adopting with each possible verdict, and describe the further hypothetical case whose resolution would discriminate between the leading contenders.
