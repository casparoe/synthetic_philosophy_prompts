I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a long feature on whether a future AI could genuinely suffer -- whole brain emulation, octopus cognition, silicon minds, the works. Before I commit to a philosophical frame for the piece, I want to know whether the views I'm tempted by actually hang together. Here's roughly where I land:

(a) Functionalism about mental states: what makes something a pain is the role it plays in the system, not the stuff the system is made of.

(b) Multiple realizability is basically the whole argument for (a): octopuses plausibly feel pain with completly different neuroanatomy from ours, and it seems crazy to say a silicon upload couldn't suffer just because it lacks C-fibers.

(c) I'm persuaded by the Twin Earth style cases: what someone's thoughts are *about* depends on their environment and causal history, so two molecule-for-molecule identical people could in principle be having different thoughts.

(d) For cognitive science to actually explain behavior, though, psychological states need to supervene on what's going on inside the organism -- the explanation of what I do bottoms out in my internal functional organization, not in facts about my surroundings.

(e) As far as actual humans go, I'm happy to say human pain just *is* C-fiber firing (or whatever the relevant neural state turns out to be).

(f) And the nation-of-China and giant lookup table examples don't move me: I don't think a population of people passing notes around, or a machine that just reads its next state off a huge precomputed table, is really in pain, whatever functional organization it technically implements.

Which of these genuinely conflict, which only look like they do, and what's the cheapest fix for the real ones? Please rank the tensions by how much damage they do to the set rather than treating them all as equally serious. I don't have a horse in the race about which commitment to drop -- I just want to know what each option costs me for the article's overall argument.
