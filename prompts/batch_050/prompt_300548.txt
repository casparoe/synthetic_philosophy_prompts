I'm the newly appointed ethics officer at a mid-sized university research institute, and I've been tasked with overhauling how our animal care committee decides which research subjects receive welfare protections. The standing rule is essentially binary: vertebrates get full protocol review, everything else gets none. That worked until recently, but we now have a neuroscience group working with octopuses, a behavioral lab running experiments on honeybees and fruit flies, an entomologist studying nociception in cockroaches, and — the case that ignited this whole dispute — a computer science group that wants to train reinforcement-learning agents through millions of simulated "deprivation" episodes and is openly irritated at being asked to submit anything at all. The committee's instinct is to dismiss the computer scientists' case as a category error and the insects as obviously beneath concern, but I've read enough of the literature on graded sentience and moral status to doubt both instincts, and I was discussing this with a colleague over coffee who pointed out that our binary rule is just a refusal to think about the problem.

Please act as a philosopher who specializes in moral status theory and has practical experience advising research ethics bodies. Think carefully about what the philosophy of sentience can and cannot deliver here before producing anything procedural — I'd like you to begin by brainstorming the key distinctions (phenomenal capacity versus behavioral evidence, probability of sentience versus magnitude of welfare at stake, the difference between a system being low on the scale and being off the scale entirely), and only then build the deliverable. What I need is a decision procedure my committee — two biologists, a veterinarian, a community member, no philosophers — can actually run in a half-hour meeting per case: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, how to proceed for species where the evidence is thin or absent (do we err toward protection, and how much protection does a low probability of sentience buy?), and how to treat systems where the live question is not how much sentience is present but whether the concept applies at all — the thermostat end of the scale, and the reinforcement-learning agents. Mark explicitly the points where the procedure must stop and hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than evidence, and be candid about where any procedure of this kind will misfire.

Then run the procedure on three cases from our actual docket: (1) a study inducing chronic stress in octopuses as a model of depression; (2) an undergraduate teaching protocol in which wax moth larvae are exposed to heat to study escape behavior, with no follow-up care; and (3) the computer scientists' training proposal. Ideally at least one verdict should come out in a way that will make my committee uncomfortable — I want to see where the procedure bites, not where it ratifies existing instinct. Finally, give me language for responding to the computer science group's expected objection: that if their agents merit ethics review, so does a thermostat, and that our willingness to review the agents therefore proves the entire framework is incoherent.
