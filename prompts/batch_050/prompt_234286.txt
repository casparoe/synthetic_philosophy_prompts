I'm a second-year PhD student working on well-being, and I got into a fight at our reading group last week over the experience machine that I keep losing, which is annoying because I think my position is the obviously sensible one. A friend (a committed hedonist, annoyingly clever) laid out this argument and dared me to find the bad step. Here it is, roughly as he gave it. (1) A person's well-being is a matter of how their life goes for them -- the person living it. (2) If two lives are experientially identical from the inside, moment for moment, then neither one goes better or worse *for the person living it*; there's simply nothing to choose between them from that person's seat. (3) A life hooked up to a sufficiently good experience machine can be made experientially identical to any real life you like -- same joys, same struggles-feelings, same sense of accomplishment. (4) So a machine life and its real-life twin are exactly equal in well-being. (5) Therefore there is no prudential reason to refuse to plug in; anyone who refuses is either confused or irrational. Then he twisted the knife: (6) by the same reasoning, if a demon had secretly swapped my brain into a simulation last Tuesday and kept my experiences perfectly continuous, nothing bad would have happened to me at all -- my well-being would be exactly what it would have been -- and it would be incoherent of me to hope it turns out the swap never happened. (7) But obviously I do hope that, and I'd be horrified to learn it happened. (8) So I must either give up (1)/(2) or admit my horror is pure bias.

The thing is, I find (5) almost digestible -- plenty of people bite that bullet, whatever, it's the classic debate. But (6) genuinely seems to prove too much. It implies that discovering you're the last brain in a vat, everyone you love nonexistent, is not bad news *for you* in the slightest, just a surprising fact, and that grief about it would be as irrational as grieving that you weren't born taller. I can't accept that, but when I poke at the argument every premise seems like something *I'd* say in other contexts -- I've definitely used (1) myself against objective-list people in seminar. Which step do you actually reject? I suspect something goes wrong in the slide from (1) to (2) -- like the phrase "for the person living it" is doing two jobs -- but I can't pin it down and my friend just smirks when I gesture at it. If that is the problem, spell out exactly what the two readings are and why the argument needs both. If it isn't, tell me which premise dies instead.

And price it out for me: if I reject the move from (1) to (2), what am I commited to -- some kind of weak objectivism about well-being, and does that mean the machine intuition is doing no work in Nozick's original case either? If instead I accept (6), what does the hedonist have to say to make the demon's-victim case feel less monstrous -- does the preference-satisfaction crowd do any better here, given that my desire that my friends exist is a desire about the world, not about my experiences? I'd most like to keep (1) and my horror at (6); tell me what that combination actually costs.
