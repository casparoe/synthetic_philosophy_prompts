For an undergraduate course on virtue ethics, I'm writing a paper on the self-improvement scheme Benjamin Franklin describes in his Autobiography, and I keep running into a comparison I want to stress-test. The scheme, as he lays it out: he settled on thirteen virtues he wished to cultivate, attached to each a short precept fixing what he meant by it, and kept a small book containing a grid with one row per virtue and one column per day of the week. Each evening he reviewed his conduct and put a black mark against every virtue he had violated that day. Rather than attack all thirteen at once, he concentrated his attention on a single virtue each week while merely recording lapses in the rest, so that the whole list cycled through in thirteen weeks and could be repeated four times a year. He eventually admitted he never achieved the spotless page he had aimed for — he struggled especially with order — and he came to think the value lay largely in the attempt itself, reporting that he was a better and happier man for the effort even though perfection escaped him. He also added humility to the list only late, at a friend's prodding, and observed ruefully that pride was the hardest fault to extinguish.

The comparison I keep encountering, in blog posts and in conversation, is that Franklin's book was "the original habit tracker": he supposedly invented streak-counting and the quantified self three centuries early, and apps that log habits and display streaks are just Franklin's grid with better technology. The analogy is almost always deployed to license a further claim: that moral improvement, like fitness or productivity, is fundamentally a matter of measurement, repetition, and feedback loops, and that anyone serious about character today should simply track their behavior the way Franklin did.

Audit the analogy. Start with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the metric, the streak, the daily check-in, the dashboard, the user. Then work through the disanalogies — for instance, Franklin's marks record failures rather than completions, so the goal is an empty grid rather than a full one; he rotates his focus rather than sustaining every streak at once; the sensor is his own conscience applying precepts he wrote himself, not an external counter; and his considered verdict elevates the striving over the spotless record, which is arguably the opposite of what a streak display teaches. For each disanalogy, say whether it matters for the conclusion the analogy is used to support or is merely cosmetic. Finally, tell me whether the "measurement plus feedback is the engine of moral growth" lesson survives the audit, and if the habit-tracker picture is beyond repair, propose a better analogy for what Franklin was actually doing and defend it.
