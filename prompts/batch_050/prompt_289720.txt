I belong to a small recreational puzzle group, and last week I posed them a problem I had come up with after a hiking trip. A hiker is lost somewhere in a park that is divided into a North half and a South half of equal area, and there is no prior reason to think she is in one half rather than the other. The Lake district occupies exactly a quarter of the South half, so, assuming she is equally likely to be anywhere within whatever region she occupies, her conditional chance of being in the Lake district given that she is in the South is one quarter. A search coordinator radios her a single message, based on terrain and weather records: if you are in the South half, there is a three-in-four chance you are in the Lake district. The question I put to the group was simple: after hearing this message, what should the hiker's credence be that she is in the South half? Two members, Maya and Dev, ended up in a disagreement that outlasted the meeting, and since they have asked me to referee it in writing, I want a neutral outside opinion first.

Their exchange, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it, went like this. (1) Maya argued that the credence should stay at one half: the message concerns only what the hiker should think on the supposition that she is in the South, and it contains no information whatsoever favoring North over South, so the probability split between the halves cannot rationally change; only the distribution within the South should move, from one quarter to three quarters on the Lake district. (2) Dev replied that this cannot be a general rule. Conditional testimony can bear on its own antecedent: had the coordinator radioed "if you are in the South, you will see the lake within the hour," and had the hiker then seen no lake, she would properly conclude she is in the North. He proposed treating the message as the plain conditional "if South, then Lake," ruling out the South-without-Lake region and renormalizing, which puts the South at one fifth. (3) Maya countered that his own arithmetic refutes him: his update makes the hiker certain of the Lake district given the South, whereas the message said three in four, so he has "learned" more than the message contained. She added that his lake-within-the-hour case smuggles in an extra, checkable prediction, and of course a failed prediction is evidence against the South; a bare statement of conditional probability makes no prediction and so cannot work the same way. (4) Dev repaired his proposal in two stages: first rule out South-without-Lake, then redistribute within the South to match the stated three-quarters, while keeping the North-to-South odds where the first stage left them, yielding a South credence of one fifth that now respects the conditional probability. He conceded the stagewise construction is artificial but insisted the deeper point stands: whether the South credence moves depends on the coordinator's messaging policy -- for instance, on whether she would have sent a message at all, or a different one, had the hiker been elsewhere -- and since my puzzle said nothing about that, Maya's claim that the credence must stay at one half is unearned. (5) Maya had the last word: even granting that some messaging policies would move the credence, the natural reading of the puzzle is a routine broadcast sent to any lost hiker regardless of location, and on that reading one half is simply the right answer; the burden is on Dev to show the message discriminates between the halves, not on her to show it does not.

Please adjudicate this dispute move by move. For each of the five moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible with qualifications, and briefly justify the verdict. I am especially interested in whether Maya's symmetry intuition in (1) survives Dev's counterexample in (2), whether Dev's repaired two-stage update in (4) is a legitimate way to take the message or a patch that concedes Maya's objection in (3), and whether the disagreement really bottoms out in the coordinator's messaging policy, as Dev claims, or in something else. Finally, tell me who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
