Yesterday evening a friend and I got into a long and rather heated argument about Jason Brennan-style epistocracy, and it forced me to write down, perhaps for the first time, what I actually believe about democracy rather than what I reflexively defend at dinner parties. I'd like a consistency audit before I put any of this in the paper I'm drafting for my political philosophy seminar. My commitments, as best I can state them, are these: (1) I'm an instrumentalist about political institutions — democracy is valuable if and only if it tends to produce good decisions, and appeals to its intrinsic fairness or symbolic equality are, in my view, ways of changing the subject. (2) I take the Condorcet jury theorem seriously: aggregating many independent, modestly-better-than-chance judgments through majority rule can outperform small groups of experts, and this is a large part of why democracy works as well as it does. (3) I think the empirical literature on voter behavior — Caplan's work on rational irrationality in particular — shows that most voters are not merely uninformed but systematically biased in predictable directions, since irrationality at the ballot box is privately costless. (4) I reject epistocracy, on the ground that there is no non-question-begging way to specify the competence test: deciding who counts as qualified to vote is itself a political decision, and whoever designs the test has in effect already voted. (5) I'm enthusiastic about citizens' assemblies — stratified random selection, a briefing period with expert testimony, facilitated deliberation — and I'd like to see them given real legislative power. (6) I consider trial by jury one of the best institutions we have and would resist replacing it with professional judges.

My friend claimed this is a mess — that (1) and (3) together commit me to epistocracy whether I like it or not, and that (4) is in tension with almost everything else I said. I'm not convinced she's right across the board, but I'm also not confident I know exactly where the real problems are, and I'd rather find out now than in seminar. What I want is an audit, not a lecture on the epistocracy debate: tell me which pairs or triples of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, how severe each tension is, and what each one would cost to repair — and rank them rather than treating everything as equally damning. I'd also like you to flag any tensions that look real but dissolve on inspection, because I suspect at least one of my friend's objections rests on conflating two things I hold that merely sound contradictory. Finally, if you had to identify the single revision that would clean up the most of the set — whether that's dropping, weakening, or re-motivating one commitment — what would it be, and what would I lose by making it? I'd ask that you engage with the internal logic of my position as stated rather than substituting a more standard one you find easier to defend, and that you not assume I already know which commitment I'd sacrifice, because honestly I don't.
