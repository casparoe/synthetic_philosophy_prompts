I've been reading Kripke's arguments about necessity and origins in a reading group, and I keep getting pulled in different directions by my own case judgments. I'd like someone to take my verdicts seriously, find the principle (if there is one) that unifies them, and tell me plainly which of them has to be sacrificed if no single principle fits.

Here are the verdicts I feel fairly confident about. (a) I could not have originated from an entirely different sperm and egg: whatever person resulted from that pairing, it wouldn't be me, even if they were raised in my house and given my name. (b) But I could have been conceived a day later or earlier than I actually was, from the same sperm and egg; timing of conception doesn't seem identity-determining. (c) I could have been adopted at birth and raised by entirely different parents in another country, and it would still have been me. (d) Now the artifacts: the oak table in my kitchen could have been built from a slightly different plank -- the carpenter reaching for the next board on the pile changes nothing about which table this is. (e) But a table built from scratch in a different workshop, from wholly different lumber, to the same plans, would be a different table, no matter how indistinguishable. (f) And here's the one that troubles me: a ship that has every plank and nail gradually replaced over decades of repairs strikes me as the very same ship, even though none of its original material remains, which seems to say material origin barely matters for ships -- while (a) says origin is nearly everything for people.

The tension I can't resolve is between (a) and (d)-(f) taken together: for persons, I intuit that even a small change in origin (the same egg, a different sperm) costs you identity, while for artifacts I tolerate small changes in original matter, and for ships I tolerate total eventual replacement of the original matter. I suspect the asymmetry might be doing real work -- maybe origins are essential to organisms but only loosely constraining for artifacts -- or maybe one of my verdicts is just confused. Please give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: the most precise candidate principle (or pair of principles) that captures my verdicts, the clause of it motivated by my hardest cases, what it predicts for two cases I didn't list (a human clone raised from my cell, and a table restored with 90% new wood), which of my six verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and what I should conclude if I refuse to give that verdict up.
