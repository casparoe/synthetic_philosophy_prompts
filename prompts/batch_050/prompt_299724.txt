I run the research arm of a small grantmaking fund, and about twice a month I face the same decision in different clothing: commission one more study, run a pilot, pay for a consultant's assessment, or just allocate the money with what we already know. I know the standard result in this neighborhood — Good's theorem, the claim that for an ideal Bayesian agent, acquiring free information before making a decision never has negative expected value — and I also know the folk version of it ("information is worth buying whenever it might change your mind") is not quite what the theorem says, since the theorem assumes costless and perfect updating, a fixed decision problem, and an agent who will actually act on what it learns. I've read enough Sequences-adjacent material to be suspicious of applying ideal-agent results to a team of six humans with deadlines.

Write a decision procedure my team can follow when a "should we learn more before deciding?" question comes up: an ordered sequence of tests or questions, grounded in what the value-of-information result actually establishes and where its assumptions fail for bounded agents. The procedure needs to handle at least these cases, which come up constantly for us: information that would be interesting but couldn't change any decision available to us; information that could change the decision but costs more than the plausible swing is worth; information we could act on but realistically won't, because the relevant program officer has already made up her mind; and information-gathering whose main effect is to delay a decision that gets worse with time. Include worked applications of the procedure to two or three realistic cases from our setting, at least one of which should deliver a verdict I won't like — e.g., telling us to skip a study everyone is excited about, or to buy information that feels pointless. Finally, mark clearly the points where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment, and say what it cannot do — in particular, whether it covers cases where learning might change what we value rather than what we believe, and cases where we suspect we're worse reasoners after receiving certain kinds of evidence.
