My roommate got obsessed with Pascal's Pensées this semester and now runs every political hypothetical past it. Her touchstone is that stretch of fragments on justice, might and custom — where Pascal says we follow the majority not because it has more reason but more power, that custom is what ends up deciding what counts as just, and that since we can't make the just strong we've settled for making the strong look just. (I'm paraphrasing, don't @ me about translations.)

Her verdicts so far, when I've thrown cases at her:

1. An old monarchy, obeyed mostly out of habit, real power, full ceremonial apparatus of justice — legitimate, she says, at least in Pascal's descriptive sense.
2. A brand-new junta, firmly in control, openly sneering at justice-talk, ruling by naked decree — not legitimate.
3. A three-year-old democratic regime with firm control, courts, elections, rituals, and a population that sincerely believes in it — legitimate.

Now the new case I want to spring on her: a foreign occupying power, only eighteen months in, with overwhelming force and an extremely slick justice-costume — courts, oaths, ceremonies — such that everyone publicly treats it as legitimate, but privately almost everyone despises it and regards the exiled old republic as the real authority.

What does she say about the occupation, and which rule is she actually running — "whatever is established by custom counts as just," or "might counts as right once it's successfully dressed in the forms of justice"? At least one of those has to bend to fit her first three verdicts, so tell me which verdict is doing the most work, and give me the follow-up case that would settle which rule she holds. Also, flag where you're confident about Pascal's actual view versus where interpreters genuinely disagree (e.g. whether he's cynically describing the world or prescribing a program), and don't dress up a contested reading as the scholarly consensus.

End with your prediction as \answer{legitimate} or \answer{not legitimate}.
