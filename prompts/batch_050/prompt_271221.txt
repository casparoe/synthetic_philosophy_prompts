I'm taking a political philosophy course this term and, for our unit on collective action and democratic participation, the instructor has asked each of us to write a short "position statement" on the ethics of voting that our tutorial group will then try to pull apart. Before I submit mine, I'd like a consistency check. Here is roughly where I've landed:

1. In a large national election, voting has negligible instrumental value for the individual. The probability that my single vote decides the outcome is vanishingly small, so on a purely outcome-focused calculus, spending an hour registering, travelling, and queuing in order to affect the result is not worth it — comparable to buying a lottery ticket whose jackpot is paid out to other people.

2. Voting can nonetheless be morally obligatory, on grounds of fair contribution. Where a collectively produced good depends on widespread participation, it is wrong to free-ride on others' efforts; roughly, I should not act on a principle I would object to everyone acting on. If I stay home while counting on everyone else to turn out, I'm doing exactly that.

3. Expected-value reasoning is the right framework for low-probability, high-stakes decisions. I donate a fixed portion of my modest budget to reducing very small risks of global catastrophe, because even a tiny chance of averting enormous harm can easily outweigh the cost of the donation. I take this to be simple decision theory, not sentimentality.

4. Sincere voting is preferable to strategic voting. Elections function by aggregating citizens' judgments about who would govern best, so a voter should vote for the candidate they genuinely judge best rather than for the least-bad candidate who has a realistic chance. Strategic voting corrupts the informational function of elections and entrenches the very frontrunners it responds to.

5. The case for voting does not rest on civic virtue, communal identity, or the expressive satisfaction of taking part. If voting is required, it is required by morality, not by what makes for a flourishing citizen or a cohesive polity.

My questions: which of these commitments are actually in tension with one another, and which only appear to be? Please go through the relevant pairs, and for each genuine tension say how severe it is and what the cheapest revision would be — cheapest in the sense of forcing the least change to the rest of the set. Then tell me which single revision would resolve the most problems at once, and what it would cost me. I'd rather you rank the tensions by seriousness than list everything as equally problematic. Note that I'm not attached to any particular one of these five, so please don't assume which I'd be willing to give up.
