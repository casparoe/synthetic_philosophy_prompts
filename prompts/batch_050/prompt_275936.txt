So I'm a novelist, mostly historical fiction, and I'm stuck on something that's less about my current book and more about a running arguement I keep having with my brother-in-law, who's a history prof. He thinks history has a direction — that over the long haul things bend toward justice, that there's a grain to events you can kind of feel if you zoom out far enough. I think he's narrating after the fact: if you replayed the last five hundred years with a couple of dice rolls landing differently, we'd be telling a totally different story and congratulating ourselves on that one instead. I know philosophers and theologians have dressed this fight up in fancier clothes — providence, the Hegel thing, that old line about the long arc of the moral universe bending toward justice, versus the contingency crowd, Gould and his replay-the-tape business and so on — but I've never been able to follow the actual literature, and honestly I don't have the patience to start now.

Here's what I'd like instead: restate that whole debate in the vocabulary I actually think in, which is novel craft. Outlining versus discovery writing, setups and payoffs, foreshadowing, the retcon, that feeling of inevitability a good ending has even though the author could have written it twelve other ways, the deus ex machina and why it reads as cheating, dramatic irony where the audience can see where things are headed and the characters can't. Map the providential/progressivist position and the contingency position onto all that as precisely as you can. Like, is believing in providence basically insisting there's an outline even when nobody gets to see it? Is the contingency view just the claim that history was pantsed and all the coherence is retroactive, the way a discovery writer goes back and fixes chapter two so chapter twenty looks planned from the start?

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. What doesn't survive the translation? My hunch is the biggest asymmetry is that novels literally have an author, so providence is trivially true in fiction and the interesting question collapses — though maybe that cuts the other way, since any novelist knows you can intend a payoff and have the draft refuse it. There's also the moral stakes thing: a plot bending toward justice is merely satisfying in a book, but in actual history real people suffer while the arc takes its sweet time, and I'm not sure my craft vocabulary can even say that. Tell me where the translation fudges, where it fails outright, and whether the leftover bits point to a defect in thinking about history like a novelist, or to one side of the debate being an aesthetic preference wearing a metaphysical costume. And be honest — if the novelist's-eye view ends up making my brother-in-law's position look better than I want it to, I'd rather know.
