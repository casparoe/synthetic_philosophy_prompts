So a guy in my reading group (longtime LW/SSC people, we do a chapter a week) has been on a Kant kick, and last week he hit the Second Analogy of Experience in the first Critique and came back to Discord with what he calls his "proof machine." For context: in that section Kant contrasts watching a ship move downstream — where the order of your perceptions is forced on you, you see the ship higher up and then lower down and couldn't have had the perceptions in the reverse order — with scanning a house, where your perceptions are just as successive (roof, windows, cellar) but nobody thinks the house itself got rearranged, because you could have scanned it in any order. Kant's claim, roughly, is that what makes the ship case a case of something actually happening, rather than just a movie playing in your head, is that the order of perceptions is bound to a rule — and that's supposed to get you that every event falls under causal law. Fine. Here's the machine:

(1) Whenever my perceptions come in a sequence, I can only take them to be perceptions of something objective — as opposed to a merely subjective play of impressions — if I take the order of the sequence to be fixed by a rule. (This is just the lesson of the ship, he says.)

(2) When I look around a room, my perceptions of the parts of the room come in a sequence.

(3) So by (1), I can only take the room to be something objective if I take the order of my looking-around to be fixed by a rule.

(4) But if the order of my looking-around were fixed by a rule, I couldn't experience the order as reversible — and Kant himself needs the house case to be one where the order IS reversible, that's the whole contrast with the ship.

(5) So Kant's own principle makes it impossible to ever experience a static object as objective. Either nothing static is ever objectively given to us (full Berkeley mode, the room is a dream) or coexistence is strictly unperceivable and everything we experience is an event.

(6) Both of those are insane, so the principle is false, and since the principle was the entire argument for "every event has a cause," the Second Analogy proves nothing — transcendental arguments in general are just the fallacy of inflating "conditions of my perceiving X" into "conditions of X."

The thing is, (6) also seems insane, or at least way too fast, and I suspect the machine proves too much somewhere earlier. But every premise looks like something Kant literally commits to. Which step do I actually reject? For the step you pick, explain why it looked fine — like, what true thing it's adjacent to — and then price out the alternatives: e.g. what does Kant lose if you restrict the scope of (1), does he end up only proving a conditional about judgments of events rather than anything about the world, and does that leave him open to the Stroud-style complaint that at best he's shown we must *believe* in causal order, not that anything is causally ordered? Also, my friend insists the same template ("you can't coherently experience/argue/deny without presupposing X, therefore X") works for induction and free will — does your diagnosis of where this instance breaks generalize, or do you have to handle those case by case? I'd like to keep (2) if at all possible because it just seems like a fact about vision, and I'd like to keep some version of Kant's conclusion about events, but I realize I maybe can't have both.
