Two friends of mine, Maya and Dan, got into a long argument over beers last week about whether language models actually understand anything, and it started from a specific case that I think made it more interesting than the usual "stochastic parrots" shouting match. A hospital had trained a model on decades of doctors' dictated case notes -- text only, no lab values, no imaging, just what physicians said out loud after seeing patients. The model flagged a patient the team had discharged as fine, and the patient turned out to have a rare condition everyone had missed. Maya's take: whatever else is going on, that thing understands something the doctors didn't. It reconstructed the signature of a disease from nothing but words, so the parrot line has to be wrong. Dan pushed back: understanding requires your knowledge to be hooked up to the actual world, and this model has never seen a patient in its life. It's exploiting statistical regularities in how doctors happen to write notes. Change the note-taking conventions and it falls apart, which no doctor who actually understood the disease would.

Then it got into the weeds. Maya said doctors' understanding is also pattern extraction -- med students learn mostly from textbooks and lectures, and a doctor trained on 1900s case notes would also fall apart in a modern ward, but nobody says they lacked understanding. Dan's move: there's a difference between prediction and explanation. Ask the model why it flagged the patient and it just confabulates a plausible-sounding rationale; a doctor can give you the actual mechanism. Maya countered that humans confabulate constantly (she brought up split-brain patients inventing reasons for their actions) and that when researchers probed the model's internals they apparently found it tracking things like fever progression over time, which sure looks like having concepts. Dan conceded it was doing something structured but shifted to: understanding is about what you can do with knowledge -- apply it elsewhere, reason counterfactually, and this model can't transfer its so-called knowledge of the disease to anything, it couldn't recognize the symptoms on a body in front of it. Maya accused him of moving the goalposts (first no concepts, now concepts aren't enough) and said by his standard a bedridden doctor who can't do rounds doesn't understand medicine either. They went home annoyed.

So: can you go through this point by point and tell me who wins each exchange -- the original flagging case, the textbook-learning reply, the confabulation point, the probing result, and the transfer/goalpost exchange at the end? And beyond scoring the moves, I'd like to know if there's something both of them are missing. I have a suspicion they're both assuming understanding is all-or-nothing, and that the medical case might be revealing precisely because it sits in some middle zone neither of their frameworks handles well -- but maybe that suspicion is itself confused, so tell me if it is.
