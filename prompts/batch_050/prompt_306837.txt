My dad was recently offered a spot in a cancer trial where they don't just flip a coin 50/50 the whole way through — the odds of getting the new drug versus the standard one shift over time depending on how earlier patients are doing. At dinner last week I mentioned this and two of my friends got into a huge argument about it, and it's been bugging me because I have to help dad decide whether to enroll and I honestly couldn't tell who won.

Dana (biostats PhD, works on trial design) said this kind of adaptive setup is basically the ethical fix for the oldest problem with randomized trials. The classic objection is that once evidence starts favoring one arm it's wrong to keep randomizing evenly, and response-adaptive randomization just builds the fix into the design itself — fewer and fewer patients get the worse arm as the picture develops, so it's strictly more ethical than a fixed trial, end of story. Marcus (masters in bioethics, now works in hospital admin) said she's got it exactly backwards: a fixed trial at least has ethical cover the whole way through because nobody looks at the results until the end, whereas in dad's trial the investigators are demonstrably tracking which arm is winning and still enrolling people — the shifting odds are themselves proof that genuine uncertainty is gone, so every patient who still lands on the losing arm is being knowingly sent there for statistical neatness. Dana said that's literally what the concept of clinical equipoise is for — the uncertainty that matters is the expert community's, not any one investigator's peek at interim data, and the data monitoring committee letting the trial continue means community-level uncertainty is intact. Marcus said that's an accounting trick, the uncertainty is manufactured by having one group of people look at the data and a different group of people consent the patients, and if equipoise depends on keeping the treating doctors in the dark then it was never really doing ethical work in the first place. Dana's last move was that trials owe something to future patients too — shifting allocation or stopping at the first whiff of benefit inflates false positives, and a trial that ends in an ambiguous result wastes everyone who enrolled, dad included. Marcus said fine, but then be honest that participants are partly instruments for future patients instead of dressing it up as a coin flip between two options nobody has a preference between.

Can you go through this round by round and say who's right about each specific move — is Marcus right that adaptive designs are actually worse on equipoise grounds, is Dana right that community-level uncertainty survives the monitoring committee looking at the data, whose account of what a trial owes current participants versus future patients holds up better? And then who's more right overall, because right now it feels like they each just won their own half. Also, if there's something both of them are missing about what I should actually care about for dad's specific decision, that's honestly the most useful part for me.
