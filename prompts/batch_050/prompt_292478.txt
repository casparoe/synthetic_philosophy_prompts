For an essay in my school's history and philosophy seminar, I want to analyze the German Historikerstreit — the public dispute among West German historians and intellectuals in the 1980s over whether the Holocaust was a singular, incomparable event or whether it could and should be compared to other twentieth-century mass crimes, in particular those of Stalin's regime — through the lens of Zygmunt Bauman's thesis in Modernity and the Holocaust. As I understand Bauman, he argues that the Holocaust was not a relapse into pre-modern barbarism or a breakdown of civilization but a product of distinctively modern features: bureaucratic organization, instrumental rationality, the division of labor that distances individual functionaries from the outcome of their tasks, and the modern state's ambition to rationally engineer society. Note that Bauman's book appeared after the dispute, so his theory should be treated as a lens on the episode, not as a contribution to it.

I want the analysis to run in both directions.

First, from theory to episode. I would like you to work with a distinction I have been trying out: genetic comparability, the question of whether the Holocaust shares its causal mechanisms and preconditions with other atrocities, versus evaluative comparability, the question of whether it carries the same moral weight. On Bauman's account, the Holocaust seems genetically comparable almost by construction — bureaucratic rationality and social engineering exist in every modern state — while its evaluative status is a separate matter his theory arguably intensifies rather than settles. Using this distinction, reconstruct what the two camps were actually disagreeing about: the comparabilist side (associated with Ernst Nolte), which treated the Holocaust as one instance of modern mass violence and partly as a reaction to Bolshevism, and the singularity side (associated with Jürgen Habermas), which insisted on its uniqueness. Does Bauman's theory dissolve the dispute by showing the camps were talking past each other, sharpen it by locating the real point of disagreement, or expose it as resting on a conflation of the two senses of comparability?

Second, from episode to theory. What does the dispute reveal about Bauman's account itself? Consider at least these pressures: if modernity is the explanation, the theory seems to overpredict — most modern bureaucratic states have not produced anything like the Holocaust, so either Bauman needs additional conditions or his account explains a capacity rather than the event; Nolte's causal story about fear of Bolshevism is a story about ideas and reactions rather than administrative mechanisms — say whether it is a genuine rival to Bauman or whether the two operate at different levels and can both be true; and Bauman's theory was presumably meant to be politically sobering, locating the danger in modernity itself rather than in any one national character, yet the Historikerstreit showed that both uniqueness claims and comparability claims were immediately politicized — say what this shows about whether a sociological theory of the Holocaust can be politically neutral at all.

End with a verdict: does the episode confirm Bauman's thesis, count as an anomaly for it, or show that the theory was simply silent on the question the dispute was actually about? Where the theory genuinely has nothing to say, I would rather you mark that silence explicitly than force it to deliver a verdict.
