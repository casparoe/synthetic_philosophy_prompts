A close friend of mine is signed up for cryonics, and over the years we've had a running argument about whether what gets revived could possibly be her. I'm a longtime reader of the usual blogs, so I've absorbed the standard moves -- the Ship of Theseus, Moravec-style gradual transfer, patternism -- and last week I tried to settle the debate by writing out what I thought was the strongest version of the pro-uploading case. The result disturbs me, because it seems to prove something I'm not willing to accept, and I can't tell whether the problem is in my reasoning or in my squeamishness. Here it is:

(1) The atoms composing your body are exchanged for new ones over time, and you plainly survive this; so what makes you *you* is not the particular matter. (2) Imagine a procedure that replaces your neurons one at a time with artificial units that reproduce each neuron's input-output behavior exactly. No single replacement could be the one that kills you -- one neuron more or less is nothing to a mind -- so by induction across all the replacements, the fully artificial brain at the end is still you, conscious throughout. (3) What survived the procedure is the functional organization, not the substrate. (4) If the organization is what matters, then anything that instantiates that organization is you, regardless of how the instantiation came about. (5) A sufficiently detailed scan captures the organization. (6) Therefore a scanned copy run on new hardware is you, and once such a copy exists, it makes no difference whatsoever to your survival whether the biological original is destroyed a moment later. (7) But now suppose two copies are run simultaneously. On this argument both are you; yet from the first moment of operation they diverge and are not identical to each other, and one thing cannot be identical to two distinct things.

So the argument seems to simultaneously establish that uploading is survival and refute itself at the last step. Here's what I'd most like to keep: the induction in (2), because gradual replacement strikes me as nearly unassailable, and the friend I'm arguing with deserves better than gut-feeling conservatism. What I cannot bring myself to accept is the corollary in (6) -- that once a copy exists, pulverizing the original is a matter of complete indifference to the person lying on the table. Which step do I reject, why did it look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost me with respect to the gradual-replacement case I want to preserve? And if the right move is instead to bite some bullet about what "survival" really amounts to, tell me plainly which bullet and whose body gets left behind.
