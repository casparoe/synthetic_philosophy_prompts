I'm teaching a seminar on partiality and special obligations this fall, and one unit is on what adult children owe their parents. The central reading is Jane English's essay What Do Grown Children Owe Their Parents?, in which she argues (to paraphrase rather than quote her) that the sacrifices parents make for minor children do not generate debts the children must later repay -- a favor requested and bestowed as a gift creates no obligation of return, and parental care is better understood as such a gift -- so that grown children's duties to their parents, where they exist at all, are just the duties of friendship: they flow from the current quality of the relationship, scale with its closeness, and dissolve when the friendship does. My students found this liberating and appalling in roughly equal measure, and the discussion kept stalling because nobody could say what it would take for a competing account to beat hers.

So here is what I need: write the specification -- the list of desiderata that any adequate theory of filial obligation must satisfy, with the case or consideration motivating each requirement, and for each one an explicit verdict on whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of a particular camp. Please build the list around hard cases rather than intuitions in the abstract: the parent who met every legal duty but was cold and is now lonely; the parent who sacrificed enormously and reminds the child of it weekly; the abusive or neglectful parent now in genuine need; the parent with dementia who no longer knows who the visitor is; the child who was never consulted about being born and resents the implied invoice; and cross-cultural pressure from traditions (Confucian filial piety, for instance) where English's conclusion reads as pathology rather than insight. The list should also force the theory to take positions on structure, not just verdicts: whether filial duties are generated by past benefits, present relationships, roles, or gratitude; whether they can persist through estrangement; whether financial support and emotional attention have the same ground; and whether the asymmetry between what parents owe children and what children owe parents is itself a datum to be explained or an embarrassment to be eliminated.

The part I care most about is consistency: if some combination of desiderata turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable -- for example, if any theory that handles the abusive-parent case in the intuitive way must concede that filial duty cannot be grounded in biological relationship alone, which in turn costs us an easy answer to the dementia case -- I want that flagged explicitly, because the incompatibility would itself be the seminar's main result. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; you may gesture at how English's friendship model, debt or gratitude accounts, and role-based or Confucian views would fare, but do not write a scorecard -- I'll do that with the students in class.
