I have recently become the unofficial archivist of my wife's family: her father spent eleven years caring for her mother through dementia, almost entirely alone, and since her death relatives keep describing him as a saint, a hero, the moral centre of the family. He waves it off. What unsettles me is not his modesty but the timing: none of these relatives visited, none of them said any of this during the eleven years, and I keep noticing that everything they now praise was invisible while it was happening. I have started seeing the same pattern elsewhere. A neighbour of ours did substantially the same thing for her husband, but she burned out in year three, placed him in a facility, and is spoken of with a delicate pity that is one degree from blame. The two stories differ mostly in stamina and in who ended up witnessing what, yet they attract entirely different moral verdicts, delivered with total confidence.

So the pattern, stated as neutrally as I can manage: we officially admire a quality -- devotion, faithfulness, integrity -- that is supposed to be wholly independent of outcomes and of recognition, but in practice our admiration only ever attaches to cases where the outcome held up and someone was around to see it. The quality is defined by its indifference to exactly the two things our verdicts actually track. I know this is adjacent to what philosophers call moral luck, and to the psychologist's outcome bias, but both of those are about results; my pattern includes a second axis, visibility, and the two seem to interact in a way I have not seen named: the unseen version of the virtue is not merely unpraised, it is practically unavailable for praise, so the virtue's supposed independence from recognition can never be tested, only asserted.

This brought me back to Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling, written under the pseudonym Johannes de silentio, which I am reading for the first time. In the opening section (called "Preparation" in the Hollander translation), Johannes writes of the man captivated by the story of Abraham: "The man we speak of was no thinker, he felt no desire to go beyond his faith: it seemed to him the most glorious fate to be remembered as the Father of Faith, and a most enviable lot to be possessed of that faith, even if no one knew it."

My questions turn on that passage. First, is the final clause doing real work, or is it decorative? "Remembered as the Father of Faith" and "even if no one knew it" sit uneasily together in a single sentence: the first fate requires witnesses, the second is offered as still enviable in their absence. Does Johannes mean that the value of faith is strictly recognition-independent, and if so, is that a claim about faith itself or only about how it feels from the inside? Second, does the pattern I describe above -- admiration that professes independence from visibility while being wholly conditioned by it -- already have a name and a literature of its own, as distinct from moral luck and outcome bias on the one side and from the "hiddenness" theme in the later Problema on the other? If it does, I want to know how the established concept carves the phenomenon and where my description overruns its edges; if it does not, I would rather be told that plainly than be handed the nearest label. And third, whichever way that comes out: is there any coherent position on which my father-in-law's eleven years would have had exactly the same moral standing had no one ever learned of them -- or does the impossibility of anyone, including him, confirming the virtue without witnesses show that recognition-independence is a necessary fiction of admiration rather than a fact about the acts admired? I am not looking for a bare name; I want the carving.
