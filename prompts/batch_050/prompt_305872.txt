In my philosophy of history seminar last week we were discussing Popper's argument in The Poverty of Historicism that there can be no laws of historical development -- only trends, which are conditional and fragile, as opposed to laws, which hold unconditionally. Midway through, a classmate waved the whole debate off with the familiar line that history doesn't repeat itself, but it rhymes. Everyone nodded as if that settled something, and it has been bothering me since, because I cannot figure out what it settles, if anything. It sounds like a sensible middle position between crude cyclical theories and total skepticism about historical knowledge, but I suspect it is one of those phrases that survives precisely because it can be filled with whatever the speaker needs.

Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing? For example: a banal claim that historical situations are merely similar in some respects (trivially true, since any two situations are); a stronger claim that there are recurring structural patterns in history, robust enough to guide prediction; a claim that there are conditional regularities -- something like Popper's trends rather than laws -- which would make the slogan a restatement of Popper rather than a refutation of him; a claim that historical knowledge works by analogy rather than by covering law, which sounds methodological but I'm not sure what it commits one to; and perhaps a purely cautionary remark about the limits of forecasting that makes no claim about the world at all. For each reading, tell me whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; what exactly the metaphor of rhyming (as opposed to repeating) is doing for it, and whether that metaphor adds anything beyond vagueness; and whether the reading is even falsifiable or otherwise answerable to evidence.

Then, most importantly: given that my classmate deployed the slogan to dismiss the question of whether laws of history are possible, which of these readings, if any, would actually justify that use? I have a suspicion the slogan functions less as an answer than as a way of declining to distinguish between the trivial and the substantive readings, since on the trivial reading it refutes nobody and on the substantive reading it is just the historicist claim in a folksy costume. If that suspicion is wrong, show me where; if it is right, say what a genuinely informative answer to my classmate would have had to commit to.
