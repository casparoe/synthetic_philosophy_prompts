Last month I was helping my teenage niece with a history assignment, and I got stuck on something her textbook seemed to take entirely for granted. The book sorts old stories into "history" and "legend," and the way it draws the line puzzles me. The Trojan War is described as a legend with a historical core, on the grounds that archaeologists uncovered a real Bronze Age city at Hisarlik in northwestern Turkey — roughly where Greek tradition placed Troy — with layers showing violent destruction in the period when the war is supposed to have happened. King Arthur, by contrast, is presented as legend through and through, even though Britons really did resist Anglo-Saxon invasions in the fifth and sixth centuries, and the famous battle of Mount Badon appears in a near-contemporary account that simply never mentions anyone named Arthur. The Exodus is treated as legend, or at any rate not history, because the Egyptian records are silent about it and no trace of a migration on the biblical scale has turned up in the Sinai — even though, I would have thought, a story that old and that central to a people presumably grew around something. When I asked my niece's teacher what rule decides these cases, she said it was just a matter of weighing the evidence, which I found unsatisfying, because the weights seem to differ from case to case.

So here is what I would like from you. Reconstruct the implicit theory behind this sorting practice: when historians say a legend has a historical core, what exactly has to match between the story and the record — the place, the event, the people, the names, or some weighted combination of these? Sharpen your answer with two test cases that occurred to me. First, suppose the destruction at Hisarlik were definitively shown to be earthquake damage rather than the aftermath of a siege: would the Iliad still be "about" a real event, or would it merely resemble one by coincidence — and what does your answer imply about what makes a story about something at all? Second, suppose an inscription surfaced tomorrow naming a British commander Arthur who fought the Saxons at Badon: would that retroactively promote the familiar tales of the round table and the grail to "history with embellishments," and if not, where exactly is the ceiling on what a single confirmed detail can do for a story? Then tell me whether the discipline is applying one standard consistently. Troy seems to get credit for a matching place plus a matching destruction; Arthur gets no credit for a matching war plus a matching battle; and the Exodus is demoted for missing evidence rather than conflicting evidence, which strikes me as a different kind of failing. In your reconstruction, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and say where it is genuinely silent, so that several rival theories would license the same verdicts.

Please give me the reconstruction in a few paragraphs of plain prose, followed by a table with one row per case and exactly these columns: Case | What matches between legend and record (place, event, persons, names) | What the story's "historical core" would minimally have to be | The smallest new discovery that would flip its classification | Verdict (does the label the discipline actually uses fit the standard you reconstructed?). Close with a short paragraph on what would have to change in how these stories are taught if the practice adopted a stricter standard, under which only confirmed names and dated events count, or a looser one, under which a matching setting is enough. Use American English throughout.
