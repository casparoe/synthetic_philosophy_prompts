I'm taking a seminar on early modern political thought, and after our session on Pascal's Pensées, two classmates got into an argument about his treatment of justice and force. Neither would concede, and I'd like you to adjudicate.

Their dispute turned on Pascal's famous line of thought that justice without force is powerless and force without justice is tyrannical, that we should therefore try to join the two, and that since we cannot make what is just strong, we have instead made what is strong just. Daniela read this as essentially cynical: on her view, Pascal is saying that talk of justice is a useful fiction draped over raw power, that people obey rulers because they imagine obedience to be just rather than because it is, and that the advice to unite justice and force is really advice to rulers on how to make domination stable and palatable. Marcus replied that this flattens Pascal: the failure to make what is just strong is presented as a tragedy Pascal is diagnosing, not a program he's endorsing, and his point that custom rather than reason grounds our obedience is a critical deflation of political authority, not a celebration of it. Daniela countered that if justice-talk functions mainly as the machinery of stable rule, then calling the account "critical" changes nothing about its content — it's cynicism with extra steps. Marcus's last move was that Pascal recommends obeying established custom not because it is just but simply because it is established, in order to avoid civil strife, and that a genuine cynic who thought justice was nothing but a fiction would have no need for that kind of prudential argument — only someone who takes justice seriously as a real but impotent standard would.

Please go through the exchange move by move: say who is right about which specific point, what each of them gets wrong or overlooks, and where your verdict lands overall. One more thing: please be careful to distinguish what is reasonably settled among people who work on Pascal from what remains genuinely contested — I know the Pensées are fragmentary and unfinished, so I'd rather not have a contested reading presented to me as the scholarly consensus.
