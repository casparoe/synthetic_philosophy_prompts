You are a senior consultant in bioethics with a specialization in procreative ethics, retained to advise a hospital's embryo selection review committee. Think carefully and methodically. Begin by brainstorming the candidate decision rules that could rationalize the committee's track record, and only then move to prediction. Reason step by step, and be honest where the record genuinely underdetermines the answer.

The committee has issued the following rulings over the past five years. In each case the parents are choosing among their own viable embryos produced through IVF, and in each case the committee ruled on whether the requested selection should be permitted.

1. Parents carried a recessive mutation for a condition involving progressive neurological degeneration, unrelieved pain, and death before age four. They requested transfer of an unaffected sibling embryo. Approved.

2. Deaf parents, both with hereditary deafness caused by a specific mutation, requested transfer of the embryo carrying the same mutation, so that their child would share their language and community. Refused.

3. One embryo carried a mutation conferring a substantially elevated lifetime risk of several cancers, with some risk reducible through surveillance and preventive surgery. The parents requested a lower-risk sibling embryo. Approved.

4. One embryo carried a mutation for a late-onset, currently untreatable neurodegenerative disease that typically manifests after age fifty. The parents requested an unaffected sibling embryo. Approved.

5. Parents requested transfer of an embryo with a gene variant associated in some studies with slightly enhanced memory performance over a sibling embryo without it. Refused, on the stated ground that the evidence of net benefit to the child was too uncertain.

The committee has now received a new request. Of two viable embryos, one carries a mutation causing certain lifelong infertility but is otherwise expected to be completely healthy. The other carries a mutation causing mild, well-managed asthma but is fertile. The parents request transfer of the infertile embryo. They note that they regard biological fertility as unnecessary for a flourishing life, that they would welcome their child choosing adoption or a childfree life, and that in their judgment the infertility imposes less of a burden than even mild asthma.

Please address the following, in order:

First, set out the plausible decision rules consistent with all five prior rulings. I expect at least three: a rule that maximizes the expected wellbeing of the future child, exercising caution where evidence of benefit is weak; a rule that permits selection only to avert disease or departures from normal functioning and forbids both enhancement and the deliberate conferral of any condition; and a rule organized around not knowingly giving a child a worse starting position than an available sibling embryo, with "worse" cashed out in terms the child would likely endorse in maturity. If any of these three in fact fails to fit the record, say so and replace it with a rule that does.

Second, for each surviving rule, state what it predicts about the new case and with what confidence. Note especially whether the rules diverge here, and if so, whether the divergence turns on an empirical question (how infertility and mild asthma actually affect lives), a conceptual question (whether infertility counts as a harm, a disadvantage, or neither), or a normative one.

Third, give your prediction of the committee's actual ruling, identify which prior ruling is the closest analog to the new case, and explain your reasoning.

Fourth, state explicitly which principle the committee would be adopting if it approved, and which if it refused. If you judge that the precedents underdetermine the outcome, say so plainly rather than forcing a prediction.

Finally, describe one further hypothetical selection case whose ruling would best discriminate between the two rules you consider most likely to be guiding the committee, and say what each rule would predict about it.
