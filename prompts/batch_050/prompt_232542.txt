I direct the diagnostic imaging department of a mid-sized hospital. Every Friday we hold a consensus conference: eight to twelve radiologists, plus two or three residents, work through ten difficult cases in about ninety minutes, and the group settles on a consensus read for each, with the attending of record signing off. The format is open discussion, and the most senior voices usually go first. A recent internal review of our diagnostic misses turned up a pattern: in several cases, at least two people in the room later admitted they had arrived at a different initial impression that they either never voiced or abandoned as soon as the first senior reading went the other way. I take this to be the information-cascade problem rather than a courage problem: each person who deferred was behaving reasonably given what the earlier speakers' confidence implied, and the failure lies in what the meeting does to information, not in the character of the participants. Exhortations to speak up will therefore not fix it, and I do not want them.

Please write a procedure I can actually run in these conferences, grounded in the theory of rational herding: what gets elicited from whom, in what order, in what form (silent written reads, stated confidence, stated reasoning), what may be revealed to the room and when, how the room is to treat a first dissenter, and how a consensus is declared at the end. For each rule, state which feature of cascades it exploits or blocks -- for instance, that publicly stated judgments are actions from which observers infer private signals, or that a long run of agreement may rest on the private information of only the first few speakers -- so that I can defend the rules to my colleagues as information design rather than as etiquette.

Two constraints the procedure must respect rather than wish away. First, deference is often rational: our most experienced neuroradiologist's initial read genuinely outperforms three juniors' reads combined, and a rule that always silences the expert early would destroy real information; the procedure needs a principled way of deciding when seniority should speak early, late, or not at all in the first round. Second, I have considered appointing a rotating devil's advocate, and I believe this fails because a dissent that everyone knows was assigned carries no evidential weight; if I am wrong about that, show why, and if I am right, the procedure must secure genuine dissent some other way.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a straightforward one where early agreement is correct and the procedure should terminate quickly without manufacturing doubt; one in which two residents privately favor a rare diagnosis that the first senior speaker dismisses; and one whose correct output I will not like -- for example, that I, as the most senior person present, must absent myself from the first round on cases where I am the attending of record. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: which failure modes of group judgment it leaves untouched, and at what points it must hand the decision back to an individual's judgment.
