I’m a PhD student in moral and political philosophy, and next month I’m presenting a chapter on the nonidentity problem to a population ethics workshop. The audience will be about eight moral philosophers who know Parfit well, mostly total or critical-level utilitarians, one committed person-affecting theorist, and one policy economist who keeps asking awkward questions about incentives and implementation. So not a general public audience, unfortunatley.

My claim is that even when a policy changes which future people will exist, we can still have a non-comparative, role-based reason not to choose a policy that predictably creates lives barely worth living when the same institutions could instead have created different people with good lives. I’m not saying the worse-off people are harmed by existing, or that possible people have rights against us. The idea is that governments and other collective agents occupy a “custodial role” over future populations, so they can wrong future people without making any particular person worse off. I use a case where a city must choose between two equally expensive flood policies: one preserves a neighbourhood where existing families will remain, the other changes migration and reproductive patterns enough that substantially different children are born there twenty years later, many with chronically poor health from avoidable chemical exposure.

Please prepare me for the objections I’m actually likely to get from this audience, rather than generic seminar objections. Give me about eight, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do. Phrase each one in the voice of the person most likely to ask it, including the population ethicists, the person-affecting theorist, and the economist. For each objection, tell me what I should concede, what I should hold firm on, and give a reply I could realistically deliver in about 90 seconds. Mark the soft point in each reply rather than pretending it is bulletproof. Also tell me which objection I should most hope nobody raises, and how I might limit the damage if it comes up anyway.

Please answer in British English, and don’t give me a bibliography or invented references.
