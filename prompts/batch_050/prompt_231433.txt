Next month I am presenting a paper titled "Fitness Without Tautology" at a two-day workshop on the metaphysics of evolution, and I would like help preparing for the question period.

The view I am defending, in outline. Fitness is a probabilistic disposition — a propensity — but the bearer of the disposition is not the individual organism, as on the standard version of the view. It is the pairing of a heritable trait-type with a lineage, and the propensity is defined over an ensemble of possible environments weighted by their objective probabilities, not over the single environment the lineage happens to occupy. The payoff, I claim, is twofold. First, the view dissolves rather than answers the familiar tautology objection: the charge that "survival of the fittest" is circular rests on reading fitness as defined by realized reproductive success, whereas my proposal is a theoretical identification — fitness stands to reproductive propensity roughly as temperature stands to mean molecular kinetic energy — and theoretical identifications are explanatory precisely because they are not definitions. Second, because propensities attach to trait-lineages rather than to token organisms, they are empirically tractable in a way single-case propensities are not: replicated experimental evolution with microbial populations gives us direct statistical purchase on the very ensembles the view quantifies over.

The audience. Roughly fifteen philosophers of biology, a majority of whom lean toward the statisticalist interpretation of selection — the view that selection is not a causal force acting on organisms but a statistical pattern in populations, and that construing fitness as a causal propensity reifies a bookkeeping device. Three or four working population geneticists will be present; they treat fitness strictly as a parameter estimated from data (selection coefficients, realized offspring distributions) and have limited patience for metaphysical glosses on it. There will also be one senior figure whose career has been built on a drift-centered, historically contingent picture of evolution, and one metaphysician of science who works on dispositions and will care about the propensity theory itself rather than the biology.

Please draft my Q&A preparation document:

1. The eight objections I am most likely to face from this audience specifically — not the generic textbook objections, but the ones these particular people, given their commitments, will actually raise — ordered by expected damage, where damage is a function of likelihood and severity. Phrase each objection in the idiom of the person who would raise it: the statisticalist's objection should sound like a statisticalist, the population geneticist's like an experimentalist with no time for ontology, and so on.
2. For each objection: a reply deliverable in roughly two minutes; an explicit statement of what I should concede and what I must hold; and a candid note on where the reply is weakest.
3. The single question I should most hope nobody asks, together with the best answer available to me and an honest assessment of why it is dangerous.

Work from the sketch above rather than asking me for clarification, and flag any point at which you judge the sketch itself to contain a move this audience will see through immediately.
