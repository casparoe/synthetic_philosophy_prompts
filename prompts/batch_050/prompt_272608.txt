I need to turn a half-formed idea into a fellowship proposal: two pages, funding one year of work in philosophy of social science. Here is the idea as I currently have it. Demographers have a standing complaint about generational talk -- Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z -- which is that the effects popularly attributed to generations are hopelessly entangled with age effects and period effects, so that survey data alone can never isolate a pure cohort effect. Social theorists, for their part, tend to reply that generations are socially constructed, but this verdict seems to prove too much: money and marriage are constructed as well, and nobody concludes that talk of them is empty. My suspicion is that both sides are arguing past the actual philosophical question. The interesting question is not whether generations are constructed but whether cohort labels differ in their status as kinds: whether some labels, like Boomers, have become causally efficacious groupings -- categories that people identify with, that institutions and markets target, so that the classification feeds back on the classified and partly makes it true -- while other partitions of the birth years (an arbitrary twenty-year slice that never caught on, say, or a label that was coined and ignored) remain mere statistical groupings with no such feedback loop. If that is right, then "are generations real?" is a bad question, and the good question is: under what conditions does a cohort label come to constitute a social kind rather than merely summarize correlations?

It should state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; explain why the two existing positions leave it open (the demographer's identifiability argument shows at most that a certain kind of evidence cannot settle the matter, not that cohort kinds do not exist; the constructionist's verdict draws no line between generational labels and social kinds everyone accepts as real); lay out the method, which I envisage as conceptual analysis of social-kindhood combined with a structured comparison of cases -- labels that caught on, labels that did not, and non-generational social kinds used as controls -- and explain why this approach rather than, say, an empirical meta-analysis of cohort studies; say what a result would look like (presumably a criterion or set of conditions, tested against the cases) and what would count as the project failing, for instance if every candidate criterion collapses into "a generation is real if enough people believe in it," or if the case evidence underdetermines any criterion at all.
