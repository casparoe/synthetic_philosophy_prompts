So I've been arguing with my roommate about the sunk cost fallacy -- he says economists have it completely right and any attention paid to money already spent is irrational, full stop. But when I go through actual cases my gut doesn't line up that cleanly, and I want to know if there's a principle behind my gut or if I'm just inconsistent. Here's where I land:

Paid $18 for a movie, 40 minutes in it's clear it's boring: leaving is obviously right, staying because you paid is dumb. Bought a nonrefundable ski weekend months ago, now I have the flu and it would be miserable: going anyway just because it's paid for is irrational. At an all-you-can-eat buffet, eating past fullness to get your money's worth: irrational too, though it feels more harmless than dumb. My car needed $1,200 of work last month, now it needs $900 more and the car itself is only worth $1,500: factoring in the $1,200 when deciding whether to pay the $900 is a mistake -- that's gone either way. And the big one: "we can't withdraw now, after how many have died" in a war -- that seems clearly fallacious, lives spent don't obligate more lives.

But here's where it gets messy. A couple stays together partly because of fifteen years of shared history and everything they've built -- that doesn't strike me as irrational at all, even though structurally it looks like the war case. Forcing yourself to use a gym membership you regret buying, because you paid for it: people do this and honestly it kind of works as motivation? And I finished a novel last month that I stopped enjoying around page 100, because I'd already read 200 pages, and I genuinely don't feel bad about that decision -- there was something like closure in finishing. Meanwhile staying in the boring movie feels completely different from finishing the book, and I can't articulate why.

My tentative principle is something like: ignore sunk costs unless honoring them produces actual future value (motivation, a completed thing, trust built over time), as opposed to just the feeling of not wasting the past. But I suspect this either smuggles in "the past mattered after all" or collapses some of my verdicts. Test it against the cases above, repair it or replace it, and tell me precisely which of my verdicts have to go if the principle is going to stay clean. And say what it predicts for cases I haven't decided: a government finishing a half-built bridge nobody needs anymore, a company shipping a product its own testing shows is obsolete, and me keeping an expensive jacket I've worn twice in four years because of what it cost.
