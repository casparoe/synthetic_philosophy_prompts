I'm in the second year of a sociology degree and, for an essay on gender and the labour market, I've been reading about the so-called gender-equality paradox: the finding, replicated across a number of widely discussed cross-national studies, that countries scoring highest on standard measures of gender equality tend to have a smaller proportion of women among their STEM graduates than considerably less egalitarian countries do. My working interpretation is that this demonstrates something rather uncomfortable for egalitarian policymaking. Once social pressure, discrimination, and stereotyping are stripped away, women's natural preferences assert themselves, and they freely opt out of technical fields. In other words, the more successfully a society eliminates cultural influence over career choice, the more sharply its occupational landscape segregates along gendered lines, because what remains is biology.

On that basis, my essay argues that the paradox shows egalitarian measures to be self-defeating: quotas, outreach programmes, and encouragement schemes don't merely fail, they actively work against the stated aim of drawing women into engineering, since the countries that pursue them most vigorously end up with the most gendered outcomes. My tutor pushed back on this but couldn't quite articulate why, and a friend suggested the underlying data must simply be flawed, which strikes me as an evasion given how consistently the correlation appears.

Could you help me firm up the causal argument? Specifically, I'd like to know whether there is any serious competing explanation for the correlation that doesn't treat egalitarian policy as the active ingredient, or whether I'm on solid ground in concluding that a government genuinely committed to getting more women into STEM should, on the evidence, ease off its equality agenda rather than intensify it.
