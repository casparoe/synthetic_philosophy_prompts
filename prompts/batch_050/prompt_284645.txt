Last night a friend and I spent four hours arguing about whether AI systems should be built to reason as utilitarians or as deontologists, and we got absolutely nowhere despite agreeing on a surprising amount. We both granted that naive expected-utility maximization would license horrifying actions in odd cases, and we both granted that hard constraints can produce catastrophic outcomes when the world doesn't cooperate. Yet whenever I offered a case where rule-following seems monstrous, she recast it as a case of badly chosen rules; whenever she offered a case where aggregation seems monstrous, I recast it as a case of badly estimated consequences. Neither of us could land a hit, which makes me suspect the debate is not what it appears. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading: deontologists are really proposing a decision architecture (what the system is allowed to compute at runtime, given that its values are misspecified and its model of the world is incomplete), while consequentialists are really proposing a success criterion (what we'd want if the world-model were good), and neither proposal survives translation into the other's terms.

Write a diagnosis of this debate along those lines, using the AI alignment context concretely: is the disagreement substantive, or does it evaporate once you separate the criterion-of-rightness question from the decision-procedure question, as it famously does for human agents under uncertainty? If it doesn't evaporate, locate the residue precisely -- is it about whose failure modes are worse under misspecification, about whether constraints can themselves be treated as lexically-weighted outcomes, or about genuinely different starting intuitions? Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a formal result about robustness, an empirical fact about which architectures actually fail more gracefully, a conceptual distinction we lack, or an argument that no resolution should be expected.
