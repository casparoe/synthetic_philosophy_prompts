A colleague in my graduate historiography seminar circulated the following argument, and after two meetings none of us has located the step where it goes wrong, though we are all convinced it must go wrong somewhere. I would like a diagnosis.

(1) The past is indefinitely rich: for any period, the events, utterances, and conditions available to be recorded vastly exceed what any history can record.
(2) Every work of history therefore rests on selection, and the principles of selection are supplied by the historian's own questions, which are questions of her own time.
(3) The historian must also choose the categories under which past conduct is described, and the only categories available to her are those her own conceptual scheme affords.
(4) In many cases those categories are not ones the historical actors possessed: describing a harvest procession as a ritual reinforcing hierarchy, or a gift exchange as economic behavior, employs descriptions the actors would not recognize and might repudiate.
(5) An account of what people were doing that the people themselves could not, even in principle, acknowledge as an account of what they were doing is not a description of their actions but an imposition on them.
(6) Hence, wherever a history redescribes the past in the historian's categories — which is to say, across a large share of its claims — it misdescribes the past.
(7) The standards by which evidence is weighed and explanations judged adequate are likewise internal to a historically situated discipline; they are the standards of our practice, not the past's own.
(8) A claim warranted only by standards internal to a practice is warranted relative to that practice; nothing about such warrant connects it to what actually happened, as opposed to what our practice is pleased to call knowledge.
(9) So no history represents the past as it was; at best it constructs a past answerable to present interests and judged by present standards.
(10) Two such constructions can differ only in which present interests they serve, and so the line between honest scholarship and tendentious myth is a political line, not an epistemic one.

I cannot accept (10), but I have not been able to say which step to give up. Premises (1) through (3) seem to me beyond dispute, and I would prefer to retain (7) as well; if I must be talked out of something, I am readiest to surrender (5) or (8). Please tell me which step you reject; explain why that step looked innocent in context; say what rejecting it costs, and in particular whether the cost is borne by the historian or by the actors she describes; and finally assess whether the argument can be repaired by weakening (9) into a conclusion I could honestly accept, or whether any such repair simply concedes, under a milder wording, everything the original conclusion denied.
