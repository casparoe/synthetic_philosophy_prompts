I am a novelist in the early stages of a book narrated in close third person from the perspective of a master luthier, and I have adopted a set of self-imposed craft rules, loosely inspired by the phenomenological observation that in absorbed work the instrument withdraws from awareness while the task fills it. Before I commit another fifty thousand words to these rules, I would like an audit of whether they cohere.

My working principles:

1. In scenes of skilled work that is going well, the prose tracks the materials and the task but never the instrument or the worker's body — the chisel, the hands, the stance go unmentioned, because that is how absorbed work is experienced from the inside.

2. A tool becomes narratively visible only at the moment it fails or goes missing, and each such failure must do double duty: it must surface something about the character's world — his dependencies, routines, obligations, the people who rely on him — that has not been stated before.

3. The narration never exceeds what the character could in principle notice at that moment; no free-floating authorial knowledge enters the prose.

4. The reader should finish the book with a vivid, almost tactile sense of the protagonist's mastery. I want reviewers to say you can feel his expertise on the page.

5. Emotional registers are rendered exclusively through disturbances in handling and attention: grief as clumsiness, infatuation as overcare, dread as a sudden, sourceless conspicuousness of ordinary objects.

6. No interior monologue and no "he felt" constructions anywhere in the book.

Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to be? Please rank the tensions by severity, say for each what would have to give, and identify the single revision that would clean up the most. I would rather you be specific about the mechanics of the conflicts than offer general encouragement.
