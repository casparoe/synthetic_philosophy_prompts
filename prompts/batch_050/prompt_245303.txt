I am grading problem sets for an undergraduate seminar in value theory. Below is one of the assigned questions, followed by a student's answer. I need a grade and justification.

**Question.** Some philosophers analyze value in terms of fitting attitudes: to call something good is to say that a favorable attitude toward it — admiration, desire, choice — is fitting or merited. A well-known objection holds that there can be reasons to favor an object that do not bear on whether the object is good. (i) State this objection and explain why it threatens the analysis, using an example. (ii) A colleague proposes the "internal endorsement test": a consideration is a reason of the right kind for an attitude only if someone who held the attitude for that reason would, from the standpoint of the attitude itself, be disposed to cite the consideration as what makes the attitude appropriate. Apply this test to two cases: (a) a powerful demon credibly threatens to punish you unless you admire a saucer of mud; (b) you learn that admiring the mud would cause you to notice its subtle textures, which you would in fact find genuinely admirable. Does the test classify the reason correctly in each case? (iii) If the test succeeds, does it amount to the same distinction as the more familiar distinction between object-given and state-given reasons? Explain.

**Student's answer.** (i) According to fitting-attitude analyses, for something to be good just is for it to be fitting to respond to it with a favorable attitude such as admiration or desire. The objection is that we can have reasons to favor an object that have nothing to do with whether it is good. If a demon credibly threatens to punish me unless I admire a saucer of mud, I have a strong reason to admire it, but the mud has not thereby become good. The analysis therefore overgenerates: it counts an attitude as fitting whenever there is sufficient reason to have it, including reasons that bear only on whether having the attitude would benefit me, rather than on whether the object merits it. (ii) (a) Someone who admired the mud in order to escape punishment would not, from the standpoint of admiration, cite the threat as what makes admiration appropriate; admiration presents its object as worthy, and the threat does not make the mud worthy. The test correctly excludes this reason. (b) Here the reason passes the test: the textures are genuinely admirable, so the agent would be citing a merit of the object, and a reason grounded in admirable features of the object is exactly what the test is meant to certify as right-kind. (iii) Yes, the test amounts to the same distinction in new vocabulary. State-given reasons are precisely those that no one could cite from within the attitude, and object-given reasons are precisely those one could so cite, so the two tests will always agree about which reasons are of the right kind.
