I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy, working on epistemic logic and its applications to debates about realism, and I keep finding myself unsatisfied with the literature surrounding Fitch's paradox of knowability. For context: from the apparently modest knowability principle -- that every truth is knowable in principle -- a few uncontroversial modal and epistemic principles yield, by a short derivation, the absurd conclusion that every truth is actually known. The responses are now a small industry: restrictionists propose weakening the principle to some privileged class of truths (basic truths, or truths whose knownness is consistent, as in Tennant's Cartesian restriction); others stratify knowledge operators or adopt type-theoretic hierarchies; some abandon classical logic in favor of paraconsistent or intuitionistic frameworks; Edgington-style replies distinguish knowing a truth from knowing it under the actual circumstances; and Williamson's structural critique suggests that any principled restriction either fails to block a version of the derivation or guts the anti-realist motivation for the principle in the first place. Six decades after Fitch published the result (crediting it to an anonymous referee), the theorem itself is entirely uncontroversial, the counterproposals are formally well understood, and yet there is no sign of convergence -- if anything, the positions have calcified.

My working hypothesis, formed after supervising an undergraduate seminar on this material, is that the debate is stalled because the parties agree on everything formal and disagree about something pre-formal: what the knowability principle was ever supposed to express. For Dummett-style anti-realists it is a thesis about the nature of truth, tied to the manifestability of meaning; for others it is a constraint on a legitimate notion of truth rather than a definition of it; for still others it was only ever a rough platitude about inquiry that was never meant to bear modal weight. If that is right, then three different questions -- about theories of meaning, about the concept of truth, and about idealizations of epistemic agents -- are being argued under a single heading, and no amount of logical work will move anyone. But I am not confident in this diagnosis. An alternative is that the real fault line is a hidden ambiguity in the modality: whether the possibility in knowable is metaphysical or epistemic, and whether the idealized knower in the antecedent is allowed to occupy a different evidential situation than we do. On that reading the dispute is substantive after all, and a clarification of the operator semantics might genuinely resolve it.

What I am asking for is a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, grounded in what the main positions actually say rather than a survey of them. Is the impasse best explained as verbal (parties using the knowability principle to express different claims), as substantive (parties disagreeing about which modal or epistemic principles are valid), or as a case where both sides share formal commitments but diverge on starting intuitions about truth and idealization that neither can argue for from within the debate? And given your diagnosis: what, concretely, would count as progress here -- a sharper semantic framework, an agreed-upon taxonomy of idealizations, a formal impossibility result about restrictions, or simply an explicit split of the paradox into separate problems? If you think no progress should be expected, say why, and what that implies for whether anti-realism about truth can be stated precisely at all.
