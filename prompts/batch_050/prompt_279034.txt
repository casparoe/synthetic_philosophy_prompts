I spent my career as an experimental scientist, and now, in retirement, I have been reading moral philosophy and finding that my intuitions about research ethics are less orderly than I had assumed. I would like help identifying the principle, if there is one, behind my verdicts on when it is permissible to enroll patients in a randomized trial. I know the standard answer invokes clinical equipoise -- genuine uncertainty within the expert medical community about which arm is superior -- but I am not sure that notion captures everything I think, and I would like my cases used as the test.

Here are my verdicts:

1. Two established drugs for the same condition, each with partisans among specialists, differing modestly in side-effect profiles and cost. A large randomized comparison: clearly permissible, indeed valuable.

2. A new drug shows such strong early results that, halfway through the trial, the accumulated data would convince most reasonable specialists that it is superior. Continuing to randomize new patients to the control arm: not permissible, whatever the original equipoise. The trial should stop or adapt.

3. A placebo-controlled trial of a new drug when an effective standard treatment already exists, so that control patients are denied treatment they would otherwise receive: not permissible. Use the standard treatment as the comparator instead.

4. A placebo-controlled trial for a condition with no effective treatment at all, so control patients lose nothing they could have had: permissible, even though half the participants receive nothing.

5. A trial in a low-income country comparing a cheap, locally affordable regimen against placebo, where the known-best treatment exists but costs more than anyone in the region could ever pay, and the trial results would determine whether the cheap regimen gets adopted there: I find myself thinking this is permissible, or close to it, because no participant is made worse off than they would be outside the trial. But I notice this sits badly with my verdict in case 3, and I cannot articulate why the two should differ.

6. A trial of a surgical technique against a sham-surgery control -- an incision and anesthesia but no operation -- where the surgeon-investigators are genuinely divided on whether the technique works: the sham control strikes me as impermissible even under genuine uncertainty, whereas a sham pill in case 4 strikes me as fine. This may be mere squeamishness, but I report it honestly.

7. A pediatric oncology trial where desperate parents beg for the new arm and view randomization as a coin flip with their child's life: permissible nonetheless, provided the uncertainty is real. The parents' framing does not settle the ethics.

Is there a single principle -- some refinement of equipoise, or some replacement for it -- that delivers these verdicts, or most of them? If a candidate principle requires giving up one of my verdicts, tell me which one and why that verdict is the expendable one. I would particularly like to know whether case 5 and case 3 can be jointly accommodated without special pleading, and whether case 6 forces a distinction between kinds of control rather than degrees of uncertainty. Finally, test the principle you land on against a case I have not listed: during a fast-moving epidemic, a ring-vaccination trial that randomizes clusters of contacts to immediate versus delayed vaccination, with no placebo at all, conducted while some experts already believe the vaccine works. Permissible or not, and does your principle's answer seem right?
