I've been thinking about whether habitat destruction might actually be good on balance for wild animals, on the grounds that most wild lives are short, brutal, and plausibly net-negative in welfare. I find I have fairly settled verdicts on a range of interventions, but I can't figure out what principle unifies them, and I suspect some of them can't be jointly saved. Here they are:

1. Draining a mosquito-rich wetland to build housing strikes me as, other things equal, a welfare improvement. The wetland generates enormous numbers of insect and amphibian lives, nearly all of which end within days or weeks, many by predation, starvation, or parasitism. Preventing those lives from existing seems to me to prevent mostly suffering.

2. Converting an old-growth forest into managed pasture strikes me as a welfare loss, even though it reduces the total animal population. The forest supports long-lived, behaviorally complex animals with what I take to be rich and plausibly net-positive lives; the pasture supports fewer of them and replaces the rest with livestock whose lives I judge net-negative.

3. Rewilding abandoned farmland by reintroducing large predators seems good for the ecosystem but bad for welfare, and on balance I lean against it when I think about the welfare side alone: the deer that would otherwise starve in winter now die by being chased down and eaten, which seems worse, and the predator's own life doesn't strike me as so wonderful that it compensates.

4. Winter feeding of deer that lets more of them survive to breed, resulting in a larger herd that starves worse the following year, is clearly bad. It manufactures additional net-negative lives at scale.

5. A cull that painlessly kills half an overpopulated deer herd before a harsh winter, sparing the survivors starvation, seems clearly good — even though, like draining the wetland, it prevents (painful) deaths rather than preventing lives.

6. Sterilizing the herd by contraceptive darting instead of culling it achieves the same population outcome with no deaths at all, yet I find myself only mildly preferring it to the cull, and I can't say why my preference isn't stronger, given that the cull involves killing animals that (I grant) have lives worth living right up to the moment of death.

A few things I want:

First, state the principle, or the smallest family of candidate principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. I note tensions I can't resolve: cases 1 and 4 seem to rest on a "fewer net-negative lives is better" logic that case 2 flatly rejects (there, fewer total lives is worse); and cases 5 and 6 seem to rest on "preventing bad deaths matters even when lives are worth living," which sits oddly with case 1, where preventing lives wholesale counts as a benefit precisely because of their bad deaths. Is there a coherent view — perhaps something with separate clauses for total expected welfare, the quality distribution of lives rather than their number, and the manner of death — that gets all six right? Or does any remotely simple principle have to sacrifice at least one verdict, and if so, which one is the weakest?

Second, whatever principle you settle on, tell me what it implies about two cases I haven't made up my mind about: (a) a gene drive that crashes a disease-vector mosquito population across a whole region, and (b) fencing off a predator-free island reserve where prey species live longer but reproduce until they hit the island's carrying capacity and begin to starve.

Third, be honest about whether the winning principle is itself defensible, or whether it looks like a patch stitched together to fit my intuitions. I'd rather learn that my verdicts are in genuine conflict than be handed a gerrymandered principle that flatters them all.
