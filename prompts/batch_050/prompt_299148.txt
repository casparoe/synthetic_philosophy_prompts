I am a PhD student in ancient philosophy working on Aristotle and the moral grammar of markets, and I keep running into the same problem when I try to explain Aristotle on money to colleagues in political economy: they either reduce him to a crude anti-commercial moralist or treat him as an early theorist of neutral exchange. I would like you to compare Aristotle’s distinction between household management and unlimited acquisition, including use-value versus exchange-value, money as a conventional measure that makes goods commensurable, the natural limit set by need and the good life, and his suspicion of usury, with a contemporary ideal type I will call platform-mediated reciprocity. Define the latter as follows: a digital platform does not issue ordinary transferable money, but allocates access through bounded reputation credits that decay unless renewed by contribution, cannot be sold directly, can be pooled only within households or associations, and are adjusted by an algorithm that claims to track contribution, need, and social trust rather than scarcity prices. Imagine that participants can trade labor, care, tools, housing time, and computing services, while the platform forbids lending credits at interest but permits subscription fees, priority queues, and insurance pools.

Do not give a generic ancient-versus-modern contrast. Instead, test whether platform-mediated reciprocity would count for Aristotle as a purified form of natural exchange because it restores limits, suppresses usury, and subordinates acquisition to use, or whether it merely relocates unlimited chrematistics into the architecture itself by making reputation indefinitely accumulable, attention infinitely scarce, and the platform the new monopolist of commensuration. Pay special attention to three pressure points: first, whether non-transferable but algorithmically adjustable credits are closer to nomisma as convention or to a rationing device that abolishes exchange while retaining hierarchy; second, whether decay and pooling create an Aristotelian natural limit or only simulate sufficiency while intensifying competition for visibility; third, whether the ban on interest is morally decisive if priority access and insurance still let the already reputable convert standing into more standing. End with a defended verdict on which side of Aristotle’s boundary the system falls, the strongest Aristotelian objection to your verdict, the strongest modern objection that Aristotle’s framework is too household-centered to judge platforms, and what would have to be true of ends, limits, and commensurability for your verdict to change.
