I write alternate-history novels, and I’m stuck on a plotting problem that has turned philosophical. In my current draft, a fictional Baltic country ends up driving on the left because of a short-lived ferry contract in 1927: local carriage makers adapted to British boats, then road rules followed. I want the contract to be a genuine hinge: remove it, and the country plausibly drives on the right decades later. But a freind of mine who reads a lot of history says this kind of claim is always illegitimate. His argument is:

(1) To say that event E locked in outcome O, one must compare the actual world with a counterfactual world in which E does not occur.  
(2) There is no coherent world where E is simply removed while everything else stays fixed, since removing E requires changing either its causes, its immediate effects, or the laws connecting them.  
(3) So the counterfactual world always differs from ours in more ways than just lacking E.  
(4) If it differs in more ways than that, then any change in O could be due to those extra differences rather than to E itself.  
(5) Therefore no counterfactual comparison can establish that E made a difference to O.  
(6) Claims about historical lock-in or path dependence depend on exactly this kind of event-level difference-making.  
(7) So there is no such thing as historical lock-in; historians and novelists merely pick one event out of a seamless causal web and call it a turning point afterward.

The conclusion seems to prove way too much. If it works here, it would apparently also show that the spark didn’t cause the fire, since removing the spark while holding oxygen, fuel, temperature, and every nearby molecule fixed is also physically impossible. Still, I can’t tell which numbered step first goes bad. Please identify the step you would reject, explain why it initially looked reasonable, and compare the costs of the main alternatives: denying that counterfactuals require holding everything else fixed, treating causes as interventions rather than magically deleted events, or defining path dependence in terms of sensitivity across a whole family of nearby histories instead of attributing everything to one hinge event. I’m especially interested in whether I can keep the novelist’s idea of a real turning point without pretending that history contains perfectly isolated variables.
