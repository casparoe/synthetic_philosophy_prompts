I'm a senior taking a philosophy elective, and for my final paper I want to defend the claim that patriotism can be a virtue. A classmate circulated an argument in our group chat that is meant to show patriotism is a vice, and I can't find the exit. Worse, I suspect that both his argument and my obvious reply to it prove too much. Here is his argument:

(1) To be patriotic is to be disposed to give greater weight to the interests of one's fellow citizens than to the interests of foreigners, simply in virtue of their being one's fellow citizens.
(2) Giving greater weight to one person's interests over another's is justified only when there is some morally relevant difference between them; where no such difference exists, unequal weighting is a form of injustice.
(3) Sharing a citizenship — having been born inside the same borders, or under the same flag — is not a morally relevant difference between people; from the moral point of view it is as arbitrary as hair color or date of birth.
(4) Therefore the patriot is disposed to systematically weight some people's interests above others' without any morally relevant difference, which by (2) is a disposition to injustice.
(5) A settled character trait that disposes its bearer toward injustice is a vice, not a virtue.
(6) Therefore patriotism is a vice.

My difficulty is this. The natural response is to reject (3): shared membership in a community, shared institutions, and a shared history are a genuine morally relevant difference, not like hair color at all. But once I say that membership in a group can ground greater weight for that group's members, I seem forced to say the same about partiality toward one's family and friends — which I am happy to accept — and also about partiality toward one's race or one's coreligionists, which I very much do not want to accept. So it looks like either my classmate's argument proves too much, or my reply to it does, and I can't tell which.

Please tell me which premise or step you would reject, and reconstruct why that step seemed plausible in the first place. Then price the rejection: what does it cost me, and in particular does it preserve the intuitive difference between partiality toward family (permissible, even admirable) and partiality toward race (not)? Finally, I'd like to know whether there is a version of patriotism weaker than the one defined in (1) — pride in one's country, say, rather than weighting of interests — that survives his argument intact, or whether retreating to that version concedes that real patriotism, the kind people actually admire, is the vice he says it is.
