I am a journalist covering science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I host a monthly live event for subscribers -- part seminar, part game night -- at which a concept from philosophy or the sciences is made vivid through structured play. The next session is on causation by omission: the idea that not doing something (failing to water the plant, neglecting to sound the alarm, abstaining from a vote) can be as much a cause of an outcome as any positive act, and that our judgments about which omissions "count" as causes seem to track expectations, roles, and norms rather than anything physical.

Design the session. The room holds about 24 attendees of mixed backgrounds, seated at tables of six, and I have 75 minutes total. I want a runnable game or staged simulation -- rules, materials, a scoring scheme that doesn't involve real money -- in which participants themselves produce the phenomenon before anyone names it. Specifically, I want the mechanics arranged so that (a) at least one outcome at each table is brought about purely by someone's inaction, not by anything anyone did, and (b) when tables later vote on what "caused" that outcome, their verdicts converge on the omission of whichever player had been assigned a special role, even though other players' physically identical inaction gets excused. Ideally the session includes a second round in which the rule sheet quietly redistributes who is responsible for what, so that participants watch their own causal verdicts shift with the norms rather than with the events.

Then write the debrief I would lead: map specific moments of the game onto the philosophical territory -- the distinction between causes and background conditions, the way causal selection seems sensitive to what is normal or expected, and the tension between a metaphysical question (can an absence really do causal work?) and a psychological one (why do we single out some absences and not others?). Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this -- rules participants will misread, ways the scoring might fail to produce the intended verdict split, a table that refuses to blame anyone -- and how to handle each. Finally, be candid about which feature of the real-world problem the game cannot reproduce (I suspect it has something to do with the stakes being fictional and the duties being invented on the spot) and what I, as facilitator, must supply in words to bridge that gap.
