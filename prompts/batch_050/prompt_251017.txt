So I'm a novelist, midway through a draft with a first-person narrator whose self-knowledge is supposed to be patchy in very specific ways, and I've realized my instincts about which of her lines "ring true" amount to some kind of buried theory of introspection. I want to dig the theory out. Here are my gut verdicts, some from the book and some from life:

- A character says her hands are shaking and she's dizzy: I basically trust this. You can be wrong that it's a heart attack, but not really wrong that it feels like something.
- A character gives polished, thoughtful reasons for why she married her husband, fifteen years on: I don't trust it. It sounds rehearsed, like a story she's told at parties.
- That famous kind of experiment where someone picks between two photos or two jams, the swap is rigged, and they happily explain a preference for the thing they didn't choose: I fully believe people do this, and that they're sincere while doing it. The explanation is invented and the person doesn't know it's invented.
- A character says "I'm not angry" while very pointedly not slamming the cupboards: I trust the cupboards.
- A character admits, in a low voice, that she donated to the fundraiser mostly because the guy running it was attractive: I trust this one completely, even though it's exactly the same kind of retrospective motive-report as the marriage one. That pair bugs me.
- A grieving character says "I honestly don't know what I'm feeling right now": somehow this reads as the most trustworthy line in the whole book, which is weird, because she's reporting that her introspection is failing.
- Someone tells me their dream, I believe them; they tell me what the dream means about their relationship, I don't.

So: is there one principle hiding under all of these, or two or three small ones? State it precisely enough that I could argue with it. Tell me which case forces each clause of it. Tell me which of my verdicts it can't save and whether I should drop that verdict or complicate the principle. And then run it on two cases I didn't give you: a kid explaining why he hit his brother, and someone who says "I knew all along that the marriage would fail."

Two favors on the epistemics: please mark clearly what's actually settled in the psychology here (I gather the confabulation stuff is on pretty solid ground) versus what's genuinely contested among people who work on this (like how far the confabulation results generalize to ordinary self-knowledge), and don't present a contested reading as the consensus view. I'll take your best guess either way, I just want to know which kind of thing I'm being handed.

Format-wise, can you do it in this order, roughly these lengths: "The Principle" (~150 words, the principle stated cold, no hedging), "Clause by Clause" (~250 words, which of my cases each piece is doing work for), "The Verdict You Can't Keep" (~100 words), "Two New Cases" (~100 words, the kid and the knew-it-all-along), and "Solid vs. Shaky Ground" (~100 words, the settled/contested split). American English, please. Casual is fine, I'm allergic to seminar voice. Oh and if it matters: the narrator's blind spots in my draft currently lean way too hard on the "unflattering = true" instinct, so if that one doesn't survive your analysis, tell me gently but do tell me.
