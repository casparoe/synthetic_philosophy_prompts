I'm TAing a first-year intro ethics course and the lecturer has asked me to run a 90 minute discussion section on the "equality of what" debate — welfare vs resources vs capabilities. The students have realistically read nothing, and most of them will walk in thinking equality of income is either obviously right or obviously the only coherent option on the table. Give me a teaching plan, not the lecture itself:

- The order in which to introduce the three currencies, and why that order — which distinctions have to be in place before the next currency even makes sense as a rival rather than just a rewording. I'm pretty sure you can't start with capabilities, but convince me or correct me.
- The one running example the whole section should be built on. I've been thinking of three people given the same cash transfer where one has a chronic illness that eats half of it in treatment costs, one is miserable no matter what they get, and one is fine. Tell me whether this case is good enough and what it needs to have and lack to carry the whole debate — e.g. does it matter whether the treatment fully restores the ill person's functioning or only partly? Should the miserable person be miserable for sympathetic reasons or spoiled-brat reasons? I suspect the details do a lot of work and I want to get them right before class, not improvise.
- The misconceptions I should expect, in roughly the order they'll surface, and where in the sequence to head each one off. My predictions: "capabilities just means what you're good at", "resources = money so resourcism is just income equality with extra steps", "welfare = happiness so this is just utilitarianism", and the inevitable student who says "why not just give people what they prefer and be done with it".
- The tricky bit: at some point someone will ask why we compensate for disability but not for expensive tastes, and I want the sequence set up so that question lands as the crux of the whole debate rather than as a gotcha against one view. Where does it belong, and what has to be on the board by then?
- A check at the end that shows they've understood the question (equality of *what*?) rather than just picked a favorite answer — probably a new case they have to analyze cold, so tell me what a good vs. bad analysis of it looks like.
- What to deliberately leave out at this level and say so out loud (primary goods? Dworkin's auction? the whole metric vs. currency technicality?), so the section feels honest rather than dumbed down.

Feel free to restructure my example if there's a better one, and flag anything in my plan where the order I have in mind would backfire.
