I teach an undergraduate seminar on classical American pragmatism -- 18 students, one 80-minute session, meeting the week we read Peirce's essay The Fixation of Belief. Students will have done the reading, but I don't want a lecture and I don't want them told, until the debrief, that the activity enacts the essay. Design a structured prediction game that makes them feel Peirce's central claim from the inside: that the three rivals to the method of science -- tenacity, authority, and the a priori method -- fail not because they land on false beliefs but because they cannot remain stable inside a community under the recurring irritation of doubt.

Here is the spine I have in mind; build it out properly. Divide the class into four groups of four or five, each secretly assigned a revision rule under a flavor name that doesn't give the game away (something like the Stalwarts, the Chancellors, the Aesthetes, the Testers). All groups face the same repeated prediction task driven by a hidden rule you specify (a card-draw or token task in the family of Eleusis or Zendo is fine, but give me a concrete rule, not a genre). Each round, groups publicly commit a prediction and score points: reward correct predictions, penalize wrong ones, no money involved. The revision rules should be: the Stalwarts lock in one conjecture by the end of round three and may never change it or discuss alternatives; the Chancellors' conjecture is dictated by a designated leader, and the group can replace the leader only by unanimous petition; the Aesthetes may revise, but every revision must be justified in purely aesthetic terms (simplicity, symmetry, elegance) and may never cite prediction error -- this should model Peirce's comparison of the a priori method to the development of taste; the Testers may revise freely but must state each new conjecture before seeing the next outcome.

Two twists I want built in, since this is where the force comes from. First, inject a small fixed rate of noise into the outcomes so no group ever achieves certainty and doubt keeps being produced. Second, at the midpoint, silently change the hidden rule -- no announcement, and the facilitator must not confirm it even when asked -- and interleave two or three "marketplace" rounds where groups see one another's running scores. The design goal is that the locked and led groups visibly suffer, that social pressure does its work on them even though their rules forbid responding to it, and that the Aesthetes drift while the Testers recover.

Give me: complete rules, a materials list, the payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline, the facilitator's exact lines for the moments participants will probe (especially "did the rule change?"), and a debrief script that maps specific in-game moments onto the essay's doubt-belief cycle and its argument that the first three methods are each undermined by something social rather than something evidential -- participants should narrate what happened to them before anyone names the methods. Then: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (noise level, students breaking character, the aesthetics justification becoming a loophole, the petition mechanic) and how to handle each. Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce -- my list includes genuine as opposed to point-loss doubt, the indefinite long run of inquiry across generations, and a reality independent of any inquirer, since my hidden rule is arbitrary and known to me -- and tell me how to say so in the debrief without deflating what worked.

Close with a calibration note: which single design decision above you are least confident in, which part of the mapping from the essay onto game rules a careful reader of Peirce would most plausibly contest, and what -- a textual consideration, a trial-run outcome, or an objection -- would lead you to change the core mechanic rather than the trimmings.
