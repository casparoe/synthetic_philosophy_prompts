"Objective Bayesianism says rational belief is fixed by evidence alone, so two agents with identical evidence must assign identical credences. The hard part is saying where priors come from without smuggling in personal opinion. Jaynes's maximum entropy principle answers this: among all distributions satisfying the constraints your evidence imposes (like a known expectation value), choose the one that maximizes Shannon entropy, -sum p log p. With no constraints beyond normalization, this yields the uniform distribution, recovering Laplace's principle of indifference as a special case. The argument for it is that any lower-entropy distribution would implicitly assume information you dont actually have, so maxent is the uniquely least-committal choice. This isn't just a slogan: Cox's theorem shows that maximizing entropy follows from elementary consistency desiderata for rational inference, so any other rule of picking priors would be inconsistent somewhere. Maxent also earns its keep empirically, since imposing only a mean energy constraint reproduces the Boltzmann distribution of statistical mechanics, which is strong evidence the principle latches onto something objective.

As for problems, the classic one is Bertrand-style paradoxes, like the wine/water puzzle: you know a mixture is mostly water but nothing else, and you get different answers depending on whether you distribute probabilities uniformly over the water-to-wine ratio or over its cube, say. But this only sinks the old principle of indifference, not maximum entropy, because Shannon entropy is invariant under reparametrization: the maxent distribution over a quantity determines the maxent distribution over any smooth function of it. So the paradox is a problem for Laplace, not Jaynes. Conclusion: the main objection misfires, and objective Bayesianism succeeds in delivering rational priors, at least wherever the constraints are well-defined."
