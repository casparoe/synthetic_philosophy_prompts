Having retired from a career in experimental physics, I have spent the past year auditing a university seminar on the philosophy of cognitive science, and the instructor has asked me to help mark the midterm examination. I would like a second opinion on one script before I commit to a mark. Below is the exam question, followed by the candidate's answer. Exam question: Compare prototype theory and exemplar theory as accounts of what concepts are and of how categorization works. For each, state what the representation of a category consists in and how it explains typicality effects. Then assess whether the theory theory should be regarded as a straightforward rival to both, or as answering a somewhat different question.

Candidate's answer: Prototype theory, developed largely on the basis of Eleanor Rosch's experiments in the 1970s, holds that a concept is a summary representation listing the features that category members tend to possess, weighted by how characteristic they are. Categorization proceeds by comparing an item to the prototype and computing overall similarity, which naturally explains typicality effects: a robin is classified as a bird faster than a penguin because it shares more features with the avian prototype. Exemplar theory agrees that categorization is similarity-based but denies that the mind abstracts any summary representation. Instead, a concept is constituted by the single most representative previously encountered instance of the category — the best example — and new items are categorized by their similarity to it. Typicality effects follow because atypical items resemble the stored best example less closely than typical ones. A known weakness of prototype theory is its difficulty with conceptual combination: a pet fish is neither a typical pet nor a typical fish, yet we grasp the compound readily. The theory theory, on this basis, is best regarded as a direct rival to both similarity-based accounts. It holds that a concept is a miniature scientific theory of its category, and it was developed primarily to explain typicality effects more successfully than similarity comparisons can. Where prototype and exemplar theories predict typicality from resemblance alone, the theory theory predicts it from the item's fit with the underlying theory, and this is the phenomenon it was chiefly designed to accommodate.
