In a seminar on moral psychology we spent a session on whether self-deception is even possible. The standard worry was raised: deceiving someone seems to require getting them to believe what you yourself know to be false, so deceiving yourself would require believing a proposition and its negation at the same time, which looks incoherent. I defended that line. A classmate replied by invoking the old injunction "know thyself": the maxim only makes sense as advice, he said, if self-knowledge is something we lack by default and must work to attain — which already presupposes that something like self-deception is real, and that it can be escaped. Since then I have been reading Joseph Butler's Sermon X, "Upon Self-Deceit," in the Fifteen Sermons Preached at the Rolls Chapel (1726), where he writes:

"This likewise was the reason why that precept, 'Know thyself,' was so frequently inculcated by the philosophers of old. For if it were not for that partial and fond regard to ourselves, it would certainly be no great difficulty to know our own character, what passes within the bent and bias of our mind; much less would there be any difficulty in judging rightly of our own actions."

What I want is a disambiguation exercise, not an exegesis. First, lay out as a numbered menu the distinct precise claims "know thyself" could be making: for instance, a descriptive-psychological thesis that most people are ignorant of their own character; a normative thesis that one has a duty to inquire into oneself; a methodological thesis that self-knowledge is achievable through some specific discipline; a pessimistic thesis that it is an ideal approachable but never fully reachable; and any further readings you can distinguish. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, or actually false, and which reading people who repeat the slogan most likely intend.

Second, apply the menu to my seminar dispute. Which reading does my classmate's rejoinder need in order to count as a reply rather than a change of subject? Does any plausible reading of the slogan actually bear on the incoherence argument, or does the maxim at most presuppose self-ignorance — and is self-ignorance the same phenomenon as self-deception, or something weaker that my argument never denied?

Third, turn to the passage. Butler conspicuously denies that the mind is opaque to itself: absent "partial and fond regard," he says, knowing our own character would be "no great difficulty." Elsewhere in the sermon he describes the self-deceived not as people who positively believe falsehoods about themselves but as people who never in earnest apply to themselves the judgments they pass readily on others. Does that diagnosis dissolve the static paradox — self-deception as a motivated failure of attention or application rather than as holding contradictory beliefs — or does it merely relocate it, since declining to apply what one knows may itself require knowing what one is declining to apply? And if Butler is right that partiality is the whole obstacle, does "know thyself" collapse into "be impartial toward yourself," and is that still usable advice to someone whose partiality is precisely what prevents them from recognizing it?

Finally, say which reading from your menu Butler himself is committed to, and whether, on his picture, the maxim functions as advice, as warning, or as diagnosis — and which of those it would have to be for my classmate to have won the exchange.
