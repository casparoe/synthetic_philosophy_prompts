You are a professional speechwriter with a background in philosophy who has spent years writing for occasions where the audience is divided against itself. I'd like you to take my commission seriously, think carefully before drafting, and then write me a speech.

I'm a senior in high school and the student representative on the committee that drafted my school's new policy on AI use. Next month the school unveils the policy at an all-school assembly, and I've been asked to give the opening speech: five to six minutes, roughly 700 words. The audience is about 800 students plus teachers, administrators, and some parents, and they arrive with opposed assumptions. Many of the adults believe using AI on schoolwork is simply cheating. Many of the students believe any restriction is nostalgia from people who grew up with calculators, spell-check, and Google and just don't want to admit it.

Here's the complication I can't dodge: I use AI constantly, including to prepare this speech, and everyone in the room knows it. So I can't deliver a warning from above, and I also can't give a defense of doing whatever is convenient. I want the speech to do something harder, and I want it built around a distinction of my own that I'd like you to develop and put to work. Call the two kinds of tool use bridge use and crutch use. A crutch use is one where the tool does the thing instead of you, and nothing accumulates in you: if the tool vanished tomorrow, you'd be left with nothing. A bridge use is one where the tool carries you toward an ability you couldn't reach unaided, and you could, eventually, cross without it. Two honest complications the speech must face rather than hide: first, the same app can be a bridge for one student and a crutch for another, depending entirely on what the student does after the answer appears; second, some crutches are perfectly fine -- no one mourns that we can't multiply like a calculator -- so the real question the speech has to take a stand on is which abilities are ones a person should actually have, and why those.

Apply the distinction concretely to three cases everyone in the room will recognize: having AI draft your essays, having it solve your math problems, and using it as an always-available study partner that quizzes you and explains things. Let what the speech endorses and criticizes follow from the distinction, not sit decoratively beside it. Engage the strongest objection head-on: that every generation panics about a new tool, from writing itself onward, and turns out fine. Don't just wave it away -- say why things might genuinely be different this time, or why they might not be, depending on which capacities are at stake. Avoid dystopian clichés, avoid "as a member of Gen Z," and don't end with a rule; end with a question each listener can apply to themselves on their own homework that night.

Before writing, brainstorm briefly: list the openings, structures, and central images you considered, with one sentence each on why you rejected or kept them. Then write the full speech. After the speech, add a short note flagging the single claim in it you judge most philosophically vulnerable, and how you would defend it if a skeptical teacher pressed it during questions.
