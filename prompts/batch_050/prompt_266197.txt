I'm a junior in high school taking an honors biology elective, and we've just finished a unit on the levels (or "units") of selection debate. On the last day before break, our teacher ran an exercise she called "selection or just bookkeeping?": she described seven scenarios and told us whether, in her view, genuine selection was operating at the level in question. Her verdicts, as I recorded them, were these. (1) A segregation-distorter gene in mice that gets itself into nearly all sperm and spreads through the population even though it lowers the survival of the mice carrying it: real selection, at the gene level. (2) Sterile worker castes in honeybees: kin selection at the gene level, not colony-level selection -- she said the colony-level story adds nothing once relatedness is accounted for. (3) Alarm calls in ground squirrels: kin selection again, not group selection. (4) Beaver dams: selection on beavers; the dam is an effect of the beaver's traits and is not itself a unit of selection. (5) The evolutionary origin of multicellularity: here selection genuinely shifted up to the level of the organism, she said, once competition among the cells was suppressed. (6) Cancer: real selection at the level of the cell, working against the organism. (7) The faster extinction of asexual lineages, sometimes called species selection: not genuine selection, in her view, but mere differential persistence, since the pattern is fully explained by properties of the individual organisms.

For our final paper, she has promised to give us a new scenario and ask us to apply her framework, and I want to anticipate her answer. Here's my guess at the kind of case she'll use, so suppose it's this: a colonial invertebrate in which new colonies are founded by budding off from parent colonies; the size a colony reaches is fairly heritable from parent colony to daughter colony; larger colonies survive storms and predators much better; every member of a colony benefits equally from the colony's survival; the colony-level trait costs no individual member anything, so there are no cheaters, no within-colony fitness differences, and no conflict between what's good for a member and what's good for the colony; and all members of a colony happen to be close kin. Is there genuine colony-level selection here?

I realize I care less about the yes-or-no answer than about the rule she is running, because as far as I can tell, more than one rule fits all seven of her verdicts. One candidate: a level counts as a unit of selection whenever there is heritable variation in fitness among entities at that level that does explanatory work beyond fitness differences at lower levels. Another candidate: a level counts only when selection there operates against resistance from a lower level, or -- as with multicellularity -- has historically suppressed it, so that levels of selection are "real" only where there is or was cross-level conflict. A third candidate, strict gene's-eye reductionism, seems to be ruled out by verdicts (5) and (6), but correct me if I'm wrong. Please tell me: which of the viable rules is she most plausibly running, what does each of them predict about the colonial invertebrate case, and what is your best guess for her actual verdict, with reasons? Finally, if her seven verdicts genuinely underdetermine the answer, describe a further scenario -- real or hypothetical -- on which the candidate rules would come apart, so that I could use it to pin down which framework she actually holds.
