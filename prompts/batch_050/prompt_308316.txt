I cover perception research and the philosophy of mind for a general-interest magazine, and I'm applying for a yearlong residential fellowship to develop a magazine-essay idea into a short book. The application requires a two-page proposal, and I want your help drafting it. The project starts from a text most of my readers have met only in paraphrase: the version of Molyneux's question that Locke printed in the second edition of An Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1694), Book II, Chapter IX, §8, quoting a letter from his friend William Molyneux:

"Suppose a Man born blind, and now adult, and taught by his touch to distinguish between a Cube, and a Sphere of the same metal, and nighly of the same bigness, so as to tell, when he felt one and t'other; which is the Cube, which the Sphere. Suppose then the Cube and Sphere placed on a Table, and the Blind Man to be made to see. Quaere, Whether by his sight, before he touch'd them, he could now distinguish, and tell, which is the Globe, which the Cube."

Locke then adds his own endorsement, which is quoted far less often than the question itself:

"I agree with this thinking Gent. whom I am proud to call my Friend, in his answer to this his problem; and am of opinion, that the Blind Man, at first sight, would not be able with certainty to say, which was the Globe, which the Cube, whilst he only saw them: though he could unerringly name them by his touch, and certainly distinguish them by the difference of their Figures felt."

My half-formed idea, which I want the proposal to sharpen: the standard journalistic line on this problem — a line I have written myself — is that science has finally answered Molyneux. In a much-discussed 2011 study from an Indian program that treats congenitally blind children and adolescents, the newly sighted could not immediately match shapes they knew by touch to shapes they saw, but learned to do so within days: no, but quickly. Reading Locke's actual sentences, though, I've come to think this verdict answers a different question from any of the ones on the page. Molyneux asked whether the man could "distinguish, and tell"; Locke, in agreeing with him, quietly re-asked the question as whether he could "with certainty ... say." Those are not obviously the same success condition, and neither is obviously the one experimenters test, which is above-chance forced-choice matching measured days after surgery. My suspicion is that the problem's whole three-hundred-year career — Molyneux's letters of 1688 and 1693, Locke's gloss, Leibniz's rationalist "yes" (which converts the task into explicit reasoning, on the condition that the man be told the shapes before him are the very ones he knew by touch), Berkeley's more radical "no" (sight and touch share no idea of shape at all), the celebrated 1728 case report of a boy couched for cataracts, and the modern experiments — is a history of silent substitutions, in which each party answers a repaired version of the question while presenting the repair as the original. The book would reconstruct which substitutions were made, which were philosophically innocent and which changed what was at stake: whether spatial ideas are amodal or sense-specific, and what empiricism about perception was ever committed to in the first place.

Draft the proposal as it should actually appear in the application. Give a precise statement of the book's central question — I want it to have a determinate answer, along the lines of: which of the several questions contained in §8 do the modern experiments genuinely bear on, and does answering that question settle the dispute Locke and Leibniz took themselves to be having? Explain why both the "science has answered it" consensus and the pure thought-experiment tradition leave this open. Lay out the method and say why it is the right one for a journalist rather than a philosopher: close reading of the canonical texts, the early surgical case reports, and the experimental papers, combined with interviews with working vision scientists, which is my comparative advantage. Provide a plausible month-by-month plan for the fellowship year, a description of what the finished manuscript argues in the best case, and a candid account of what would count as failure — for instance, if the textual differences turn out to make no philosophical difference, or if the project collapses into a survey of everyone who has ever discussed the problem. End with an honest scope assessment. Don't include a bibliography or any citations; I'll add those myself.
