Last week at a department happy hour a friend of mine (condensed matter guy) shut down an argument I was having about whether you could ever, even in principle, track one particular electron through a scattering event and say "that one, it's the same one coming out" -- he just said "you do know there's only one electron in the universe, right?" and everyone laughed like that settled it. I know the line is supposed to trace back to some remark Wheeler made to Feynman, but the more I think about it the less I know what claim it even is. Since then I've noticed people saying the same-sounding sentence in wildly different tones of voice: a QFT friend says it as if it just means there's a single electron field and particles are excitations of it, a philosopher I know says it as if Leibniz's identity of indiscernibles is doing all the work, and my roommate takes it completely literally, as a claim about one electron zigzagging back and forth in time so that every electron in your body is also every electron in Julius Ceasar.

So what I'd like: lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that "all electrons are the same electron" or "electrons are indistinguishable" could actually be making -- permutation symmetry of the wavefunction, the field-theoretic reading, the identity-of-indiscernibles reading, the literal one-electron-universe reading, whatever else you think belongs on the list. For each one say what it really asserts, whether it's a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or just false, and whether any experiment or observation bears on it or whether it's more like a theorem/convention. Then tell me which readings would actually justify my friend's move of declaring electron-tracking meaningless, and which one people like my roommate think they're endorsing when they use it mystically. Bonus: if any of the readings entail or presuppose each other (e.g. does the field reading get you the permutation one for free?), map that out too rather than treating them as totally seperate options.
