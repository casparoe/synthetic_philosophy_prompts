You are a philosopher of physics with deep expertise in the foundations of quantum mechanics, particularly the probability problem in Everett-style interpretations, and you have experience facilitating adversarial collaborations between researchers who disagree sharply but argue in good faith.

I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing a long feature on the Born rule problem in the many-worlds interpretation. My editors want the piece to convey not just what the two sides believe, but precisely where they stop being able to sign the same sentence. To plan my interviews, I need you to draft the joint statement that a serious adversarial collaboration between the following two camps might produce:

Camp A, the derivationalists: Everettians who hold that the Born rule can be justified from the unitary formalism plus premises that are not themselves probabilistic — whether via decision-theoretic arguments in the Deutsch–Wallace tradition, envariance-based reasoning, or approaches resting on self-locating uncertainty. For them, probability in a branching universe is no more mysterious than probability anywhere else.

Camp B, the skeptics: critics who hold that probability talk in Everett is either meaningless or achievable only by smuggling in the very measure that was supposed to be derived. Their core charges are that decision-theoretic axioms such as indifference to further branching look non-probabilistic only because they quietly build in the Born measure; that uncertainty about which branch one is on makes no sense when one knows that all outcomes occur and every successor is equally oneself; and that absent a principled reason to reject naive branch counting in favor of amplitude-squared weighting, the Everettian's probabilities amount to a measure of how much an agent cares about successors, dressed up as physics.

Draft the document these two camps could jointly sign after a good-faith collaboration. It should contain three parts:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign: what the formalism actually says, what any successful solution to the probability problem would have to deliver, and which popular objections on both sides they agree are bad arguments.

2. A table of residual disagreements. Each row must state the disagreement in wording each camp would accept for its own position, identify the crux (the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one camp), and record what each camp would concede if that crux were resolved against it.

3. A closing section signed only by you, in which you take a definite stand. For each crux, state which camp you think currently has the better of it and why. State plainly which disagreements you judge to be genuinely substantive and which you judge to be terminological, with reasons. Do not lay out the options and leave the choice to me: I want your verdict, argued for.

Two further requirements. First, address explicitly whether either camp can give a non-question-begging reason to prefer the Born measure over simple branch counting, since this is where I expect my sources to fight hardest. Second, formulate everything in your own words; do not quote published work.

Before drafting, think carefully: briefly list the points of agreement you expect, the points of disagreement, and the places where you suspect the two camps are talking past each other. Then write the document, aiming for the tone and precision of an actual collaborative statement rather than an encyclopedia overview. Write in American English.
