I spent my career as an experimental physicist and only took up philosophy seriously after retiring, when I joined a reading group that recently worked through Epicurus's contention that death cannot be an evil for the one who dies, since where we are, death is not, and where death is, we are not. Our group spent an entire evening on the standard replies — the deprivationist view that death is bad because it deprives us of future goods, Nagel-style appeals to what a person would have had, Lucretius's symmetry argument comparing posthumous non-existence to prenatal non-existence, and the various attempts to bite the bullet and deny that death harms us. During the discussion I devised a variant case that left us stuck, and I would like your help with it.

The case is this: imagine a person placed in perfect cryonic suspension for three hundred years, with no brain activity whatsoever — functionally indistinguishable, during that interval, from a corpse — and then revived with memories and personality intact. During those three centuries she misses her grandchildren's entire lives, and by deprivationist lights she is "deprived" of goods in roughly the way a dead person is. Yet most of us intuit that her situation is far less bad than actual death, indeed that revival makes the whole episode benign. Now modify the case: suppose the revival procedure has only a fifty percent chance of success, known in advance. Intuitions in our group diverged wildly over whether the suspended-but-possibly-never-revived person is harmed during the interval, and over whether the harm, if any, depends on facts that obtain only after the interval ends.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher specializing in the metaphysics of death. First, brainstorm briefly the main candidate positions on what makes death bad (or not bad) for the one who dies — including any positions beyond the four I mentioned that you think deserve a hearing. Then produce a tiered ranking of these positions, ordered by how well each handles my cryonic cases: which positions deliver a principled verdict, which collapse into contradiction or counterintuitive commitments when faced with reversible or probabilistic non-existence, and which must be patched with ad hoc clauses. For each tier, explain in a few sentences why the positions land where they do, and conclude by telling me which single position you would advise a stubborn materialist like myself to adopt, and at what cost.
