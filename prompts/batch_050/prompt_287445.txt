I serve on my company's incident review board, the body that signs off on postmortems for serious outages and assigns the follow-up action items. We have a standing dispute I need to settle, and I want your help writing the memo that settles it.

The case: we had a three-hour payments outage last month. The timeline is uncontested. A routine deploy at 14:02 introduced a latent bug; a traffic spike at 14:37 triggered it; the automatic failover that would have masked the whole thing had been manually disabled three weeks earlier for a maintenance task and never re-enabled. The postmortem template has a single "root cause" field, and the committee is split into two camps about what belongs there.

Camp one says the deploy is the root cause, full stop: had that deploy not shipped, there is no outage, and that's what "cause" means. They point out that the disabled failover was the system state for three uneventful weeks, and calling an absence a cause has no principled stopping point — we also "caused" the outage by not buying a second failover, not load-shedding, and so on, so the field becomes a list of everything that could have been different. Camp two says the failover toggle is the root cause, because a root cause is supposed to be something we can grab and change. You cannot adopt a policy of never deploying bugs; you can adopt a policy of expiring manual overrides. On their view the deploy is just the occasion — bugs will always ship eventually — and the togglable thing is the only entry that generates a useful action item. Camp one's sharpest reply: by that logic the root cause of every incident is whatever is cheapest to fix, and the engineer who shipped the bug gets written out of the record while an SRE who forgot a checkbox owns it — which is neither accurate nor fair under our blameless-but-honest culture.

Draft a memo to the board recommending a policy for the root-cause field. It should give a bottom-line recommendation (one entry, several ranked entries, retire the field, or something else), state criteria an engineer can actually apply when filling it in rather than a slogan, explain what each camp's test gets right about causation and where it breaks on this timeline, and take seriously the strongest argument of whichever side loses. Around 800 words, in a tone a VP of Engineering would circulate.
