I'm writing the lit review section of a paper on axiomatic bargaining (it's overdue, don't ask) and I need to make sure I haven't missed any major criticism of the Kalai--Smorodinsky solution. My draft currently covers four objections:

First, once you leave the two-player convex case it only guarantees weak Pareto efficiency, so with three or more players it can land on a point that's weakly dominated. Second, it has that odd cross-player sensitivity to the utopia point: if player 1's best feasible outcome improves, player 2 can walk away with more even though nothing about player 2's own situation changed. Third, it fails independence of irrelevant alternatives, since deleting options nobody was going to pick can shift the outcome -- which is a bit awkward given that dissatisfaction with that axiom in Nash's setup was part of the motivation. Fourth, the equal-ratios-of-the-ideal-gain rule bakes in one particular fairness intuition without any argument for why proportions of utopian gains are the right currency rather than, say, equal absolute gains.

Here's the task: act as a cooperative game theorist who has spent years on bargaining theory, and give me one more objection or limitation that isn't on my list. It needs to be genuinely distinct. Then tell me what your objection shows that mine don't, and be honest about whether it actually lands or whether a Kalai--Smorodinsky defender has a decent comeback ready. If your best candidate turns out to be weak, say so rather than overselling it -- I'd rather know.
