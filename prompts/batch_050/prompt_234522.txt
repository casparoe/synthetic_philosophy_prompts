A friend of mine who fancies himself a bit of a Stoic keeps reaching for the Lucretian symmetry line whenever death comes up down the pub: being dead is just like the stretch of time before you were born, and since nobody lies awake regretting all those billions of years they missed out on, it's irrational to mind the billions you'll miss after you're gone. He wheeled it out again last week to settle an argument we're having about whether a shortened life is genuinely worse for the person whose life it is, or whether it only looks that way from the outside.

Audit the analogy for me, please. I want the correspondence spelt out properly rather than vibes: what plays the role of what, which features of pre-natal nonexistence are meant to carry over to post-mortem nonexistence, and where the mapping breaks. In particular, cover the bits my friend glosses over: whether death can deprive you of goods in a way your late birth never could (the thing about how you couldn't have been born much earlier and still been you, whereas you plainly could have died later); whether there's any principled reason we care about future nonexistence but not past nonexistence beyond a brute bias towards the future; and whether that bias is itself irrational or just a deep fact about how we're built — Parfit's thought experiments about preferring pain to be behind you, if that's doing any work here. Then give me a verdict on whether the conclusion my friend is after, i.e. that death is nothing to him and a shorter life isn't worse for him, actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the symmetry was carrying the whole arguement from the start.
