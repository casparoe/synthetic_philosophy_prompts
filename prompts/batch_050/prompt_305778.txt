I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on well-being (16 students, one 75-minute session), and this unit has become oddly personal for me. Over the summer I ran what I called a happiness project: I kept a log, rated my enjoyment of activities hourly, and deliberately scheduled the things I like most. It backfired in a very specific way. The moment I caught myself mid-concert or mid-dinner checking whether I was enjoying it as much as I'd planned, the enjoyment thinned out -- while on a few occasions I only realized afterward how absorbed and content I'd been. My students have read the standard discussion of the paradox of hedonism and dutifully agree it's plausible, but I can tell it lands as a cute puzzle rather than something with real force. I want them to feel it in the room before anyone names it: no lecture up front, and the phrase "paradox of hedonism" should not be uttered until the debrief.

Design me a runnable activity with two contrasting conditions so participants can compare, not just be told, the difference between pursuing an absorbing task for its own sake and pursuing it under a mandate to maximize and continuously self-report their enjoyment. I want the measurement itself to do philosophical work here: some version of live self-rating (they should be interrupted, or required to log, or score themselves in real time) versus purely retrospective report, since my sense is the paradox has (at least) two layers -- a moment-level one, where attending to your pleasure while having it degrades it, and a life-level one, where making happiness your dominant aim makes you worse at the pursuits that happiness consists in -- and I'd like the activity to target the first directly while setting up an honest discussion of whether a 75-minute game can say anything at all about the second. Give me concrete rules and roles, all materials (nothing requiring money or technology beyond paper and a timer), a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto the two layers above and onto the idea that a certain kind of self-monitoring is the mechanism of the interference. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first two times I run this (e.g., competitive students gaming the self-ratings, or the "absorbing task" condition being too easy to self-monitor anyway) and how to handle each.

At the end, include a structured summary in two parts. First, a table of the session with columns: phase, minutes, facilitator instructions verbatim, intended participant experience. Second, a JSON object with these fields: target_layer (which layer of the paradox the activity primarily demonstrates), secondary_layer_reach (an honest one-sentence verdict on whether the life-level layer is reachable), predictable_failures (array of objects with fields failure and mitigation), irreproducible_feature (what the activity structurally cannot reproduce and I must supply by testimony), and materials (array).
