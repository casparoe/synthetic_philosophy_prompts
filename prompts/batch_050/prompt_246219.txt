I'm much more fluent in game theory and mechanism design than in democratic theory, and I want to know how much of the boundary problem survives translation. Take the problem in its standard form: the demos cannot democratically settle its own membership, since any vote on who belongs presupposes an answer to who votes. Restate it in the vocabulary of games with endogenous player sets — who counts as a player when the rules of the game partly determine the player set? Then work through the three main proposed solutions one at a time (all-affected-interests, all-subjected-to-coercion, and the self-determination of an already-existing people), stating each as a rule for fixing the player set and reporting, for each: what translates cleanly, what must be distorted to fit (for instance, what coercion becomes once influence is continuous and diffuse rather than binary), and what has no expression at all. Close with a residue report: for whatever fails to translate, say whether the gap exposes a limitation of the game-theoretic frame — which takes players and utility functions as given — or a sign that the original claims were leaning on normative vocabulary that wasn't doing real work. I'm particularly interested in whether the circularity survives translation intact: does "the players cannot play a game to decide who the players are" have the same structure as the original problem, or is it a different problem wearing the same clothes?
