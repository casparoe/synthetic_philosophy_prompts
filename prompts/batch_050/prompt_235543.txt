I play a lot of one-shot games with strangers and keep catching myself using a pattern of reasoning I can't name. Example: in a symmetric prisoner's-dilemma-style game against an anonymous opponent I'll never meet, with no communication allowed, I find myself thinking -- whatever I end up choosing, the other player will probably choose the same, because they were selected by the same process that selected me and they're reasoning about the same payoff matrix I am. So I cooperate, not out of altruism and not because I think my choice causes theirs, but because our two choices seem to be outputs of one shared computation, and I'd rather that computation output cooperation-cooperation. The same feeling shows up outside games: I'm tempted to vote on the grounds that people similar to me are facing the same temptation not to bother; I return the shopping cart in an empty lot because "everyone like me" is deciding right now. I know this smells like a fallacy -- my defection doesn't causally produce theirs -- and I know it isn't just tit-for-tat, because there's no future round. Is there an established name and literature for this exact pattern, how does it differ from Kant-style universalization and from plain correlated behavior, and does it survive contact with standard decision theory or does it need a nonstandard one? If nothing established fits precisely, coin a term with a clean definition and a couple of boundary cases. Please answer in American English, and after your discussion give me a structured summary as a JSON object with these fields: "phenomenon_label" (the established name, or "none established" plus your coined term), "nearest_concepts" (an array of objects, each with "concept", "overlap", and "key_difference"), "verdict_on_rationality" (one of "defensible on standard theory", "requires nonstandard theory", "irrational", with a one-sentence justification), and "boundary_cases" (an array of short strings describing situations where the pattern clearly applies, clearly fails, or is genuinely ambiguous).
