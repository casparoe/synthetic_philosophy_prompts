Say Aristotle turns up today and is asked to advise a tenants’ cooperative. The co-op collects rent in an ordinary bank account, but its treasurer wants to move most of the reserve into a dollar-pegged stablecoin because transfers are quick and it earns a bit of yield. Members mainly need the reserve for repairs, legal fees and emergency accommodation; they are not trying to get rich from it. The coin is privately issued, backed by short-term government debt, and can temporarily trade below a dollar during a panic.

What would Aristotle advise, and why? I’m less interested in modern regulatory details than in how far his distinction between household management and unlimited money-making can be extended to this case. Please separate at least these questions: whether money earning a return is inherently objectionable; whether the problem changes when the yield comes from funding public debt rather than lending at interest to an individual; whether a stablecoin is money in Aristotle’s sense or merely a promise conventionally treated as money; and what he would say about exposing other people’s basic security to small financial risks for modest gain.

Don’t just say “he’d hate crypto”. Use his views about exchange, commensurability, use versus accumulation, and the proper limits of acquisition, while flagging any places where the modern financial system makes the extrapolation genuinely indeterminate.
