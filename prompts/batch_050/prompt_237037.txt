I teach a kind of quirky quantitative reasoning elective at a small liberal arts college, mixed crowd of econ, math, and philosophy majors, about 18 students, and I have one 75-minute session left at the end of the semester. I want to spend it on the ergodicity economics critique of expected value -- the idea that for multiplicative dynamics (like your actual wealth over time) the time average growth rate can diverge from the ensemble average, so maximizing expected value across parallel copies of yourself is a terrible guide for what to do with your one actual life. I've tried just explaining this with the classic coin-toss setup on the whiteboard (heads multiplies your stake by 1.5, tails by 0.6, positive expectation but almost everyone who keeps playing goes broke) and half the class just flat out refuses to believe it. They keep saying "but the EV is positive!" So I want them to *live* it instead.

Please act as an experienced designer of classroom simulations who also knows this literature well, and think carefully through the mechanics before writing them up -- maybe brainstorm a couple of candidate designs and then commit to the strongest one. I need a runnable game with: concrete rules and materials (no real money, but I want the stakes to feel real somehow -- maybe the points convert into something students care about, I'm open to ideas), a structure where the ensemble average is visibly positive across the room while almost every individual player's trajectory trends downward, and a moment where they can *see* the class average being propped up by one or two lucky outliers while the median student is ruined. Crucially, students should not be told the point in advance -- they should be offered the game on its positive-EV merits and discover the divergence themselves. Also include: a debrief plan that maps what happened onto the time-average vs ensemble-average distinction and connects it to why we hold savings, buy insurance, and don't bet everything on favorable bets; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I assume someone will refuse to keep playing, or accuse me of rigging the coin, or a student will spot the trick early and spoil it); and an honest note about which features of the real ergodicity argument the game can't capture -- e.g. we can only fake an "ensemble" with ~18 students, and risk aversion alone explains some of what they'll feel, wich muddies the lesson.

If it helps to calibrate: I want the students who refuse to play more rounds to look *rational* by the end, and the ones who cite expected value to feel the floor drop out from under them. Don't let the activity collapse into "gambling is bad" -- the lesson is specifically that the average across people and the average across time answer different questions.
