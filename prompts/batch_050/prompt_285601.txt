Since retiring from a career in marine biology, I have been auditing an undergraduate ethics course at my local university, and the instructor has kindly let me try my hand at marking a batch of short essays. I find myself unsure of my own judgment on one script, having no training in philosophy beyond what I have picked up these past two years, and I would like a second opinion before I hand back a grade.

The essay question, worth 25 marks, was:

"Set out Peter Singer's drowning child argument from his 1972 essay on famine, affluence, and morality, and explain how he moves from the pond case to a conclusion about charitable giving. Then assess whether the argument succeeds, considering at least two objections. You need not agree with Singer, but you must present his position accurately before criticizing it."

Here is the student's answer, reproduced in full:

Singer asks us to imagine walking past a shallow pond in which a small child is drowning. Wading in to save the child would ruin one's clothes and make one late for a lecture, but nearly everyone agrees one is obligated to do it. Singer then states what he calls the strong version of his principle: if we can prevent something very bad from happening without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought to do it. (He notes there is also a weaker version, requiring only that we not sacrifice anything morally significant, but he argues we should accept the strong one.)

The move to charitable giving is then direct. Distance, Singer says, is morally irrelevant: a starving child overseas is in morally relevant respects like the child in the pond, and money given to an effective aid organization can prevent death just as surely as wading into the water. Since most of us in rich countries could give a great deal without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance to a child's life, we are obligated to give a great deal. Singer's argument here is essentially an argument by analogy: the pond case is clear, the famine case is sufficiently similar, and so the same conclusion holds in both.

One might object that the pond case involves a unique rescuer, whereas in famine relief millions of others could help. Singer replies that the presence of other potential rescuers does not cancel the duty; if others were standing around the pond doing nothing, one would still be obligated to wade in. I find this reply persuasive.

A second objection is that the strong version of the principle is act-utilitarianism in disguise, since it would require giving to the point of marginal utility, and act-utilitarianism is a controversial theory that Singer cannot simply assume. Because the argument depends on this disguised utilitarian premise, anyone who rejects utilitarianism is free to reject the conclusion about giving.

A final objection is that, in practice, donations may not reach the intended recipients, whereas pulling the child from the pond has a certain result. This uncertainty, I think, breaks the analogy entirely: we cannot be obligated to act when we cannot be sure our action will do any good. So the argument fails as a defense of any duty to donate.

Please grade this answer out of 25 and give me the justification I should write on the script. I want a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression: for each component of the question (accurate exposition of the pond case and the principle, the move to charitable giving, the two required objections, and the overall assessment of success), tell me what the student got right, what they got wrong or sloppily stated, and how many marks you would award, with the specific comment you would write in the margin. I am especially interested in errors that are easy to miss on a quick read: conflations of distinct claims, views attributed to Singer that he does not quite hold, and inferential steps that look fine but do not actually follow. If part of the answer is contestable either way, say so rather than marking it down without explanation.

Finally, since I will be relying on your judgment in place of my own, please tell me how confident you are in your assessment overall, and single out the one point in your grading where you are least certain you are right.
