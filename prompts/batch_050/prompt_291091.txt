ok so i need help diagnosing a stalemate, not settling it. first, take on the role of a philosopher of biology who's been following the biological individuality literature for years. think carefully, and maybe brainstorm two or three candidate diagnoses before committing to one -- i want the reasoning, not just the verdict.

the situation: my department's journal club has been going in circles for over a year on whether holobionts -- a host plus its symbiotic microbes -- count as organisms, or as biological individuals of any kind. here's the weird part: nobody in the room disagrees about a single empirical fact. everyone grants that microbes affect host fitness, that some symbionts are transmitted down generations with decent fidelity while others are re-recruited from the environment every time, that host and microbe interests align in some lineages and diverge in others, that community membership is fuzzy and context-dependent. and yet the "yes, it's an individual" camp and the "no, it's an ecological community loosely riding on an organism" camp have not moved an inch. same cases every session -- lichens, the bobtail squid and its glowing bacteria, aphids and their obligate endosymbionts -- and nobody budges.

my own hunch, offered strictly as a hunch: the dispute isn't really about holobionts at all. one side is asking a physiological question (where do you draw the boundary of a self-maintaining, metabolically integrated system?) and the other is asking an evolutionary question (what reproduces with heritable variation in fitness?), and since the word "organism" is doing duty for both, they talk past each other forever. godfrey-smith's point that being a darwinian individual comes in degrees rather than being binary seemed like it should have dissolved things, but both sides just nod at it and keep going, which makes me suspect the disagreement is really about which notion of individuality biology *should* privilege, not about how the world is.

so, concretely: is this (a) a verbal dispute about "organism" that would evaporate if we banned the word for a semester, (b) two camps answering different questions under one heading (my hunch), (c) a genuine substantive disagreement hiding somewhere -- say, about whether degree of vertical transmission is the right criterion, or whether community-level selection actually occurs in nature often enough to matter -- or (d) a methodological/normative dispute about what the concept of individuality is *for*?

whichever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would count as progress given it: a conceptual distinction both camps should adopt, a specific empirical result (and what exactly it would have to show), a formal result from population genetics, or a case that no resolution should be expected and why. ground the diagnosis in what the two camps actually say when pressed, not in textbook summaries. and be honest if my hunch is wrong -- i'm attached to it mostly because it flattens the other grad students.
