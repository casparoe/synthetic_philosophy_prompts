A colleague has been pressing an argument against me that seems to prove too much, and I want a careful diagnosis. Background: Scheffler's afterlife conjecture holds that if we learned that humanity would end shortly after our own deaths, most of what we value -- our research, our relationships, our conservation and reform projects -- would lose its point for us, even supposing our own lives went on unchanged. My colleague argues as follows. (1) If the conjecture is true, then the worth of our projects depends on humanity's survival beyond our deaths. (2) This dependence is a dependence on how the future actually unfolds, not merely on what we happen to believe about it: no one is comforted by the thought that their projects are worthwhile so long as their confidence in posterity remains undisturbed. (3) We cannot now know, or even assign stable probabilities to, whether humanity will persist for centuries. (4) If the worth of a project depends on something we cannot know, we cannot be warranted in treating that project as worthwhile. (5) Therefore either the afterlife conjecture is false, or we cannot be warranted in treating most of our projects as worthwhile. I find the second disjunct absurd and the first disjunct unwelcome -- the thought experiments seem to me nearly irresistible -- so I take the argument to have gone wrong somewhere. Identify the step you would reject, explain why that step looked innocent (in particular, what slide, if any, occurs between the scenario in which I learn of humanity's end and the scenario in which humanity's end simply occurs), and say what it costs to reject your chosen step rather than each of the alternatives. If on your analysis the argument is valid, tell me which premise is least costly to surrender and why.
