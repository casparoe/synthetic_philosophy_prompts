A few months ago I volunteered to lead a session of our local philosophy reading group, and we are working through Plato's Meno. The dialogue has taken on an odd personal urgency for me because of an argument I keep having with a colleague at work: I help train new hires, and he insists that all that matters is drilling people in procedures that produce correct results, whereas I maintain that someone who merely follows correct procedures without grasping why they work is missing something genuinely valuable. I turned to the end of the dialogue expecting vindication and came away more confused than vindicated. The passage is Meno 97c–98a. Socrates first concedes that a man who merely has a correct opinion about the road to Larissa, without ever having travelled it or knowing it, would guide others there just as well as the man who knows the way; so far as right guidance goes, true opinion is no worse a guide than knowledge. Meno then presses the obvious question — why, in that case, do we prize knowledge so much more highly — and Socrates answers that true opinions are fine things while they stay, but they tend to run away from the soul like the runaway statues of Daedalus, and are worth little until they are tied down by working out an account of the reason why, the aitias logismos; once bound, he says, they first become knowledge and then remain in place, and this is why knowledge is more valuable than correct opinion.

Here is where I need help. First, I cannot tell whether the two halves of the passage are even consistent with one another: having just granted that true belief guides action exactly as well as knowledge does, Socrates seems to locate knowledge's extra value purely in its stability — it stays put — but if the whole advantage of the belief was already exhausted by the guidance its truth provides, why does making the same true belief harder to lose add value of a different or greater kind, rather than just giving us a more durable route to the identical destination? Second, I do not understand what the tying down is supposed to consist in psychologically: is working out the reason why something like being able to explain or teach, something like having justification that would survive counter-argument, or something more like the recollection doctrine Socrates immediately identifies it with, and how literally am I meant to take that identification given how the dialogue ends? Third, and this is what my colleague pressed on me when I showed him the passage: does Socrates actually answer Meno's question about why knowledge is better than true belief, or does he merely redescribe the difference between them — telling us what knowledge is (bound true belief) while assuming, rather than establishing, that the binding is worth prizing? My colleague's gloss is that the passage quietly changes the subject from practical value to security against loss, and that once you notice this, the stability answer fails for exactly the reason later epistemologists press under the label of the swamping problem: if truth is what we cared about, a true belief already has everything of value on offer.

Could you walk me through the passage itself: what the structure of Socrates' answer is, how the guide-to-Larissa concession is meant to sit alongside the tethering metaphor, and what the most defensible reading of the aitias logismos is such that the passage answers Meno's question rather than evading it? I would like you to adjudicate between my colleague's reading and mine — he says the passage conflates a definition with a justification; I suspect the connection to recollection and to the dialogue's opening question about whether virtue can be taught is doing more work than his deflationary reading allows. Please stay close to the text rather than surveying the secondary literature. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
