Just got back from a long argument with a friend about when it's rational to believe something on purely statistical grounds, and the annoying part is we kept catching ourselves flip-flopping depending on the case. Here are my gut verdicts — I want to know what principle, if any, sits under them.

- Million-ticket lottery, one winner: seems perfectly rational to believe my ticket loses.
- But it would be irrational to believe, of every ticket, that it loses — one of them wins, so I'd be committing to a contradiction.
- I wrote a long blog post with maybe 200 factual claims in it. I believe each one, and I'm also quite sure at least one is wrong somewhere. That feels fine, not inconsistent — even though it looks structurally identical to the all-tickets case.
- The gatecrasher setup: 1000 people at a fair, only 100 paid, no way to tell who. Convicting a random attendee purely because it's 90% likely they didn't pay seems wrong, even though 90% is a high bar.
- But a DNA match with a one-in-a-million error rate seems like fine grounds to convict, even though that's also just statistics.
- It's completely fine to board a plane believing it'll land safely based on crash stats alone.

The DNA one and the gatecrasher one especially feel like they can't both stand, and I genuinely can't tell wich of them I'd give up. So: is there a principle that delivers most of these? State it precisely, show which case motivates each part of it, and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop. Also tell me what the principle says about a case we didn't get to: an AI system flags bank transactions as fraudulent and is right 99.9% of the time — is it rational for the auditor to believe a flagged transaction is fraud, and does it matter whether the system can say anything about *why* it flagged it?
