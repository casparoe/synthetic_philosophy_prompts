So I've been a science journalist for about twelve years, mostly covering consciousness research and the weird edges of psychiatry, and next month I'm giving a talk at a university philosophy colloquium based on my new book. The thesis in a nutshell: Metzinger's idea that the self-model is phenomenally transparent — that we look through it rather than at it, so the model doesn't show up in experience as a model — explains why people are chronically overconfident about introspection. My book's spin on this is that a whole wave of tools (VR embodiment setups, meditation apps, psychedelic-assisted therapy) are best understood as what I call "opacity technologies": they briefly make the self-model visible *as* a model, which I claim is epistemically valuable but genuinely dangerous, because stripping transparency away too fast is roughly how you end up with depersonalization/derealization territory. I also argue that law, journalism, and everyday relationships have racked up a kind of "transparency debt" by treating first-person reports as direct readouts when they're anything but.

The audience: a philosophy of mind colloquium, maybe 25 people. I know there's one enactivist who thinks Metzinger-style representationalism was dead on arrival, one illusionist-ish type who thinks phenomenal consciousness is itself the illusion and will probably find my whole framing confused from the other direction, plus a psychiatrist who actually treats depersonalization patients and was invited because my chapter leans on that clinical literature pretty heavily. The rest are faculty and grad students, mixed sympathies. I get 30 minutes to talk and then a long Q&A, which is the part I'm sweating.

For each one, tell me what I should concede gracefully versus hold firm on.
