I'm trying to pin down my own intuitions about Maxwell's demon and when information processing has a thermodynamic price, and I'd like you to find the principle behind them (or tell me which one has to go). My verdicts:

1. Szilard's one-molecule engine with a finite memory: no free lunch. Measuring which side the molecule is on costs nothing, but the cycle only closes once the demon's record is reset, and that's where the kT ln 2 gets paid.
2. Same engine, but the demon has an endless supply of blank memory: I think it really does beat the second law for as long as the tape lasts. The books never balance, the memory just fills up with junk.
3. Copying a bit, or recording a measurement: can be done with arbitrarily little dissipation.
4. Resetting a bit to 0 when you already know it's 1: no minimum cost -- it's just a controlled flip.
5. Resetting a bit to 0 when you have no idea what it is (50/50): costs kT ln 2, even though the physical operation looks identical to case 4.
6. A computer that never erases anything can in principle compute as long as you like for as little energy as you like.
7. Taking a blank tape and writing random noise onto it: not a cost at all, you could actually extract work from doing that.

Cases 4 and 5 seem to contradict each other, and case 2 doesn't sit well with the usual "erasure is the whole answer" story, which is why I'm asking. Is there one principle that delivers all seven verdicts (I suspect the answer has something to do with cost tracking entropy of the memory states rather than the act of measuring or erasing per se)? State it precisely, show how it handles 4 vs 5 and 2, and then tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: a demon whose memory holds two perfectly correlated copies of the same bit, who erases both. Does the second copy cost another kT ln 2 or not?
