I run development at a mid-sized British production company that makes dramatisations of real events -- biopics, true-crime limited series, the occasional feature about a historical episode. I personally review about one treatment a week, and at present our only gate is legal review, which settles almost nothing: in this jurisdiction the dead cannot be defamed and privacy rights lapse at death, so the lawyers tell me what we may do and I am left alone with the harder question of what, if anything, we owe the real dead when we dramatise them. I have read enough to know that philosophers disagree about whether the dead can even be harmed -- whether a person's interests survive their death, so that posthumous misrepresentation wrongs the person themselves rather than merely distressing the living or corrupting public memory -- and I do not want the company to take a position on that dispute. What I want is an ethical vetting procedure I can run on a treatment in a single forty-five-minute meeting, by someone (me) with no philosophical training, that works whichever side of that debate turns out to be right. Some distinctions I already use informally and would like preserved or improved: what my staff call load-bearing inventions (fabrications that change what an audience will believe about a real person's character or culpability -- an invented affair, an invented act of cowardice) versus scaffolding inventions (compressed timelines, composite minor characters, invented dialogue consistent with the record); public figures versus private individuals swept into events; the recently dead with living children versus the historically remote; projects made with estate cooperation versus against the family's wishes; and the rendering of a real person's actual suffering or dying as spectacle. There is also what I think of as viewer residue: audiences treat dramatisation as evidence, the invented detail is what survives in public memory, and a disclaimer in the opening titles does not undo that, so please do not let the procedure treat disclosure as a solvent.

The deliverable is a set of tests to apply in a fixed order, stating what counts as passing each test, where a failure kills the project outright, where it triggers a redesign (for instance, fictionalising the character and changing the name), and where it escalates -- to me, to a conversation with descendants or affected communities, or to a judgement call that the procedure should flag as a judgement call rather than disguise as a rule. Be explicit about which steps encode contestable values and where the procedure will misfire. Then apply it to three cases, including at least one where it gives an answer I will not like: first, a prestige drama about a revered twentieth-century statesman in which the writer wants to invent a cruel remark made privately to his dying wife, defended as emotionally true to the marriage; second, a true-crime series reconstructing the final hours of a recently murdered young woman, drawn entirely from court records, over her parents' objections; third, a composite journalist character in a dramatisation of a decades-old political scandal who is shown doing something professionally disgraceful that none of the real journalists in the story did. Please say at the end what the procedure cannot do at all. British English throughout, please.
