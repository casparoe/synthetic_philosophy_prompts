I have no academic training in philosophy, but I have been thinking about panpsychism on my own — the idea that experience is a fundamental feature of matter, present in some minimal form even in the smallest constituents of things. What I keep getting stuck on is the ethics. Some writers suggest that if experience is everywhere, suffering may be everywhere too, and that this should change how we treat the physical world. Others seem to hold that panpsychism leaves our moral life almost untouched. I suspect the disagreement turns on a distinction that is usually left implicit: whether the minimal experiences at the bottom of things are merely experiences, or whether they are valenced — capable of feeling faintly good or faintly bad.

I would like a thought experiment built to order. Construct a scenario in which one perfectly ordinary action comes out morally unremarkable on two views — a standard materialism that confines experience to brains, and a panpsychism on which fundamental experiences exist but are entirely neutral in valence — yet comes out morally grave on a third view, a panpsychism on which fundamental experiences can be faintly pleasant or unpleasant. The scenario must meet these constraints:

1. The action must be mundane: heating a room, cooking a meal, demolishing a shed — something an ordinary person does without a second thought. No laboratories, no exotic physics, no futuristic devices.
2. Do not assume a solution to the combination problem. The countless tiny experiences must not fuse into a single larger subject: there is no wall-mind or kettle-mind whose suffering one could consult. Whatever moral weight the case generates must come from the micro-experiences themselves.
3. The disagreement must not rest on any empirical claim the materialist would contest, such as hidden harm to an animal. All three positions agree on the physics and the ordinary biology; they may differ only on where experience lies and what it is like.
4. The scenario itself should be a single paragraph.

Afterwards, show me that the case works. State the verdict each of the three positions delivers and the reasoning that produces it, so that the verdicts genuinely fall out of the views rather than being stipulated. Identify the one feature of the case on which the valenced panpsychist's verdict depends, and tell me what happens if that feature is removed — does the case collapse into one where all three views agree? Finally, give the strongest reply available to the valenced panpsychist who refuses to accept that the action is wrong: is the escape to deny that micro-valences aggregate into anything morally significant, to deny that we could ever know their sign, or something else entirely — and what does each escape route cost the view?

My purpose in asking is to work out whether panpsychism's ethical implications are real or illusory: whether they follow from the view itself, or only from the further assumption of valence — and perhaps from a principle about how vast numbers of trivial experiences weigh against familiar ones.
