I am applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship in the philosophy of social science and need a two-page research proposal. The topic is the problem of cultural explanation, approached through Max Weber's thesis in The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism — the claim that ascetic Protestantism, particularly the Calvinist doctrines of predestination and the worldly calling, helped bring about the distinctive ethos of modern capitalism.

My half-formed idea, which I want sharpened rather than merely endorsed: most of the debate over whether Weber's thesis is "true" conflates three quite different claims about how a value system could causally influence economic life. First, values as internalized motivation: Protestant teachings change what individual believers want, so they work, save, and invest differently. Second, values as legitimation: Protestant ideas do not change preferences but lower the social cost of practices (lending at interest, relentless accumulation, treating time as a resource) that would otherwise be stigmatized. Third, values as an institutional selection mechanism: the causal work is done not inside individuals' heads but by churches, schools, and sects that recruit, screen, and discipline members, so that Protestant communities end up disproportionately populated by people with the relevant dispositions whatever those people privately believe. My suspicion is that the thesis fares very differently depending on which mechanism is meant, and that the best-known empirical objections — findings about Catholic regions with comparable capitalist development, about timing, about the weak correlation between doctrine and behavior at the individual level — cut hard against the first mechanism while leaving the second and third largely untouched.

Turn this into the proposal. Specifically, I need: (a) a precise statement of the research question, framed so that it has a determinate answer rather than being a verdict on the thesis as a whole; (b) an account of why existing positions leave the question open — both the motivational reading most critics attack and the sociological readings (Weber's own talk of elective affinity between ethos and economic form seems to me deliberately ambiguous between all three mechanisms, and the proposal should take a stand on whether that ambiguity is a defect or the point); (c) the method, meaning what I would actually do across the twelve months: how much of the year is conceptual reconstruction versus engagement with the historical literature, and what strategy I would use to figure out what historical evidence could in principle discriminate between the three mechanisms, given that they often predict the same aggregate outcomes; (d) an explanation of why this conceptual-mechanistic approach is preferable to the obvious alternative, namely yet another empirical test of the thesis; (e) what a finished result would look like, including the possibility that the honest conclusion is that the mechanisms are empirically inseparable with the evidence historians can supply — and what the project will have achieved if so; and (f) an explicit statement of what would count as the project failing, plus a candid judgment about the main scope risk for a one-year timeline.

No bibliography or citations — I will add those myself, so refer to positions and arguments by description rather than by reference. Finally, close the proposal with a short section on confidence: state how confident you are that the three-way mechanism split is the right carving of this debate rather than an artificial taxonomy, and single out the one point in the proposal you are least sure of, explaining why.
