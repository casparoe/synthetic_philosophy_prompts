Two of my friends argued through most of a road trip about something Marcus did before we left. He'd just bought the used car we were in and didn't know if the fuel gauge worked. His method: every so often he'd note "the gauge says a quarter tank, and the tank is a quarter full" -- where his only reason for thinking the tank was a quarter full was that the gauge said so. After a dozen such 'confirmed' readings he announced the gauge was reliable. Denise said that's just circular, you can't check an instrument against itself. Marcus said it's all valid deduction: the gauge read P, the tank was in fact P, so the gauge was accurate that time; repeat and generalize. If that's illegitimate, then deducing consequences from things you know is illegitimate, and half of ordinary reasoning goes down with it. Denise replied that his procedure would 'certify' a broken gauge stuck at half-full just as easily -- every check passes no matter what, and a test nothing can fail isn't a test. Marcus said she's in the same boat: she trusts her memory because it's been reliable, but her evidence for that is memories; everyone bootstraps somewhere, so why pick on his gauge? Denise said fine, maybe basic faculties get a pass, but a pass for unavoidable circularity doesn't extend to optional circularity -- he had a dipstick in the trunk the whole time. Who's right at each step here? Go move by move, say which moves are solid and which fail, and whether there's something they're both missing.
