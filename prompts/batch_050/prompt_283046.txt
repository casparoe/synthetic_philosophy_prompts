I have come to hold the view that the other side of my country's main political divide does not, for the most part, argue in good faith: their stated reasons are rationalizations of positions fixed by identity and interest, their professed concerns are tactical, and their moments of apparent reasonableness are positioning rather than sincerity. What would count against this? I want more than the bare observation that some individuals are surely sincere — I want to know which kinds of evidence should actually move me, and which I would merely absorb, since a view like mine has a self-sealing structure: any display of good faith can be reinterpreted as strategy, any concession as a feint, any exception as statistically irrelevant. Please address how to distinguish bad faith from sincere motivated reasoning, from deep disagreement about values, and from honest error; what my view predicts that these rival explanations do not; how selection effects — the loudest and most visible voices being the least representative — bear on the quality of my evidence base; and whether the symmetry test (would my own side pass the standards of proof I demand of theirs?) is a fair challenge or a false equivalence. Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and indicate which of them a committed holder of my view would concede as genuinely counting against it, and whether those concessions and dismissals are themselves defensible. Finally, if the view turns out to be unfalsifiable in practice, is that a defect of the view, or an accurate reflection of a situation in which the available evidence is genuinely ambiguous?
