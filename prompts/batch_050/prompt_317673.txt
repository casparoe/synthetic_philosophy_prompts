A few months ago I had a strange experience on a meditation retreat — a stretch of maybe an hour where, as best I can describe it, there was clearly experiencing going on but nobody it was happening *to*. Since then I've fallen down the rabbit hole of Thomas Metzinger's self-model theory: roughly, the idea that what we call the self is just the content of a transparent phenomenal self-model, a representation the brain generates but cannot recognize *as* a representation, which is exactly why it seems like there's directly "someone" there rather than a model of someone. The problem is I've been arguing about this for weeks with a friend from my reading group who's into phenomenology and defends the "minimal self" view — that every experience whatsoever has a basic for-me-ness or first-personal givenness built into it, which is not a model, not a representation, not something the brain constructs, and which Metzinger-style theories allegedly get backwards by explaining the self as content when it's really a structural feature of consciousness itself.

We've decided to stop going in circles and run a proper adversarial collaboration, ending in a joint statement we could both sign. Draft that statement for us. Specifically I want: (1) a numbered list of claims both sides can genuinely sign — I expect these include things like "there is no homunculus or inner observer," "neuroscience and psychopathology constrain any adequate theory of selfhood," and some agreement about what out-of-body experiences and the rubber hand illusion at least show — and be careful these aren't so watered down they're vacuous. (2) A disagreements table where each disagreement is stated in wording its own side would actually accept (e.g., don't describe her view as "posits a metaphysical ego" — she'd reject that, and don't describe mine as "denies consciousness exists"). (3) The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one of us to actually update. I suspect one crux is how to interpret reports of "selfless" experience in deep meditation and in pathologies like depersonalization — do they show that minimal for-me-ness can genuinely be absent, or only that narrative and reflective self-reference drop out while a minimal self remains? Another might be whether "for-me-ness" names a piece of phenomenal character at all, or something thinner that couldn't show up in any experiment. (4) For each crux, the cases, experiments, or arguments we both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down *in advance* so neither of us can wiggle afterwards.

And if part of our fight turns out to be verbal — I have a nagging suspicion we just mean different things by "self" (subject of experience vs. narrative identity vs. the body-model) and have been trading equivocations — then say which part is verbal and why, and rewrite that part of the disagreement so it becomes substantive or gets removed from the table entirely.
