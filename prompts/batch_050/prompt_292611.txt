I chair the methods committee of a small fiscal-policy institute that runs an internal forecasting panel. Roughly twenty staff analysts make probabilistic forecasts about events bearing on our published outlooks — recessions within a stated horizon, breaches of budget-balance targets, sovereign rating changes, and the like. For each question an analyst submits a probability (or, for multi-outcome questions, a distribution), and once outcomes are known we score the forecasts. The scores feed into annual performance reviews and a modest bonus pool, so the choice of scoring rule matters to people and is contested every time we revisit it.

For years we have used the quadratic (Brier) score. Our head of research now proposes switching to the logarithmic score. His argument is that the logarithmic rule is the only strictly proper rule that is local: it assesses a forecast solely on the probability assigned to the outcome that actually occurred, not on probabilities assigned to outcomes that did not, and it punishes overconfident error far more severely, which he considers a feature. Several analysts object, on two grounds. First, the logarithmic score is unbounded: an analyst who assigns probability zero to an outcome that then occurs receives an infinitely bad score, and the objectors say this makes multi-year standings hostage to a single bad call and, in practice, pressures everyone to nudge probabilities away from 0 and 1 out of superstition rather than judgment. Second, one analyst argues that the whole exercise rests on a philosophical confusion. In her view, the only principled justification for requiring forecasts to obey the probability axioms at all is the family of accuracy-dominance arguments — results to the effect that credences violating the axioms are dominated, accuracy-wise, by credences satisfying them — and she claims those arguments establish that incoherent forecasts are defective, but do not single out any one strictly proper scoring rule as the uniquely correct measure of accuracy. So, she says, the head of research's talk of the logarithmic rule being the right one, full stop, is overreach; the dominance results underdetermine the choice among proper rules. The head of research replies that the choice of scoring rule simply is our choice of what we mean by accuracy in this institution, and that we should make it deliberately rather than by inertia.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the committee before our next meeting. It should:

1. Recommend a concrete decision — retain the quadratic rule, adopt the logarithmic rule, or adopt some specified hybrid (for instance, different rules for binary versus multi-outcome questions, or a logarithmic rule with a stated convention for handling probabilities at or near 0 and 1) — with enough implementation detail that the committee could vote on it directly.
2. Engage seriously with the accuracy-dominance considerations the dissenting analyst raised: state what those arguments do establish about our panel's requirements, what they leave open, and whether their reliance on a fixed measure of accuracy creates a circularity worry when the very question before us is which measure to adopt.
3. Take a position on whether choosing between strictly proper scoring rules is a factual question about which rule truly measures accuracy, a pragmatic question about incentives and panel behavior, or a policy choice about what the institution values — and explain what follows for how the committee should deliberate.
4. Present the strongest version of the case for whichever option you recommend against, and say why it ultimately loses.

Write it as a formal committee memo of roughly 800 to 1,200 words. At the end, state how confident you are in the recommendation overall, and single out the one point in the memo you are least sure of, with a sentence on what would change your mind about it.
