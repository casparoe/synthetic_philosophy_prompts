ok so my reading group is working through Augustine's On Lying (De Mendacio) and early on, in section 3, he lays down this test for what a lie is:

"Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind. Whence also the heart of him who lies is said to be double; that is, there is a double thought: the one, of that thing which he either knows or thinks to be true and does not produce; the other, of that thing which he produces instead thereof, knowing or thinking it to be false. Whence it comes to pass, that he may say a false thing and yet not lie, if he thinks it to be so as he says although it be not so; and, that he may say a true thing, and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, although in reality it be so as he utters it. For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie."

I tried to sharpen this into an argument for the group and it seems to prove way too much, but I can't figure out which step to cut:

(1) On Augustine's test, whether I lie depends entirely on the match between what's in my mind and what I produce in words or signs, together with wanting to deceive; the actual truth of what I say is irrelevant. That's just the passage.
(2) What I "produce in words" is what I can reasonably expect my audience to take my words to mean. Nobody produces a meaning their hearers have no way of getting.
(3) My old intro physics lecturer told us, flat out, that falling objects accelerate at the same rate regardless of mass. She believed that was false outside a vacuum — she admitted as much in a later lecture — and she wanted us to believe it flat out. So she had one thing in mind and produced another, intending to deceive.
(4) Therefore, by Augustine's test, she lied. Every year, to a few hundred freshmen.
(5) Same goes for "I'm fine, thanks" when you're not, "we should get lunch sometime," and my dentist's "you'll just feel a bit of pressure."
(6) Augustine himself sets jokes aside because the joker's tone of voice shows he means no deceit — so he agrees that whether the audience is clued in matters, and my lecturer's students were definately not clued in.
(7) Augustine goes on to hold that every lie is a sin.
(8) So all these people are sinning constantly just by talking like normal humans.
(9) That is absurd.

I refuse to accept that my physics education was built on an ongoing mortal sin. Which step do I reject? Name it, explain why it looked fine to begin with, and tell me what each alternative costs — e.g., if I keep (2) do I just have to say Augustine bit the bullet, and if I drop (2), what goes in its place that still catches actual liars? If forced to choose I'd rather keep the passage's test than accept (8), but I want to know what I'm paying either way.
