Here's the exam question (students had ~20 min):

"Psychologists have proposed several accounts of how our concepts are represented. Explain the core difference between prototype theory and exemplar theory, using the concept BIRD as your example. Then explain what motivated the 'theory theory' of concepts, and describe one phenomenon it claims to handle better than either of the other two."

And here's what the student wrote:

"Prototype theory says that a concept is represented by a summary of the features that members of the category tend to have, so categorizing something means checking how similar it is to that summary. For BIRD the prototype would be a robin: small, flies, sings, lays eggs. That's why people are faster to judge that a robin is a bird than that a penguin is, and why membership comes in degrees -- an ostrich is a worse bird than a sparrow. Exemplar theory instead says you don't build any summary at all; you just store the individual birds you've encountered and classify a new thing by its similarity to those stored instances. So the two theories differ in what is stored (one abstract representation vs many concrete ones), but they make essentially the same predictions about typicality, since a robin will be similar to both the prototype and to lots of stored exemplars.

The theory theory (associated with Rosch) was motivated by the thought that similarity alone can't explain our concepts. On this view concepts are embedded in intuitive, quasi-scientific theories of the world -- our concept BIRD includes beliefs about what birds are like inside, where they come from, what makes them develop. One thing it handles better: people treat features differently depending on their theoretical role. If a creature looks exactly like a robin but scientists discover it has the internal organs and DNA of a lizard, people say it's not really a bird, whereas if a creature looks weird but shares the robin's 'essence' it's still a bird. Prototype theory can't explain this because it would put the robin-lookalike squarely in the bird category, since surface similarity is all it has to go on.

Also, because exemplar theory stores every encountered instance, it predicts people should be unable to categorize genuinely novel objects, which is false, so that's another problem for it."
