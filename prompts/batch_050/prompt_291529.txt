I want an analysis of Cold War nuclear deterrence through the lens of updateless decision theory (UDT), and I want the analysis to run in both directions: the theory illuminating the episode, and the episode testing the theory.

Take UDT, roughly, as the view that a rational agent should act as though it were selecting, once and for all, the output of its own decision procedure for every situation in which that procedure might run, rather than conditioning on the fact of finding itself in one particular situation. On this view, what matters is which policy is best in expectation across all the branches where the procedure operates, and an agent who reasons this way can thereby reap the benefits of commitments that an agent who re-deliberates in the moment cannot sustain.

Now consider the episode. Both superpowers relied on threats of massive retaliation that, by ordinary moment-by-moment reasoning, would be irrational to execute once deterrence had failed, since striking back after an attack serves no defensive purpose; each side therefore invested heavily in making its threats credible, through delegation, doomsday rhetoric, and sometimes cultivated opacity about its own procedures. In the 1980s the Soviet Union reportedly deployed Perimeter, known in the West as Dead Hand, a semi-automatic system designed to guarantee retaliation even if the leadership were destroyed; accounts differ over how much human approval remained in the loop once it was activated. And on 26 September 1983, Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov, the duty officer at the Oko early-warning command centre, judged that the satellite system's report of incoming American missiles was a false alarm and declined to pass the warning up the chain as protocol directed; he had no launch authority himself, but his superiors, told an attack was under way and given minutes to decide, might well have retaliated.

Please address all of the following. First, what does UDT say the superpowers faced and what it would counsel: is a semi-automatic retaliation system UDT embodied in hardware, and does the theory vindicate the deterrent logic of binding oneself in advance? Second, what does Petrov's decision look like from UDT's standpoint: an updateful deviation from the policy one would have chosen ex ante, or exactly the output the best ex ante policy would have specified for that situation, and can the theory even distinguish these? Third, where the mapping breaks: states are not unified agents with identifiable decision procedures, deterrence turns on the adversary's beliefs about you rather than on your policy's logical properties, and no human being selects a policy once and for all. Finally, the reverse direction: does the fact that the system nearly produced catastrophe through a false alarm, and was arguably saved by a man reasoning about his particular situation, count as evidence for or against the updateless approach?

Open with two or three sentences stating your overall verdict, then give the analysis. Maximum 900 words.
