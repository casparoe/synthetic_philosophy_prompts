I work as a data scientist and have a solid working command of Bayesian inference, decision theory, and the evaluation of classifiers: priors, likelihood ratios, posterior thresholds, expected loss, calibration, error-rate trade-offs, and so on. I recently encountered a debate in the philosophy of law that I suspect maps onto things I already know, and I want you to test that suspicion honestly rather than flatter it.

Here are the two canonical puzzles, as I understand them. First: a pedestrian is sideswiped at night by a bus whose driver fled; the only evidence is that one company operates 80% of the buses on that route and a second company operates the remaining 20%. Courts are generally said to be unwilling to find the first company liable on that evidence alone, even though the relevant liability standard only requires that the claim be more probable than not. Second: a thousand people attend an event for which only four hundred tickets were sold; the organizer sues a randomly selected attendee for the price of admission, offering as its entire case the statistic that there was a 60% chance this person gatecrashed. Again, the statistical case is said to be insufficient on its own, whereas if the same organizer could point to something particular about this attendee — testimony, a torn fence nearby, whatever — a far weaker statistical case would apparently suffice. The literature calls evidence of the first kind "naked" statistical evidence and argues about what, if anything, is wrong with it.

Your task has two parts. First, restate this entire debate in my vocabulary, as rigorously as you can: priors and posteriors, base rates, likelihood ratios, decision thresholds under asymmetric costs, calibration, the distinction between a well-calibrated population-level prediction and an individual-level determination, selective labeling and distribution shift, reference-class problems. Be specific about which legal concepts correspond to which statistical ones, and say explicitly when you are making an interpretive choice (for example, about what "proof beyond a reasonable doubt" or "more probable than not" becomes in decision-theoretic terms, since that mapping is itself contested). Second, give me the residue report: which elements of the legal discomfort translate cleanly (for instance, objections that are really about calibration, unmodeled bias in the base rate, or incentive effects on future data generation), which translate only under distortion, and which have no statistical counterpart at all. For whatever fails to translate, I want your judgment on whether it points to a genuine normative commitment that statistics cannot capture — something about what it takes to justify a finding against a particular person — or whether it is a cognitive confusion that the legal doctrine has merely dignified. I am genuinely unsure whether the law has spotted something my framework is blind to, or whether this is a base-rate fallacy with a pedigree, and I'd rather be told the uncomfortable answer in either direction.
