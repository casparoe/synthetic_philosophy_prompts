**The Best Bad Geometry Ever Written**

Thomas Hobbes spent the last twenty-five years of his life trying to square the circle, and he was wrong every single time. The first attempt appeared in De Corpore in 1655, it was refuted within the year by John Wallis, and Hobbes responded by publishing corrected versions, each of which was also wrong, for decades. Historians usually treat this as a sad coda to a great career. I want to suggest it's actually the most philosophically consistent thing Hobbes ever did.

The key is that Hobbes didn't think of geometry the way we do. For him, geometry is the science of bodies in motion: a line is what a moving point traces out, a surface what a moving line traces out. And a point, he insisted against Euclid, isn't some partless abstraction — it's a body whose size we simply agree not to consider in the demonstration. Geometry is continuous with physics all the way down.

This matters because of Hobbes's deeper epistemology. On his view, you only genuinely know what you can generate — knowledge of a thing is knowledge of its construction, of how it's made. We know geometric truths better than anything else precisely because we make the figures ourselves, with ruler and compass, out of regulated motions. A figure you can't generate is a figure you don't understand.

Now look at the squaring attempts from this angle. Everyone else in the seventeenth century was happy to reason about the circle through equations and infinitesimals, treating the figure as given and computing away. Hobbes couldn't accept that as knowledge at all. If you haven't exhibited the circle's area as something constructible — as the trace of a motion you could actually perform — then for Hobbes you don't know it, you've just pushed symbols around. His quadratures weren't a hobby; they were attempts to supply the one thing his theory of knowledge demanded and his opponents never provided: a generation of the square equal to the circle.

That's also why the feud with Wallis was so total and so unresolvable. Wallis was the great symbol-pusher of the age — his arithmetic of infinites was exactly the kind of uninterpreted manipulation Hobbes thought produced answers without understanding. They weren't disagreeing about theorems. They were disagreeing about what a demonstration is. Wallis kept showing Hobbes his errors; Hobbes, I suspect, could never quite register them as errors, because a refutation couched in algebraic manipulation was, by his lights, not the right kind of speech.

And here's where I'll risk the analogy. A century and a half later, the intuitionists would again insist that mathematical existence means constructibility, that a proof which doesn't show you how to build the thing doesn't deserve the name. Hobbes got there early, with cruder tools, driven by materialism rather than by scruples about infinity — and unlike Brouwer, he was simply wrong about the math, repeatedly, in print. But the structure of the mistake is the structure of a real position. He wasn't a crank who happened to do philosophy. He was a philosopher whose crankery was the philosophy, cashed out in compass and straightedge.

There's something almost admirable in that. Most of us, when our deepest commitments collide with the facts, quietly revise the commitments. Hobbes revised the construction and republished. Twenty-five years of being publicly, provably wrong, because the alternative was admitting that knowing might not mean making.
