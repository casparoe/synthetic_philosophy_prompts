I'm a first-year graduate student in cognitive science, and last month a friend of mine who writes a popular newsletter on AI sent me her summary of John Searle's Chinese Room argument, because I'd asked her whether the debate about whether current language models "really understand" anything had older philosophical roots. Her summary ran roughly as follows. Searle asks us to imagine someone locked in a room who doesn't speak Chinese but has an enormous rulebook telling him how to match incoming Chinese symbols with outgoing ones, so that to people outside, the room's responses are indistinguishable from those of a native speaker. The man plainly understands no Chinese -- he's just following rules -- and since a computer running a program is in exactly his position, no computer could ever understand anything merely by running the right program, no matter how convincing its output. Hence, she concluded, Searle proved that artificial intelligence in the strong sense is impossible, and that today's chatbots, however fluent, lack understanding in just the way the man in the room does. She added that Searle's own view is that genuine thinking requires the right biological material, which is why brains can think and silicon systems can't, and that the standard objection -- that the man doesn't understand but the whole system of man-plus-rulebook does -- is easily answered, because the man could simply memorize the rulebook, at which point he'd still understand nothing, so the objection collapses.

Something in this strikes me as off, but I can't locate the problem precisely. I'm fairly confident she's right about the basic setup and about what Searle takes the thought experiment to show at first pass. What I suspect, without being able to articulate why, is that the move from "running a program isn't sufficient for understanding" to "no machine could think, and here's the biological reason" smuggles in a claim Searle himself would not put that way, and that her dismissal of the "whole system" objection is quicker than the actual literature's treatment of it.
