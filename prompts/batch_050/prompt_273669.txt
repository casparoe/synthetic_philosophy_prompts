I'm not an academic — I work in logistics and never studied political theory — but I've spent a couple of years turning over a question in my spare time that I've come to call "who gets a say." It started when my city debated letting non-citizens vote in local elections, and it's grown into a general puzzle about who belongs to the group that decides. I've written down my gut verdicts on eight cases. What I'd like is to know whether there's a single principle underneath them, or whether my instincts are just a pile.

Here are the verdicts, as honestly as I can report them:

1. A woman who moved here twenty years ago, works, pays every tax the rest of us pay, and is fully subject to our laws, but has never been able to naturalize: she should get the vote. I'm confident about this one.
2. A tourist here for two weeks, subject to our laws the whole time he's visiting: no vote. Also confident.
3. Sixteen-year-olds: they should get the vote, at least in municipal elections. They work, they pay some taxes, and they'll live with the results longest.
4. People not yet born who will live with what we decide about, say, a nuclear waste site or a pension system: they deserve some weight in the decision, though obviously not a ballot. I'm firm that the weight should be real, not ceremonial.
5. People just across the border whose air and water are fouled by a factory on our side: they should have some say about the factory, but plainly no say about our school curriculum. The line feels obvious even if I can't draw it.
6. A citizen who emigrated thirty years ago, owns nothing here, follows nothing about the country, and never intends to return: I don't think he should keep voting in our elections.
7. Prisoners serving sentences: they should keep the vote. Taking it away feels like doubling the punishment.
8. Workers who live one town over but commute in daily, and whose livelihoods hinge on our referendum about closing that same factory: here I keep flip-flopping, sometimes within the same day.

Now, the trouble I can see for myself. Verdicts 1 and 6 together seem to say the answer is residence or subjection — where you actually live under the rules. But 4 and 5 don't fit that at all; the unborn and the neighbors across the border are neither residents nor (mostly) subject to our law, and the tourist in case 2 is both. So maybe the real principle is that whoever is significantly affected by a decision gets a say in it — but that seems to hand the tourist a say, hand the emigrant in case 6 a say whenever our policies touch him, and make it mysterious why the border residents in case 5 get the factory but not the schools.

So here is what I'm asking. Is there one principle, or a principled combination of two, that yields all or most of these verdicts — and can you state it precisely enough that I could apply it myself rather than just admire it? If a clause of the principle exists only to rescue one verdict, tell me which verdict it's hostage to. Tell me honestly which of my eight the best principle simply cannot accommodate, rather than bending the principle into shape. And then apply whatever survives to two cases I haven't settled: the commuters in case 8, and a citizen who moved abroad only two years ago, still owns an apartment here, still pays tax on it, and might return — does she get to vote, and would your answer change year by year?

One request on form: I'd rather get a sharp principle that overturns one or two of my verdicts than a mushy one that flatters all eight.
