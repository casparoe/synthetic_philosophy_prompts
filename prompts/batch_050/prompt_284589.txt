i'm a biologist by training (evolutionary, mostly paleo-adjacent) and i keep getting pulled into arguments with friends about societal "collapse" -- the late bronze age, the classic maya, easter island, the usual suspects. the thing that bugs me is that everyone talks about these as failures or deaths, when in my world the interesting question is usually extinction versus transformation. a lineage that turns into something else isn't a failure in the way a lineage that just ends is, and the fossil record is full of cases where "disappearance" is really just us drawing a line where a name stops applying.

so: translate the debate about societal collapse into the vocabulary i actually think in. take the standard cases (bronze age palace systems, classic maya, rapa nui under the ecocide reading) and restate the claims people make about them using concepts like extinction vs. anagenesis/pseudoextinction, background vs. mass extinction, adaptation vs. drift, punctuated change, maybe niche construction if it's doing real work. for each case, tell me what the translation buys you -- does anything about collapse debates get clearer when you force it into this frame?

but the part i actually want is the residue report. what doesn't survive the translation? my suspicion is that words like "failure" and "decline" carry a value judgment that has no biological equivalent (selection doesn't grade on a curve, nothing in evolution is *supposed* to keep existing), and i want to know if that's right and whether it means the collapse literature is smuggling in normativity, or whether it means the biological frame just can't say the most important thing -- e.g. that the people living through the classic maya "transformation" were worse off, whatever you call the process. be honest about which claims translate cleanly, which get distorted, and which have no expression at all.
