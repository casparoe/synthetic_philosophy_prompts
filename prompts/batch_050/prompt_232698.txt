I teach a second-year undergraduate seminar on moral responsibility, about 14 students, one 75-minute session. Design an in-class activity that makes students feel the force of Strawson's account of reactive attitudes — specifically three things: that we routinely soften or suspend resentment and indignation toward a particular person on a particular occasion when we learn the wrong was accidental, coerced, or ignorant (excuses); that we sometimes withdraw the whole repertoire of reactive attitudes toward someone we see as not a full participant in adult interpersonal relations (exemptions, where we take what Strawson calls the objective attitude, treating the person as something to be managed rather than answered); and his further claim that we could not, and would not wish to, take the objective attitude toward everyone all the time, even if we became convinced that determinism is true.

Constraints on the design. First, the activity must produce real mild indignation or gratitude in the room — staged events with small genuine stakes (tokens, snacks, a class-points leaderboard), not imagined cases or vignettes — but with no deception: students may be surprised, never lied to. Second, there should be a structured sequence of staged reveals of mitigating information, so students can watch their own reactive attitudes weaken and can feel the difference between the two kinds of softening Strawson marks. Third, a final stage should push students to attempt the fully objective stance toward each other and let them discover, in action, where it breaks down.

Give me concrete rules and materials, the stakes scheme, a script for sequencing the reveals, and a debrief mapping specific moments of the session onto Strawson's distinctions. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle that, and be explicit about which feature of Strawson's view a one-off classroom game cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in discussion. Close by saying how confident you are that the activity produces the intended experience, and single out the design element you are least sure will work as intended.
