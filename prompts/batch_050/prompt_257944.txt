So I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training, but I picked up Sartre's "Existentialism is a Humanism" after a friend kept bugging me about it, and one part snagged on something from my day job. I work on recommendation and churn-prediction systems, and lately I've noticed people talking about these models like they're verdicts. A coworker said the compatibility score on a dating app told him his relationship "statistically won't last," so he ended it. Another friend didn't apply to grad school because some tool said people with her profile rarely get in. I got into a dumb argument about whether this is just being sensible with evidence or something weirder.

Then I hit this bit of Sartre where he's complaining about the supposedly hard-headed "realism" of his day:

"We all know how many common sayings can be quoted to this effect, and they all mean much the same – that you must not oppose the powers that be; that you must not fight against superior force; must not meddle in matters that are above your station. Or that any action not in accordance with some tradition is mere romanticism; or that any undertaking which has not the support of proven experience is foredoomed to frustration; and that since experience has shown men to be invariably inclined to evil, there must be firm rules to restrain them, otherwise we shall have anarchy."

And he adds a couple sentences later that what's actually alarming about his doctrine is that "it confronts man with a possibility of choice."

So here's my question: if Sartre were around today, would he file deferring to algorithmic predictions about yourself under the same bucket as these "dismal proverbs" he mocks — i.e., bad faith, hiding from your freedom behind a prediction? Or is there a real difference between a folk saying and a trained statistical model, such that ignoring the model's output would be the opposite sin — denying your facticity, pretending you're not the kind of thing that can be predicted at all? He seems to say freedom always operates within a situation full of facts you don't get to choose, so surely "people with my profile usually fail" is just one more fact? I genuinly can't tell where he'd draw the line between honestly reckoning with your situation and using it as an excuse. Work from the passage and his broader framework (bad faith, facticity vs transcendence) rather than giving me a survey of Sartre scholarship, and steelman the side you reject. End with a last line starting with "Verdict:" followed by one sentence on whether Sartre would call deferring to such self-predictions bad faith.
