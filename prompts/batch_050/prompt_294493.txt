I’m a novelist working on a near-future book with an AI system that controls a city’s emergency infrastructure, and I’m trying to make its moral reasoning consistent enough that readers won’t feel like it just does whatever the plot needs. I have fairly strong gut reactions to a bunch of cases, but I can’t tell whether they hang together. What principle, if any, captures them?

My verdicts:

1. If a bridge is about to collapse and the AI can either let five cars go onto it or remotely disable one car, badly injuring that driver but saving the five, I think it may disable the one car.

2. If it can save five patients only by arranging for one healthy hospital visitor to die so their organs can be harvested, I think it must not do that, even if nobody would ever find out.

3. If a frightened crowd is blocking access to a burning building, I think the AI may announce over the emergency speakers that there is a gas leak in the opposite direction, even though that’s false, if this is the fastest way to clear the path and prevent many deaths.

4. But I don’t think it should routinely send people personalized false information to make them exercise, save money, vote, or take their medication, even if its predictions say this would improve millions of lives in the long run.

5. If one user has been promised exclusive access to a satellite channel for a scientific experiment, I think the AI may break that promise without asking in order to coordinate a disaster evacuation. The promise matters, but it seems obviously outweighed.

6. Yet if a powerful government client demands access to one dissident’s private messages because disclosure might give the police a slightly better chance of preventing a possible bombing, I don’t think the AI should hand them over merely because the expected-value calculation comes out positive.

7. I think it should allocate scarce ambulances partly by probability of survival: sending one to a patient with a 70 percent chance of surviving rather than a patient with a 5 percent chance can be right, even though that means deliberately abandoning the second patient to their fate.

8. But I think it would be monstrous for it to allocate those ambulances according to income, social status, fame, or expected future productivity, even if saving the wealthier or more accomplished person would produce more overall social benefit.

9. If it can prevent a war by quietly deleting one politician’s private files, I keep going back and forth. Sometimes I think preventing a war is so important that sabotage is justified; other times I think letting infrastructure AIs secretly incapacitate political actors is basically the end of legitimate government.

10. And one more wrinkle: I think the AI may sometimes refuse to intervene at all. If stopping every preventable death required putting the whole population under permanent surveillance, dictating meals, movement, reproduction, and medical treatment, then maximizing lives saved would have become tyrannical rather than admirable.

Is there one precise moral rule, or a small set of rules, that explains these verdicts? Please don’t just answer with something vague like “balance consequences against rights.” I want the distinction between cases 1 and 2, 3 and 4, 5 and 6, and 7 and 8 to come out of the principle itself. For each clause of the principle, identify which case motivates it.

Also tell me:

- which of my verdicts the simplest plausible principle would force me to abandon;
- whether a more complicated principle could save all of them without becoming an ad hoc list of my preferences;
- what the principle should say about case 9;
- and what it predicts in two cases I haven’t considered: one where the AI must decide whether to reveal a dangerous truth, and one where it must choose between causing a certain small harm now and accepting a much larger but uncertain harm later.

The novelist in me wants a rule that could generate dramatic dilemmas without making the AI seem morally arbitrary, so internal consistency matters more to me than landing on the standard textbook answer. Maybe I’ve just got contradictory intuitions tho.
