I recently began volunteering with a community historical society, helping decide what from a backlog of donated materials gets preserved, how it is described, and who may consult it, and it has become obvious to me that these are not clerical choices: the archive we assemble will largely determine what the future can know about this town's past. Before I read any more archival theory, I want a specification rather than another manifesto. Write the desiderata for an adequate theory of archival selection and description: the phenomena it must explain, the distinctions it cannot collapse (for instance, records never created versus records deliberately destroyed versus records kept but rendered invisible by how they are catalogued), the cases it must handle (colonial administrative archives, police files on surveilled communities, the papers of ordinary families), and the practical constraints it cannot wave away (finite budgets, the privacy of the living, the researcher's legitimate interest in access). For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to rule out the position I keep hearing from colleagues, that the archivist is merely a neutral custodian of whatever happens to survive, and briefly score against the list the three stances I encounter most: that custodial-neutrality view, the activist view that archivists should deliberately correct for the overrepresentation of the powerful, and the view that all description is interpretation and therefore neutrality is a fiction.
