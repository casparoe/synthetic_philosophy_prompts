So I'm a novelist, and the book I'm working on is basically about how a place goes bad without anyone ever quite deciding it should — language itself is kind of the antagonist. Orwell is my obvious touchstone and I've been picking through his stuff on language and politics for months now. Which means the following mechanisms are old news to me, please dont repeat them or dress them up as new:

1. The anesthetic function: inflated, abstract, euphemistic phrasing that stops you from picturing what you're actually describing — his point about how bombing defenseless villages from the air gets called pacification, and how the naming lets you defend things you'd flinch from if you saw them plainly.
2. Prefab phrases and dying metaphors doing your thinking for you: you stop choosing words, the phrases assemble themselves, and a ready-made thought comes bundled inside them.
3. Words drained of meaning: terms like democracy or fascist that decay into pure applause/boo signals with no agreed content, so nobody can even tell when a sentence is false.
4. State-engineered vocabulary shrinkage, the 1984 version: cut the language down until the heretical thought literally has no words to happen in.
5. Insincerity as the engine: political language as mostly the defense of the indefensible, with bad motives producing bad words producing bad thoughts in a loop.
6. Jargon and borrowed foreign-sounding diction lending fake scientific neutrality to whats really just a partisan judgment.

What I want is one more mechanism — one Orwell never got to — by which the ordinary English of a free country, with no Ministry and no dictionary police, corrodes political thought. It has to be a mechanism, not a topic: something that operates on ordinary speakers the way 1–6 do. Not a rewording of anything above, not a special case, and not just two of them stapled together. If it helps calibrate, I want to steal it for the novel, so the more it lives in daily speech rather than in propaganda offices, the better. Then give me your honest verdict on whether it's any good, and whether Orwell would have taken it as a friendly extension of his argument or as a rebuke to it — those would be different kinds of useful to me, so say which.
