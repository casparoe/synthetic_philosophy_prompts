Bit of backstory: I'm in a reading group that mixes scientists and churchgoers, and last week we got onto whether Genesis conflicts with cosmology. Someone shut the whole discussion down with "the Bible tells you how to go to heaven, not how the heavens go", and everyone just nodded and moved on, which annoyed me because I'm not sure it actually settles anything.

Anyway, it sent me back to Galileo's Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, where at one point he quotes St Augustine with approval, on the topic of making pronouncements about the heavens:

"Now keeping always our respect for moderation in grave piety, we ought not to believe anything inadvisedly on a dubious point, lest in favor to our error we conceive a prejudice against something that truth hereafter may reveal to be not contrary in any way to the sacred books of either the Old or the New Testament."

So here's what I want. Take the slogan and lay out the different precise claims it might be making. Off the top of my head I can see it might mean something modest (scripture's purpose is salvation, so don't look to it for astronomy), or something stronger (scripture makes no claims about the physical world whatsoever), or something methodological about how to read the bits that *look* like physical claims, and there are probably other readings I haven't thought of. For each one, tell me whether it's a platitude, a live and defensible thesis, or actually false, and which of them would really justify using the slogan to end a discussion the way it got used at mine.

Then the bit I'm most curious about: which, if any, of those readings does the Augustine passage above actually support? Because to me it reads less like "scripture says nothing about nature" and more like advice about epistemic caution — don't rashly weld scripture to one side of an open empirical question in case you embarass the sacred texts later. But maybe I'm misreading it, and Galileo quoting it in that letter is doing a different kind of work entirely. If none of the slogan's readings match the passage, say so plainly — I'd rather know the slogan and the letter are pulling apart than have them forced together.
