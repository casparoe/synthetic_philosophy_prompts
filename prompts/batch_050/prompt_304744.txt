I am setting the final examination for a third-year undergraduate module in formal epistemology and decision theory, and I need one problem question on undefined expected values, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The examination is closed-book; candidates have 75 minutes for this question; it carries 25 marks.

You may assume candidates have studied the St. Petersburg and Pasadena games, but both are summarised on the paper for the avoidance of doubt. St. Petersburg: a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads; if the first heads occurs on toss n (probability 2⁻ⁿ), the payoff is 2ⁿ pounds, and the expected payoff diverges, so the expectation is infinite. Pasadena: the same coin procedure, but the payoff for a first heads on toss n is (−1)ⁿ⁺¹ · 2ⁿ/n pounds (positive for odd n, negative for even n); the expected-payoff series is the alternating harmonic series, which sums to ln 2 in the natural order of tosses, yet the positive payoffs alone have infinite expectation and the negative payoffs alone have infinitely negative expectation, so the expectation is strictly undefined.

The question must centre on two further games, which I want defined verbatim on the paper:

Game B (the Square game): the same coin procedure; the payoff on a first heads at toss n is −2ⁿ/n pounds if n is a perfect square and +2ⁿ/n pounds otherwise. (The negative part of its expectation sums to a finite quantity, since the reciprocals of the squares converge, while the positive part diverges; so its expectation is defined, in the extended sense, as +∞.)

Game C (the Sweetened Pasadena game): identical to the Pasadena game except that the payoff on n = 1 is raised from 2 to 2.50. (C pays strictly more than Pasadena in at least one state and no less in any state, yet its expectation is undefined for exactly the same reason.)

Draft requirements:

1. The exam question should be worded exactly as it would appear on the paper, be answerable from the summaries and definitions above alone, and have three or four parts which together oblige the candidate to:
   - demonstrate, rather than merely assert, why Pasadena's expectation is undefined whereas Game B's is +∞, despite both games paying positive and negative amounts on infinitely many outcomes;
   - explain why ln 2 is not a defensible value for Pasadena's expectation, with explicit appeal to the behaviour of conditionally convergent series under rearrangement (i.e., that re-enumerating the very same outcomes yields a series summing to any preassigned real number);
   - adjudicate the apparent tension between two principles — that rational choice should be guided by expected value, and that a statewise dominant option is rationally preferable — using Game C versus Pasadena as the test case, since expectation is silent about a pair that dominance plainly ranks;
   - assess one proposed repair (for instance: value such games by the limit of expectations truncated at the nth toss, letting n tend to infinity) and say whether it survives the rearrangement point.

2. Supply a model answer of roughly 700–900 words, written at the standard of a strong first-class candidate.

3. Supply a marking scheme organised around five must-hit points, stating the marks each is worth (totalling 25) and, for each, a one-line note on the mistake candidates most commonly make instead — for example, treating conditional convergence as ordinary convergence, or claiming that dominance comparisons lapse whenever expectations are undefined.

One constraint above all: the question must not be answerable by reciting a summary of the topic. A candidate who has merely memorised that the Pasadena game's expectation is undefined should be unable to earn more than about a third of the marks.
