I'm a hobbyist who reads about probability and rational choice on weekends, and a puzzle I cooked up has turned into a running argument with a friend who teaches mathematics. I'd like you to answer as a philosopher of probability and decision theory, and to think carefully and slowly about it, because I suspect the interesting part is not the arithmetic but what the arithmetic does to the concept of a fair price. The game is this: I flip a fair coin until the first heads appears. If the first heads comes on toss number n, then when n is odd I receive 2-to-the-n divided by n dollars, and when n is even I pay that same amount. So I might win 2 dollars, or owe 2 dollars, or win 8/3 dollars, or owe 4 dollars, and so on. When I compute what the game is worth in the standard way -- multiplying each outcome by its probability and adding the terms in the natural order, from the most likely outcomes outward -- I get the series one, minus a half, plus a third, minus a quarter, and so on, which settles at about 0.69. So the fair entry fee is about 69 cents, or so I thought.

My friend then pointed out something that has kept me up at night. If I add the very same terms in a different order -- taking one winning term, then two losing terms, then the next winning term, then the next two losing terms, and so forth -- the sum comes out to exactly half of what I got before, about 0.35. And with other orderings I can make the total come out to 1, or to negative 5, or apparently to anything I like. Every outcome is counted exactly once in each ordering; nothing is added or dropped; only the sequence of the addition changes. Her conclusion is that the game simply has no definite value -- not that the value is hard to compute, but that there isn't one. I tried to settle this empirically by simulating the game a few million times and watching the running average of my net winnings, and the average never settled anywhere; it just wandered, which felt like a point in her favor. I should say that I already know about the St. Petersburg game, where the expected value is infinite, and this seems different in kind: there the problem is that the price is too big, here the problem seems to be that the price won't hold still. I also note that if I cap the game at any finite number of tosses, however large, the strangeness vanishes completely, so the pathology lives entirely in the unlimited version.

So here is my question, and I won't be satisfied with a bare name. Does this phenomenon -- an expectation that exists or fails to exist, or takes different values, depending purely on the order in which the possible outcomes are tallied -- have an established name in the literature, and is this specific game a known puzzle, or have we stumbled onto it independently? More importantly, I want the carving, not just the label: what exactly is the defect, and which games have it -- is the right characterization that the winnings-side and the losses-side of the game are each individually ruinous in expectation, so that the net is only ever a difference of two infinities? How does it relate to, and differ from, the St. Petersburg game on one side and from ordinary heavy-tailed games on the other? And what do decision theorists actually do with such a game -- refuse to price it at all, assign it a range of acceptable prices, or declare it outside the jurisdiction of expected value theory? If there is a named concept that fits only imperfectly, tell me which one fits best and where the mismatch lies; if there is none, say so and propose one with a definition and some boundary cases.
