I am a doctoral candidate in history, and I sit as the junior voting member of my county's heritage commission. I need a written opinion to circulate before our next meeting, recommending a decision the commission must take, and I want it to do real philosophical work rather than merely split the difference.

The case. In 1927, thirty-one men died in a colliery disaster here. The coroner's register and parish burial records account for all thirty-one in marked graves across three churchyards. But for four generations, the mining families have maintained that the dead included a further group of itinerant laborers — men who appear in no parish register precisely because they were itinerant, unmarried, and unrecorded — and that these men were buried in an unmarked pit behind the workings. The annual memorial walk, the community's central act of remembrance, ends at that spot. Last year, ahead of a housing development on the site (which the commission cannot block but whose heritage conditions it sets), the commission commissioned a ground-penetrating radar survey. Two passes by two contractors found no anomalies consistent with a mass grave — though the report itself notes the ground was bulldozed and regraded in the 1960s, which limits what a negative result can show. The descendants' association regards the site as a grave, believes the survey was commissioned to clear the way for the development, and has threatened to withdraw cooperation and loaned artifacts from our centenary exhibit if the panel contradicts their account.

The commission must decide two things: the wording of the interpretation panel at the site, and whether to recommend a full archaeological excavation, which would consume roughly our entire annual budget and delay the development. Our charter obliges us to present the county's history "faithfully," a term no one has ever defined. Some commissioners say a panel should simply follow the evidence. Others say a commemorative panel is not a journal article and belongs to the community. Note the epistemic structure: the record does not contradict the community's memory so much as fall silent exactly where the memory speaks, and the silence is arguably a product of the same marginalization that left oral tradition as the only archive these men ever had. Meanwhile the survey's null result is real but weakened by the regrading.

The opinion should take a definite position and not merely survey the options. It should also state a general principle: when a community's memory and the documentary record conflict, what determines which gives way — and is "we do not know" a neutral formulation here, or itself a partisan one? I would like it structured in exactly these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

1. Recommendation (~150 words): the bottom line on the panel and the excavation, stated up front.
2. What the Evidence Actually Shows (~300 words): what the record establishes, where it is silent, and how much weight the survey's negative result can bear.
3. The Strongest Case for the Community's Account (~250 words): steelmanned, including the point about silence in the archive.
4. The Strongest Case for the Skeptical Reading (~250 words): including the precedent set if firmly held memory can override null evidence, and what excavation costs in forgone work.
5. The Governing Principle (~250 words): when memory and record conflict, which gives way and why; whether the panel is an epistemic document or a memorial one; and whether dual presentation of both accounts is principled or an evasion.
6. Proposed Panel Text (~100 words): the actual sentences I can put to a vote, consistent with the recommendation in section 1.
