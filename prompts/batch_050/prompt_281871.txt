I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working up a piece on whether you should rationally defer to your future self. The hook is van Fraassen's Reflection principle: roughly, that your credence in something now, conditional on learning that you'll believe it later, has to equal that later credence, on pain of being Dutch-bookable. The version I've ended up defending in the piece is that Reflection is a genuine requirement of rationality for agents who are sure they'll stay rational and keep gathering evidence -- the standard memory-loss and drunk-driver counterexamples just get handled by restricting the principle's scope.

Here's what I need: what would actually count against that restricted position? I know the usual list of cases, but defenders treat each one as a reason to add a clause, not to abandon the principle. So tell me which of those restrictions are principled and which are ad hoc patches quietly making the view unfalsifiable. What would have to turn out to be true -- about the Dutch book arguement itself, about the accuracy-based defenses, about self-locating cases like Sleeping Beauty -- for the framework to collapse rather than absorb one more exception? Which defeaters would a committed defender themselves concede count, versus which ones they'd dismiss on grounds a neutral should find suspicious? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and give me a straight answer on whether Reflection has been qualified into something that risks nothing -- and if so, whether that's a vice for a principle of this kind or just what mature philosophical theses look like.
