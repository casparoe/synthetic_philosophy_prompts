So I just started my first real job and got my first bonus, and I mentioned at lunch that I was thinking of putting some of it into a few individual stocks I'd been reading about. This kicked off a whole argument between two of my coworkers and I genuinely can't tell who won.

(1) Dana said picking stocks is basically irrational for someone like me. Her argument: any time you buy a stock, someone is selling it to you, and their willingness to sell is itself information -- if both sides are rational and know the other is rational, you each update on the other's willingness to trade until you converge and no trade happens. So if a trade is on offer, either your counterparty knows something you don't, or you're the mark. She called it the no-trade theorem.

(2) Marcus pushed back that this proves way too much. Trillions of dollars change hands every day, and most of it isn't two people disagreeing about value -- people sell because they need cash, retire, rebalance, whatever. Trade doesn't require disagreement, so the theorem can't condemn ordinary trading.

(3) Dana said fine, that rescues trade in general, but not *my* trade. I'm not a pension fund rebalancing; I'd be making a directional bet, and my counterparty on the other side of a retail order is probably an institution with better models and faster data. The theorem's logic applies to me specifically even if not to everyone.

(4) Marcus then said there's a deeper problem with her view: if everyone actually followed her reasoning, nobody would ever bother gathering information about companies, so prices wouldn't reflect anything, and then there'd be free money sitting there for anyone who did research -- which contradicts the no-trade conclusion. So the argument eats itself: someone has to find trading rational, or prices stop meaning anything.

(5) Dana's last move was basically: sure, somebody has to do the information-gathering, but that somebody isn't me. Buying index funds means free-riding on the informed traders' work, and that's the rational move precisely *because* stock-picking is a losing game for retail. Marcus said that's pretty rich -- she's telling me to free-ride on the very activity she just called irrational, so her position depends on other people being "irrational" forever.

I'm now paralyzed about the bonus, lol. Can you go through this move by move and tell me who's right about what -- including whether Dana's free-riding response actually works, and what (if anything) both of them are missing? Please answer in American English.
