I host a podcast about famous lost lands and the scholarship surrounding them, and next month I'm interviewing a classicist who defends a strong version of the "Plato invented Atlantis" position: on her view, the island was never garbled memory or travelers' tale, but a deliberate philosophical fiction that Plato constructed as a foil for his idealized primeval Athens, and she thinks virtually everything later written "in search of" Atlantis rests on a category mistake. My listeners, though, keep sending me the same passage from the Timaeus (23b), where the Egyptian priest chides Solon: "the genealogies which you related just now, Solon, concerning the people of your country, are little better than children's tales; for, in the first place, you remember but one deluge, though many had occurred previously." They take this as evidence that Plato was transmitting a genuine tradition of cyclical cataclysms and forgotten advanced civilizations — the same motif that modern lost-civilization writers and ancient-astronaut authors mine so heavily. I want to give her view a serious hearing while pressing it where it is most vulnerable, rather than letting the episode become a victory lap.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions. I'd like at least one question on each of the following, and for each, a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give: First, the truth-markers problem — why would an inventor of fiction have Critias wrap the story in sworn provenance (Solon, Egyptian temple records, precise chronology) and insist it is factual rather than mythical; her likely reply will be that verisimilitude is simply Plato's literary method, so the follow-up should press whether that answer erases any principled difference between Plato's device and a modern writer who dresses invention up as suppressed history. Second, the quoted passage itself — why a purely invented tale would bother giving Greece a deep, repeatedly destroyed past, and whether the naturalistic rival reading (a distorted memory of the Thera eruption and the fall of Minoan Crete, with the famous "misplaced zero" defense of the 9,000-year date) isn't just more economical than wholesale invention; anticipate that she'll answer that the geography, date, and scale match Thera poorly, and prepare a follow-up on whether the decimal-shift rescue is special pleading. Third, the hyper-specific detail in the Critias — the measurements in stades, the alternating belts of land and sea, the orichalcum — and what work such concreteness does on the parable reading. Fourth, the demarcation question I most want answered: if she is right that Plato fabricated the island, what general criteria separate philosophical myth-making from pseudohistory, such that citing Plato as a *source* (as Ignatius Donnelly and his successors do) is an error rather than merely a minority interpretation — and, conversely, what her view implies we should say about the grain-of-truth reading she rejects. Finally, one question on the earliest reception of the story: a dismissive remark along the lines of "the man who dreamed up the island also sank it" is attributed to Aristotle by way of Strabo, though the chain of transmission is murky, so frame the question to be illuminating whether or not she trusts the attribution.
