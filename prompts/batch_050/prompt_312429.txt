I got into an argument with a friend last week about internet memes and it sent me down a rabbit hole about the old memetics research program. In the late 90s there was this whole thing: Dawkins' replicator idea extended to culture, Blackmore's book, a dedicated journal, serious philosophers treating memes as units of cultural selection the way genes are units of biological selection. And then it more or less died. The journal folded, the dissertations dried up, and the word "meme" got repurposed to mean a captioned image with impact font on it. Meanwhile "cultural evolution" as a field is doing great, but as far as I can tell it mostly gets by without the meme concept at all -- it talks about transmission biases and population dynamics of traits and stays carefully agnostic about whether there are discrete replicating units in there somewhere.

My working hypothesis is that the debate stalled because the two sides were never actually arguing about the same thing. The critics were litigating whether culture literally contains replicators -- copying fidelity, something like a genotype/phenotype split, identifiable units you could count -- while the defenders were really making a methodological bet, that culture can be usefully modeled as if it did, and nobody ever specified what empirical fact would settle the literal question. But maybe that's too charitable: maybe there was a genuine empirical bet and the field just lost it. Or maybe the replicator question is ill-posed in a specific way, because cultural "copies" are reconstructed by minds rather than copied the way DNA is, so you're demanding a binary verdict on a graded phenomenon.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a book report on memetics. Is this disagreement verbal, empirical, about standards of evidence, or about which cases count as data? (Chain letters, melodies, folktales, religions, and internet memes seem to pull in different directions, which is suspicious in itself.) And given the diagnosis: what would actually have settled it -- a formal result, an experimental paradigm, a distinction like splitting "replication" into two or three notions -- or is this one of those cases where the question itself should just be abandoned, and the success of meme-free cultural evolution is itself the proof? Definately argue for your answer rather than surveying who said what.
