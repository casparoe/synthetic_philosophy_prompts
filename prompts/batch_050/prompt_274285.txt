I want to defend a position on Keynes and probability, and I want to know what it would take to lose. The position: Keynes's early claim that rational degrees of belief are only sometimes numerical, and sometimes not even comparable, together with his later distinction between the probability of a conclusion and what he called the weight of the argument behind it, identifies something real that orthodox Bayesianism does not capture. The standard rejoinders — Ramsey's subjectivist critique, the de Finetti and Savage representation results, and the contemporary move to imprecise or interval-valued credences — do not, on my view, refute this so much as decline to engage it: they show how a coherent agent must act given her credences, not whether the evidence itself warrants a unique credence in the first place. So: what would count against this position? Which formal results, cases, or arguments would show that non-numerical comparative probability is incoherent, or that weight collapses without remainder into second-order probability, ambiguity aversion, or confidence in a prior — and which objections would merely be awkward, forcing qualification rather than surrender? Please distinguish what would be decisive from what would be uncomfortable, and say whether defenders of the Keynesian view have anything they themselves concede as a defeater, or whether the position has been insulated by the standing accusation that its critics are answering a different question. Finally, indicate how confident you are in each part of your assessment and single out the claim you are least sure of.
