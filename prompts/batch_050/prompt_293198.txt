My philosophy reading group spent last month on decision theory, and we got bogged down in an argument about Hofstadter-style superrationality -- the idea that in situations where all parties reason alike, you should choose on the assumption that everyone will converge on the same answer you do, and so pick whichever answer it would be best for everyone to arrive at. Half the group insists superrationality is just evidential decision theory wearing a costume, since both tell you to cooperate in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a similar other. The other half insists the two must come apart somewhere, but nobody could actually build the case where they do. So: construct a thought experiment in which superrational reasoning and evidential decision theory prescribe different actions. Constraints: no perfect predictors, no stipulated clones or mind readers, and no exotic physics -- the similarity between the players must be something the protagonist knows only fallibly, the way you actually know that your neighbors or coworkers think like you. The scenario should be one paragraph, set in ordinary life (a neighborhood, an office, a volunteer effort), with payoffs a normal person could face, and it may involve more than two players if that helps. The two theories' verdicts must genuinely diverge, not merely diverge under a tendentious description of the same reasoning.

After presenting the scenario, show the work: first walk through the evidential decision theorist's calculation, including what correlation between the protagonist's choice and the others' choices the EDT reasoning relies on; then walk through the superrational agent's reasoning; then name the single structural feature of the case that makes the two theories peel apart -- my own suspicion is that it has something to do with how each theory handles imperfect correlation between like-minded agents, but say whether that is right. Next, tell me what happens if I strengthen the scenario so the players are perfectly symmetric in their reasoning: do the verdicts converge again, and if so, does that mean superrationality is EDT plus an idealizing assumption about convergence, or is the extra assumption doing real work that no amount of evidence about similarity could substitute for? Finally, play both sides of my reading group: give the best attack an evidential decision theorist could mount against the superrational verdict in your case (I expect it will turn on the fact that the others might not be superrational, and that the protagonist cannot assume they are), and then give the best superrationalist reply, being honest about whether the reply smuggles in a premise the EDT theorist has no reason to grant.
