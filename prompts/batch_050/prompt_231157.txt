So I'm leading a 75-minute seminar next week on the master-slave dialectic for second-year philosophy students. They've done Descartes' cogito and a bit of Kant on self-consciousness last term, but no Hegel at all, and most of them know the phrase "thesis antithesis synthesis" from somewhere, which I already dread. I don't want you to explain the dialectic to me -- I want a plan for how I should explain it to them.

Specifically: what has to come first and why. My instinct is to start with the life-and-death struggle, but I suspect something about self-consciousness needing *another* self-consciousness (and why desire/eating-the-object doesn't cut it) has to be in place before the struggle makes any sense, otherwise it just looks like two guys fighting. Tell me the dependency order of the pieces.

Second: the carrying example. Everyone seems to teach this with some variant of two strangers meeting in the state of nature. A colleague suggested I instead build the whole thing around a workplace case -- an overworked manager who is completely dependent on an assistant the manager doesn't respect, so the assistant does all the actual work while the manager's recognition of the assistant is worthless because it comes from someone the assistant can't respect. I'm tempted because it makes the "slave wins through work" bit concrete, but I'm worried it smuggles in the resolution too early or distorts the fear-of-death stage, which no office job has. Should I use it, use the state of nature version, or use something else, and what exactly must the example have and *lack* (e.g., should the two parties start equal? is it a problem that the workplace case has a third party, the company?).

Third: the misconception map. Give me the wrong readings these students will predictably form, in the order they'll form them as the seminar unfolds, and where in my sequence I should head each one off rather than letting it crystallize. On my list so far: (a) thesis-antithesis-synthesis with the master as thesis and slave as antithesis, (b) reading it as actual history or anthropology, (c) "recognition" heard as mutual respect so they expect a nice liberal ending where both sides learn to value each other, (d) the slave's victory read as a feel-good underdog story rather than something ambiguous, (e) the fear of death as just "being scared of dying" rather than doing structural work. Correct me if any of these aren't actually live risks for this audience, and add ones I've missed.

Fourth: what should I explicitly leave out at this level and *say* I'm leaving out, so they know it's there -- I'm guessing the Stoicism/Scepticism/Unhappy Consciousness continuation, Kojeve's reading, and maybe the lord/bondsman translation thing, but you tell me.

Finally, one question I can put to them in the last ten minutes that would show whether they got the *dialectical* part -- i.e. why the master's victory is self-undermining on its own terms -- rather than just the plot summary. Ideally something that students who only absorbed "slave good, master bad" will get wrong.
