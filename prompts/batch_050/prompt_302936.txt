I cover the online-politics beat for a mid-sized magazine, and there's a phrase that has basically taken over our pitch meetings: that the communities I write about -- supplement-trading longevity forums, certain corners of finance Twitter, a fandom or two -- are "basically cults." A colleague of mine used it last week to justify a fairly aggressive editorial stance: if these spaces really are cults, then the members are effectively brainwashed, which licenses writing about them as victims to be rescued rather than adults with views, and licenses platforms treating exit from these groups the way you'd treat cult deprogramming (cut the person off from the group first, reason with them later). I've pushed back, mostly on gut feeling, and I want to actually stress-test the analogy rather than win by vibes.

Here's what I'd like: first, spell out what the cult analogy gets right, with an honest mapping table -- what plays the role of the charismatic leader, the sacred doctrine, the isolation from outsiders, the sunk costs of leaving. Then take seriously a distinction I read somewhere (I think it was a philosopher named Nguyen?) between an epistemic bubble, where contrary voices just never reach you, and an echo chamber, where you've been pre-equipped to distrust contrary voices on arrival -- disagreement itself becomes evidence that the dissenter is compromised. The cult comparison seems to lean hard on the second mechanism, since actual cults run on engineered distrust of family and outside media. So: does the mapping survive once you separate bubbles from chambers? Does the average longevity forum or fan community actually run on active distrust-manufacture the way a cult does, or is something thinner going on -- algorithmic sorting, identity, hobbyist epistemics? And the big one: if the analogy partially breaks, what happens to my colleague's two conclusions? Does the "they're victims, not reasoners" framing survive? Does the "cut them off first" prescription survive, given that cult deprogramming works (when it works) on people physically removed from the group, which is nothing like a guy closing an app? If there's a better analogy waiting in the wings -- an immune system that treats outside arguments as pathogens, a guild, a parish -- tell me which one does the most work and which conclusion it would actually support.
