I spent my career in experimental physics and have only recently taken up philosophy of science, so I would like an analysis that is philosophically careful without assuming I know the literature's labels. Take the displacement of Newtonian gravitation by general relativity and analyze it as a test case for the notion of verisimilitude -- the idea, associated with Popper, that a strictly false theory can nonetheless be closer to the truth than another strictly false theory, and that science progresses by such approximations. First, work out what any verisimilitude account must claim about this episode: that Newton is false yet nearer the truth than, say, Descartes' vortex mechanics, and that Einstein is nearer still, and what standard of "nearness" could deliver those judgments when both theories are, on the realist reading, simply untrue. Then press on the points where the mapping gets uncomfortable: general relativity does not retain Newton's gravitational force as an approximately correct posit but eliminates it in favor of curved spacetime, so in what sense is Newton's *theory* -- as opposed to its predictions in a limited regime -- close to Einstein's? Does this episode vindicate the verisimilitude project, or does it suggest that the intuition of "getting closer" survives only if cashed out in terms of empirical success rather than approximate truth of content, in which case the realist gloss was doing no work? Please note explicitly where the theory falls silent rather than forcing a verdict.
