I work as a statistician on predictive modeling, and that is the vocabulary in which I do my clearest thinking: estimands and estimators, latent parameters versus realized counts, identifiability, held-out prediction, misspecified models, confounded covariates. I have been reading about the propensity interpretation of fitness in the philosophy of biology, and the old charge that "survival of the fittest" is a tautology, and I suspect the whole dispute has an exact counterpart in my world -- but I want the translation done carefully, by someone fluent in both vocabularies, and I want to be told what does not survive it.

The material to translate, as I understand it. First, the tautology objection: if "the fittest" are defined as those that in fact survive and reproduce, then the theory's central claim reduces to "those that survive, survive," and nothing could count against it. Second, the propensity reply: fitness is not actual reproductive success but a probabilistic propensity or disposition to leave descendants, which an organism can possess even when, by bad luck, it dies childless -- just as a biased coin can land tails. Third, the difficulties that reply inherits: the propensity seems to be measurable only through the very outcomes it is supposed to explain, which looks like the circle reappearing at the level of measurement; fitness appears to be relative to an environment, so the same trait carries a different fitness in a different setting; and there is a persistent ambiguity about whether fitness attaches to individual organisms or to trait types across a population.

Please restate each of these as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary. My instinct, for calibration, is that the propensity reply says fitness is a latent parameter and reproductive success is a realized count drawn from it, and that the tautology objection says the parameter is only ever estimated from the counts, so the framework cannot be disconfirmed -- but I want you to correct my instinct wherever it distorts the philosophy rather than translate it.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care most about. Which philosophical claims translate cleanly? Which translate only with distortion, and what exactly is added or dropped? Which have no counterpart in statistical vocabulary at all -- I suspect the dispositional, counterfactual reading of "propensity" itself may be one -- and in each such case, tell me whether the gap is a limitation of my framework or evidence that the original philosophical notion was less substantive than it appeared.

I want a committed answer, not a survey of options: take a definite position on whether the tautology objection, once translated, is a genuine and unresolved problem (say, a real identifiability failure that the propensity interpretation merely relabels) or whether the translation dissolves it, and defend that verdict with reasons.
