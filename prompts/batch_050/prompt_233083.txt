So here's the situation. Our professional society (an academic association, about 3,000 members) elects its officers by plain plurality with one nominee per faction usually, and there's been grumbling for years about strategic voting and spoiler candidates. I got roped into the governance committee and, being the only person on it who's read any social choice theory, I've designed a proposal we're presenting at the next open meeting: switch to ranked ballots using a Condorcet method, specifically one where if there's a Condorcet winner they win, and if there's a cycle you look at the three candidates with the most first-place support and elect whichever of them has the smallest worst pairwise defeat. My pitch, which I've written up in a little memo, is basically: yes, Gibbard-Satterthwaite tells us no reasonable deterministic rule with three or more candidates is fully strategy-proof, so stop chasing that; instead pick the rule where manipulation is rarest and hardest in practice, and I have simulations (spatial preference models plus some ballot data borrowed from another organization's past elections) suggesting my rule gets manipulated less often than instant runoff and less often than Borda, and that profitable manipulations require coordinating lots of voters on fine-grained information about everyone else's rankings, which almost never happens in our electorate.

The meeting audience is: four or five actual social choice and voting theorists from the econ and CS departments (two of whom work on computational social choice and one of whom I'm pretty sure has written on manipulation complexity), a bunch of empirically-minded political scientists who mostly know Duverger and Arrow but not the details, and then the general membership, i.e. ordinary academics who care about legitimacy and simplicity and whether they'll understand the ballot.

Prepare me for the Q&A. I want the objections roughly in the order they'll come and weighted by how much damage they do, each one phrased the way that specific audience member would actually phrase it (the theorists will talk about unrestricted domain, single-peakedness doing all my work, whether my simulations say anything about equilibrium incentives rather than sincere-preference manipulation rates, later-no-harm and exhausted ballots; the political scientists will talk about legitimacy and about Arrow as if Arrow settles things; the members will ask why not just do instant runoff like the city council does). For each one give me what to concede outright, what to hold firm on, and a reply I can give in under two minutes, plus a note on where the reply is soft.
