I am designing the opening session of a graduate seminar on decision theory and want to hand students, before they encounter any formal models, a specification of what an adequate normative theory of choice under ambiguity must accomplish. Please write that specification for me: a list of desiderata that any such theory should meet, with the case or consideration motivating each, and each marked as non-negotiable for you versus a preference characteristic of a particular camp (strict Bayesianism, multiple-priors approaches, the smooth-ambiguity tradition, and so on).

Assume the theory's purpose is normative: it is a standard to which a rational agent facing genuine uncertainty, not mere risk, could reasonably be held -- not a predictive model of how experimental subjects in fact behave.

The list should address, at minimum, the following questions, in each case framed as a desideratum rather than a history lesson: whether the theory must reduce to expected utility maximization whenever objective probabilities are supplied; whether it should accommodate Ellsberg-type preferences as permissible, require them, or treat them as rationally neutral; how it should handle choice problems in which probabilities are not merely unknown but undefined or adversarially determined; what it demands regarding completeness of preferences, reduction of compound lotteries, dynamic consistency, and the possibility of separating an agent's beliefs from her attitudes toward uncertainty; and how it should treat symmetric uncertainty -- cases where the agent's ignorance is itself evenly balanced, such as a bet on a coin known to be biased in an unknown direction.

To keep the specification honest, I have constructed one test case it must be checked against. An investor may buy a bond whose payoff depends on a publicly audited coin flip, or an otherwise identical bond whose terms are set by an informed counterparty who chooses the win probability, anywhere within stated bounds, after learning the investor's position. Should an adequate theory treat these as the same problem, and if not, which desideratum forces them apart?

The part I care most about: state whether some subset of your desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable -- for instance, completeness together with reduction of compound lotteries together with permissibility of Ellsberg preferences -- and, if so, present that incompatibility as a result in its own right rather than papering over it. Conclude with a brief, restrained scoring of the main contenders (maximin expected utility, Choquet expected utility, the smooth model, and alpha-maximin) against the list, identifying which single desideratum does the most work in excluding candidates.
