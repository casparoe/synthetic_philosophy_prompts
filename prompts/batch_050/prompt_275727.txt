I'm drafting a short position paper for a graduate seminar on conditionals, and before I commit to it I want an audit of whether my views are jointly tenable. Here is the position as I currently hold it:

1. The distinction between indicative and subjunctive (counterfactual) conditionals is a genuine semantic divide, not a superficial matter of grammatical mood. The standard Oswald/Kennedy pair — where the indicative version of the conditional about Kennedy's assassination strikes nearly everyone as acceptable while the subjunctive version seems doubtful or false — shows that the two constructions are evaluated against different things: roughly, the speaker's available information in the first case, and how the world would have gone in the second.

2. Linguistic meaning is compositional: the semantic value of a complex sentence is determined by the semantic values of its parts and the way they are combined.

3. Indicative conditionals are truth-apt. They express propositions that are simply true or false, and their truth values do not vary with who is speaking or what the speaker happens to know.

4. The acceptability of an indicative conditional goes by the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent — and I want this to be more than a pragmatic observation about assertion. Whatever proposition an indicative conditional expresses, the probability of that proposition's being true should equal the corresponding conditional probability.

5. Subjunctive conditionals are to be evaluated by similarity among possible worlds: a counterfactual is true just in case its consequent holds at the worlds most similar to actuality in which its antecedent holds.

6. At least some conditionals with impossible antecedents are substantive. Disputes about, say, what would follow arithmetically if some particular well-known conjecture turned out to be false have correct answers — some such conditionals are true and others false — and the parties to these disputes are not all speaking vacuously.

Please treat this as an audit rather than a survey: I am not asking for references or for a census of positions in the literature. What I want is (a) which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, ordered by severity, with enough of the reasoning shown that I can see the collision rather than just be told it exists; (b) which pairings look like conflicts but dissolve once the commitments are stated carefully, and what the careful statement is; and (c) for each genuine tension, what the least costly revision would be and what it would cost me elsewhere in the position. Finally, if one single revision would relieve the most pressure across the whole set, tell me which it is. I have no candidate revision in mind, so please do not assume I am attached to any particular member of the list.
