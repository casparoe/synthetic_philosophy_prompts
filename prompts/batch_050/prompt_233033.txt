I'm a journalist who covers behavioral science and technology, and I'm working on a long-form piece about the quiet delegation of choice — recommender systems that decide what you watch, AI assistants that plan your week, wellness apps that tell you what to eat and when to sleep. My editors keep circling one question: what, exactly, would be lost if delegating more and more of our decisions made people reliably happier? In reporting the piece I've kept returning to the Grand Inquisitor episode in The Brothers Karamazov — the Inquisitor's insistence that human beings do not want freedom, that it only makes them miserable, and that they will gratefully hand it to whoever relieves them of the burden of choosing, through some combination of bread, miracle, mystery, and authority. Dostoevsky's answer within the story is famously not an argument at all. I want a constructed thought experiment as the centerpiece of the piece, one that forces the Inquisitor's challenge in its hardest possible form rather than the easy form where coercion or hidden costs do the moral work.

Build me a single scenario, set in a plausible near-present society, with no dystopia, no coercion, no deception, and no catastrophe lurking offstage, in which a competent and fully informed adult voluntarily and stably hands over a substantial domain of life decisions — career moves, daily schedule, major purchases, perhaps more — to an institution or system of your design. By every measure the protagonist would themselves endorse — their own self-reports, health, relationships, long-run life satisfaction — they must be durably and significantly better off than when they chose for themselves, and they must know this. The trade has to be clean: no manipulation induced the consent, no alternative was foreclosed, they could exit at any time and choose not to, and the gains must not come from escaping some pathology or addiction, since that would let a defender of freedom dismiss the case as a treatment rather than a trade. In short, I want freedom and happiness to come apart with every usual confound removed, so that the only thing that could possibly be lost is the freedom itself.

Then demonstrate that the case meets its specifications. State exactly what the Inquisitor would take the scenario to prove and why his three temptations analysis predicts its outcome. State what a defender of freedom as intrinsically valuable must locate as the loss, given that nothing welfare-like is missing, and whether that loss is something the piece's readers would recognize as a real cost or only a philosopher's bookkeeping. And state what Dostoevsky's own counterweights — the silent kiss, and Zosima's ethic of active love later in the novel — could say about a surrender the protagonist continues to ratify with open eyes. Finally, do the engineering review: name the single feature of your scenario that does all the work, describe what happens to each side's verdict if that feature is removed, and give the strongest objection available to someone who argues the scenario cannot be coherently specified at all — for instance, that holding fully informed and stably better off fixed simultaneously smuggles in a contested theory of what happiness is.
