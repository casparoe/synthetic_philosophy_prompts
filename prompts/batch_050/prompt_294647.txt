Is there an established name for the following configuration, and if not, what should it be called? Two instances first. (1) A foundation divides a grant among three recipients who disagree about what makes a life go well: the first holds that welfare is pleasant experience and spends her share accordingly, the second holds that it is getting whatever one most wants, and the third that it is accomplishing objectively worthwhile projects. Each recipient is, by their own account, strictly better off than before — yet fix any one of the three accounts, apply it to everyone, and at least one recipient comes out worse than under some available alternative division. (2) An employer replaces a uniform benefits package with a menu from which each employee assembles their own; everyone ends up with what they judge best for themselves, though by any single theory of well-being applied across the whole workforce, some of the choices look like mistakes. The pattern: an outcome every affected party endorses as an improvement for them, under their own standard, while no shared standard certifies it as an improvement for all. This is not a Pareto improvement in the ordinary sense, since there is no common measure on which everyone gains, and it is weaker than the condition of being an improvement under every candidate theory of welfare on the table, since here each party passes under only one theory — their own. Does anything in welfare economics or political philosophy pick out exactly this? If so, how does it relate to ordinary Pareto improvements, to theory-robust improvements, and to ideals of anti-paternalism or state neutrality, and is it ever a condition a policymaker should deliberately aim at? If nothing established fits, coin a term, define it, and give a couple of boundary cases — for instance, where one party holds no coherent view of their own good at all.
