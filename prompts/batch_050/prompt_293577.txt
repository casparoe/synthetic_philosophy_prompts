So I'm a backend/ML engineer, no philosophy training, and I recently fell down a wikipedia rabbit hole about the "problem of verisimilitude" -- the idea that even if all our scientific theories are strictly false, some are *closer to the truth* than others, and science makes progress by moving closer. From what I gather Popper proposed a formal definition of this in the 60s, and then in the mid-70s two guys (Miller and Tichý, I think?) independently proved that on his definition no false theory can ever be closer to the truth than any other false theory, which kind of detonated the whole project. Since then there's been decades of repair attempts and apparently nobody agrees on any of them.

Here's why this bugs me as an engineer. In ML we compare approximate models all the time using loss functions, so "which false model is closer" feels like it should be a solved-shaped problem. But then I tried a toy case. Say the true state of some system is three facts: A true, B true, C false. Theory 1 says A true, B false, C false (two right, one wrong). Theory 2 says A false, B true, C true (one right, two wrong). Any Hamming-distance-style metric says Theory 1 wins, easy. But now redescribe the exact same situation using different primitive variables -- like define D = "A and B have the same truth value" and E = "B and C have the same truth value" -- and I found I could make the ordering flip depending on which description I used, even though nothing about the world changed. Apparently this is close to one of the actual known objections, but I arrived at it by accident and it made the whole enterprise look less like "find the right metric" and more like "the metric smuggles in a choice of vocabulary, and nobody has a principled way to pick the vocabulary."

So my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the debate has stalled because it's not really a technical disagreement about the right formalism. It's two (or more) groups answering different questions under one name -- one group wants a logical relation between theories and the actual truth (which nobody can observe), another group wants a methodological rule for which theory to *prefer given the evidence*, and maybe a third just wants to vindicate the intuition that Newton is closer to the truth than Aristotle. And a distance-to-a-point-you-can't-see might just be the wrong kind of object to define. Is that right, or is there a real substantive disagreement hiding in here -- about facts, about which desiderata are non-negotiable, about which cases count as data? I'd like a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the proposals, plus an honest answer about what would count as progress: a killer example, an impossibility theorem over a fixed list of desiderata, everyone agreeing to split the concept into pieces, or should the whole notion get retired like caloric fluid? If the answer is "nothing will resolve it," explain why rather than just saying philosophy is like that.
