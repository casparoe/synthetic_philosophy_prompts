Exam question: "Consider the sorites paradox as it arises for the predicate 'heap.' (a) Explain how epistemicism and supervaluationism each diagnose what goes wrong in the paradox, making clear what each view says about the truth-value of a borderline application of a vague predicate. (b) State what each view implies about the law of excluded middle and the principle of bivalence. (c) Present what you take to be the strongest objection facing each view, and then say which view you find more defensible overall, giving reasons."

Student's answer:

(a) Epistemicism holds that vague predicates draw perfectly sharp boundaries: there is an exact number of grains at which a heap ceases to be a heap, but we cannot know where the boundary falls. Our ignorance is not a merely practical limitation that finer measurement could overcome; it is a necessary consequence of the epistemic position of speakers whose usage fixes the predicate's meaning without ever fixing it precisely. The sorites argument is accordingly valid but unsound: its induction premise, that removing a single grain never turns a heap into a non-heap, is simply false, even though we are in no position to identify the false instance. A borderline predication, such as calling a borderline man bald, is either true or false — we just cannot know which.

Supervaluationism instead denies that our linguistic practice settles a single boundary. A sharpening is an admissible way of making a vague predicate fully precise; a sentence is supertrue when true on every admissible sharpening, superfalse when false on all of them, and otherwise without a truth value. With a borderline Harry, "Harry is bald" is neither true nor false. The sorites is blocked because its induction premise is not supertrue: on each sharpening there is a cutoff, but the cutoff's location varies from sharpening to sharpening, so the premise fails on none of them individually yet is not true on all. This yields a curious but correct consequence: the claim that there exists an exact number of grains separating heaps from non-heaps is supertrue, since it holds on every sharpening, even though for every particular number n, the claim that n is the cutoff is not supertrue.

(b) Because a classical theorem such as "Harry is bald or Harry is not bald" comes out true on every admissible sharpening, supervaluationism preserves classical logic, including bivalence. Epistemicism likewise preserves both excluded middle and bivalence, unproblematically, since every sentence is determinately true or false even when unknowably so.

(c) The strongest objection to epistemicism is the apparent mystery of the boundary: our use of "heap" never involved any decision about exact grain counts, so the view seems to require semantic facts that float free of anything speakers ever did. The strongest objection to supervaluationism is higher-order vagueness: the line between admissible and inadmissible sharpenings looks as vague as the original boundary, so positing a sharp distinction there seems to reintroduce sharp cutoffs at the metalinguistic level. The supervaluationist can defuse this objection by treating "admissible" as itself vague, so that there is no sharp line anywhere in the theory.

On balance I find supervaluationism more defensible. It respects the thought that vagueness is a semantic phenomenon rather than a form of ignorance, and its costs — truth-value gaps and the witnessless existential noted above — strike me as intelligible prices rather than mysteries.
