In August 2010, thirty-three miners were trapped deep underground after the collapse of the San José mine in Chile. The Chilean government, with substantial international assistance, mounted a rescue operation that took 69 days and cost, by common estimates, tens of millions of dollars, and the effort was celebrated around the world as obviously the right thing to do. Analyze this episode through the lens of the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism. Begin with the uncomfortable direction: a straightforward aggregate-welfare calculation plausibly condemns the rescue, since the same resources devoted to public health or road safety in Chile would likely have saved more lives. Then work out what the separateness objection actually contributes here. The objection holds that summing costs and benefits across distinct persons ignores that the burdens and gains fall to different individuals, so that trading off within one life is morally unlike trading off between lives — but does that vindicate the rescue, or is the objection simply silent, since it criticizes aggregation without supplying a decision rule of its own? Consider whether the distinction between identifiable and statistical victims is doing the real moral work in the public reaction, and whether that distinction follows from taking separateness seriously or is instead a rival to it. Finally, run the analysis in reverse: non-aggregative views inspired by the separateness objection notoriously struggle to explain why we should save the greater number when claims conflict, so does the miners case — where nearly everyone's intuition favored enormous expenditure to save a known few — count as evidence against the objection, or does it show that the objection was never meant to settle rescue cases at all?
