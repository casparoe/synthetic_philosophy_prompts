I've been working through Spinoza's Ethics and have hit what looks to me like a structural incoherence at the heart of the whole book, and I'd like to know whether it's real or whether I'm missing something.

Part I establishes that nothing is contingent: everything that exists and everything that happens follows from the necessity of the divine nature, with the same kind of necessity by which it follows from the nature of a triangle that its angles equal two right angles. Human decisions are no exception; the will is not a free cause but is determined to each volition by another cause, and so on ad infinitum. Fair enough — that's the necessitarianism Spinoza is famous for.

But then Part V opens by promising something that seems to presuppose exactly the freedom Part I ruled out. Spinoza says the final part is concerned with "the way leading to freedom," and that he will treat of the power of reason, "showing how far the reason can control the emotions, and what is the nature of Mental Freedom or Blessedness." A way to freedom, advice about controlling the emotions, a promise that the wise man is more powerful than the ignorant — this is the language of guidance, addressed to a reader who could take the way or fail to take it.

Here is my difficulty. If every volition is necessitated, then whether I come to understand my emotions, whether reason gains strength in me, and whether I end up among the wise or the ignorant are all already fixed by the infinite causal chain. If I am determined to become wise, Spinoza's counsel is superfluous; if I am not, it is useless. Either way, "the way leading to freedom" reads like a map offered to someone whose destination was never in question. It seems as though Part V can only be a description of what will happen to me, dressed up in the grammar of an exhortation — and that Spinoza either didn't notice this, or quietly reintroduced the free will he demolished in order to give the book a point.

So my question: is this a genuine contradiction, with Part V undermining Part I (or vice versa)? Or is there a way to make sense of rational counsel, self-improvement, and "the wise man is more powerful than the ignorant" under strict necessitarianism — such that Spinoza's advice can be genuinely efficacious rather than merely predictive? If I'm conflating something here — necessity with fatalism, determination with compulsion, or freedom with uncaused choice — I'd like the conflation identified precisely, with an account of what Spinoza's freedom actually consists in and what follows about how the Ethics is meant to be used by the reader it addresses.
