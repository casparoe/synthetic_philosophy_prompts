My roommate just got back from her epistemology seminar with an argument she swears proves that nobody knows anything at all, not even boring stuff like my keys being in my jacket, which I checked ten seconds ago. It goes like this. (1) If you know something, you're always in a position to know that you know it. Her defense: imagine someone who knows it rained yesterday but is genuinely unsure whether they know it — she says that person doesn't exist, because if you can't even tell whether you know, then what you had was at best a lucky belief; knowledge just is the state where the question is settled. (2) If you're in a position to know something, then a moment of reflection on your own state is enough for you to actually know it — there's no further fact about the world you still have to go check, it's all upstairs already. (3) So a single piece of knowledge generates an endless tower: you know p, you know that you know p, you know that you know that you know p, and so on forever. (4) Every rung of the tower is a new claim that could have been false — that the rung below was really knowledge and not just confident belief is a substantive claim each time — so each rung needs its own justification, you can't just ride the justification from the rung below for free. (5) But no finite human mind contains infinitely many seperately justified beliefs. (6) So the tower never gets started: nobody ever knows anything in the first place.

I obviously refuse to accept (6), but when she says each premise out loud with enough confidence they all sound atleast sort of plausible. Tell me which step I should actually attack, and why that step manages to look fine even though it's the weak point. Then walk me through what it costs to reject each of the others instead, because I want to know my options. Fair warning, I have a soft spot for (1) — it seems like the entire difference between knowledge and a lucky guess — and (5) feels non-negotiable too, I definately don't have infinitely many beliefs. If your fix ends up being that knowledge doesn't have to be fully visible from the inside, say so plainly, but then explain why that isn't just giving up on the concept of knowledge altogether.
