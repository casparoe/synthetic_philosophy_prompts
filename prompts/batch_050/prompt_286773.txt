Ok here's a thought experiment for thinking about the Hart–Devlin debate (the one that kicked off around the Wolfenden report and whether private, consensual, "immoral" conduct can be criminalized just because it's immoral).

Imagine a country where engineers have perfected "vice simulators": devices that plug into the brain and let the user vividly experience committing acts like torture or murder from the perpetrator's perspective, in hyper-realistic detail. The experiences are entirely private, no real victims exist, and — stipulate this as fact — long-term studies show users become slightly *less* violent afterwards. Still, when the public finds out about the devices there's massive outrage and disgust. Most citizens say a society that tolerates this sort of thing isn't one they recognize as theirs, and some worry their kids will grow up in a culture where the line between fantasy and reality gets fuzzy, though nobody can point to any concrete harm. The legislature is now considering banning the manufacture, sale and use of the devices on purely moral grounds.

Write an essay that does three things. First, work out how Devlin's legal moralism and Hart's critique of it would each handle this case — be specific about wich of their actual arguments does the work (e.g. Devlin's shared-morality-as-social-bond idea versus Hart's point that a society doesn't need one frozen moral code to survive, and that moral change isn't treason). Second, give the strongest objection each thinker could raise against the other's treatment of the case — including whether Devlin can tell genuine moral consensus apart from mere disgust-driven prejudice, and whether Hart's harm principle secretly smuggles in moral premises of its own. Third, reach a verdict: should the simulators be banned or not, and which framework (or what repair to one of them) actually delivers the right answer? Don't sit on the fence.
