For my senior-year independent study in philosophy, I am writing a paper on Leibniz's claim that ours is the best of all possible worlds, and before I evaluate any actual theodicy I want to know what I should be demanding of one. In the Theodicy (1710), Leibniz insists that what is being called best is not a planet, a people, or a century, but literally everything, in a passage I keep coming back to:

"I call 'World' the whole succession and the whole agglomeration of all existent things, lest it be said that several worlds could have existed in different times and different places."

Write the desiderata for an adequate solution to the problem of evil that takes this passage seriously — that is, for any account that defends a perfect creator by appealing to the goodness of a whole world-history rather than the comfort of any part of it. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it. I am especially interested in requirements that the passage itself seems to generate, such as:

- how goodness can be predicated of an entire "succession" at all — whether a world's goodness sums across times and persons, and what follows if it does not;
- whether "best" even has a clear meaning when the candidates are infinitely many whole universes;
- what must be true of particular evils (a plague, a crime, a wasted life) for them to be justified as parts of an unsurpassable whole, without making the creator their author and without implying that nothing here could have been locally better;
- what, if anything, a being situated inside the world, with access only to a fragment of the succession, could be in a position to check.

Mark which requirements are non-negotiable for the project to count as a theodicy at all, and which are preferences of a particular theological or philosophical camp. If some subset of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied — for example, if the demand that the whole be unsurpassable pulls against the demand that every part be improvable — say so explicitly and show the collision, since that is the part of the paper I most want to get right. Then, briefly, test Leibniz's own solution, as you understand it, against the finished list: which requirement does it satisfy most convincingly, and which does it fail most conspicuously?
