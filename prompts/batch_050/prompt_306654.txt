On May 6, 2010, the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell roughly 1,000 points — about nine percent — in a matter of minutes, and recovered most of the loss almost as quickly. The immediate trigger, according to the subsequent regulatory investigation, was a single very large sell order in stock-index futures, placed by a mutual-fund company and executed by an algorithm with simple instructions: sell at a rate equal to a fixed percentage of the previous minute's trading volume, with no constraints on price and no overall time limit. As the algorithm sold, high-frequency trading firms absorbed and then re-offloaded the contracts, liquidity providers withdrew, and the selling algorithm — keying its pace to volume that its own activity was helping to inflate — kept selling into the cascade. At the height of the episode, shares of at least one major blue-chip company changed hands for a penny while others were quoted at $100,000; exchanges later cancelled thousands of trades. No one had intended, predicted, or in any meaningful sense chosen any of this.

I want an analysis of the Flash Crash as a case study in AI alignment — specifically in the theory of misspecified objectives: proxy optimization, Goodhart's law, specification gaming, and corrigibility. Not the market microstructure, which I understand reasonably well: the alignment theory. Please address the following.

First, the mapping. Who or what is the agent here, and what is its reward function? Is the execution algorithm best understood as misaligned with its operator (it did what was asked, but the ask was bad), as perfectly aligned with a misspecified objective (and so a pure outer-alignment failure), or as a case where that very distinction breaks down? A good answer should be disciplined about this rather than letting the vocabulary float free of the mechanics.

Second, the harder question. Arguably every algorithm involved behaved as its operator intended: the seller's algorithm followed its instructions, the market-makers widened spreads and withdrew exactly as their risk parameters dictated, and no system defected from its specification. Yet the ensemble produced an outcome no principal wanted. Most alignment theory is developed in the idiom of one agent and one principal. Does the Flash Crash show that catastrophic misalignment can be a property of systems of individually aligned agents — and if so, is that an anomaly the theory has not absorbed, or simply Goodhart's law operating at a level of description the theory already permits? Say where you think the theory is genuinely silent here rather than forcing it to speak.

Third, work the analysis in the other direction. Alignment theorists often frame catastrophic risk as requiring highly capable, possibly adversarial optimizers. The Flash Crash involved systems that were, by the standards of the field, neither. Does the episode therefore undercut the claim that advanced capability is the crux, confirm it (nothing irreversible happened, after all), or show that the real variables are autonomy, speed, and tightness of feedback coupling rather than intelligence? And consider the remedy actually adopted — exchange-level circuit breakers, a kind of institutional kill switch. Is that a success story for corrigibility-by-design, or does the fact that it works only by halting the entire system illustrate how little the episode resembles the alignment problems that trouble theorists?

Where the alignment framework simply has nothing to say about some feature of the episode — for instance, whether the original order was a reasonable human judgment — I would rather you mark the silence explicitly than stretch the theory to cover it.
