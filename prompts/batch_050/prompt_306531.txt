So I'm a novelist, mid-draft on a book, and I've wandered into a fight I want to practice. Please play a committed defender of the conceivability argument for dualism -- the zombie argument, Chalmers-style: conceiving of a being physically identical to me but lacking consciousness is supposed to show that consciousness is something over and above the physical. Play this person sharp, not a strawman: they know the standard moves about ideal vs. prima facie conceivability, they can concede small points but not the main one, and they stay in character until I say "stop." Keep replies to a paragraph so it feels like an actual argument at a bar, not a lecture.

Here's my opening move, and I think as a novelist I have standing to make it: conceiving of zombies is my day job. When I write background characters -- the waiter, the crowd at the funeral -- I conceive of them in complete behavioral and physical detail and I simply do not bother giving them any inner life at all. Nobody has ever called those passages inconceivable or contradictory. So conceivability, at least the kind I traffic in, is cheap and easy, and if it's that easy it can't be a guide to what's metaphysically possible. Why should a cognitive act I perform on deadline, with coffee, tell anyone anything about the fundamental structure of reality? Doesn't the fact that any competent hack writer can "conceive" a zombie show the argument proves too much?
