Construct a thought experiment in which Sen's liberal paradox shows up — weak Pareto plus minimal liberalism (at least two people each decisive over at least one pair of social states differing only in thier own personal business) jointly forcing an intransitive social ordering — but where none of the agents has nosy, meddlesome, or spiteful preferences. Every preference that does any work in the case has to be either purely self-regarding or genuinely benevolent: altruistic concern for the other person's welfare, deferring to the other's enjoyment, wanting the joint evening to go well, that sort of thing. In the standard telling (prude and lewd, one copy of the scandalous book) the conflict runs on meddlesomeness — the prude doesn't want the lewd reading it, the lewd wants the prude to read it — and I want a version that makes the paradox look like a problem about politeness or mutual deference instead of nosiness.

Constraints: two agents if you can pull it off, three only if you really need them; an everyday setting (flatmates, siblings, coworkers splitting shifts — no trolleys, no aliens); each agent's decisive pair must differ solely in that agent's own choice; and the individual preference orderings have to be complete and transitive — no smuggling the cycle in at the individual level.

This is for a seminar handout, so after the scenario do the following. (1) Write out the social states and each person's full preference ordering over them, with a sentence of motivation for each ordering. (2) Show which social rankings weak Pareto forces and which the decisiveness conditions force, and exhibit the cycle explicitly. (3) Say which single individual preference in your setup is doing the real work, and what happens to the case if you flip just that one — does it collapse back into a harmony between rights and efficiency, or can the paradox be reconstituted nearby? (4) Take seriously the obvious objection that your benevolent preferences are just meddlesomeness in disguise: paternalistic concern about what someone else does in their own sphere is arguably exactly what Sen's example already had. Where's the line between caring about someone and meddling in thier choices, and does your case actually stay on the right side of it? If you conclude it doesn't fully, say so and explain what that shows about the paradox itself.
