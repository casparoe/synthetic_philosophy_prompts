Before I commit to a method for a long essay on the historiography of science and of politics, I want the specification first. Write the desiderata for an adequate account of historical narration with respect to what Butterfield called the Whig interpretation -- the temptation to write the past as if it were striving toward the present, with the present's winners as its protagonists and its losers as obstacles or forerunners of things they never intended. What must such an account deliver to count as a cure rather than a different flavor of the disease?

Concretely, the list should state: the cases any anti-Whig methodology must handle without falling apart (among them: the history of a science whose current theories really are better than its past ones, where writing the story as progress seems not merely tempting but true; a moral episode such as abolition, where refusing progress-talk seems itself a failure of judgment; an abandoned research program like phlogiston, which was rational by the standards of its time and wrong by ours; and a movement that lost, whose participants can only be understood through hopes the historian knows were disappointed); the distinctions the account must keep separate (explaining an outcome versus vindicating it, judging the past by our values versus judging it by values we claim were already available to it, progress in knowledge versus moral progress, and what actors thought they were doing versus what they turned out to have been doing); and the constraints it must respect (contingency of outcomes, the obligation to reconstruct dead alternatives as live ones, the historian's own unavoidable standpoint).

Give the case or consideration motivating each requirement, mark which are non-negotiable for any serious historiography and which look like the preferences of particular camps (internalist historians of science, moralists, genealogists in the Nietzschean line, and so on), and flag any subset of the desiderata you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable -- in particular, I want to know whether full fidelity to actors' self-understandings can coexist with any legitimate use of hindsight, and if not, which horn the better methodologies take and what they give up. Identify which single requirement, if enforced strictly, disqualifies the largest share of widely admired historical writing, and name the casualties. Finally, in a brief coda only, test the completed list against two opposed stances: a purely internalist, actors'-categories reconstruction, and an avowedly present-committed moral history. Keep the weight on the criteria; I will do the scoring of particular historians myself.
