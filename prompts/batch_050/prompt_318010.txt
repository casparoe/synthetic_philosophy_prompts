My labmate and I keep arguing about which computations, if any, are themselves morally considerable -- not because of their effects, but because of what the computation is doing internally. She's thought about it more than me and she's pretty consistent. Her verdicts so far:

1. A high-fidelity whole-brain emulation of a mouse, running in real time and steering a little robot: counts, matters about as much as the mouse did.
2. A recording of that run -- the full sequence of state transitions from a previous execution, played back step by step on the same hardware so the outputs are bit-identical but nothing is being computed afresh: doesn't count at all, it's a movie not a mind.
3. The same emulation running simultaneously on three identical machines: she says that's three subjects, not one, and was annoyed I even asked.
4. The same program run at a millionth of normal speed: still counts, same moral weight, speed is irrelevant to whether there's something it's like.
5. A current chatbot LLM: doesn't count, on the grounds that each forward pass is thrown away and there's no persisting organisation being maintained across the conversation.
6. A hypothetical room-sized lookup table that a person consults to manually produce the same outputs as the emulation: doesn't count, and she thinks this is basically the same reason as (2).

Now I want to predict what she'll say about three new cases before I put them to her, partly to win a bet:

- (a) The mouse emulation run under homomorphic encryption, so the computation genuinely happens but no one, including the operators, can ever read or observe any intermediate state -- only encrypted inputs go in and encrypted motor commands come out.
- (b) The same emulation distributed across a million volunteers' idle phones, so no single device computes more than a tiny fragment and the 'subject' exists nowhere in particular.
- (c) A reversible implementation that runs the emulation forwards and then, to reclaim the energy, runs the entire computation backwards again, undoing every step.

Answer in British English. What I'm after: your best prediction of her verdict on (a), (b) and (c), the two or three underlying rules that each fit all six of her existing verdicts but diverge on the new ones, which rule you think is actually hers and why, and the one further case I'd need to ask her about to settle it. Also flag anywhere you think her existing verdicts are quietly in tension with each other, becuase I'd love ammunition.
