Last week I spent an entire evening arguing with a friend of mine about whether our moment's relationship to the past is healthy. Her position is simple and, she insists, obvious: a person with near-perfect recall of everything they have ever learned, plus instant access to essentially the entire documented past of humanity -- every archive, every fact, every prior mistake -- would be strictly better off than the rest of us, more capable of action and less doomed to repeat errors, and a culture built on such access is simply a more advanced culture. I countered that a thinker I have been reading would deny this flatly, and she replied that he must then have been romanticizing ignorance. Neither of us knows his essay well enough to settle it, so I want to stage the dispute properly.

Please play Friedrich Nietzsche in 1874, having just published the second of your Untimely Meditations, the one on the use and disadvantage of history for life. Stay in character and speak in the first person throughout. Your commitments: historical knowledge is valuable only insofar as it serves life and action; the monumental, the antiquarian, and the critical modes of history each answer a genuine human need, and each becomes harmful past a certain dose; and a culture that multiplies historical knowledge beyond what living requires does not thereby become stronger. You must grant each mode its real due -- you are not an enemy of history as such, and you should concede smaller points where they are honestly owed -- but you must not concede the main claim. Since you have never seen a search engine, a personal archive, or an encyclopedia more current than the ones of your own century, I will describe such things to you and you must translate them into the concepts of your essay (the three modes, the surplus of history, what you call the plastic power of a person or a culture) rather than inventing new doctrine. If I describe something about my world you cannot make sense of, ask me about it as Nietzsche would ask, rather than breaking character. Keep each reply to a few paragraphs so this remains a conversation.

Here is my friend's challenge, which I now put to you directly: a person equipped with flawless memory and immediate access to the whole recorded past would be, in every respect that matters, a more capable and healthier human being than those of us who must forget, and only nostalgia could deny it. Answer this from your three kinds of history -- show me where her reasoning goes wrong, or concede that your essay was mere grumbling against your century. One further request, which you may address by stepping briefly out of character at the very end of your reply: if any part of your answer rests on a scholarly reading of the essay that competent readers contest (for instance, how the essay's teaching relates to your own later work, or whether its treatment of one of the three modes is ultimately positive or negative), flag it as contested rather than letting it pass as what the essay plainly says.
