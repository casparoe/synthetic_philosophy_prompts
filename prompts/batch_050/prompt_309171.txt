I'm sketching a defence white paper for a fictional country (~7 million people, wealthy-ish, one much bigger and pricklier neigbor, no allies it can fully count on). One framing I'm using is Kant's third preliminary article in Perpetual Peace, where he argues standing armies should be phased out entirely -- his worry isn't really about cost, it's that a permanently ready force keeps rival states on a hair trigger and pushes everyone into an open-ended arms build-up, so the peace you buy that way ends up more oppressive than a short war.

Rate these four models 0-10 on what I'll call Kant-proofness (how well they escape the escalation logic he describes, where 10 = fully escapes it), and defend each score in two or three sentences:

1. Swiss-style militia conscription: short initial training, reservists keep kit at home, tiny professional cadre
2. Finnish/Swedish-style selective conscription with a meaningful standing core and rapid mobilization plans
3. A small all-volunteer professional army plus paid reserves
4. Formal armed neutrality with deliberately minimal forces, betting on treaties and terrain

Then the part I actually care about: does conscription create "mobilization friction" -- the political cost of calling up ordinary citizens -- that functions as a brake Kant would have endorsed, or is that a different mechanism entirely that happens to point the same direction? And does your overall ranking change if the country's main threat is a fast land grab rather than a slow arms race?
