During the 1994 Rwandan genocide, the United States government pointedly avoided the word genocide for weeks: officials were permitted to acknowledge only that acts of genocide may have occurred, on the widely reported reasoning that full use of the term would create legal and political pressure to intervene under the 1948 Genocide Convention. Analyze this episode as a case study in the politics of naming atrocities, using Raphael Lemkin's coinage of the term as your lens. Lemkin wanted a word that would do moral and legal work that mass murder or atrocity could not: a named crime carrying obligations. Address the following. First, does the episode vindicate or embarrass Lemkin's project -- did the word carry the force he hoped, or did its very force give states a reason to withhold it? Second, distinguish among naming as a legal trigger, naming as a moral verdict, and naming as a historical judgment, and identify which of these the officials were actually resisting, and whether resisting it was coherent. Third, consider what the case shows about categories whose classification thresholds are contested precisely because of their consequences, and compare briefly the later Darfur episode of 2004, in which the US did apply the term while declining to intervene -- does that second case sharpen or blunt the lesson of the first? Conclude with a verdict: does the Convention's definitional precision serve prevention, or does it mainly hand states a lawyerly escape hatch?
