I recently defended the propensity interpretation of fitness in my philosophy of biology seminar and came away convinced it's right, so now I want to stress-test it. The version I've settled on says, roughly, that the fitness of an organism or type is its objective propensity to leave descendants -- often cashed out as the expected number of offspring in a given environment -- where actual reproductive output is merely a noisy manifestation of that underlying disposition. Like everyone else, I think the old tautology objection is defused: the fittest are not defined as whoever survives, because the relevant propensities could in principle be estimated independently of their outcomes, via components like viability and fecundity. So please set that objection aside and tell me what else would count against the view.

Concretely, I'd like your assessment of at least these candidate defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with a note on whether a committed propensity theorist would concede each one or dismiss it as changing the subject: (a) populations small enough that drift dominates and realized outcomes systematically diverge from any reasonable expectation, so the propensity ascriptions seem explanatorily idle; (b) cases where offspring distributions are highly skewed or correlated across individuals, so that the type with the highest expected number of offspring reliably loses out in the long run to a lower-expectation, lower-variance rival -- which seems to force a choice between the propensity reading and what population genetics actually tracks; (c) the reference-class problem for assigning a single-case propensity to a token organism, given that its expected reproductive success depends on which environment, which conspecifics, and which background conditions we hold fixed; and (d) the objection that fitness differences don't cause anything -- that selection is a purely statistical phenomenon and the propensities are bookkeeping rather than powers in the world.

Finally, I want a verdict on the meta-question. My version of the view is environment-relative, type-relative rather than token-relative, defined over expectations rather than outcomes, and glossed as a long-run tendency. Each qualification answered some objection, but taken together I worry the position no longer risks anything: any recalcitrant case can be absorbed by re-specifying the environment, the reference class, or the time horizon. Is a theoretical term in science allowed to be insulated like this -- perhaps fitness is a primitive that earns its keep through the theory as a whole -- or does the insulation show that the propensity interpretation has quietly stopped making a claim at all? What would personally move me is a concrete biological scenario in which the correct propensity ascription is in principle undeterminable even though selection demonstrably proceeds; if you can construct one, I'd like to see it, and if you think no such case is possible, I'd like to know why that doesn't trouble the view either.
