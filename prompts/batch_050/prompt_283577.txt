Here's the exam question (25 points): Intuitionists are often said to reject the law of excluded middle. Using the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov interpretation of the logical constants, explain (a) what this rejection amounts to, (b) whether an intuitionist should go further and regard LEM as false, and (c) what the rejection implies for the use of proof by contradiction in constructive mathematics.

And here's the student's answer:

On the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov reading, the logical constants are explained in terms of proofs and constructions rather than truth values. A proof of A ∧ B is a pair of a proof of A and a proof of B. A proof of A ∨ B is a proof of one of the disjuncts, together with an indication of which one. A proof of A → B is a construction that turns any proof of A into a proof of B. A proof of ¬A is a construction that turns any proof of A into a proof of ⊥, where ⊥ is a contradiction that has no proof. A proof of ∃x A(x) requires exhibiting a particular object together with a proof that it satisfies A, which is why a constructive existence proof always yields, at least in principle, a method for computing the thing claimed to exist.

(a) On this picture, asserting P ∨ ¬P for arbitrary P would require a general construction that, for any proposition, either produces a proof of it or produces a refutation of it. We have no such universal decision method, so the intuitionist declines to accept LEM as a logical law.

(b) The rejection is principled, not just cautious. On the intuitionistic view LEM is false: since asserting it would amount to claiming a decision procedure over all propositions, and no such procedure exists, the negation ¬(P ∨ ¬P) is the correct thing to assert — it records that there can be no construction of the kind LEM describes.

(c) This also shows why proof by contradiction is unavailable constructively. No argument that derives an absurdity from an assumption counts as a genuine proof, because the BHK clauses demand positive constructions rather than indirect arguments. In particular, a proof of ¬¬A is only a way of turning a hypothetical refutation of A into a contradiction; it contains no construction of A itself, so double-negation elimination fails in general. This matters because DNE and LEM stand or fall together: over the rest of intuitionistic logic each is derivable from the other — from DNE one gets LEM because ¬¬(P ∨ ¬P) is itself intuitionistically provable, and from LEM one gets DNE by a case analysis on P ∨ ¬P. Admitting either principle therefore collapses the system back into classical logic, which is exactly what the proof-theoretic semantics rules out.
