I'm a high school senior who has been reading a popular physics book about electromagnetism and quantum mechanics. The book explains gauge symmetry with an analogy it returns to several times: choosing a gauge, it says, is like choosing the grid lines on a map. The terrain -- mountains, rivers, distances between towns -- is what's real; the grid is a human overlay, and nothing physical changes when you rotate or rescale it. On this basis the book concludes that the electromagnetic potentials are mere bookkeeping devices, that only the electric and magnetic field strengths correspond to anything in nature, and that physicists who speak of gauge structure as physically meaningful are confusing the map with the territory.

When I mentioned this to my physics teacher, she objected that the Aharonov--Bohm effect shows the analogy must break down somewhere: in that setup, charged particles are affected by a potential even in regions where the electric and magnetic fields are exactly zero, and the measurable effect depends on a quantity built from the potential taken around a closed loop. She says this doesn't simply vindicate the potential as real, either, but that the map analogy hides exactly the feature that matters.

Can you audit the analogy for me? Specifically: (1) lay out explicitly what is supposed to map onto what -- terrain, grid lines, rescaling and re-centering the grid, distances between towns -- and check whether each correspondence actually holds for gauge transformations and gauge-invariant quantities; (2) identify where the analogy breaks down, distinguishing the disanalogies that are fatal to the book's conclusion from ones that are merely imprecise (my teacher hinted that the loop quantity and the shape of the space the particles move in are doing the crucial work, and that a flat map is misleading in a specific way); (3) say whether the book's conclusion -- potentials are not real, only field strengths are -- survives once the disanalogies are made explicit, or whether some third option is needed; and (4) if the map analogy is unsalvageable, propose a better analogy that a popular book could actually use, and note what residual inaccuracy even the better analogy would carry.
