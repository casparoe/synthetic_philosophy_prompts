I'm a journalist covering an upcoming national referendum, and the deeper I get, the more the institution itself puzzles me. A referendum takes one policy question, frames it as yes or no, and treats a simple majority as the legitimate decision of the people. What does that practice presuppose, philosophically? It seems to assume there is such a thing as the people's will on a single question, discoverable by aggregating individual judgments -- yet social choice theory suggests aggregating preferences even across a few related issues can yield incoherent results, and a referendum simply isolates one question and assumes that's enough. It also seems to presuppose that legitimacy flows from the vote itself rather than from deliberation, and that the framing of the question can be neutral even though wording demonstrably shifts outcomes. Reconstruct the implicit theory of popular sovereignty and political judgment the practice rests on, tell me which parts of it are coherent and which are in tension with each other, and say what would have to change in how we run referendums if we took the objections to those presuppositions seriously.
