Philosophers have been arguing about collective intentionality for roughly four decades, and as far as I can tell the positions are not converging. As I understand the main camps: Bratman-style accounts analyze shared intention as a structure of interlocking individual intentions, meshing subplans, common knowledge, and appropriate dispositions to coordinate; Gilbert-style accounts insist that genuinely shared activity requires a joint commitment, which creates obligations the participants owe one another and which no sum of individual intentions can generate; Searle-style accounts treat we-intentions as primitive, irreducible mental states that individuals can have. Each side has a standing reply to the others' standard objections, the counterexamples are all familiar, and the same handful of cases — two people walking together, painting a house together, pushing a stalled car — keep getting re-run with no movement.

My working hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but two fused under a single label: a metaphysical question about whether anything over and above individual mental states is needed to explain joint action, and a question about which phenomenon the theory is supposed to capture — small-scale casual cooperation, institutional group action, or the phenomenology of doing something with someone. If that is right, the impasse is partly verbal, in that 'shared intention' picks out different targets for different authors, and partly a bedrock difference over reductionism as a methodological commitment that no further thought experiments will settle. But I may be wrong: perhaps there is a genuine disagreement here about the structure of agency that could in principle be resolved.

Please give me a diagnosis, supported by what each position actually commits itself to, rather than a survey of the literature. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or competing answers to different questions wearing a common name? Where exactly does it bottom out — in facts about human psychology, in intuitions about cases, in methodological commitments, or in views about what a theory of social action owes us? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: an agreed taxonomy of the phenomena, results from developmental or comparative psychology, a formalization precise enough to reveal whether the accounts are extensionally equivalent, or something else — or else argue that no resolution should be expected, and explain why the question as posed resists one.
