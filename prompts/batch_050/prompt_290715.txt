So I got weirdly obsessed with this passage from Machiavelli's The Prince (Chapter VIII, in the old public-domain W. K. Marriott translation) at like 1am last night. He's describing Agathocles, a guy who murdered the entire senate of Syracuse in one morning, seized power, and then turned out to be a genuinely brilliant and effective ruler who defended the city against the Carthaginians. And then Machiavelli writes this:

"Yet it cannot be called talent to slay fellow-citizens, to deceive friends, to be without faith, without mercy, without religion; such methods may gain empire, but not glory. Still, if the courage of Agathocles in entering into and extricating himself from dangers be considered, together with his greatness of mind in enduring and overcoming hardships, it cannot be seen why he should be esteemed less than the most notable captain. Nevertheless, his barbarous cruelty and inhumanity with infinite wickednesses do not permit him to be celebrated among the most excellent men."

This has been bugging me for days. Write me a dialogue between two people arguing about what Machiavelli is actually doing in this passage and whether it holds up. One of them thinks he's onto something real and important: a leader can commit horrific acts that are effective, maybe even justified by the circumstances, and it still makes sense to withhold honor and praise from him — the hands stay dirty even when the decision was right, and "empire but not glory" is a coherent line to draw. The other thinks this is Machiavelli chickening out at the last second: if the massacres actually bought stability and saved the state, then seperating "empire" from "glory" is just squeamish bookkeeping; and if they didn't work, then of course we condemn him, but then the glory talk adds nothing. Either way, on this view, the distinction collapses.

Some ground rules: both people should be equally sharp, no strawmen, and they need to actually engage each other's arguments instead of reciting prepared statements. It's fine (encouraged even) for them to concede points and shift ground as the argument develops — e.g., on whether "glory" is doing genuine moral work here or is just PR, whether this passage is even consistent with the rest of The Prince, and whether you can judge an act justified while judging the person who did it dishonorable at the same time. At some point they should stress-test the disagreement on a modern-ish political case of their choosing (a wartime bombing decision, torturing a suspect, lying to the public during a crisis, whatever). And please don't wrap it up with a cheesy "both sides have a point" ending — let the dialogue end wherever the argument actually lands. Somewhere in the 600-900 word range is good.
