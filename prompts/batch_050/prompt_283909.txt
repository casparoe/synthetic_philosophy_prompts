I chair the ethics review panel at my company, and I'm sick of our decisions tracking outcomes instead of culpability -- the guy whose fraud got caught by accident before it hurt anyone gets a slap on the wrist, the one whose identical scheme tanked a pension fund gets fired, and everyone treats that as obviously right. I got interested in Abelard's old line that the sin is in the consent, not the act: what you willed, knowing it was wrong, is the whole of your guilt, and what actually happened is morally beside the point. I want to actually run the panel on something like this basis, so write me a decision procedure we can follow on each case: what questions to ask in what order, what evidence can count for or against someone having consented to something they knew was wrong (since we can't see into their heads), where the procedure should stop with a no-fault finding even when harm was huge, and where it has to punt to a judgment call because consent just isn't determinable from the outside. It needs to handle at least: attempts that failed through luck, people who genuinely believed what they did was fine, people who were pressured by their boss, and people who were careless about finding out whether it was wrong. Then run the procedure on three worked cases, one of which should be a case where following Abelard strictly gives a verdict my panel will hate (e.g. letting someone off, or convicting someone harmless-seeming). Also flag where you think Abelard's principle itself breaks down when operationalized rather than just being hard to apply. Max 700 words.
