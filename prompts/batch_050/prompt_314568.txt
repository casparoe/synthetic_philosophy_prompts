I am drafting a survey chapter on the semantics and pragmatics of slurs, and I want to be sure the chapter on the empirical landscape is complete. As I understand the field, every major family of theories is, at bottom, an attempt to explain a fairly small set of robust data points. The main families I have in mind are: truth-conditional content accounts (the slur itself contributes derogating content to what is said), expressivist accounts (the slur conventionally expresses contempt outside the truth-conditional dimension), presuppositional accounts (the slur triggers a presupposition that the target group is contemptible), prohibitionist accounts (the slur is a tabooed word whose offensiveness derives from the prohibition on its use rather than from any content), and perspectival accounts (the slur presents its target from a derogating perspective the speaker signals occupying).

Here is my list of the phenomena any adequate theory must handle (I use "[slur]" as a placeholder throughout):

1. Projection. The derogation survives embedding under negation, conditionals, questions, and epistemic modals. Asking "Is he a [slur]?" or saying "If a [slur] moves in next door, I'm selling" offends roughly as much as the plain assertion, and the offense cannot be cancelled by denial.
2. Neutral counterparts. Co-referring neutral terms for the same group do not offend, and swapping the slur for its neutral counterpart seems to leave the truth conditions of the sentence intact while removing the offense.
3. Reclamation. Members of the targeted group can, in some in-group contexts, use the slur without derogating — sometimes affectionately or as a marker of solidarity — while out-group uses remain derogatory.
4. Differential potency. Distinct slurs targeting the same group differ in offensiveness, and slurs targeting dominant groups strike most speakers as less potent than slurs targeting subordinated groups, even holding the speaker's contempt fixed.
5. Speaker-independence. The offense does not require that the speaker endorse the derogation: mentions in metalinguistic discussion, quotations of others, and utterances by speakers known to harbor no contempt can still register as offensive, if often less so.
6. Ineffability. Attempts to state, in neutral vocabulary, "what the slur adds" either fail to capture the derogation or themselves become offensive, suggesting the derogating dimension resists paraphrase.

My concern is that the literature has converged on this list as the canonical data set, and that theories are now being graded on a curve they collectively set. I would like one further phenomenon: a robust, checkable linguistic datum about slurs or slur-like expressions that is not on the list. It must be genuinely new — not a rewording of one of the six, not a special case of one, and not a conjunction of two. Ideally it should be a datum on which at least two of the theory families above make different predictions, so that it could actually adjudicate between them rather than merely sit alongside the others.
