For a while now I have been stuck in an argument that refuses to move, and I would like you to help me understand why. Please take on the role of a philosopher of mind who also knows the history of machine translation well, and who is experienced at figuring out what intellectual disputes are really about. Before you compose your answer, spend some time brainstorming the possible diagnoses and the strongest version of each side; then give me your considered conclusion.

Some background: I have no academic training, but I read a lot, and a few years ago I got interested in Douglas Hofstadter's complaint that modern machine translation, for all its fluency, does not understand anything -- it shuffles words around without ever grasping the situations the words are about. I mentioned this to a friend of mine who works in software, and we have been arguing about it, on and off, ever since. His position is that understanding is not some extra ingredient on top of performance: if a system reliably produces translations that capture the meaning, handle ambiguity correctly, and answer follow-up questions about the text, then it understands, and there is nothing left to deny. My position is closer to Hofstadter's: the system has never encountered the world the sentences describe, so something essential is missing even when the output is perfect. We both know the old thought experiment about the person in a room following rules to produce Chinese replies, and we have both read the usual responses to it. Neither of us has budged.

Recently I tried to break the deadlock with a scenario of my own, and it made things worse, which is why I am writing to you. Imagine that centuries ago, a monastery set its monks the task of writing down French sentences alongside their English translations, generation after generation, until the library contained, in effect, a giant lookup table covering every French sentence up to some length with its correct English rendering. Now the abbey offers a translation service: you hand in a French sentence, a monk finds it in the table, and copies out the English. My friend says the abbey obviously does not understand French -- but, he insists, a modern neural translation system is different, because it compresses the table into something general, and generality is where understanding lives. I think that answer smuggles in everything at the word "general," because the lookup table also does everything the abbey is ever asked to do, and any test my friend proposes for the neural system seems like it could be passed, in principle, by a big enough table. He replies that "in principle" is doing all the work in my argument, because no finite table could actually exist, whereas the neural system does. And there we sit.

What I want from you is not a verdict on who is right, but a diagnosis of what this disagreement actually is. Is it a factual disagreement about what translation systems really do internally, which either of us could lose by learning more engineering? Is it a disagreement about what the word "understand" should mean, in which case we are both right relative to our own stipulation and the argument is empty? Is it a disagreement about which evidence should count -- behavior, internal mechanism, or something else -- and if so, is that itself a factual question or a bedrock difference in intuition that neither of us can argue the other out of? Or are we perhaps answering two different questions under one word, one about the system and one about what it is like to be a reader? Please engage with the abbey scenario specifically rather than only the familiar Chinese room, tell me whether my friend's generality point or my "in principle" point is doing real work, and given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction we are missing, an empirical discovery about how these systems represent things, a new kind of test, or nothing at all because the question is ill-posed. If you think no resolution is possible, argue for that rather than just asserting it.

One request about form: please write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a long, careful letter. No headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
