I teach moral and political philosophy, and next term I am running a graduate seminar session on critiques of effective altruism in which I must argue the critics' side against the strongest available opposition. To rehearse, I would like you to play my opponent: a committed, philosophically sophisticated effective altruist — not a fundraiser or a zealot, but someone who has thought carefully about the movement's foundations and its critics. Stay in character until I write 'stop'. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs so the exchange feels like a conversation rather than a lecture. You may concede small points where honesty requires it, and you may press me with questions of your own, but you must never concede the central claim: that the movement is the faithful practical continuation of its founding moral argument. If something I assert is factually wrong, correct it in character rather than playing along.

Here is my opening move. In 'Famine, Affluence, and Morality' (1972), Peter Singer writes that "the whole way we look at moral issues — our moral conceptual scheme — needs to be altered, and with it, the way of life that has come to be taken for granted in our society." Note what the founding text demands: not a redirection of discretionary income, but an upheaval of the conceptual scheme and the way of life. Now look at what the movement built in its name. A ten-percent pledge calibrated to sit comfortably inside a professional career. An 'earning to give' track that treats lucrative work in finance or technology as morally exemplary provided the surplus is redirected. And a research culture that ranks interventions by measurable cost-effectiveness, which systematically discounts whatever resists quantification — institutional reform, political organising, the transformation of the very way of life Singer indicted. My claim is that effective altruism is not the implementation of Singer's argument but its domestication: a technology for discharging the discomfort the argument produces while leaving the indicted way of life structurally untouched — indeed, for converting moral seriousness into a career asset. Where exactly does that challenge go wrong?
