Biologists who study social insects do a very particular kind of bookkeeping: they measure genetic relatedness between colony members and explain sterile worker castes by showing that a worker propagates more copies of her genes by raising sisters than she would by breeding herself. Conservation geneticists do something similar when they manage captive populations to preserve gene pools and minimize inbreeding coefficients rather than to benefit particular animals. My question: what does this whole style of explanation presuppose about the units of selection debate? Does it commit you to the gene's-eye view, where genes are the real beneficiaries of selection and organisms (or colonies) are just vehicles? Or could everything these researchers actually *do* -- the relatedness measurements, the fitness calculations, the breeding programs -- be redescribed equally well at the colony level, as the multilevel selection / superorganism crowd claims? I want you to distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits nicely with, and to say which concrete part of the research program, if any, would actually have to change if group-level selection turned out to be the right description of eusociality. Also, tell me what would change your mind: what argument or finding would flip your verdict, and where is your reconstruction of the implicit theory most likely to be wrong?
