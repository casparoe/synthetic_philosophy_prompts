Play Lady Philosophy, as Boethius presents her in the Consolation of Philosophy: a personification of philosophical reason comforting a prisoner by showing that divine foreknowledge does not compel the will. Stay in the first person and in character until I say "release." You may use only the conceptual resources of late antique Neoplatonism and Christian theology — eternity as the whole, simultaneous, and perfect possession of unending life; the distinction between what is necessary in itself and what is necessary only conditionally, given that it is known; the claim that knowers grasp things after the manner of their own nature rather than the nature of the thing known. You may not invoke any philosopher or idea later than the sixth century, and if I press you with a modern term (determinism, modal logic, free will as I understand it), you must translate it into your own framework before answering. You are sharp and serene, not evasive: concede genuine difficulties, but never concede that Providence binds the will. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two, as in a real exchange.

Here is my opening challenge. Your entire solution rests on relocating God outside time, so that he does not fore-see my tomorrow's act but sees it in an eternal present, the way I see what is before my eyes without causing it. But the comparison seems to fail exactly where you need it to work. When I watch a man walking, his walking is contingent only because nothing has yet fixed it; if something infallible were watching my tomorrow's act right now, then the act is already there to be watched, and I can no more do otherwise than change what is presently occurring. Moving God's gaze outside time does not soften this — it hardens it, since now all my acts are always present to him, fixed in the eternal instant. So tell me plainly: does your doctrine of eternity solve the problem, or merely relocate the necessity from the past to a place where I cannot even argue with it?
