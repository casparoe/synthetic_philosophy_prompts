I work at an edtech startup and we're building an AI tutoring app for kids aged 8-12. I have no philosophy training, but a colleague mentioned that John Locke wrote extensively about education, so I've been reading summaries of his views and I want to stress-test our design against them. From what I understand, Locke opposed rote memorization and corporal punishment, thought children should be motivated by esteem and disgrace rather than rewards and beatings, emphasized habit formation over rule-learning, and wanted learning to feel like play. Please correct me if I've gotten any of that wrong, but then here's what I actually want:

Take the position Locke held on education and extrapolate, feature by feature, to the design decisions we're facing. For each one, tell me what you think Locke would say, and -- crucially -- ground your answer in his actual commitments, not generic "Locke liked freedom" gestures. Where his framework genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so and explain why the question has no analogue in his context rather than forcing a verdict. The features:

1. Gamification. We award points, badges, and streaks for completing lessons. Does this count as the external "rewards" Locke objected to, or is it closer to the esteem/disgrace mechanism he endorsed? These seem to collapse into each other and I can't tell where the line is for him.

2. Spaced repetition. Our app drills vocabulary and math facts on a decay schedule. This looks like exactly the rote learning Locke hated, but the mechanism (retrieval practice, not recitation in front of a teacher) is nothing like seventeenth-century drilling. Does the objection survive the change in mechanism?

3. Adaptive difficulty. The system keeps each child working at the edge of their ability, which means the child fails constantly by design. Locke's tutor seems to manage the child's experience very closely to protect their confidence and disposition toward learning. Is relentless algorithmic failure what his "play" model was supposed to avoid, or is tailored challenge what he'd want a good tutor to do?

4. Parental dashboard. Parents get detailed logs of everything the child does. Locke wrote for fathers overseeing tutors, but our surveillance is continuous and fine-grained in a way no human tutor's reporting could be. Does that difference in degree become a difference in kind for his view?

5. AI companion. Kids can chat freely with the tutor character. Locke put enormous weight on the personal relationship between tutor and child. Say something honest about whether a simulated relationship can play that role in his framework, or whether his reasons for valuing it rule the substitution out entirely.

End with the two or three places where you think extrapolating Locke is least secure -- the features where the honest answer is "his framework has no resources to settle this" -- and say what additional premise you'd have to smuggle in to get a verdict.
