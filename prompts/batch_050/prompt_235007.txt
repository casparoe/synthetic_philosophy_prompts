Two friends of mine co-run a little giving circle at their company. There's $10k a year to donate and the two of them have to agree on where it goes unanimously -- if they deadlock, the money reverts to the company's general budget and helps nobody. Dana wants it all going to malaria prevention; Marcus wants it all going to animal welfare (he thinks factory farming is a moral catastrophy and malaria nets are a rounding error by comparison; she thinks roughly the reverse). They deadlocked, then had this exchange, which I listened to without jumping in:

(1) Dana proposed a deal: this year all $10k goes to malaria, next year all of it goes to animal welfare, alternating from then on. Her pitch was that by each of their own lights this beats deadlock -- deadlock funds zero bednets and zero spared animals, forever.

(2) Marcus refused. He said moral convictions aren't preferences you can trade like baseball cards, and that signing off on malaria funding means actively supporting what he believes is the wrong cause, which is different from just losing a vote. He'd rather the money revert than put his name on a grant he thinks shouldn't exist.

(3) Dana said his clean hands are costing the very beings he claims to care about: by his own lights, alternation gets animal welfare an average of $5k a year, deadlock gets it $0. And his complicity worry proves too much -- his taxes fund things he considers immoral every year and he still pays them.

(4) Marcus answered that taxes aren't voluntary and he protests them, whereas this would be his actual signature. And he said her trading logic has no brakes: if a third member joined who wanted the money for something Dana considers genuinely evil -- not just less effective, but evil -- her own logic tells her to alternate with them too. If she'd refuse, then she also thinks some lines can't be traded across, and drawing lines is exactly what she was mocking him for.

Please adjudicate this move by move and give me a committed verdict, not a "both sides make good points" shrug. Rate each of the four moves and pick an overall winner. Put it in a table with these columns: Move (the number), Core claim (one short sentence), Rating (one of: clearly right / mostly right / partly right / mostly mistaken / clearly mistaken), and Why (one or two sentences). Below the table, give me: Overall winner (Dana, Marcus, or genuinely tied -- but only say tied if you really can't separate them) with 2-3 sentences of defense, and the single biggest thing you think both of them are missing.
