I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a long feature about two philosophers at the same university who have spent years publicly disagreeing about why promises bind. One defends the practice-based view: promising binds because we live inside a social convention of promising, and breaking a promise exploits the expectations and trust that the convention makes possible -- roughly the line associated with Hume and later developed by Scanlon. The other defends the normative-powers view: promisers bind themselves directly by exercising a standing moral power to assume obligations, the way they might waive a right or give consent, and the social practice at most explains how we signal that we've done so, not why the obligation exists. After a panel discussion that (in my editor's words) degenerated into the two of them restating their papers at each other, they've agreed to produce a joint adversarial-collaboration statement for the next department colloquium, and I've been asked to draft the working version they'll revise. Draft that document.

The document should have three parts. First, a numbered section of claims both sides can sign -- and here I want more than banalities like "promises matter." Candidates I'd like you to test and refine include: that the obligation generated by a promise is not reducible to the duty to avoid disappointing expectations; that promises procured by fraud or coercion fail to bind, and that any adequate theory must explain why; that there exist puzzling edge cases both theories must handle somehow (uncommunicated promises the promisee never learns of; promises to a dying person with no surviving interests; promises made to someone who pointedly refuses to form any expectation of performance); and that both views must say something about how binding force survives when the surrounding practice of promising breaks down or is absent. Second, a disagreements table stating the precise remaining fault lines, each row phrased so that the side it describes would accept the wording of its own position -- I expect at least one row on whether the obligation is prior to or derived from expectations and uptake, and one on whether a hypothetical community with no established practice of promising could still contain binding promises. Third, for each disagreement, the crux: the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side, together with a case or argument both parties agree bears on it and each side's prediction about how that case resolves, recorded in advance.

Two constraints on the drafting. Where a disagreement turns out to be partly verbal -- for instance, if "convention" is being used in a thinner and a thicker sense -- the document should say so explicitly and quarantine the verbal residue from the genuine dispute. And I don't want a document that splits the difference into mush: the agreed claims should be stated at full strength, not hedged until they're empty, and the disagreements should be sharp enough that an outsider could tell what evidence or argument would embarrass each side. Please write it as the actual statement, with headings, not as a description of what such a statement would contain.
