Okay, here's an exam-style problem I've been trying to get right for my Indian philosophy seminar, and I want you to actually build it for me rather than gesture at it.

You're an expert in Madhyamaka Buddhist philosophy, Nāgārjuna especially, and you've also thought carefully about dialetheism, vagueness, and presupposition failure, so you can tell the difference between a genuine tetralemma case and a cheap paradox. Think carefully before you commit: do some brainstorming first, sketch at least two candidate setups, say briefly why you're rejecting the loser(s), and then develop the winner.

Construct a thought experiment in which one clearly stated question about some everyday thing pushes four different respondents toward the four corners of the catuṣkoṭi: it is; it is not; it both is and is not; it neither is nor is not. Constraints:

1. Not the stock examples. Nothing about the Tathāgata after death, nothing about whether the world is eternal or finite, and no fire-and-fuel. Those are the ones everyone reaches for and I'm sick of grading them.
2. The subject of the question must be mundane -- a person, an object, an institution, a border, something a student could point at. No abstracta like truth or the absolute.
3. All four respondents are rational, well-informed, and speaking ordinary language. Nobody is confused and nobody is being cute with words. Each corner has to be motivated by a seperate feature of the case -- it has to fall out of the scenario, not just be a position someone happens to hold. If you find yourself writing "the fourth respondent simply denies all of the above," start over, that's stipulating the verdict rather than generating it.
4. The case has to make the Madhyamaka move (rejecting all four corners) come out as the motivated response, not as a cop-out or a mystical shrug.

After the scenario: walk through each corner and show exactly what in the case motivates it. Then give the Madhyamaka diagnosis, which has to work by exposing a bad presupposition in the question itself -- roughly, that the subject term is being treated as picking out something with svabhāva, own-being, when nothing in the situation answers to that -- and not by smuggling in a fifth option or cheerfully embracing a contradiction.

Finally, the part I care most about: (a) tell me which single feature of your case is doing the real work, and what the case collapses into if you remove it -- an ordinary vagueness problem? a liar-style paradox? a boring disagreement where one respondent is just wrong? (b) how would a dialetheist like Priest try to redescribe your setup so that the "both" corner is simply true and there's no need for the fourfold denial at all, and does your construction block that redescription or leave the door open to it? Be honest if it leaves the door open.
