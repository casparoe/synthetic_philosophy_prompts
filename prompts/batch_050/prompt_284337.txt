So I'm a retired organic chemist, spent forty years on reaction mechanisms, and about a year ago I fell down a philosophy rabbit hole and haven't climbed out. Lately I've been chewing on David Benatar's argument against having kids, specifically the asymmetry he builds it on. As I understand it the idea is: for someone who never exists, the absence of pain is good (or at least not bad -- there's no one suffering), but the absence of pleasure is NOT bad, because there's nobody around to be deprived of it. Meanwhile for someone who does exist, pain is bad and pleasure is good. So the ledger doesn't balance the way your intuition says it should -- coming into existence is always a harm, or at least never a benefit compared to never existing. I keep going back and forth on whether step with the absent pleasure actually holds or whether its smuggling something in.

Anyway. My daughter just had her first kid, my first grandchild, and obviously I didn't say any of this at the christening, but the contrast is rattling around in my head and I want to write a poem about it. Here's what I'm after:

- Around 20-28 lines. No set traditional form, but I want the form itself to enact the asymmetry. My idea is the poem should look like it's going to be symmetric -- two halves, maybe, the first about my granddaughter existing, the second about the counterfactual where she never did -- and then the symmetry should visibly fail at a specific point. A line that should have a mirror image but doesn't, or a stanza that's one line short, or a rhyme whose partner never shows up. The reader should feel the missing counterpart rather than just be told about it.
- The absent-pleasure-is-not-bad move is the crux and it should land there -- the poem's hinge should be that nothing is lost in the half where she never was, even though everything is.
- But please don't write a Benatar pamphlet in verse. I adore this kid. The poem should hold both things at once -- the cold logic of the ledger and the fact that I drove two hours last weekend just to watch her nap. If it resolves cleanly in either direction I'll consider it a failure.
- No need to name Benatar or antinatalism in the poem itself. I'd rather someone who knows the argument recognizes it from the shape, and someone who doesnt just reads a good poem about a grandfather thinking too hard.

Bonus points if there's a line or image that nods to the chemistry -- a reaction that never ran, an uncatalyzed pathway, something like that -- but only if it earns its place and isn't just me showing off my old career.
