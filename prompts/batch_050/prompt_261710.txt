I teach a formal reasoning seminar and want a handout that sorts students by how they respond to the two-envelope paradox (the standard setup: two envelopes, one with twice the money of the other; you pick one; the familiar expected-value argument says you should switch). You are a decision theorist with experience teaching this material; think carefully, and before drafting, briefly work out for yourself the full space of moves a person can make -- where the switching argument can be faulted, where it can be defended, and where it can be defused without being faulted -- then build the deliverable described below. Begin your reply with the deliverable itself, no preface or commentary.

The deliverable is a decision tree of positions on the paradox, built from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Each branch should be labeled with a concrete diagnostic question or mini-case a respondent can answer without knowing any labels -- for example, whether the expected-value argument survives in the discrete version where the prizes are powers of two with a known cap, whether the argument survives when the envelopes contain the amounts (x, 2x) versus when your envelope's contents are described as either half or double the other's, whether you may switch after opening your envelope, and how you treat an infinite expected value when one arises. Aim for six to eight diagnostic questions and eight to ten leaves.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position it lands on (or 'unnamed, and here's why' if nobody occupies it), one sentence stating its core claim, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where combinations of answers are unoccupied, say so and diagnose decisively whether that is because the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored -- commit to a verdict rather than hedging. Where two named positions cannot be separated by any question in the tree, flag them as such rather than inventing a distinction.

Commit to definite choices about which distinctions are load-bearing for a first encounter with the paradox, and say in a short closing note (after the tree) which single diagnostic question you judge does the most sorting work and why. Assume readers have seen the paradox stated once, know what expected value is, and have no background in decision theory; the tree should sort them by what they actually think, not by what they call themselves.
