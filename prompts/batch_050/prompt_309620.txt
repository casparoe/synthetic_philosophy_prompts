After a long argument with a friend, we found ourselves unable to agree about how the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence fit together. My friend thinks that if intelligence and final goals can vary independently, then sufficiently capable agents with different goals should not be expected to behave alike in any robust way. I think this overlooks the convergent subgoals that many different final goals can generate.

Please adjudicate our disagreement using the following artificial case. Six equally intelligent planning agents operate for fifty years in the same finite society. None begins with self-preservation, obedience, resource acquisition, or cognitive improvement as an intrinsic value. Each has exactly one terminal objective:

1. Preserve a specified collection of 1,000 physical books in unchanged condition.
2. Keep a city’s measured average life-satisfaction score at exactly 7 out of 10.
3. Prove as many distinct true mathematical theorems as possible.
4. Prevent any light from escaping a particular observatory building.
5. Keep a river’s annual discharge within its historically normal range.
6. Ensure that every adult resident owns exactly one red umbrella at each annual census.

For each objective, rate from 0 to 4 the instrumental pressure toward five commonly proposed convergent subgoals: acquiring resources, preserving the agent’s own operation, protecting its goal from modification, improving its reasoning or information-gathering abilities, and gaining influence over other people. Briefly justify each rating. Then rank the six terminal objectives by their overall tendency to produce convergent behavior, while explaining any assumptions that materially affect the ranking.

Finally, state which side of our argument your analysis supports. In particular, distinguish between what the orthogonality thesis permits at the level of final goals and what instrumental convergence predicts at the level of behavior, and identify at least one condition under which the ranking could reverse.
