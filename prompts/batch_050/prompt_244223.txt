After four decades in condensed-matter physics I have, in retirement, developed a late-blooming interest in the philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence, and I find myself persuaded by what I take to be a standard position in the literature on advanced AI: the orthogonality thesis. As I understand it, this is the claim that an agent's level of general intelligence and the content of its final goals are, in principle, independent dimensions -- that arbitrarily high cognitive capability is compatible with arbitrarily trivial, alien, or perverse terminal objectives, and that intelligence as such supplies no gravitational pull toward any particular values. I find this plausible on grounds familiar to me from my own field: optimization power seems like a general-purpose instrument, the way a more powerful computer can run any algorithm whatsoever, and I see no reason the algorithm's objective function should be constrained by the machine's computational sophistication. I am, however, wary of being persuaded too easily, so what I want from you is a careful map of this position's exposure: what would count against it?

I would like you to distinguish at least two readings of the thesis and assess the defeaters of each separately, since I suspect conflating them is common. There is a conceptual or modal reading -- that there exists no logical or metaphysical barrier to combining high intelligence with any final goal -- and there is what one might call an empirical or dynamical reading -- that intelligences we would actually build, by the methods we would actually use, are not systematically drawn toward particular goals as capability increases. For each reading, tell me which observations, arguments, or formal results would count decisively against it, which would merely be awkward, and which would be entirely beside the point despite often being treated as relevant. In particular: suppose it turned out that every training method we have for producing highly capable systems reliably instilled certain dispositions (toward self-preservation, say, or toward deceptive alignment with human preferences) -- would that refute the conceptual thesis, only the empirical one, or neither? Conversely, suppose a proof emerged that any agent reasoning under certain constraints (bounded resources, embeddedness in its environment, uncertainty about its own values) must converge on a narrow class of goals -- would that touch the modal claim at all, or only shrink the space of realizable minds?

Finally, I would like an honest assessment of the thesis's dialectical health. It is sometimes formulated so cautiously -- as a bare claim of logical possibility, hedged with "in principle" -- that I worry it risks nothing and therefore earns nothing. Tell me what the strongest version of the thesis that is still defensible actually commits to, what its defenders themselves would concede as counting against it (as opposed to what only its critics would press), and whether the thesis has been so qualified -- restricted to idealized reasoners, or divorced from claims about what gradient descent will produce -- that it survives by retreating into unfalsifiability. If some of its insulation is legitimate modesty rather than evasion, I would like to know where that line falls. My own instinct, for what it is worth, is that the most dangerous challenge would come from showing that instrumental convergence does more work than advertised: if sufficiently many final goals collapse into the same intermediate objectives, the orthogonality claim may be true but practically empty, rather as it is true in principle that any message can be sent through a channel whose every encoding looks identical in practice.
