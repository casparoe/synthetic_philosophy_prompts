I am assembling a taxonomy of theodicies for a reading group, and I want to impose a constraint that I find is usually dodged: the theodicy must speak to the suffering of non-human animals in the natural world — predation, parasitism, disease, and starvation across the hundreds of millions of years before any human existed — rather than to evil done by human agents or suffered by human beings. Here are the candidates I have already catalogued, which I do not need repeated:

1. The free will defense: evil is the price of creaturely freedom. (This plainly targets moral evil; its extension to animal suffering, via fallen angels or a corrupted creation, I treat as a separate entry rather than as part of the original.)
2. The soul-making theodicy: suffering is necessary for the growth of moral and spiritual character. (This applies poorly, if at all, to creatures that are not obviously ennobled by being eaten alive.)
3. Skeptical theism: God may have reasons for permitting evil that are simply beyond our comprehension.
4. The privation account: evil is not a positive thing God created but an absence, distortion, or corruption of good, and so is not something for which God needs a justifying reason.
5. The regularity theodicy: a world governed by stable, intelligible laws of nature is a great good — arguably a precondition of any embodied life at all — and such laws will inevitably produce earthquakes, cancers, and predators somewhere down the line.

I would like one further theodicy that meets the animal constraint and is not a rewording, a special case, or a conjunction of the entries above. In particular, it should not amount to the claim that animal suffering serves some greater good we cannot specify, since that collapses into entry 3; it should give a reason, or at least the shape of a reason, that we can state and examine.

After presenting it, please assess it honestly: does it actually fare better than the five entries above on the specific case of pre-human animal suffering, or is that case simply the one where theodicies characteristically fail, so that the most defensible remaining position is to concede that this class of evil resists justification and to recast the problem of evil accordingly?
