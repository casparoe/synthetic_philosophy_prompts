I've spent about eleven years working in information security — threat modeling, access control, incident response — and I have no formal training in philosophy at all. Lately, though, I've been reading about workplace whistleblowing and the ethics of loyalty, and I keep trying to translate what I read into the vocabulary I actually think in.

So here is my request. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher who is also genuinely fluent in information-security practice — not someone who has skimmed a glossary, but someone who could hold their own in an incident postmortem. Think carefully about this before writing: first sketch, for yourself, a list of candidate mappings between the two vocabularies (for example, between "exhaust internal reporting channels before going public" and coordinated vulnerability disclosure, or between "loyalty to one's employer" and any notion of trust a security model can carry), and only then write the translation. Show me your final mappings, not your scratch work, but let the scratch work inform the answer.

The three positions I keep encountering, as I understand them:

1. Whistleblowing is a breach of loyalty. Employment, like friendship, creates special obligations, and disclosing the organization's secrets is a kind of betrayal that requires extraordinary justification — grave public harm, nothing less.
2. Loyalty to a company is a category error. A firm is an instrument for making money, not a person or a cause, and it cannot be the object of loyalty at all — so whistleblowing needs no special justification beyond what any disclosure of wrongdoing needs.
3. Whistleblowing is justified under procedural conditions: the harm must be serious and documented, internal channels must have been tried or shown futile, and the disclosure must go no further than necessary to stop the wrong.

Translate each of these positions, one at a time, into the vocabulary of security engineering — trust, authorization, threat models, least privilege, defense in depth, auditability, disclosure norms, whatever fits. For each position, I want a residue report in three parts: what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion (name the distortion explicitly — for instance, whether you had to treat a relationship as a contract, or a person as a principal in an access-control sense, to make the sentence parse), and what has no expression in the security vocabulary at all.

Two things I'm especially curious about: whether the concept of loyalty has any home in a framework whose professional instinct is zero trust, and whether the difference between "this action was authorized" and "this action was right" is something my vocabulary simply cannot say.

Then, the verdict: does the residue show that security thinking is missing something the ethics of betrayal genuinely contains, or does it show that some of these claims were vaguer than they looked — surviving only because ordinary language lets them? Please commit to an answer rather than splitting the difference. Don't smuggle in ethical assumptions disguised as translations; if a sentence only seems to translate because you've quietly reintroduced the very concept under a technical-sounding name, flag it.
