I'm a senior in high school and I help run our mock trial club, and I've gotten stuck on a question that keeps coming up in our practice cases: when, if ever, can statistics alone count as proof? Below are my gut verdicts on six cases, plus a tentative principle of my own. Please tell me what principle actually captures my verdicts -- or, if none does, which verdicts I have to drop. Please write your answer in British English.

1. A county fair admits 1,000 people but sold only 400 tickets; the rest climbed the fence. The fair organizers sue one attendee picked essentially at random, arguing that it's more likely than not (60 percent) that he never paid. My verdict: he shouldn't owe a cent on that evidence alone.

2. A cyclist is sideswiped at night by a bus that doesn't stop, and she never sees its markings. Two companies run buses in town, and 80 percent of the buses on that route at that hour belong to BlueLine. She sues BlueLine on that basis alone. My verdict: BlueLine shouldn't be found liable.

3. A burglar cuts himself on a window and leaves blood behind. DNA from the scene matches a man living in the city, and the probability that a randomly chosen person's DNA would match is about one in ten million. There's little other evidence. My verdict: convicting him mostly on the DNA feels fine -- even though this is "just statistics" too, which is exactly what I can't square with verdicts 1 and 2.

4. An eyewitness picks the defendant out of a lineup, and studies of witnesses in similar conditions suggest they're right about 80 percent of the time. My verdict: this feels like real evidence in a way the bus case doesn't, even though the reliability number is identical to the BlueLine figure.

5. A man wins the state lottery against odds of one in twenty million, and an investigator argues that the sheer improbability of an honest win is itself evidence he cheated. My verdict: absurd. Improbability alone proves nothing here, and yet I can't articulate why that doesn't undermine the DNA case.

6. Twenty students were in the locker room when a wallet was stolen, and it must have been one of them. The principal proposes suspending one student chosen by lot, on the ground that guilt is certainly somewhere in that group. My verdict: clearly wrong -- certainty that someone is guilty doesn't make any particular student guilty.

My tentative principle: statistical evidence can support a verdict only when that specific evidence probably would not exist if the defendant were innocent. The DNA match would almost certainly not be there if the man were innocent, whereas the 60 percent figure at the fair would be true of whomever the organizers happened to sue. Please test this principle against all six cases, repair it where it breaks (I suspect case 4 and case 5 break it in opposite directions), and state your best final version precisely, noting which case motivates each clause. Then apply it to two cases I haven't considered: a shoplifting prosecution resting almost entirely on a facial recognition match with a false-match rate of one in fifty thousand, and a lawsuit by a resident with a rare illness against a factory, where epidemiology shows that living near the plant roughly doubles the risk of that illness. Finally, be honest about which of my six verdicts your principle cannot save, and tell me whether you'd sooner abandon the DNA verdict or the fair-and-bus verdicts, and why.
