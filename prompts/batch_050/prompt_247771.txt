In my seminar on the philosophy of artificial intelligence we spent several weeks on the debate about AI takeoff speeds, and I came away less certain about what the debate is even about. The two positions are familiar. On one side stands the tradition running from I.J. Good's 1965 speculation about an ultraintelligent machine designing still better machines through to modern advocates of a fast or discontinuous takeoff: once a system crosses some threshold of capability, recursive self-improvement could carry it far beyond human level in a short time, because intelligence is in some sense a compact algorithmic insight that a single system can possess and then amplify. On the other side stand the gradualists, who argue that what we call intelligence is a large bundle of complementary inputs -- accumulated knowledge, tools, embodied skills, social and economic infrastructure -- and that capability growth historically comes through broadly distributed processes with diminishing returns, so that any AI system will be absorbed into a growing economy the way earlier general-purpose technologies were, and there will be no discontinuity to speak of.

What strikes me as a philosopher of science is the following. This disagreement has now persisted for well over fifteen years among serious, technically informed people, and during that period the world has delivered a great deal of apparently relevant evidence: the scaling behavior of large neural networks, the unexpectedly smooth emergence of capabilities with compute, the speed at which recent systems diffuse through the economy. Both camps claim this record vindicates them, and neither has moved. That pattern -- abundant new data, zero convergence -- usually signals that the disagreement was never primarily about the data.

My working hypothesis is that the dispute rests on two different background pictures of what intelligence is: on one picture it is a thing, a general algorithmic faculty that can be fully instantiated inside one artifact and then turned on itself; on the other it is a process or a property of whole systems of people, machines, and institutions, so that a single artifact recursively improving itself in isolation is close to a category error. On this hypothesis, the arguments about return curves, optimization power, and historical analogy are surface manifestations, and the real crux is metaphysical or at least conceptual -- which would explain why empirical findings keep being absorbed without residue by both sides.

Is that diagnosis correct? I would like you to examine the structure of the disagreement as each side actually presents it: is it a substantive empirical dispute about the shape of returns to cognitive investment that simply hasn't been settled yet; a disguised definitional dispute about what counts as 'fast,' 'discontinuous,' or 'recursive self-improvement'; a methodological disagreement about whether historical analogy or first-principles modeling is the right instrument here; or, as I suspect, a deeper conceptual disagreement about the nature of intelligence itself? Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress. If the dispute is empirical at its core, specify what observation, over what timeframe, should shift a reasonable person on each side. If it is conceptual, say what distinction or reformulation of the question would dissolve or sharpen it. And please be candid about the possibility that the debate as standardly framed is simply ill-posed -- that 'fast versus slow takeoff' conflates several independent questions (about capability growth, about economic diffusion, about concentration of power) whose answers need not covary -- and if so, set out the separated questions and indicate which of them is genuinely open.
