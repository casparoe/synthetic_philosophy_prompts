After a long argument with a friend last week about whether anything is genuinely experienced while we dream — she insisted the dream simply happens to us in sleep, while I kept pressing that all we ever have is the story we wake up with — I've decided the dispute deserves to be an exam question. I'm setting the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of mind course. Draft one essay question on whether dreams are experiences, answerable in 75 minutes, built around the following crux: candidates must determine whether the disagreement between Malcolm (dreams are not experiences had during sleep; the waking report is the sole criterion of having dreamt, and there is no independent fact of the matter it answers to) and Dennett (the dream narrative may be composed on waking, so the report need not record anything that occurred earlier) is an empirical dispute that further evidence could settle, or a conceptual one about what could even count as evidence here. To force candidates out of summary mode, the question should hand them a concrete case: lucid dreamers, instructed beforehand, signal with prearranged eye movements while polysomnography confirms REM sleep, and afterwards report experiences timed to those signals. Ask them whether this settles the question — and, crucially, whether it settles it differently for Malcolm than for Dennett.

Provide a model answer and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, the marks each carries (totalling 20), and for each point a one-line note on the way candidates typically mishandle it (e.g., treating the signalling experiment as a straightforward vindication of the received view, or taking Malcolm to deny that people dream at all rather than to deny that dreaming is an experience during sleep).
