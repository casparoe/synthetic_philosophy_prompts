So I'm a novelist, about 2/3 through a comic novel set in a town dominated by a failing tech startup called Bertrand Industries, whose only product is the ChordMaster -- a brass machine that randomly inscribes chords on a circular plate, marketed to casinos, music theorists, and "probability hobbyists." The running joke of the book is that the company's three factories each built the machine using a different method of "randomly" picking chords (one picks two random points on the rim, one picks a random radius and then a random point along it, one picks a random point inside the circle as the chord's midpoint), and each factory is serenely confident that its method is the only truly random one. The lawsuits begin when a casino notices the three models give different odds for the same bet: whether a random chord is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle. Factory A's answer is 1/3, Factory B's is 1/2, Factory C's is 1/4, and all three have the math to prove it.

For the chapter where the company goes into crisis mode, I need an artifact: the official Bertrand Industries FAQ pamphlet that gets mailed to customers after the scandal breaks. Write it for me, roughly 500-650 words, in flawless deadpan corporate-reassurance voice -- headers, numbered questions, a "Troubleshooting" section, maybe a warranty clause or two. The humor has to come from the actual math and philosophy: the pamphlet should keep insisting the probability of the chord bet is "well-defined and unique" while the answer visibly changes depending on which model the customer owns, and it should treat the choice of parametrization as a lifestyle preference or a warranty issue rather than the whole problem. It should blame customers for asking the question wrong, recommend the Principle of Indifference as the solution while accidentally illustrating why it generates the paradox in the first place, and end with a smug note about a paid consultant (a Dr. J.) whose new method is "provably invariant" and whose results the company definately hasn't checked.

The target of the satire is the slide from "I have no reason to prefer one outcome over another" to "all outcomes are equally likely" -- the way indifference reasoning quietly depends on how you happened to describe the setup. A probabilist should be able to reverse-engineer all three construction methods and their answers from the jokes alone and laugh rather than wince; a non-mathematical reader should still find it funny as corporate doublespeak. No actual equations needed, keep it readable, but don't fudge which answer goes with which machine.
