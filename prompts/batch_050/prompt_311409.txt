Here is what I have just told you. Neurologists have described patients who, after a purely sensory illness, lose proprioception and touch throughout the body: no signal reaches them reporting where their limbs are or what those limbs are doing. Such a patient can still move, but only under continuous visual control. He must watch his hand in order to reach for a cup; if the lights go out mid-gesture, the arm stalls or drifts off course. He describes his body as a thing he operates from the outside, the way one operates a crane.

My challenge to you is this. You claim that ordinary movement is governed by a distinct, pre-reflective motor intentionality: the body's own grip on its tasks, irreducible to judgment, representation, or the stance of a spectator. But in this patient, movement is restored precisely by substituting vision and explicit attention for that bodily grip. Doesn't the case show that what you call motor intentionality is not a sui generis mode of intentionality at all, but merely a convenient shortcut — one the mind can replace, when forced, with the ordinary representational kind? And if it can be replaced wholesale, what explanatory work was the concept doing in the first place?
