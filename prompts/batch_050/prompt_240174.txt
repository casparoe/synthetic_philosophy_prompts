I'm a high school junior preparing for an Ethics Bowl-style tournament, and one of our cases asks whether famines should be classed as natural disasters or as crimes for which governments bear responsibility. In practice rounds my team keeps talking past our opponents because everyone uses the same labels — "avoidable," "man-made," "genocidal" — for different bundles of claims. What I need is not another argument but a classification instrument: a decision tree that takes the established facts of a particular famine as input and outputs a verdict category.

Please build the tree from six to eight branching questions. Each question must be answerable from the historical record of a case by someone who knows no theory names — so not "do you accept Amartya Sen's entitlement approach?" but something like "did the total food physically available in the region fall below its pre-crisis level, or did the collapse occur mainly in certain groups' ability to obtain food?" The questions should split on the claims where positions genuinely come apart: whether aggregate food availability declined at all, whether the authorities knew or could reasonably have known the scale of the crisis, whether a feasible policy existed that would have averted mass death, whether the deaths were an intended outcome or a foreseen side effect of a policy pursued for other ends, and whether the affected population had political channels (elections, a free press) through which a response could have been forced. Sen's well-known claim that no substantial famine has occurred in a functioning democracy should surface somewhere in the tree as a branching question rather than being assumed.

The tree should end in five to seven leaves, such as: genuine act of nature; market or entitlement failure without culpability; negligence; reckless policy disaster; and deliberate starvation used as an instrument. Give each leaf a name (or "unnamed" if nobody defends it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment — for instance, what the "act of nature" leaf is forced to say about the fact that droughts of similar severity often produce no famine at all.

Then run three real cases through the finished tree — the Irish famine of 1845–52, the Bengal famine of 1943, and the famine of the Great Leap Forward — stating which leaf each reaches and, crucially, at which branching question a reasonable respondent could answer differently and thereby route the case to a different leaf. Also design one hypothetical edge case (for example, a famine caused jointly by crop failure and a wartime blockade) that separates leaves the three real cases cannot separate. Finally, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot distinguish, and any logically possible combination of answers that has no defender, with one sentence each on whether that combination is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.
