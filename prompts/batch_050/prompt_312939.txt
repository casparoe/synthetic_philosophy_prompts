For a philosophy of mind reading group, I put together the following argument, which I now suspect proves too much, since its conclusion is one I cannot bring myself to accept. I'd like a diagnosis: which step should go, why it looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs.

(1) Whether a system has a belief is settled by whether attributing that belief to it yields predictions you could not otherwise get as cheaply; this is the core of the intentional-stance picture, and I take it to be independently plausible because any stronger condition on belief (intrinsic content, causal history, phenomenal feel) seems to demand more metaphysics than the concept can support.

(2) Attributing beliefs and desires is predictively useful, in at least some contexts, for an enormous range of systems: chess engines, thermostats, ant colonies, markets, nations, even a jammed door that "doesn't want to open."

(3) Predictive usefulness is a matter of degree and of the predictor's purposes and computational budget; there is no non-arbitrary threshold below which the usefulness "doesn't count."

(4) Therefore either beliefs are everywhere, or there is simply no fact of the matter about what anything believes, since every belief attribution is just a more-or-less handy instrument.

(5) But neither disjunct is true: there is a fact of the matter about what I believed yesterday morning, and my thermostat does not believe that the room is cold.

(6) So (1) is false, and the intentional-stance picture of belief must be rejected wholesale.

A friend suggests the argument actually collapses at (2) or (3), on the grounds that a pattern is real only when a description in terms of it genuinely compresses the data — when it buys you predictive power you could not have had more cheaply from a lower-level description — and that the thermostat and the jammed door fail this test because the physical stance already explains them with no loss. If that's the move, I want to know whether it can be made precise enough to do the work, or whether "genuine compression" just re-smuggles in the arbitrariness that (3) complains about. Alternatively, if (4) is the culprit — a false dilemma smuggled between (3) and the conclusion — say exactly where the slide happens. I'd rather keep (5) than (1) if forced to choose, but I'd like to know what I pay: does defending (5) against this argument commit me to a realism about content that needs its own defense, and what do the standard alternatives (eliminativism, interpretationism, brute intrinsic-content realism) each have to concede to block the argument at their chosen step?
