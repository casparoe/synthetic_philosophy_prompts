So I run the graduate colloquium series in our department, and in a couple of weeks we've got a guest whose new book I've just finished. She defends a pretty uncompromising position on forgery: roughly, that aesthetic value is exhausted by what's perceptually available in the object itself, so once a fake is genuinely indistinguishable from the original — not just to the untrained eye, but to any observer with human sensory capacities, in principle — it has exactly the same aesthetic value as the real thing. The astronomical price difference and all the fuss about provenance she dismisses as a mixture of status-signalling, insurance economics and what she calls "residual magic thinking" about objects. She explicitly bites the bullet on van Meegeren: the Vermeers were just as good after the attribution collapsed, and the fact that everyone suddenly found them less impressive shows something embarassing about us, not about the paintings.

I want to draft about eight to ten questions for the Q&A, and I'd like them to actually engage the view rather than just invite her to restate it. Could you write them for me, and for each one give me a follow-up keyed to the reply she's most likely to give? A few things I definitely want pressed on:

- The standard Goodman-style line that even a currently undetectable difference matters because we might later learn to discriminate the two, and our present perceptual practice is partly training for that. She must have a response; I want to know if it survives a follow-up.
- The autographic/allographic contrast: her view seems to work hardest for painting, but what does she say about cast sculpture made from the artist's own mould after her death, or a second printing of a poem? Is everything allographic on her view in the end, and if so hasn't she just changed the subject?
- Restoration. Originals are almost never fully original — cleaned frescoes, replaced bits of instrument, rebuilt temples. If provenance is magic thinking, it's odd that she (as she does in the book) still prefers heavily restored originals over perfect copies. That smells inconsistent to me.
- The achievement point: part of what we admire in a painting is what the artist pulled off, given the means and moment, and the forger is piggybacking on that. She calls this "confusing art with sport", but I want to push on whether aesthetic appreciation can really be quarantined from appreciating what someone did.
- End with something on what could possibly count against her position, since I'm honestly not sure what she'd accept as a defeater.

Tone-wise, keep the questions collegial but pointed — she's sharp and enjoys a fight, so no softballs, and please nothing generic like "what got you interested in aesthetics". Write the whole thing in British English, please.
