Next month I'm stepping down after six years as chair of our philosophy department, and at the end-of-year dinner I get ten minutes for a farewell speech. Half the room will be philosophers, the other half admin staff, partners, and a few students, so it can't be inside baseball but it also can't be pure mush. I want to build the whole thing around the absent-minded driver problem — the one where you're driving home, there are two exits in a row, you need the second one, but at each exit you can't remeber whether you've already passed the first. I've always thought the real punchline of that puzzle isn't about driving at all: it's that a plan can be optimal when you make it and yet stop being rational to follow once you're in the thick of things and can't tell where you are. Which is exactly what being chair felt like — you make a sensible plan in September, then some crisis hits in February and you genuinely can't tell whether this is the first exit or the second, whether you should stick to the plan or re-deliberate from scratch. So, three requirements: explain the puzzle cleanly enough that the non-philosophers get it in about ninety seconds; draw the lesson that written-down plans, standing norms, and colleagues you trust are the commitment devices that save you (the departmental version of a note taped to the dashboard); and end with actual advice for my successor that follows from the puzzle rather than being stapled on. Warm, a bit self-deprecating, no jargon beyond what the puzzle itself needs.
