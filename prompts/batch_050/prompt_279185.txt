Write a sonnet about the necessary a posteriori, taking as its subject the discovery that Hesperus and Phosphorus are one and the same body. Use the octave to render the situation before the discovery: two names introduced through different descriptions, two appearances at opposite ends of the night, and the genuine epistemic possibility that they belong to distinct things. Let the volta be the astronomer's identification itself, and let the closing couplet land the distinctly Kripkean point that the apparent contingency was never real: since each name rigidly designates Venus in every possible world, the identity could not have turned out false, and what felt like a way things might have gone otherwise was only a qualitatively identical situation involving different objects. A reader who knows Naming and Necessity should recognize the argument itself at work in the poem, not merely the example named.
