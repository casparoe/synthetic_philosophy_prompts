So I've been going down a rabbit hole on Berkeley's so-called "master argument" for idealism, the passage in section 23 of the Principles of Human Knowledge where he answers the obvious comeback about imagining trees in a park or books in a closet with nobody around to perceive them. His reply, roughly, is that when you do this you're just framing ideas in your own mind and leaving out the idea of a perceiver -- but you're perceiving or thinking of them the whole time yourself, so you haven't actually conceived them existing unconceived, and trying to do so is, he says, a manifest repugnancy.

Here's where you come in: act as an early modern specialist who's also fluent in the contemporary secondary debates, and think carefully before answering. I'd actually like you to start by brainstorming freely -- list every angle you can think of, even bad ones -- and only then settle on your final answer.

The thing is, I already know the standard objections to this argument, and I don't want any of them rehearsed back at me. The ones on my list:

1. The act/object conflation: from the fact that my conceiving of the tree is mine and mental, nothing follows about the tree conceived -- the object of the act isn't identical to the act, so there's no contradiction in the object being mind-independent.
2. The scope or de re/de dicto reply: "I conceive, of some tree, that nothing perceives it" is perfectly consistent; the contradiction only shows up if you illicitly read it as "I conceive that: a tree exists and nothing, including this very conceiving, relates to it." Berkeley trades on sliding between the two.
3. The conceiving-vs-picturing reply: at most Berkeley shows you can't form a mental image of something unperceived-by-anyone without your picturing it, and that's a triviality about imagery, not a metaphysical result about existence.
4. The representationalist reply: even granting I'm contemplating only my own ideas, an idea can represent something beyond itself without resembling it or containing it, so contemplation of ideas doesn't fence me inside them.
5. The parity objection: if the argument worked it would prove too much -- by the same form, nothing could exist unthought by me, which would collapse other minds, the past, and arguably even God's unperceived-by-me objects into my own ideas, a result Berkeley himself can't want.

What I want from you: one more objection to the master argument that is genuinely NOT on this list -- not a rewording of any of these, not a special case of one, and not two of them stapled together. It should target the actual passage I described above (the business about leaving out the idea of a perceiver while you're thinking of the trees all the while), not some other part of Berkeley's system. After giving it, tell me honestly whether it's actually any good or whether it collapses into one of the five under pressure, and say how it interacts with the strongest reply Berkeley could make to it. If you genuninely can't find a sixth objection that isn't a disguised variant, say that instead of forcing it -- I'd rather hear that than get a dressed-up version of #2.
