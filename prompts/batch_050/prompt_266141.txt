I spent forty years in a lab (marine biology, mostly) and only started reading philosophy in retirement, so bear with me. I've been thinking about what makes something the *same thing* over time, and I wrote down my gut verdicts on some cases. I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures them.

- The ferry I took to the islands for thirty years got new planks, a new engine, eventually a new hull, piece by piece. Still the same ferry, obviously.
- If someone had collected all the old rotted planks and reassembled them in a shed, that thing in the shed is NOT the ferry. It's a pile of old planks in ferry shape.
- My body has turned over most of its atoms since I was twenty. Still me, no question.
- An amoeba splits in two. My gut says the original amoeba is just *gone* -- neither daughter is it.
- A candle flame is not the same flame from one minute to the next. A flame is a process, not an object. But the river by my house is the same river it was fifty years ago, even though it's also just stuff flowing through. This pair bothers me.
- The fission case philosophers love: my brain halved, each half in a new body, both waking up with my memories. I refuse to say I'm neither of them, but I can't be both, they're in different rooms. Stuck.

Is there one account of persistence that gets all of these, or do I have to give one up? And what does your answer say about the case I keep flip-flopping on: the old family barn we disassembled and rebuilt board by board at a museum -- same barn or not?
