I have recently been appointed to the five-member board of a mid-sized regional arts foundation, and my first substantive task is to help settle a dispute that has divided the board since the results of our annual public-art commission came in. This year we ran a two-track selection: a jury of five credentialed critics, curators, and practicing artists ranked the twelve finalists, and in parallel we held a well-publicized public vote that drew about nine thousand ballots. The two outcomes diverged as sharply as they possibly could have. The jury's clear first choice, a restrained and technically demanding sound installation, finished eleventh in the public vote. The public's runaway favorite, a monumental sentimental sculpture of two children with a rescue dog, was ranked last by the jury, whose written comments described it as kitsch engineered for applause.

The board must now decide how future commissions will be awarded, and there are three live options: (a) give the jury sole authority; (b) give the public vote sole authority; (c) adopt some standing weighting of the two. The debate has become unexpectedly theoretical. One board member argues that the jury's prestige rests on a kind of circularity: we certify judges as experts because their verdicts agree with those of previously certified judges, so their authority cannot be invoked to overrule the public without begging the question. Another counters that this is roughly the position Hume defused in Of the Standard of Taste — judgments of beauty are not mere reports of personal preference, because some judges possess sounder faculties, more practiced and delicate perception, wider experience of comparison, and greater freedom from prejudice, and their joint verdict is the closest thing to an objective standard we can hope for. A third member, who has actually read the essay closely, objects that Hume concedes near the end that certain differences of taste are blameless and irreconcilable, owing to age, temperament, and the customs of one's country — and she argues that a chasm between a small professional jury and nine thousand of our own neighbors looks far more like that kind of blameless diversity than like a correctable error on the public's part.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the board ahead of next month's meeting. It should recommend one of the three options (if a weighting, specify concrete proportions and any safeguards, such as jury-selection reforms or a threshold the public vote must clear), state the reasoning the board can actually act on, and engage seriously with both the circularity worry and the question of where, on something like Hume's view, this particular disagreement falls: a deficiency in one side's judgment that a standard of taste can in principle correct, or a blameless difference that no honest standard should pretend to settle.
