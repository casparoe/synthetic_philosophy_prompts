There's a question I keep coming back to in the literature on moral responsibility: do psychopaths know that what they do is wrong? It sounds like it should have been settled decades ago by empirical work, but as far as I can tell it hasn't moved. On one side, people argue that psychopaths genuinely lack moral understanding -- that their deficits in empathy, guilt, and affective response mean they never really grasp moral reasons, the way a congenitally blind person doesn't grasp color -- and so they are excused from moral responsibility much as the severely mentally ill are. On the other side, people point out that psychopaths can reliably say which actions are wrong, can draw the moral/conventional distinction in experimental settings, often conceal their crimes and express strategic remorse at parole hearings, which suggests they know perfectly well what morality requires and simply don't care. Both camps cite largely the same behavioral and neuroimaging data. I've been reading this literature for a seminar and my working hypothesis is that the debate is stalled because the two sides are operating with different conceptions of what it takes to *know* something is wrong -- one requiring that moral knowledge be intrinsically motivating or at least emotionally registered, the other requiring only the ability to make correct moral judgments -- and that this is a conceptual disagreement dressed up as an empirical one.

But I'm not confident in that hypothesis, because there are live empirical disputes too: for instance, about whether psychopaths' success on the moral/conventional task is robust or an artifact of test design, and about whether their ability to mimic moral talk shows understanding or just learned verbal behavior. And there's a further tangle I can't sort out: some participants frame the whole thing as a question about moral responsibility and blame, others as a question about the nature of moral knowledge itself, and others as a question about the psychology of a specific clinical population -- and it's unclear to me whether these are one debate or three debates sharing a name.

So here's my request. Diagnose this disagreement rather than adjudicate it. Using what each side actually argues -- not caricatures -- tell me which parts of the impasse are empirical questions that better experiments could settle, which parts are conceptual disagreements about what moral knowledge consists in, and which parts are disguised normative disagreements about who deserves blame. Given your diagnosis, tell me concretely what would count as progress: is there an experimental result, a conceptual distinction (say, between kinds or grades of understanding), or a reframing of the question that would actually move it? Or is this one of those debates where no resolution should be expected because the question 'do they know it's wrong?' is itself ill-posed -- and if so, what question should replace it?
