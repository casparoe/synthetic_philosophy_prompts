In my graduate seminar on democratic legitimacy, a colleague closed down a discussion of the boundary problem with the familiar one-liner that everyone affected by a decision should have a say in making it. He used it to conclude, without further argument, that residents of one state ought to have some voice in a neighboring state's industrial policy, since its emissions cross the border. I suspect the slogan hides several quite different principles, not all of which he can afford. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses the slogan could express: different readings of what it is to be 'affected' (having an interest touched, having one's rights at stake, being subject to coercion, being causally impacted at all), different scopes (affected by this decision, by any decision of this body, by the existence of the body itself), and different strengths of 'say' (a vote, consultation, a veto, mere consideration). For each, say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or self-undermining -- in particular whether it presupposes a prior decision about who counts, and so cannot itself settle the boundary question without circularity. Then identify which readings, if any, would actually support my colleague's conclusion about cross-border voice, and which he probably meant. Conclude with a last line of the form \answer{...} giving the number of the reading his argument minimally requires, or \answer{none} if no coherent reading gets him there.
