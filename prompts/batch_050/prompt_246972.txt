So here's something that's bugged me since a crim law lecture last year. In the US (and most places, I think) the law draws a weird line around selling silence. If I know some embarassing true fact about you, it's perfectly legal for me to just go public with it. It's also perfectly legal for you to come to me first and pay me to sign an NDA about it -- people do this all the time, hush-money settlements are routine. But if *I* come to *you* and say I'd like some money to stay quiet, that's blackmail, a felony, even though the end result (money for silence) is literally identical to the legal NDA. The same transaction is legal or a crime depending on who proposed it and what got said first.

And it gets stranger when you look at what the law *does* allow. A union can say "double our wages or we all walk off the job" -- a threat to do something lawful (quit) in order to extract money -- and that's protected. A lawyer can say "settle for 200k or I'll file this lawsuit" -- threat to do something lawful (sue) to extract money -- totally fine. But "pay me or I tell your wife" is prison. So the law clearly doesn't run on any simple principle like "threatening to do a legal thing in exchange for money is wrong," because it blesses that exact structure in other contexts.

My question: what theory of wrongness does the criminal ban on blackmail actually presuppose? Like, what's the implicit account of what makes the forbidden case forbidden -- is it about coercion, about the information not being mine to sell, about threats vs. offers, about who's allowed to initiate the deal? Reconstruct the theory that makes sense of the whole pattern (NDAs fine, strikes fine, settlement demands fine, blackmail criminal), and be honest about where the practice is underdetermined -- i.e. where several rival theories would all license the same pattern and the law just doesn't commit. Then tell me which parts of the pattern each of the standard candidate explanations *can't* explain, because I have a feeling none of them fits all four corners, and if that's right, what would the law actually have to look like if we took the best-fitting theory seriously?
