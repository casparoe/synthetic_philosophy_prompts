My housemate has stopped buying chicken and last week I tried to argue him out of it, because I reckon one shopper more or less changes nothing. Problem is I argued myself into a corner and now I can't get out. Here it is numbered, because otherwise I lose track:

(1) An action is only wrong if it makes some difference to how much harm there is.
(2) Whether I buy one extra chicken makes zero difference to how many chickens get raised and killed — supermarkets order in bulk, production is decided miles upstream, and one customer is a rounding error.
(3) So my buying a chicken isn't wrong.
(4) But whatever makes (2) true of me makes it true of every other shopper too, one by one.
(5) And if each purchase on its own is fine, then all the purchases together are fine — you can't pile up harmless things and end up with a harmful heap.
(6) Yet all those purchases together are exactly what keeps the whole industry running, and that's obviously an enormous harm.
(7) So either the industry's harm is nobody's fault at all, or a heap of harmless acts can somehow be wrong. Both sound mad to me.

Here's the bit where my housemate turned it round on me. There's a butcher near us who only rings the farm to place an order once he's got 50 confirmed customer orders, and he tells you where the counter's at. So if I order when the counter says 49, my one purchase definitely triggers fifty more chickens being killed — vastly more harm than the single chicken I take home. By (1) that purchase is clearly wrong. But nothing about me, my reasons or my chicken changes between count 48 and count 49, so it's bizarre that one is a moral disaster and the other is totally fine. Worse, the supermarket is just the same setup with the counter hidden — which means my argument seems to show that whether your shopping is wrong depends on whether some shopkeeper happens to tell you a number, and that can't possibly be how morality works.

So which step do I reject? If I get to choose, I'd rather keep (1), because "you have to actually make a difference" feels like the bare minimum for a moral rule, and (2) seems like just a boring fact about supply chains. But I want to know what I'd be paying to keep them — i.e. if the fix is elsewhere, spell out exactly which premise goes, why it looked innocent, and what the obvious replacements (expected harm? sharing the blame? being part of a group that does harm?) each force me to swallow.
