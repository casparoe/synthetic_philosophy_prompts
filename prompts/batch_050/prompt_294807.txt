I hold that wide reflective equilibrium — the mutual adjustment of considered judgments, moral principles, and background theories until coherence is achieved — does not merely organize our moral beliefs but actually justifies them: reaching equilibrium confers epistemic warrant on its deliverances, even absent any independent foundation. Tell me what would count against this position. In particular: (a) Would a formal demonstration that the method is path-dependent — that agents with identical evidence but different revision orders reliably arrive at incompatible equilibria — be fatal, or could a defender absorb it by adding constraints on permissible revisions, and at what cost to the view's appeal? (b) Which empirical findings about the origins of our considered judgments (say, that they systematically track morally arbitrary features of one's upbringing) would undermine the justificatory claim while leaving the weaker coherence claim intact? (c) Is the possibility of a fully coherent but morally monstrous equilibrium a defeater, or does the position have principled, non-circular resources for excluding such states? Distinguish the defeaters a committed defender of the method would themselves concede as genuine threats from those they would dismiss as changing the subject, and rank them from fatal to merely awkward. Please answer in American English.
