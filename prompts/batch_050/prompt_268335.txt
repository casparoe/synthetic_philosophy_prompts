I have come to accept a version of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against ultimate moral responsibility: you act as you do because of the way you are; to be ultimately responsible for what you do you would have to be responsible for the way you are; but responsibility for the way you are would require having intentionally shaped yourself, which you could only have done in light of principles and preferences you already had -- a regress no finite agent can originate. What I want is a map of this argument's exposure. Which premise, if it turned out to be false, would do the least collateral damage to our ordinary practices of praise and blame, and is there any premise a defender could actually concede without the argument collapsing? Are there serious candidate regress-stoppers -- a first self-forming choice that is neither determined by prior character nor a mere accident -- and would any of them, if coherent, be decisive rather than a technicality? The argument seems to rest on a transfer principle (non-responsibility transmits from inputs to outputs); if that principle misfires elsewhere, say in cases of culpable ignorance where we do hold people responsible for downstream results of earlier negligence, does that count against the argument, or can the principle just be restricted? And is there anything empirical -- say, about how character actually develops -- that could threaten it, or has it been so carefully insulated by qualifications that nothing could count against it, and would that itself be a defect? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and distinguish what a committed Strawsonian would concede from what critics merely assert.
