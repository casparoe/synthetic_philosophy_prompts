I am preparing the central ninety-minute session of an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of criminal law, devoted to the legality objection against the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals. The students are a mix: the philosophy majors arrive knowing nullum crimen sine lege as a slogan and little else; the history majors know the trials as events but have never asked whether waging aggressive war was a crime for individuals, as opposed to states, in 1939. I do not want a lecture script; I want the plan for the explanation.

First, the order of ideas, with reasons. Should the session open with the legality principle and its justifications — notice, restraint on judges, the separation of powers — and then confront it with 1945, or should it open with the defendants' conduct and let the principle emerge as the uncomfortable reply? Commit to one ordering and state what it buys and what it forfeits.

Second, the carrying example. I want one episode through which the whole problem is taught, and it must satisfy three conditions: it cannot rest on the extermination camps alone, because that makes retroactivity feel obviously permissible and the interesting question evaporates; it must involve defendants with a genuinely arguable legality defense; and it must let students see that retroactivity is not binary — a charge can fail because the conduct was not criminal at all, because it bound states rather than individuals, or because no international tribunal could reach it. Tell me which single episode meets these conditions — the aggressive-war charge, the crimes-against-humanity charge with its required connection to the war, the Tokyo conspiracy count, or something I have not listed — and what I lose by not building on the others.

Third, the misconception map. I expect students to assume the objection is dissolved by the enormity of the crimes, and to treat the tu quoque reply — Dresden, a Soviet judge on the bench — as a knockout rather than a distinct and weaker move. Identify the further misconceptions they will form, in the order they will surface, and mark exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than answered after the fact.

Fourth, the closing check: give me one question for the final ten minutes — I am imagining a compressed hypothetical in which a victorious alliance tries enemy ministers under a charter drafted after the war — whose handling would show that students grasp that the tribunals had several distinct responses to the legality objection available and chose among them, not merely that the defendants were guilty.

Finally, tell me what to leave out and to say I am leaving out — the subsequent occupation-zone trials and the Yamashita precedent strike me as candidates — and where Tokyo diverges enough from Nuremberg to warrant its own beat (Pal's dissent, the emperor's absence) rather than being folded in as a parallel case.
