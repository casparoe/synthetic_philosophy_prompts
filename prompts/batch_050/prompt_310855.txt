Is there a name for this? I cover science and philosophy for a general-audience magazine, and in interviews I keep running into the same move, and I want to know if philosophers have a word for it or if I need to invent one for a piece I'm writing.

Here's the pattern. A physicist tells me she can't picture how subjective experience could be nothing over and above neurons firing -- and then, in the next breath, concludes that physicalism about the mind must be wrong. An AI guy tells me he cannot imagine a pile of matrix multiplications genuinely understanding anything, and treats that as basically a knock-down arguement against machine understanding. What strikes me is that in neither case did the person actually picture some coherent scenario that rules the thing out. Nobody sat there and vividly imagined a world physically identical to ours but with nobody home inside. They just tried to picture the mechanism, drew a blank, and then treated the blank itself as evidence against the view.

This feels different from a couple of nearby things, and here's where I need help. It's not quite the classic argument from ignorance -- the person isn't saying we lack evidence for the view, they're pointing at a gap in their own imagination. And it's also not the standard philosophical move you hear about where conceiving of something gets taken as a guide to what's possible, because in these cases nobody successfully conceived of anything. The imagination just failed, and the failure got upgraded into a conclusion about reality. It's like the negative space of the conceivability move.

So: is there an established name for this specific inference, from "I try and fail to conceive of P" to "P is impossible"? And has anyone thought carefully about when it's legit, if ever? If there's no clean established concept, give me a name and a definition sharp enough to distinguish it from (a) plain argument from ignorance, (b) the conceivability-to-possibility inference, and (c) just having an underpowered scientific imagination. And then the thing I really want: what separates a case like "I can't conceive of a square circle, and that seems like pretty solid grounds for thinking there couldn't be one" from the consciousness case? Because the first one feels fine and the second one feels fishy, but I can't articulate why, and the difference matters for whether my interviewees are reasoning well or not.
