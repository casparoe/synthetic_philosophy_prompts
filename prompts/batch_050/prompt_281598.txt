I’m a novelist working on a chapter where my protagonist keeps reaching the same two motorway exits with no memory of whether she has already passed the first one, and I want the philosophy underneath it to feel right rather than like set dressing. She can, before getting in the car, choose a policy: always take exit one, always take exit two, or randomize with some fixed probability. Once driving, though, every time she sees an exit she has the same evidence: headlights, same song on the radio, same little pain in her neck, no reliable marker saying first or second. If she defects from the ex ante plan at a single exit, she is also defecting at the other indistinguishable one. Can you explain, in a way that gives me a gut-level model, why the absent-minded driver problem makes ordinary maximize-expected-utility-at-this-moment reasoning wobble, and why the answer changes depending on whether we evaluate a person-stage, an information set, or a whole contingent plan?

The part I most need for the chapter is the tension between what she would sign before losing memory and what seems compelling inside the fog. Please walk through why precommitment can make a mixed policy look best from the outside, while at each actual choice point she might think that a tiny tweak is harmless, and why that harmless tweak is secretly being made twice. Don’t just name-drop Sleeping Beauty or decision theories; connect it to the more general issue of whether rationality belongs to agents, to temporal slices, or to policies when evidence is coarse and memory is partitioned. I’d especially like you to use my invented wrinkle: the toll company sometimes scrambles only the memory of having chosen, not the memory of intending to choose, so she can remember that she meant to randomize but not whether the coin already landed. Does that make her more like one self implementing a plan, or more like two interchangeable drivers who happen to share a body and a payoff?

Keep it intuitive but still precise enough that a philosopher wouldn’t groan. No bibliography or citations, please; I’m offline in a cabin and only care about the conceptual mechanics. If there are a few natural candidate answers, say which one you’d bet on and what would make you change your mind. Also, small request: flag where the novelist is tempted to cheat by giving the character a narratively convenient clue, because my draf has one and I suspect it ruins the paradox.
