A popular science book I read summarizes the cognitive science of religion like this: "Evolutionary researchers have shown that religious belief is a by-product of ordinary mental machinery rather than an adaptation. Our ancestors evolved a hyperactive agency detection device to spot predators in the grass, and this device misfires, making us perceive gods in thunder, intentions in illness, and spirits in the dark. Experiments also show that people remember concepts about minimally counterintuitive beings -- ghosts that pass through walls but still eat dinner, for instance -- better than ordinary concepts, which is why these ideas spread so successfully. Since religion is therefore an accidental side effect rather than something selection built, the theory predicts that religious belief will fade as scientific literacy spreads, and it implies that religious experiences are, in the end, just neural glitches." Something about the last part seems off to me, but the first half sounds like what I've heard elsewhere. How fair is this summary overall? Please separate what a researcher in this field would accept, what counts as a reasonable simplification, and what is a misrepresentation or an inference the theory does not license -- in particular, does anything in the accurate part of the summary actually support the prediction that religion will fade, or the characterization of religious experience as a glitch?
