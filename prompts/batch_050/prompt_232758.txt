Question: "Pain asymbolia is sometimes brought up in debates about what makes pain bad. Describe the condition accurately, explain why it seems relevant to the debate between views on which pain's badness is located in the sensation itself versus in our aversive reaction to it, and say what, if anything, it settles. (About 300 words.)"

Student's answer: "Pain asymbolia is a condition, usually resulting from damage to the insular cortex or from cingulotomy, in which patients still report feeling pain and can identify its location and intensity, but say it no longer bothers them. They withdraw their hand from the flame, so to speak, but without the urgency -- the unpleasantness is gone while the sensation remains. (This should be distinguished from cases where people never learn to avoid painful stimuli at all and end up with repeated injuries from childhood onward, which is a different developmental condition.) The condition matters for value theory because it seems to prise apart two things we normally experience as one: the sensory character of pain and its badness. If the sensation is still there but the patient genuinely doesn't mind it, then whatever made normal pain bad can't be the sensation alone -- it must be the aversive reaction, the dislike. And since these patients don't have the dislike, their pains plausibly aren't bad for them at all. So asymbolia more or less vindicates the attitudinal or motivational account of pain's badness over the sensational account: the debate is essentially settled by the neurology. Defenders of the sensational view might say the patients still have a reason to avoid pain even if they don't mind it, but this just reasserts the view under dispute without engaging the evidence. The remaining question is why natural selection glued the two components together in most of us, presumably because a sensation that reliably triggers avoidance is more useful than one that merely informs."
