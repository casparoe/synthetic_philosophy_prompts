I am in the second year of a PhD in philosophy, working on the normative foundations of decision theory, and I am drafting a chapter in which I defend a package of views centred on risk-weighted expected utility in the style of Buchak. Before I commit to it, I want to know whether the package is coherent. My commitments are these:

1. Risk-weighted expected utility is the correct normative theory of choice under risk: an agent's attitude to risk is captured by her risk function, and her utility function measures the value of outcomes themselves, not her attitude to gambles over them.
2. Permissivism about the risk function: any strictly increasing risk function is rationally permissible, so a maximally risk-avoidant agent and a maximally risk-seeking agent with identical beliefs and utilities are equally rational, and neither can criticise the other.
3. Dynamic consistency: a rational agent never adopts a plan she can foresee she will later abandon, and needing to bind, constrain, or manage one's future self is a symptom that something has gone wrong in one's decision theory.
4. The St Petersburg puzzle is adequately resolved by the diminishing marginal utility of money, roughly as Bernoulli proposed.
5. The Allais common-consequence preferences are rational, and rational preferences over prospects must be complete, transitive, and continuous.

I suspect there is at least one genuine problem here and at least one merely apparent one, but I cannot see clearly which is which. Please give me a committed audit, not a menu of options: tell me which commitments genuinely conflict, which only appear to, and which single revision you would actually advise me to make, with your reasons. Format your answer as exactly five numbered items, each no more than two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: order the genuine tensions by severity first, then the apparent tension and why it dissolves, and end with your definite recommendation about what has to give.
