In a philosophy of art seminar I'm taking, we've been discussing whether photographs differ from paintings in kind or merely in degree. A classmate defended the strong view with an analogy that initially struck me as obviously right: a photograph, he said, is like a telescope, a periscope, or a pane of glass -- an optical prosthesis. Just as I literally see the moon through a telescope rather than seeing a mere picture of it, I literally see my late grandfather when I look at a photograph of him, whereas a painted portrait of him, however accurate, only ever shows me a representation. He used this to argue that the special epistemic and emotional authority of photography -- why courts trust photographs, why we flinch at certain images -- rests on the fact that photographs are transparent: we see through them to the world. I'm sympathetic, but the analogy feels load-bearing in ways I can't quite locate.

Please audit the analogy carefully. First, lay out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the lens, the intervening medium, the viewer, and the thing viewed, and which features of the telescope case -- counterfactual dependence of the image on the scene, mechanical rather than intentional production, temporal structure -- are supposed to carry over to photography. Second, identify where the mapping strains. I'm especially interested in: whether the mechanical-causal story actually supports the perceptual conclusion or merely an epistemic one (a seismograph's trace is mechanically caused too, but I don't see the earthquake through it); the role of the photographer's choices, which have no analogue in the telescope; what happens to the analogy when the photograph is of something I could never see unaided, or is heavily processed; and whether temporal displacement -- seeing the dead, the distant past -- is a feature telescopes share or a disanalogy that breaks the transparency claim. Third, say which of these disanalogies matter for my classmate's intended conclusion about photography's authority and which are harmless, whether that conclusion survives once the audit is done, and, if the telescope analogy fails, whether a better one exists for capturing what is genuinely distinctive about photographs.
