Write a decision letter, approximately 650 words, from an imagined Bureau of Priority and Historical Inevitability, denying an applicant's claim to be credited as the discoverer of something significant. You may invent the discovery or let the applicant be a figure resembling one of the famous near-simultaneous cases (a naturalist whose essay on the origin of species arrives in the post of an older gentleman who has been sitting on the same theory for twenty years, or a mathematician whose calculus manuscript is alleged to have appeared suspiciously soon after a rival's letters). The Bureau operates on a strict inevitability doctrine: since the relevant ideas were, as it puts it, in the air, the discovery would have been made by someone within a few years regardless, and therefore no individual deserves priority, credit, or a named law. The humor must be deadpan and bureaucratic, and it must come from applying this doctrine with perfect internal consistency and watching it devour everything in reach: the applicant is congratulated on behalf of the spirit of the age rather than on his own behalf; the Bureau declines to commend the clerk who drafted the letter, on the grounds that any clerk would have drafted it; the appeals procedure exists but has never changed an outcome, since every ruling was bound to be issued by someone. The letter should get the real philosophical tension right -- that if multiples and simultaneous discovery are the norm, the entire institution of credit (prizes, eponymous laws, the rule that at most three can share a Nobel) rests on a fiction, and yet the reader should feel, without the letter ever admitting it, that something genuine is lost when the applicant's decades of work are refunded as a contribution by the zeitgeist. Include at least one point where the doctrine turns on the Bureau itself, played entirely straight. Do not state the objections to the inevitability thesis; let the reader's recoil do that work. The reply should be continuous prose in the manner of an actual formal letter -- salutation, paragraphs, sign-off -- with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
