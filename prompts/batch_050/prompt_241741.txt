I want to stress-test a position I've held for years, largely absorbed from the rationalist blogosphere's reading of Aumann-style agreement results and Robin Hanson's writing on disagreement. The position, as I hold it, is roughly this: when two honest, competent reasoners know each other's track records, have access to each other's arguments, and persistently disagree about a question of fact, at least one of them is doing something epistemically wrong, and the community around them is right to treat that unresolved disagreement as a defect to be repaired rather than a stable equilibrium to be tolerated. I take the spirit of the formal result to be that rational agents with shared priors and mutual knowledge of each other's estimates should converge, so the rationalist movement's culture of "you cannot agree to disagree" is not just a social norm but an approximation of a theorem.

I've become less sure the position survives contact with its own foundations, and I'd like a map of its exposure rather than reassurance. Specifically:

1. What formal or technical results would count against the *inferential step* from the theorem to the norm? I have in mind results about bounded computation, about agents who cannot fully communicate their private evidence, or about what convergence guarantees actually require. Which of these, if established or made precise, would be decisive, and which would merely complicate the picture?

2. What empirical observations would count against the position? For instance: if it turned out that the forecasters with the best track records systematically retain more residual disagreement with each other than the mediocre ones do, would that damage the position or be explained away by it? I'd like candidate observations sorted by how decisively they'd cut, including things that arguably have already been observed.

3. What would the position's own defenders concede as counting against it? I'm less interested in what critics of the rationalist community say and more interested in what someone committed to the convergence picture would have to accept as a defeater — e.g., a demonstration that the common-prior assumption does the real work and is indefensible for actual humans, or a case where the rational response to a peer's stubbornness is to update *away* from their stated estimate.

4. Has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability? In my experience, every apparent counterexample gets absorbed: persistent disagreement is attributed to unstated private evidence, to differing priors that are themselves beyond adjudication, or to one party's concealed irrationality. If the view can absorb anything, does it still risk anything — and for a view of this kind (a normative claim dressed in formal clothing), is that insulation a fatal flaw or just the nature of heuristic principles?

One further thing. A friend proposed a diagnostic she calls the *convergence audit*: pick a disagreement between two respected forecasters, have each privately write down what evidence would move them to the other's position, and check whether those conditions are symmetric and checkable. Her claim is that if the audit reliably fails — if the conditions are asymmetric, uncheckable, or absent — that's evidence the theorem's idealizations, not anyone's irrationality, are doing the work. Is she right that a failed audit cuts against my position, or would a defender of the view predict failed audits for reasons fully compatible with it? I'd like this assessed as part of the overall map, not as an afterthought.
