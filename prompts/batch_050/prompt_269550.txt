I'm taking an undergraduate course on personal identity, and my roommate and I have spent the semester arguing about teletransportation cases (we've read some Parfit, among other things). She has a settled pattern of verdicts, always elicited the same practical way: I describe a procedure, and she tells me whether, if she underwent it, she would regard whoever exists afterward as her — that is, whether she could accept the procedure without feeling she was signing up for her own death.

Her verdicts so far:

1. Ordinary survival. Going to sleep and waking up the next morning is survival. Uncontroversial baseline.
2. Whole-brain transplant. Her brain is moved intact into a healthy donor body; the original body dies. Survival — she'd accept.
3. Simple teletransportation. A scanner records her body down to the last detail, destroying the original in the process, and a single perfect replica is assembled on Mars from new matter. Survival — she'd accept, though she admits she'd be nervous stepping in.
4. Gradual silicon replacement. Her neurons are replaced one at a time with functionally identical synthetic substitutes over several years, with no break in her experience at any point, until her brain is entirely artificial. Survival.
5. Degraded replica. As in case 3, but the transmission is lossy: the replica wakes with roughly ninety percent of her memories and a slightly flattened version of her personality. Survival — she'd still accept, though she'd consider it a worse outcome than case 3.

I have never put a fission case to her. Here is the new case I want to raise: the scanner destroys the original exactly as in case 3, but the receiving station builds two perfect replicas from the same data, each fully psychologically continuous with her, each indistinguishable from the other and from her pre-scan self, and they go on to live separate lives. The machine cannot be set to produce only one; it is both or neither.

Please do the following, and commit to definite answers rather than surveying the options for me to choose between:

(a) Predict her verdict on the new case: accept it as survival, reject it as death, or some third response. Give me one answer with your reasoning.

(b) Identify the rule that best explains her five existing verdicts and therefore grounds that prediction, and identify at least one other rule that fits all five verdicts equally well but delivers a different verdict on the new case. Say which of her earlier verdicts does the most work in eliminating further candidates (for instance, views on which physical or brain continuity is what matters).

(c) Suppose my real aim is to figure out which rule she is actually running, not just to predict this one case. Name the single further case I should put to her next to discriminate between your two best candidate rules, and say what each rule predicts about it.

If you think her five verdicts genuinely underdetermine the new case — that no rule is better supported than its rivals — you may say so, but only if you can show that the live candidates are equally good fits; otherwise I want your best guess, defended.
