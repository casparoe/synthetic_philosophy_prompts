I run a little philosophy of mind reading group, eight of us, mostly grad students from different departments, and we meet for 90 minutes once a week. Last session got weirdly heated: we started talking about LLMs and ended up in a fight about whether concepts are fixed things that analogies merely decorate, or whether analogy-making is somehow the core of having concepts at all. Someone brought up the Copycat stuff — Hofstadter's letter-string microdomain — and it turned out half the group had never actually sat down and solved any of the puzzles, only heard them described. For anyone rusty: you get transformations of short letter strings, like abc goes to abd, and you have to find the "same" transformation applied to a new string, like iijjkk. Easy enough (iijjll). But then mrrjjj resists the obvious rule — there is no single letter at the right end to take the successor of, it's a group of three j's — and something like iijjll vs mrrjjjj forces you to decide whether "the thing at the right end" meant a letter or a group. And xyz is the nasty one, because z has no successor, so people start slipping on "rightmost", "successor", even the direction the string reads. The point my friend was making is that concepts like "rightmost letter" or "successor" feel rigid until the situation pressures them, and then you can watch yourself let one stretch.

So: design me an activity for the next session that makes people feel that slippage in themselves before anyone names it. I don't want to lecture about fluid concepts and then nod at a few puzzles on a whiteboard — I want them to experience their own concept of, say, "the same transformation" buckling under pressure in real time, ideally in some structured game format rather than just everyone silently solving strings on paper. Give me concrete rules and materials (index cards? teams? some kind of bidding or betting mechanic where people stake confidence on their answer before reveals?), a round structure that escalates the pressure cases in the right order, and a scoring scheme that doesn't need real money but still makes committing to an answer cost something. Then a debrief plan: how to walk them back through specific moments in the game and map them onto the claim that concepts are fluid and context-pressured, without me having to do all the work in the debrief because the game already did it. Also tell me what's predictably going to go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing someone will insist mrrjjjj is just wrong and the puzzle is broken, and someone else will treat the whole thing as a trick IQ test), and be honest about what the activity can't establish — a microdomain of letter strings is a long way from concepts like justice or knowledge, and I'd rather they see that gap clearly than overgeneralize.
