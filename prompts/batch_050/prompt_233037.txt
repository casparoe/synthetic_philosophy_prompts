In the rule-following debate, my settled position is that Kripke's skeptical argument stands: no fact about a speaker — dispositional, introspectible, or behavioral — constitutes her meaning plus rather than quus, so any viable account of meaning must be non-straight solution, for instance a skeptical one resting on justified assertibility rather than meaning-constituting facts. Map this position's exposure. For each candidate kind of meaning-constituting fact — dispositions, primitive sui generis mental states, teleological facts about proper function, facts about an idealized linguistic community — say what the world would have to be like for that fact to do the work the skeptic demands, and whether it is a live option or merely a logical possibility. Then answer two sharper questions. First, is the normativity objection — that no descriptive fact can make a response correct rather than merely expected — itself open to refutation, or does it insulate skepticism against every counterexample? Suppose neuroscience identified a brain state that covaries perfectly with a speaker's arithmetical judgments and drives her self-corrections: would I have to concede anything, and if not, does that show the position is deep or merely unfalsifiable? Second, distinguish what my own side would concede as decisive against skepticism from what would merely be awkward, and say whether a position this well insulated is in good shape or in trouble.
