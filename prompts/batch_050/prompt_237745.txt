I serve on my jurisdiction's Criminal Law Revision Commission. The legislature has asked us whether the blackmail statute should be amended in light of a contested prosecution. The facts: an accountant at a pharmaceutical firm discovered that the CEO was quietly diverting company funds to pay for her brother's medical treatment -- technically embezzlement, though a jury might well sympathize. The accountant wrote to the CEO stating that he would report the matter to the board unless she resigned and forfeited her bonus; he sought nothing for himself. Prosecutors charged him under a statute criminalizing threats to reveal information made "with intent to obtain something of value," and the defense argues that the CEO's resignation confers no value on the accountant. The Commission must recommend one of three positions: (a) retain the statute unchanged; (b) narrow it so that only threats aimed at extracting money or property for the threatener count; or (c) decriminalize the demand entirely where the threatened disclosure is itself lawful and the information true.

Please draft the memorandum I will circulate to the Commission before our vote. It should recommend one of the three options and state the recommendation plainly at the top, but its core work is conceptual. The case exposes a structural puzzle: each of the accountant's acts, considered separately -- revealing the embezzlement, and urging the CEO to step down -- is lawful and arguably commendable, yet their combination is prosecuted as a serious felony. The memo should say whether this puzzle, of a crime built entirely from permissible parts, is a genuine defect in the law or an artifact of how the facts are described, and it should defend that verdict rather than merely assert it. It should also give the strongest version of the position it rejects (if you recommend narrowing or repeal, take seriously the intuition that the combination is worse than either part; if you recommend retention, take seriously the charge that the statute criminalizes leverage as such, which lawful bargaining uses constantly). Finally, test the recommended rule against two harder variants: the accountant who demands $50,000 for himself instead, and the accountant who demands that the $50,000 be paid to a charity he supports. The memo should be roughly 900 words, written for legally trained readers who have not studied the philosophical literature on blackmail.
