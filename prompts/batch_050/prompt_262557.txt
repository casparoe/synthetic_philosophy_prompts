I'm a high school department chair helping to draft our school's policy on student use of AI writing assistants, and I keep running into the same analogy from both sides of the debate. Defenders of permitting the tools say that ChatGPT is to writing what the calculator was to arithmetic: we stopped drilling long division by hand, nothing essential was lost, and students were freed to focus on higher-order problem solving -- so we should stop drilling essay composition and let students focus on "higher-order" thinking about ideas. Opponents reply with a different analogy: using an AI to write your essay is like using a GPS to navigate a city you claim to know -- the output arrives, but the capacity the exercise was supposed to build quietly atrophies, and you may not notice until the device is gone. Both sides treat their analogy as nearly decisive, and I suspect neither has earned that confidence.

I'd like you to act as a philosopher of education with a side interest in cognitive offloading, and audit both analogies carefully. For each one, spell out the mapping explicitly: what in the source domain corresponds to what in the AI case, what conclusion the analogy is being used to license, and which features of the source are doing the argumentative work. Then identify the disanalogies and -- this is the part I most care about -- sort them into those that matter for the intended use and those that are merely cosmetic. Some candidates I'd want you to assess, though you should push past my list: the calculator always returns a determinate, checkable answer whereas the AI's output can be fluent and wrong; arithmetic is arguably a means to an end while writing is sometimes claimed to be constitutive of the thinking itself rather than a report of prior thinking; calculator use was typically permitted only after manual competence was established, which has no clear parallel in the proposals on our table; and GPS navigation replaces a skill that is purely instrumental, whereas the skill of articulating a position may be entangled with having one.

Please think through the strongest version of each analogy before criticizing it -- no strawmen, since I'll be presenting your analysis to partisans of both. End with a verdict: for each analogy, state what conclusion, if any, it can actually support once the relevant disanalogies are on the table, and whether some third analogy (you might consider spell-check, the assembly line, a human research assistant, or weight training at a gym, or propose your own) better captures what's at stake in students outsourcing first drafts. If you think the honest answer is that analogical reasoning is doing less work here than both camps assume and the dispute really turns on an empirical or normative claim the analogies are disguising, say so and identify that claim.
