The following paragraph comes from a lecture handout for my philosophy of mind course. The handout was written by a teaching assistant rather than the professor, and before I rely on it in an essay I would like to know how much of it I should trust.

"Eliminative materialism is the thesis that beliefs and desires do not exist. Its defenders argue that folk psychology — our ordinary practice of explaining behavior by citing what people believe and want — is an empirical theory of the mind, and a deeply defective one: it has stagnated since antiquity, it explains nothing about sleep, memory, learning, or mental illness, and it resists integration with the rest of science. Just as phlogiston and witches were eliminated when the theories positing them collapsed, eliminativists hold that neuroscience has shown there is nothing in the brain answering to our talk of beliefs and desires, so that future science will describe cognition without them. The position is generally regarded as self-refuting, however: the eliminativist presumably believes that beliefs do not exist, and wants us to believe the same, so the thesis undermines itself in the very act of being asserted. This is why nearly all philosophers of mind have moved on from it."

My own sense is that the self-refutation point is too quick, but the handout is otherwise competent, and I cannot tell where competence ends and caricature begins. Please audit the paragraph claim by claim. For each, say whether a careful defender of eliminative materialism would accept it as stated, whether it is a defensible simplification, or whether it misrepresents the position. Where it misrepresents, explain what a defender would say instead: in particular, how eliminativists actually respond to the charge that their view cannot be consistently believed or asserted, and whether their thesis is a report of results neuroscience has already delivered or a prediction about what a mature science of the brain will look like. Then assess the handout's closing verdict: once the inaccurate or overstated parts are corrected, does the claim that eliminative materialism has been refuted — and that the field has accordingly moved on — follow from what survives?

Finally, please state what would change your mind about your audit. Which fact about the eliminativist literature or the surrounding dialectic, if it turned out to hold, would make the handout's characterization fairer than you judge it to be? And at what point in your reconstruction of the eliminativist's replies are you least confident that you are being even-handed — where, in other words, is your own reasoning most likely to fail?
