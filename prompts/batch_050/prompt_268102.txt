I teach aesthetics and the philosophy of race at a mid-sized university, and I have been invited to chair a public conversation series in which contemporary scholars take questions posed to major historical figures "in character," as it were -- a colleague of mine who specializes in Du Bois will respond as Du Bois, defending his considered views on art. The figure at issue, of course, famously declared in a 1926 address on the criteria of Negro art that all art is propaganda and ever ought to be so, positioning himself against both the genteel universalism of critics like Alain Locke and the art-for-art's-sake sensibility that flourished in the Harlem Renaissance. I want the evening to do more than rehearse the standard summary (Du Bois the propagandist versus Locke the aesthete), because that framing papers over what I take to be the genuinely hard material: whether the propaganda thesis is a definition of art, an ethical demand on artists, or a sociological observation; how it is compatible with his simultaneous insistence that beauty and truth are the ends of art rather than mere instruments; and what he makes of the fact that the art he condemned -- white-authored caricature of Black life -- counts as propaganda on his own terms, which seems to make propaganda a neutral category rather than a complaint.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions I can put to my colleague-as-Du Bois. The questions must press where the scholarship presses, not ask him to restate the view. Include at least one question on the apparent tension between art as propaganda and art as oriented toward beauty for its own sake, with a follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give (namely, that in a world structured by racial hierarchy there is no disinterested beauty, only beauty whose politics is hidden); at least one on whether the demand that Black artists produce positive, uplifting images of Black life amounts to a new constraint on artistic freedom replacing the old one, with a follow-up anticipating the response that constraint is unavoidable and honesty requires choosing one's constraints openly; and at least one on what his view implies for works that are politically retrograde but formally masterful -- whether aesthetic achievement can redeem, excuse, or is simply inseparable from the propagandistic content. For every question, supply the follow-up you would ask after his most probable first answer, so the conversation cannot stall at a recitation of the address. Close with one question about what would have to be true of the world for art finally to be free of propaganda, and whether he regards that condition as desirable or even coherent.

Format the response as a numbered list, each item containing the question followed by a single labeled follow-up, with the whole thing capped at 500 words. No preamble, no closing remarks, no framing notes -- just the list itself.
