Next month I'm giving a colloquium paper in the history faculty of the university where I did my doctorate. I'm a philosopher of history by training, and this will be the first time most of this audience has heard a philosopher defend a counterfactual as something more than a dinner-party game. My thesis has two halves, and I want the room to accept the first while taking the second seriously: first, even if Hitler had been shot at the Bürgerbräukeller during the 1923 putsch attempt, Germany would very likely still have ended up under some form of radical-right, revanchist dictatorship by the mid-1930s — the Depression, the collapse of parliamentary legitimacy, the fragility of the Stresemann-era settlement, and the depth of völkisch and antisemitic currents made some outcome of that kind strongly convergent. Second, what Hitler himself specifically explains is narrower than usually assumed: the genocidal trajectory, the timing of the war, and its recklessness — not the authoritarian turn as such. I tried this argument over dinner with two historian friends last month and got a frostier reception than I expected, which tells me I am underprepared for what this particular room will do to me.

The audience will be working historians, mostly modern German social and political historians, plus a couple of intellectual historians and at least one emeritus who I suspect regards the Historikerstreit as the last worthwhile controversy in the discipline. None of them practise counterfactual history; several have dismissed it in print. Please draft my Q&A preparation sheet: the eight or so objections I should genuinely expect from this crowd — not the ones I'd get from philosophers — ordered by how much damage each does if I handle it badly, each phrased in the vocabulary a German historian would actually use, with a reply I can deliver in under two minutes and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and identify one concession I should make up front to buy credibility with this audience. For every objection, state how confident you are that it will actually come up, and close by singling out the one prediction in the sheet you are least sure about. The whole sheet must fit within 1,200 words, since I need to be able to reread it in the twenty minutes before I walk in.
