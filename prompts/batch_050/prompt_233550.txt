For my IB philosophy extended essay I want to write about Scanlon's contractualism — the view that an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by principles that nobody could reasonably reject — but before I commit to it, my supervisor wants me to spell out the standards by which I should judge the theory. So: write the desiderata that a contractualist theory of this kind must meet in order to count as an adequate account of the part of morality we owe to each other, with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

Some of the requirements I suspect belong on the list, but I want the full thing built out and stress-tested:

- It must handle the aggregation problem, i.e. the complaint that individualist, person-by-person comparison cannot justify saving the many over the few, since no single person's burden in the larger group is worse than the single burden in the smaller one. State the case behind this clearly (e.g., a rescuer who can save either one person or five from equal peril) and explain whether a successful theory must deliver the intuitive answer here or may bite the bullet.
- It must say what makes a rejection *reasonable* without either collapsing into the utilitarianism it was meant to replace or becoming so open-ended that it delivers no verdicts. What work is the reasonableness condition doing, and how would we know if it were doing none?
- It must explain the scope of the theory: who counts as a party to the agreement (future people, animals, the severely cognitively impaired), and whether the theory is even trying to cover all of morality or only a fragment.
- It must handle claims of different strengths — minor burdens versus death — and explain whether the person-affecting structure can weigh them without smuggling in aggregation through the back door (e.g., tie-breaking, complaints, or similar devices).
- It must account for wrongs that seem not to be wrongs *to* anyone in particular (or at least say honestly that it restricts itself to those that are).

For each desideratum, give the motivating case or intuition, and mark it as non-negotiable or as something a defender of the theory could plausibly concede or revise. Most importantly: flag any pair or subset of these requirements that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — if contractualism's core machinery makes two of my demands incompatible, that is itself a result I can build the essay around. Finally, sketch briefly how Scanlon's own version and one or two nearby rivals (e.g., a utilitarian view and a Scanlon-inspired view modified to permit aggregation) fare against the list — but keep this short, since the criteria are what I actually need.
