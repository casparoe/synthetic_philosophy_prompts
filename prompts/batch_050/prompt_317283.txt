Two of my friends (both LessWrong-adjacent, both have read some Turchin) had a fight at a meetup last night about cliodynamics and I got stuck refereeing, so I'm outsourcing. Roughly how it went:

(1) Maya: Turchin called rising political instability in the US around 2020 back in like 2010, a decade out, based on secular-cycle dynamics and elite overproduction. That's a successful advance prediction, which is the gold standard everyone says social science never meets. So structural-demographic theory has real predictive content and the "history can't be science" crowd is wrong.

(2) Dan: One hit proves nothing. The secular cycles model is basically fitted on a handful of cases per civilization, the key variable "elite overproduction" is measured however the analyst wants post hoc (surplus lawyers? degree holders? elite aspirants per office?), and "instability will rise sometime in the 2010s-2020s" is a wide enough target that some parameter setting was always going to hit it. With that much flexibility the prediction wasn't risky, and a non-risky prediction that comes true barely counts as evidence.

(3) Maya: It's not curve fitting, because the mechanism was specified ex ante: elite overproduction plus immiseration plus state fiscal distress produces intra-elite conflict and state breakdown. Fitting a curve doesn't come with a causal story about why; this does. And "instability" wasn't that vague -- the political stress indicator stuff gave something much closer to a timed peak than "things will get worse eventually."

(4) Dan: A mechanism doesn't rescue you if the mechanism's inputs can't be measured independently of the outcome you're predicting. And the backtests are less impressive than advertised: plenty of crises happen off-cycle, plenty of cycle-peaks fizzle, so the model's error bars are enormous in both directions. Meanwhile the only thing the theory has to beat is "history is just a bunch of one-off stories," which is a strawman null -- the actual comparison class is quantitative social science generally, which also has models, mechanisms, and occasional correct forecasts, and we rightly don't treat it as having cracked prediction.

(5) Maya: Sure, but even a noisy, error-barred cycle beats the narrative approach where every expert had a different story and nobody's forecast was checkable. Turchin at least put something on the record that could have failed. That's what progress looks like.

Adjudicate move by move -- for each of (1) through (5), say what's right and what's wrong with it. Then the harder questions I couldn't settle: does a correct ex ante prediction confer confirmation even when the model was tunable, or is Dan right that only *risky* hits count (and was this one risky)? Is Dan's "measure the inputs independently of the outcome" demand actually satisfiable in macrohistory, and if not, does that sink the whole project or just cap how confident anyone should be? And who's more right overall?
