A friend of mine recently got me reading about a view in value theory sometimes called tranquilism: the claim that nothing is intrinsically good except the absence of suffering and craving, and that experiences we ordinarily call good — pleasure, joy, delight — matter only insofar as they relieve some need, disturbance, or dissatisfaction that was already there. On this view there is no positive good to be added to a life, only bads to be subtracted. I've been testing my intuitions against a series of cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, lies behind my verdicts, because I'm no longer confident they're consistent.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. (1) Scratching an itch feels good, but I judge it good only because the itch existed — a person who never itched is not missing anything by never getting to scratch. (2) The intense pleasure of eating when ravenous is greater than the mild pleasure of eating when not hungry, yet I don't think the person who was never hungry is worse off than the one who got to satisfy ravenous hunger. (3) A meditator who has extinguished craving and rests in undisturbed equanimity seems to me to be in an excellent state — better than a thrill-seeker's cycle of desire and satisfaction — but I balk at the apparent implication that a person in a dreamless coma, who likewise suffers nothing and craves nothing, is equally well off. (4) Relief from severe chronic pain strikes me as a genuine improvement in how well things are going, not merely the removal of a bad — that is, the relief itself feels like something positively good, and I can't talk myself out of this one. (5) Offered a future of eternal ecstatic bliss on the sole condition of one barely noticeable pinprick once a century, I hesitate, which seems to support the tranquilist suspicion that no positive good can outweigh even trivial suffering; yet offered a trade of my ordinary happy life for a permanent, perfectly comfortable, entirely neutral unconsciousness, I refuse, which seems to refute that same suspicion outright.

What I want from you: state precisely the principle or small set of candidate principles that best captures verdicts (1) through (4), show how each clause is motivated by which case, and then confront (5) honestly — if no single coherent axiology delivers all six verdicts, say which verdict is cheapest to abandon and what the resulting view implies for cases I haven't considered (for instance: pleasures a person never desired and never misses; whether the best view commits us to preferring that a person never have existed if their life would contain any unsatisfied craving at all; and whether 'relief' can be assigned fleeting positive value without collapsing the whole position back into ordinary hedonism). I'm less interested in a verdict on tranquilism as a doctrine than in a disciplined piece of reflective equilibrium: the principle, its costs, and an explicit account of where my own intuitions have to give way.
