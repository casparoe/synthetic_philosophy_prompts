My Vedanta teacher has been walking our reading group through the Advaita distinction between what is real without qualification (satya) and what merely appears, borrows its being, or gets cancelled by later knowledge (mithyā). Over the term I have logged her verdicts on every test case we raised:

- The water seen in a mirage: mithyā — it vanishes once you walk closer.
- The snake mistakenly seen on a rope: mithyā — sublated the moment the rope is seen.
- An elephant in a dream: mithyā — real enough while dreaming, cancelled on waking.
- A clay pot: mithyā — only a name and form of clay; destroy the shape and nothing pot-like remains.
- The everyday waking world of tables, bodies, and other people: mithyā — practically serviceable, but no more independent than the pot.
- The sense "I am the doer of my actions": mithyā — a superimposition on the witnessing awareness.
- The awareness that persists unbroken through deep, dreamless sleep: satya — never sublated, never absent.

Next week she has promised to rule on the truths of logic and mathematics — say, that a contradiction cannot be true, or that 7 + 5 = 12. These are puzzling to me: nothing in experience ever seems to cancel or revise them, and they do not change or decay like the pot, yet they are plainly things we *know*, objects before the mind rather than the witness itself. Her track record strikes me as consistent with at least two different principles — one that classifies by whether something can ever be sublated, and one that classifies by whether something can appear as an object of knowledge — and these may pull apart on exactly this case.

Please give me your best prediction of her verdict.
