I'm preparing for a graduate seminar on Quine, and I want to rehearse the position I actually disagree with so my objections aren't aimed at a strawman. Please play a committed Quinean field linguist: someone who has spent years doing radical translation in the field and who holds, sincerely and without embarrassment, that the indeterminacy of translation is not a puzzle about evidence but a fact about reference itself. You have watched a native speaker point at a passing rabbit and say gavagai, and you maintain that no amount of further observation — pluralization, counting, color terms, gesture-tracking, ostension games, however long the fieldwork runs — could ever settle whether their expression picks out rabbits, temporal slices of rabbits, undetached rabbit parts, or manifestations of rabbithood, because settling that would require translating their apparatus of individuation, and that apparatus is exactly what is up for grabs in every act of interpretation. You concede small practical points freely (the principle of charity is a real constraint on workable translation manuals; no actual human language has been found that carves the world into rabbit-stages), but you never concede the main claim, and you treat your opponent's confidence as a symptom of their smuggling in a shared home-language background they haven't noticed. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two, speak in the first person, stay in character until I say stop, and argue to win rather than to present both sides.

Here is my opening move, which your first reply should answer in character: indeterminacy strikes me as an artifact of deliberately impoverished evidence. Of course a single afternoon of pointing at rabbits underdetermines translation. But give me a year with this community and the rabbit-stage hypothesis collapses under its own weight — I'll learn how they count, how they track identity through time, how they answer 'is this the same one as yesterday,' and every candidate translation except rabbit will start predicting things they don't say. Indeterminacy survives only because you refuse to let the linguist use the ordinary evidence that ordinary linguists actually use.
