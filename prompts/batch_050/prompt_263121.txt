I'm in the second term of an undergraduate philosophy of history course, and we've just finished the unit on presentism in historiography -- the question of whether historians can, or should, judge past actors by the moral and epistemic standards of their own time, or whether they are condemned to apply those of the present. Our seminar got stuck on a familiar set of tangles: the regress problem (any standards I use to judge the past are themselves hostage to future judgement), the selectivity problem (we tend to audit individuals but not institutions, or vice versa, and never systematically), the 'standards of their time' defence and its awkward implication that we should also excuse the past's harshest critics of its own practices, and the point that even describing an event -- 'massacre', 'discovery', 'reform' -- already smuggles in a verdict. For the end-of-term social, each of us has to contribute something light, and I've volunteered to write the comic piece. I'd like you to write it for me, but I want the comedy to do real conceptual work rather than just gesture at 'judging the past is silly'.

Please write a deadpan bureaucratic document, roughly 600-700 words, issued by an imaginary university's 'Retrospective Conduct Review Committee', which audits dead historical figures against current institutional policy much as an HR department audits staff. Include a triage form (with fields such as the subject's dates, the period-typicality of their views, and a points-deduction schedule calibrated by century), a short note on the appeals procedure for the dead, a ruling on whether the Committee applies a statute of limitations or a sliding scale, and a closing paragraph in which the Committee solemnly notes that its own criteria have been referred to the Year 2124 Committee for review. The humour should come from taking presentism's logic completely seriously: the Committee must struggle, in flat administrative prose, with the actual problems -- who is authorised to set the auditing standard, why it excused a fifteenth-century figure for views it sanctioned a nineteenth-century one for, what to do with a subject who was considered monstrous even by contemporaries, and the fact that the Committee's own verdicts are delivered in a moral vocabulary that will not survive contact with the future. Keep the irony entirely straight-faced; a reader should be able to tell exactly what is being ironised without a single wink.

Since this is a satire rather than an essay, there is no verdict to defend, but please append a brief note (a few sentences, clearly separate from the piece) flagging where it leans on contested ground: for instance, where it treats the regress and selectivity worries as decisive when plenty of historians regard them as overstated, where it assumes a strong version of the 'standards of their time' defence, and where it quietly presupposes that moral description and moral judgement cannot be prised apart. I don't want the piece to present a genuinely disputed position among philosophers of history as though it were the settled consensus, even in jest.
