I run a graduate seminar on longtermism and the ethics of the far future, and at a guest lecture last week one of my sharper students tried to shut the speaker down with a one-liner I've been hearing more and more: "we're probably living in a simulation, so none of this far-future stuff matters." The speaker, to her credit, just said "does that follow?" and moved on, but afterwards the student pressed me on it over coffee and I realized I couldn't give him a clean answer either. His thought was roughly: if we're sims, our universe might be switched off at any moment and there may be no astronomical far future for us to affect; and if we take the argument seriously we also can't trust extrapolations about posthuman civilizations, so the whole longtermist project of accepting tiny probabilities of astronomically large payoffs (what he kept calling "fanaticism," following that objection in the literature) collapses either way. I suspect he's conflating about four different claims under one slogan, and I want to give him a proper written reply rather than improvise again.

For what it's worth, the text everyone gestures at here is Nick Bostrom's 2003 paper Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?, and I dug out the passage my student is presumably leaning on: "Suppose that these simulated people are conscious (as they would be if the simulations were sufficiently fine-grained and if a certain quite widely accepted position in the philosophy of mind is correct). Then it could be the case that the vast majority of minds like ours do not belong to the original race but rather to people simulated by the advanced descendants of an original race. It is then possible to argue that, if this were the case, we would be rational to think that we are likely among the simulated minds rather than among the original biological ones." Note that this is a conditional about what we should think *if* a certain scenario obtains -- which already seems like a much weaker thing than the slogan my student was wielding.

So here's what I'd like. First, give me a menu of the distinct precise theses "we're probably living in a simulation" could be expressing -- e.g. a claim that a particular disjunction holds, a straight high-credence claim about one disjunct, a frequency claim about minds like ours plus some indifference step, a claim about our epistemic access to posthuman scenarios, and whatever readings you think I'm missing. For each reading, say whether its a platitude, a live and defensible thesis, or straightforwardly a misreading of the argument. Second, for each reading, does it actually dampen longtermist fanaticism the way my student needs? (Does simulated suffering matter less? Does the switch-off risk lower expected value of far-future interventions, and does the disjunction itself presuppose that posthuman computation is at least possible somewhere?) Third, get into the weeds of the passage: what exactly licenses the step from "the vast majority of minds like ours" to "we are likely among the simulated," and does that step say anything at all about the value or duration of simulated futures? I'd like something I can hand to him, so lay out the readings explicitly rather than picking one and defending it.
