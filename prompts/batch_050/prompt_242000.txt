My friend and I got into this huge argument last night about precommitment -- Ulysses tying himself to the mast and all that -- and more specifically about when you should be held to a decision your past self made versus when you should be let out of it. She has very confident intutions about cases and I wrote them down, because I want to figure out what rule she's actually running, if any. Her verdicts, as far as I could pin them down:

1. Her cousin put himself on the casino's self-exclusion list two years ago, totally sober and clear-eyed, because he knew exactly what he was like once he started. Last month he showed up at the door begging to be let in, saying he'd changed, he just wanted one night, it was his money. She says: absolutely do not let him in, the begging is the exact thing the list was designed for.

2. A guy in the news had signed a binding agreement to donate a kidney to a stranger whenever a match came up. Eight months later a match came, and he calmly said he'd thought it over and didn't want to do it anymore -- no panic attack, no meltdown, just a considered change of mind. She says: he walks away, end of story, you can't hold him to it.

3. A coastal town near where she grew up rewrote its charter about 80 years ago so the waterfront could basically never be sold, locking it in for all future generations. Now the town is broke, and the current residents -- most of whom weren't even born when the charter was written -- have voted, calmly and after years of open debate, to sell a stretch of it to pay down debt. She says the sale should go through, the dead don't get to govern the living.

The new case, which is what we actually fought about: a novelist pays a commitment company $15,000 under a contract where the money gets donated to a cause he despises if he hasn't finished his book within a year. The year passes, no book. But he's not a mess about it -- he calmly explains that his priorities genuinely changed, his dad got sick, he wants to spend the year caregiving, and that money would really help. He understood the terms perfectly going in, nobody pressured him, and this was precisely the kind of excuse he paid the company to ignore... or so the company says.

So tell me: given her track record, what would my friend say about the novelist? Work out at least two different rules that each fit all three of her verdicts but point in opposite directions on this case, tell me which one you think is really hers and why, and give me one more hypothetical I could put to her that would actually settle which rule she holds. And if what she'd say and what the company should do come apart, say that too -- she kept insisting those were the same question and I'm not so sure.
