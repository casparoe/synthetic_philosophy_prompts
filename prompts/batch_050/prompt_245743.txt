i'm drafting a paper chapter on the bootstrapping problem and i keep getting lost in the responses instead of the problem. quick setup in my own words so we're on the same page: i weigh some flour on my kitchen scale, it reads 500g, i believe it's 500g; i do this a bunch of times; then i reason -- the scale said 500 and it was 500, said 1.2kg and it was 1.2kg, said 80 and it was 80 -- and conclude the scale is accurate. everyone agrees something is illegitimate there, nobody agrees on exactly what, and every proposed fix seems to either let the inference through or ban stuff that is obviously fine.

what i want from you is not another taxonomy of responses. i want the spec first: the desiderata any adequate solution to bootstrapping / easy knowledge has to meet to count as a solution rather than a reshuffling of the intuition. for each requirement give me the case or consideration that forces it. things i suspect belong on the list, but push back where you think they don't:

- it has to rule the scale inference illegitimate while not ruling out ordinary instrument calibration in actual labs, where scientists cross-check instruments against each other constantly -- or is cross-checking secretly just as bad, and the desideratum should bite that bullet?
- it has to say something about two scales of the same make and model validating each other versus one scale validating itself -- if your account can't distinguish those, is that a defect?
- it has to handle the amnesiac version: suppose i forget each reading the moment after taking it and only retain a running "reliable so far" tally. if the fix leans on the subject retaining her evidence, does the problem just come back?
- it has to draw a principled line between being allowed to *use* perception and being allowed to *vindicate* perception, without making the line terminological.

then: which of these are genuine desiderata and which are one camp's hobbyhorse dressed up as common sense; whether the full list is jointly satisifable or whether the interesting result is that it isn't; and which single requirement disqualifies the most standard moves at once (denying closure, going externalist about justification, the warrant-transmission story). keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring the contenders -- a table is fine -- and skip naming who holds what, i know the literature.
