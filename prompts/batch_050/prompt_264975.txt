I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a stratigrapher, and I have run into a conceptual problem that I need settled before I can write her honestly. In my research I keep meeting the same comparison, deployed by journalists, essayists, and at least one character I've overheard at a conference: humanity's impact on the Earth is like the asteroid that ended the Cretaceous. I have noticed the analogy being made to do two quite different jobs. In the first, it supports a procedural conclusion: because the asteroid left a sharp, globally synchronous marker in the rock, the Anthropocene likewise deserves formal recognition as a geological interval with a crisp boundary, and the geologists' official procedures are the proper court for deciding when it began. In the second, more troubling use, it supports a moral conclusion: what we are doing is a natural event, something that happens to planets, and no more blameworthy than a collision, so treating the Anthropocene as a matter of guilt or responsibility is a category error. My character leans on the first use while being quietly soothed by the second, and I need to know whether the analogy will bear either weight before I put it in her mouth.

Please audit the analogy for me. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine — identifying what plays the role of the impact, the extinction pulse, the worldwide marker layer, and the instantaneity of the event, and noting where the mapping is strained: the difference between an afternoon and a few centuries (and whether that difference survives compaction into sediment), the fact that one of the proposed agents can foresee and deliberate while the other cannot, the question of whether the impact is globally synchronous in the way the analogy assumes given how many candidate starting points are on the table, and the awkwardness that humanity is not a single projectile striking from outside but something distributed very unevenly across populations, such that one might ask who exactly is the asteroid and who is the dinosaur. Tell me which of these disanalogies actually matter for each of the two conclusions above and which are merely decorative; tell me what each use quietly imports — I suspect the first smuggles in an event-shaped picture of history that flatters a particular dating, and the second smuggles in an exoneration — and whether either conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying them the whole way. If there is a better comparison from the geological record for what a technological species does to its planet — I have wondered whether some slow, life-driven transformation of the atmosphere might fit the facts better than a bolide — say what it is, what it would preserve and what it would cost my character's preferred conclusions.

Write your answer in British English, and feel free to be direct: I would rather give my character a defensible analogy than a flattering one.
