In The Subjection of Women (1869), John Stuart Mill writes:

“In all other cases, the burthen of proof is supposed to lie with the affirmative. If a person is charged with a murder, it rests with those who accuse him to give proof of his guilt, not with himself to prove his innocence. If there is a difference of opinion about the reality of any alleged historical event, in which the feelings of men in general are not much interested, as the Siege of Troy for example, those who maintain that the event took place are expected to produce their proofs, before those who take the other side can be required to say anything; and at no time are these required to do more than show that the evidence produced by the others is of no value. Again, in practical matters, the burthen of proof is supposed to be with those who are against liberty; who contend for any restriction or prohibition; either any limitation of the general freedom of human action, or any disqualification or disparity of privilege affecting one person or kind of persons, as compared with others. The à priori presumption is in favour of freedom and impartiality. It is held that there should be no restraint not required by the general good, and that the law should be no respecter of persons, but should treat all alike, save where dissimilarity of treatment is required by positive reasons, either of justice or of policy.”

Please explain what work this passage does in Mill’s treatment of appeals to women’s “nature”. In particular, use the following distinctions:

1. A characteristic is source-natural if it would have emerged even in the absence of the social institutions whose justice is under dispute.
2. It is consequence-natural if it predictably results from those institutions, even though it would not have emerged without them.
3. It is fittingness-natural if its existence would make unequal treatment appropriate rather than merely intelligible.

Explain why evidence that a characteristic is natural in the second sense does not establish that it is natural in the first or third senses. Then clarify whether Mill’s “à priori presumption” is:

- an empirical claim that men and women have identical capacities;
- a moral claim that liberty is intrinsically valuable;
- a procedural rule about who must justify coercion;
- or some combination of these.

Address the objection that Mill is cheating by placing the burden of proof on defenders of subordination before he has independently established equality. Does this objection confuse the truth of an empirical claim with the justification for acting upon it under uncertainty?

Finally, apply your explanation to this case: after generations in which women have been excluded from advanced engineering education, a state observes lower average performance among women on an engineering examination and therefore prohibits all women from entering the profession. The state argues that, because no one can conclusively separate biological aptitude from unequal training, uncertainty should favour restriction. What would the passage imply about the state’s evidence, its use of an average to disqualify every individual, and the kind of “positive reasons” that could justify the policy?
