At a recent panel on franchise reform, I watched the phrase 'one person, one vote' do duty for three incompatible arguments within the space of an hour: first against compulsory voting (the state may not conscript an equal say), then for lowering the voting age to sixteen (sixteen-year-olds are persons, after all), and finally against municipal voting rights for long-term non-citizen residents (they are not part of 'the' people). The slogan arose, as I understand it, in litigation over legislative districting — a dispute about the equal weight of cast ballots and the equal population of districts, not about who is entitled to cast a ballot at all — yet every panelist wielded it as though it settled franchise questions directly.

Please do the following. First, enumerate the distinct precise theses that 'one person, one vote' could be expressing: at minimum, distinguish a claim about the equal weighting of cast ballots, a claim about an equal franchise, a claim about equal representation per capita, a claim about equal opportunity for political influence, and any further reading you take to be genuinely different rather than a restatement. Second, classify each thesis: which are platitudes no one disputes, which are substantive principles that need and admit of defense, and which dissolve on inspection (for example, because they presuppose a contested criterion of membership in 'the people'). Third, identify which thesis is actually doing the work in each of the three panel arguments, and say whether that thesis — once made precise — really delivers the conclusion the panelist wanted. Fourth, test each of the three reforms (compulsory voting, a voting age of sixteen, local voting rights for non-citizen residents) against each plausible reading, and show where a reading supports a reform, where it undermines one, and where it is simply silent. Finally, where the slogan turns out to be silent on a question the panelists treated it as decisive, say what kind of consideration — empirical, normative, or about the boundary conditions of democratic inclusion — would genuinely have to carry the argument instead.
