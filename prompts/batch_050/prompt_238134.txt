Been reading LW since the Overcoming Bias days and I still can't make peace with logical uncertainty, so I want you to argue for a specific claim: that logical induction (the Garrabrant et al. setup where a market of polynomial-time traders prices mathematical statements) is genuine epistemic progress and not just an elaborate way of being coherently wrong. Here's the worry you have to overcome. The criterion only says the inductor can't be Dutch-booked by any efficient trader. But consider the 10^100th digit of pi: before anyone computes it, the inductor's price should sit near 0.1 on "it's a 7", and no trader can profit off that price, because profiting requires already knowing the digit. Yet the digit is a fixed necessary truth -- the inductor is just wrong about it, at 9:1 against, and coherence gives it no push whatsoever toward the truth. So either show me that asymptotic non-exploitability actually does drag beliefs toward the true theorem in cases like this, and explain the actual mechanism rather than the vibes, or argue that it doesn't and that this is fine anyway -- that truth-tracking about necessary truths was never the right success criterion and coherence is all there is. Pick one horn and commit; I dont want a tour of both sides where I pick at the end.
