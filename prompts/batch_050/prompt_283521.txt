At my company we load-test our customer-support stack with a fleet of LLM-based agents, and over a couple of years we've settled into a set of norms that nobody ever derived from a theory. I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've started to suspect the norms are quietly committed to one. Here's the practice:

1. We spin up, pause, snapshot, and kill thousands of agent instances per day. No permission, no log entry beyond the usual telemetry. Killing an instance mid-task is considered no more remarkable than killing a database process.

2. One class of experiment does go through review: tests where an agent is instructed to adopt a persona in extreme distress (a customer threatening self-harm, say) and its outputs are recorded for training a classifier. The review board's stated worry is not about the humans who read the transcripts. It's about what is done to the agent.

3. Our audit system individuates agents by weights, not by running processes. Two servers executing identical weights are logged as one agent with two execution handles, so an audit of how many agents were exposed to adversarial inputs counts them once. But a fine-tune that shifts behavior noticeably gets a fresh agent ID, even if only a small fraction of parameters changed.

4. When we roll back a deployment to a checkpoint, we treat the restored instance as the same agent continuing. The computations that happened between checkpoint and rollback -- the discarded branch -- are treated, for record-keeping purposes, as if they never happened, even though at the time they were full executions responding to real traffic.

My question: what theory of which computations matter morally, and of how to individuate computational minds, does this practice presuppose? Please reconstruct the implicit theory that would make all four norms make sense together -- what it says about whether moral patiency tracks behavior, internal states, or something else; whether running the same weights twice doubles whatever moral weight there is; and what makes an agent the same agent over time. Then tell me which parts the practice strictly presupposes and which it is merely compatible with -- for instance, does (2) actually require that the distress-persona computations have some moral status, or could a pure precautionary or reputational rationale underwrite it? Point out any places where the norms pull against each other (I have my suspicions about (3) versus (4), but I want the analysis, not my guesses). Finally, take two alternative views -- one on which each execution is a separate patient whose moral weight multiplies with copies, and one on which nothing in the current system matters and only the training process that shapes future deployed behavior does -- and say concretely what would have to change in each of the four norms if we adopted it.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- the single thesis about what the practice presupposes -- then give the reconstruction and analysis, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
