I'm a third-year undergraduate taking an upper-level philosophy of mathematics seminar, and each student has to run one 75-minute session on a topic of their choice for a group of about 10 classmates. I chose Skolem's paradox: the tension between the downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem (a first-order theory with an infinite model, like ZFC if consistent, has a countable model) and the fact that the theory itself proves there are uncountable sets. Everyone in the group has taken a first course in formal logic and can state the theorem, but most of them treat the paradox as a curiosity that evaporates once you mumble "relativity of cardinality." I want them to actually feel why it doesn't evaporate so quickly.

Design a structured activity for the session -- a game, simulation, or role-play with explicit rules, roles, materials, and a timeline -- in which participants run into the phenomenon themselves before I name it. My rough idea, which you should feel free to replace with something better, is to split the group into an "inside" team that may only use a fixed, countable supply of tokens and function-cards I hand them, and an "outside" team that can see everything; the inside team should be able to correctly conclude, by their lights, that a certain set is uncountable (no enumeration card exists), while the outside team can plainly enumerate it. The specifics are up to you, but the activity needs to produce a genuine moment where the two perspectives collide and participants have to articulate what "uncountable" could even mean given the collision -- not just a demonstration where the trick is obvious from the start.

Please include: (1) full rules and roles, precise enough that I could run the session without improvising; (2) a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes, including when the paradox is first named; (3) the debrief, mapping concrete moments in the activity onto the components of the paradox (the theorem, the internal proof of uncountability, the sense in which the model "misses" functions); (4) the two or three ways this will predictably go wrong with smart but impatient undergraduates -- e.g., the outside team spoiling the reveal too early, or someone objecting that the activity confuses counting with cardinality -- and how to handle each; and (5) a frank statement of which feature of the actual mathematics the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply by lecture instead (I suspect this includes the first-order/second-order distinction and what changes under full semantics, but tell me what you think).

Finally: for each substantive claim you make about what the paradox does or does not show, indicate how confident you are, and at the end single out the single point in your whole answer -- whether about the mathematics, the pedagogy, or the debrief framing -- that you are least sure of and why.
