ok so I'm in this seminar on the ethics of war and we just spent like three weeks on the moral equality of combatants (the whole Walzer vs. McMahan debate). For my final paper I'm trying to map out the main strategies for defending the equality thesis, i.e. the idea that soldiers fighting for an unjust cause are just as entitled to fight and kill as soldiers on the just side. Here's what I have so far:

1. The self-defense argument: once the war is actually happening, soldiers on both sides pose lethal threats to each other, so each is permitted to kill the other in self-defense, whatever the justice of their cause.
2. The excuse argument: most unjust combatants fight under duress, conscription, or heavy propaganda, and can't reasonably be expected to figure out that their cause is unjust, so they're excused rather than blameworthy, and excused attackers don't lose their rights.
3. The division-of-labor argument: the decision to go to war belongs to political leaders, and soldiers just carry it out, so the responsibility for an unjust war lands on the state and its leaders, not on the individual soldier.
4. The pragmatic/conventionalist argument: treating combatants as equals is what makes the laws of war workable at all. If the rules of war don't judge who's in the right, everyone has a reason to actually follow them (no torture, no targeting civilians, humane treatment of prisoners, etc.).

What other strategy for defending the equality thesis is there that isn't on my list? It has to be genuinely distinct — not just a rewording or special case of one of the four, and not two of them stapled together. Also tell me which of the four it's closest to and wether you think it actually holds up, since I'll have to defend the taxonomy in the paper lol
