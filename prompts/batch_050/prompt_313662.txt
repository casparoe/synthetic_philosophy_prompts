My grandmother has been in hospice care for four months with a terminal illness, and our family has been arguing about whether she should be allowed to end her life with medical help, which is legal where we live. At a family dinner last week, my uncle ended the argument with a comparison he clearly thought settled it. Last year we had our old dog put to sleep when her cancer made every day miserable, and nobody considered that cruel -- the vet called it the last kind thing you can do for an animal you love. So, my uncle said, either we were wrong to do that to the dog, or we're being cruel to my grandmother now by insisting she endure what we would never make a dog endure. He added that if anything it should be easier to justify for a person, because unlike the dog, my grandmother can actually say that's what she wants.

My mother pushed back with what I can only describe as sputtering -- she said a person is not a dog, and that was about the whole of her argument. I find myself stuck in the middle. My uncle's analogy feels powerful and I can't locate the flaw in it, but I also can't shake the feeling that something important is being flattened.

So here is what I'd like. First, lay out the analogy explicitly: what plays the role of what -- the dog, the cancer, the vet, the family's decision -- and what conclusion each correspondence is supposed to be pulling toward. Second, tell me where the mapping breaks, and be honest about which breaks actually matter for my uncle's conclusion and which are just differences that don't touch it. I suspect the fact that the dog never consented and my grandmother can is the crux, but I can see that cutting both ways and I'd like that untangled: does the dog's inability to consent make the analogy stronger for my grandmother, or does it quietly mean the two cases were never the same kind of act to begin with? Third, tell me what the analogy smuggles in -- what it assumes about what makes the dog case compassionate (the suffering alone? the incurability? the fact that we decide?) and whether that assumption survives being stated out loud. Finally: if the analogy can't carry the weight my uncle puts on it, is there a better comparison for thinking about my grandmother's situation, and if it can, should my mother concede?

I want the audit, not a verdict on what our family should do -- but if you think the whole framing of the dinner-table argument is off, say that too.
