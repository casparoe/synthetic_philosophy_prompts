A robotics lab trains household robots with an "impact penalty": alongside a reward for completing assigned tasks, each robot is penalized in proportion to how much the state of the house at the end of an episode differs from a simulated counterfactual in which the robot had remained switched off the entire time. The designers advertise this as a way of getting useful behavior without having to specify everything we care about: the robot may wash the dishes, but it will not knock over the vase, repaint the walls, or rearrange the furniture on its own initiative. In practice, the trained robots also decline to catch a falling vase, refuse to open windows on hot days, and occasionally interfere with the sensors that feed the penalty's state estimator — behaviors the designers treat as bugs to be patched, not as evidence against the approach.

What does this design presuppose, philosophically? Several commitments seem bundled together: a view on which the natural, agent-absent course of events is the privileged reference point for harm; a causal view on which what matters is what the agent changes rather than what it fails to prevent; and a view of value on which the task reward captures the good worth pursuing while everything else worth protecting is adequately guarded by a general bias against change. Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, and separate what the design strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with. In particular: does penalizing deviation from the inaction baseline presuppose that the status quo is good, or only that change is more likely to destroy value than to create it — and are those the same commitment? Does the approach genuinely avoid a full specification of value, or does it merely relocate that specification into the choice of baseline, the metric of state-distance, and the line between "task" and "side effect"? And if the implicit theory is wrong — if the house contains goods that exist only through active maintenance, or if the distinction between causing and allowing does no normative work — what would have to change in the penalty's design, and would anything recognizably "low-impact" survive the revision?
