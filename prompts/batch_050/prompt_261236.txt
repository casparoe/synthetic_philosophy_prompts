I'm drafting an essay for a philosophy blog (smart general readers, not specialists) and I want sharp comments before I submit. It's about advance directives and whether your earlier self gets to boss around your later self. Numbered paragraphs so you can refer to them.

The essay:

[1] A case that's become standard in the literature: a woman in her fifties, sharp and unsentimental, watches her mother die slowly of dementia and writes an advance directive — if she's ever severely demented herself, no life-prolonging treatment, not even antibiotics for pneumonia. Twenty years on she is severely demented, and by all accounts happy: she potters around the ward, enjoys meals, loves music. She gets pneumonia. The directive says let her die. Should we follow it?

[2] Ronald Dworkin's answer, roughly, is yes. His idea is "precedent autonomy": autonomy isn't just about what you want right now, it's about your life going according to the story you've authored. The directive expresses her critical interests — convictions about what makes a life good or bad as a whole — and those interests outlast the capacity to reflect on them. Ignoring the directive doesn't spare her; it betrays her.

[3] Rebecca Dresser pushes back. Once dementia is advanced, she argues, critical interests are simply gone, and what remain are experiential interests: comfort, pleasure, freedom from pain. The directive then becomes an instruction to harm a real, living, presently content person for the sake of a biography she can no longer even understand. On Dresser's view, the patient's current wishes settle the matter, and the earlier self is, morally speaking, a stranger with a pen.

[4] Behind this sits a deeper worry about identity. Think of Parfit's Russian nobleman: a young socialist aristocrat knows that as he ages he'll grow conservative, so he extracts a promise from his wife not to let his future self keep the estates he now wants to give to the peasants. Parfit's point is that the later self's change of heart isn't obviously a lapse — it might be growth — and the young man's grip on the old man is morally shaky. Dementia is that case with the volume turned up. If psychological connectedness between me-now and me-in-twenty-years thins out enough, then strictly speaking the demented woman is someone else, and her directive has no more authority over her than my will has over my neighbour.

[5] So here's my proposal, which I haven't seen defended anywhere: advance directives should come with sunset clauses. Just as some legislation lapses unless renewed, a directive should expire — say five years after signing — unless the author reaffirms it while still competent. Reaffirmation isn't red tape; it's what keeps the directive anchored to a living endorsement rather than a dead hand. It also handles a problem the standard debate ignores: we are demonstrably bad at imagining our future selves. Healthy people wildly overestimate how miserable disabled life is (researchers call this the disability paradox), and the contented dementia patient on the ward is the extreme version of a mistake the directive-writer was almost guaranteed to make.

[6] The obvious objection: sunset clauses can't do anything once capacity is gone, so at exactly the moment it matters, the directive's authority still rests on an endorsement that is decades stale — and lapse-by-forgetfulness is a grotesque trigger for life-and-death defaults. My reply: split directive content in two. Some directives express identity-constituting commitments (refusing states of existence one considers degrading); others express merely contingent treatment preferences. Sunset clauses apply only to the second kind. The first kind stays in force until revoked.

[7] So directive authority is borrowed authority: like a currency, it needs periodic refinancing by the person whose life is at stake, or it quietly devalues into tyranny by the dead.
