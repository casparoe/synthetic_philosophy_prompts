I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on the foundations of decision theory, and for my term paper I need to take a position on the so-called Pasadena game, a variant of the St. Petersburg paradox designed to produce an undefined rather than merely infinite expected value. I'd like help structuring my assessment.

The game works as follows. A fair coin is tossed repeatedly until it lands heads for the first time. If the first heads occurs on toss n, the player receives a payoff of (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n utils — so the possible payoffs, in order of the toss on which heads first appears, are +2, −2, +8/3, −4, +32/5, −16/3, and so on, each occurring with probability 1/2^n. The expected value is therefore the series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + 1/5 − ..., which converges to ln 2 only in this particular ordering of the terms. Since the series does not converge absolutely, the Riemann rearrangement theorem tells us the terms can be reordered so as to sum to any real number whatsoever, or to diverge. And there is no privileged ordering of outcomes in a state-by-state calculation of expected value. The expected value of the game is thus undefined, even though each individual outcome is unproblematic. This is awkward for expected utility theory: it seems to be a well-defined gamble that the theory simply cannot evaluate, and pairwise comparisons involving it (e.g., the Pasadena game versus a sure gain of ln 2 utils) appear to have no principled answer.

In my reading and in seminar discussion I've encountered the following five lines of response to the puzzle:

(A) Bounded utility: The game presupposes an unbounded utility function, but on any adequate theory of rational preference, utility must be bounded. So the game is impossible in the relevant sense, and expected utility theory is under no obligation to pronounce on it.

(B) Require absolute convergence: Adopt, as a standing convention, that expected value is only defined when the payoff series converges absolutely. Then the game has no expectation, full stop — but this is a verdict the theory delivers cleanly, not an embarrassment, and rational agents may simply be indeterminate about such gambles, just as they may be indeterminate about incomparable options elsewhere.

(C) Truncation to finite play: Evaluate the game as the limit of truncated versions in which play stops after N tosses. Every truncated game has a well-defined expectation, and the limit as N grows is ln 2. So the game does have a defensible value, arrived at by the standard mathematical practice of extending finite cases by limits.

(D) Dominance and comparative reasoning: Even where expectation is undefined, expected utility theory can be supplemented with dominance principles and pairwise comparisons. For instance, the Pasadena game strictly dominates a version of itself with each payoff reduced by one util, and such constraints, collectively, may fix rational attitudes toward the game without needing a point-valued expectation.

(E) Bite the bullet about pathology: Concede that the theory falls silent here, but argue this is no real cost. The game cannot actually be offered by any physically possible agent (it requires arbitrarily large payoff magnitudes and arbitrarily long play), and a normative theory of decision need only guide choices that could genuinely arise.

Please rank these five responses from most to least satisfactory as defenses of expected utility theory in the face of the Pasadena game, assigning each a score out of 10. For each response, explain in a paragraph why it lands where it does in your ranking, attending in particular to: whether the response addresses the rearrangement problem itself or merely changes the subject; what it costs elsewhere in decision theory (e.g., for unbounded utility, for handling infinite state spaces, or for comparisons between two games both of undefined expectation); and whether it generalizes to related problematic gambles, such as one whose payoffs are 2^n / n on every toss (all positive, giving a divergent-to-infinity series). Finally, state which response you would defend in a term paper and sketch the strongest objection it would face from a critic.
