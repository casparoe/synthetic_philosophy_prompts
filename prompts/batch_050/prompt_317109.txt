My roommate and I have this recurring argument about conspiracy theories that we can't seem to resolve. His position is basically that the whole category is epistemically rotten and rational people should adopt a standing policy of dismissing anything that smells like a conspiracy claim. I keep bringing up that COINTELPRO, the Tuskegee experiment, and the tobacco industry hiding what it knew about cancer were all "conspiracy theories" before they were confirmed facts, and he says those are exceptions that prove the rule and that I'm being naive about base rates. What we figured out last week is that we're stuck becuase neither of us can actually say what a good account of when conspiracy belief is rational would even have to accomplish. We keep trading cases without a standard for judging them.

So write me the spec first: the desiderata any adequate epistemology of conspiracy belief has to meet, with the case or consideration behind each one. Off the top of my head it seems like the theory has to explain what's different between believing the tobacco thing and believing something like Pizzagate, without the difference just being "one turned out true." It can't simply outsource to trusting official sources, since official denials have sometimes been documented lies, but it also can't treat official denial as evidence *for* the conspiracy, or nothing could ever count against anything. It has to handle the fact that almost all our evidence is institutionally mediated, and somehow deal with the self-sealing problem, where believers predict in advance that evidence against the theory will have been manufactured — although real conspiracies do actually destroy evidence, so I'm not sure unfalsifiability is always a vice. It should distinguish defects in the theory from defects in the believer, since someone can hold a true belief for garbage reasons. And it should handle cases like my coworker who grew up under a government that really did run informant networks in activist groups, for whom "the authorities wouldn't do that" is not a plausible default.

For each desideratum, say what's motivating it and mark whether you think it's a genuine requirement or a prejudice of one camp — I have in mind the fight between people who think conspiracy claims are prima facie unwarranted as a class and people who insist each one has to be evaluated on its own merits, but feel free to carve it differently. The part I care most about: tell me which pairs of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, becuase I have a feeling that "give institutional sources default credibility" and "take documented institutional deception seriously" pull against each other in a way that can't be fixed, and if the spec itself is inconsistent that's a result I want to know about. Then say which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the positions people actually defend. A brief scoring of the main camps against the list at the end is fine, but keep it short — the criteria are the deliverable, not the scorecard.
