I'm trying to get clear on my own attitudes toward risky gambles, and my gut reactions are confusing me. Going by cases:

- The classic St. Petersburg game (fair coin, pays 2^n dollars if the first heads comes up on flip n): I wouldn't pay more than about $25 to play, even though the expected value is infinite. Comfortable with that.
- Same game but capped at 20 flips, so max payout is around a million and the EV is $10: I'd pay maybe $8. Slightly under the "fair" price, seems fine.
- Same game capped at 40 flips, max payout in the trillions, EV of $20: now I'd pay *less* than for the 20-flip version, maybe $4. Even though the EV is higher.
- A one-off gamble: 50% lose $1000, 50% win $3000. I'd take it. Positive EV and I can absorb the loss.
- A one-off gamble: 99% win $10,000, 1% lose my house and savings. I wouldn't take it at basically any upside.
- I pay for home insurance without resentment, i.e. I happily accept a negative-EV bet when the tail risk is ruin.
- I also occasionally buy a lottery ticket, which is a negative-EV bet in the other direction, and somehow that doesn't feel inconsistent with the insurance thing, though I can't say why.
- My friend offers: pay $5000 for a 5% shot at $200,000, so EV $10,000. This is the one I keep going back and forth on -- by expected value it's the best deal on the whole list.

Is there a principle that gets most of these? Diminishing marginal utility of money explains the insurance and maybe the house bet, but I don't see how it explains preferring the 20-flip game to the 40-flip one, since the extra flips only add upside on top. And it makes the lottery ticket look flat-out irrational, which I'm not ready to concede. Maybe I need one principle for gains and a different one for losses, or maybe some verdict of mine just has to go. Give me the principle (or a couple of candidate principles) that best captures the pattern, say which case motivates each clause, tell me what it implies about my friend's offer, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't save.
