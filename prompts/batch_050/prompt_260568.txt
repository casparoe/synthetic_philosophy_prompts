I'm a PhD student in political theory with a background in formal social choice, and I've been asked to give a single ninety-minute guest lecture on the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem to second-year undergraduates in a comparative politics program. They have taken an introductory course on democratic institutions but have no game theory beyond the prisoner's dilemma and no exposure to Arrow's theorem. I'd like you to plan the lecture rather than give it.

Specifically, I want the plan to lay out the explanatory arc in terms of dependency: which notions must be in place before the theorem's statement will even parse for this audience, and in what order they should be introduced. My instinct is that I need them to understand, in sequence, what a voting rule is as a function from profiles to outcomes, what it means for a ballot to misrepresent a preference, and why strategy-proofness is a demand on the rule rather than an accusation against voters -- but I'm unsure whether the dictatorship condition should be introduced before the theorem (as a seemingly absurd extreme case) or after (as the surprising punchline), and I'd like a reasoned recommendation, not just a sequence.

Second, I need a single carrying example. The standard choice in the textbooks is a three-alternative election, but I'm considering instead building the whole lecture around a hiring committee of three members choosing among three candidates, because the audience cares about institutions rather than mass elections. Say what the carrying example must have and must lack to do its job -- I'm particularly worried about whether letting the committee chair have agenda-setting power early on will smuggle in or obscure the dictatorial structure the theorem points to -- and give a verdict on the hiring committee versus the election, or a third option if both are flawed.

Third, map the misconceptions I should expect, in the order the students will form them, and mark exactly where in the arc each should be headed off. My own predictions: that the theorem will be heard as saying all voting is pointless; that it will be conflated with Arrow's impossibility result as just the same fact relabeled; that students will treat manipulation as a claim about what voters actually do rather than a property of the rule; and that someone will propose randomization or restricted preference domains as escapes and need to be shown why those change the question rather than answer it. Tell me whether this prediction list is right, what I'm missing, and which misconception, if unaddressed, most corrupts the rest of the lecture.

Finally: give me one end-of-lecture question I can pose whose answers would let me distinguish students who have grasped the theorem as a possibility result about rules from students who merely remember a pessimistic slogan, and tell me what, at this level, I should openly decline to prove or explain -- the proof itself, presumably, but I want a principled account of where that line should sit and how to justify it to the room without making the result sound like revealed doctrine.
