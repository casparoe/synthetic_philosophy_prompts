I am a graduate student in philosophy of mind drafting a paper on the paradox of phenomenal judgment: roughly, the worry that if phenomenal states are causally inert, as epiphenomenalism holds, then our introspective beliefs and reports about them cannot be explained by, or even reliably about, the experiences themselves. My advisor's complaint about the first draft was that it simply reruns the standard devices — zombie twins, the self-stultification charge, fading qualia — without contributing a case of its own. So I would like you to construct a new thought experiment, engineered to the following specifications. First, the case must target the content of phenomenal judgments, not merely their causal etiology: it should force the question of what makes a subject's judgment be about the quale itself rather than about the quale's neural correlate. Second, it must concede, for the sake of argument, the existence of physically and functionally identical subjects who differ only in phenomenal character; the case loses its point if the respondent can simply deny that such duplicates are possible. Third, although the underlying contrast is modal, the scenario should be narrated in near-realistic terms — a neurosurgical clinic, a brain-computer reporting channel, a procedure that allegedly toggles phenomenal character while leaving function untouched — rather than by asking the reader to imagine a remote possible world. Fourth, the scenario must be built so that the epiphenomenalist faces a forced choice between exactly two exits, each costly: either the judgment's content is fixed purely functionally, in which case the inert quale drops out of what the judgment is about, or content is fixed by some acquaintance-like relation to the quale, in which case the quale-lacking duplicate's sincere introspective reports come out false or empty despite being physically identical to ours.

Along with the scenario itself, which should be statable in a single paragraph, please demonstrate that it meets each of the four specifications: walk through the verdicts, showing precisely where the forced choice arises and why neither exit is available cheaply. Then identify the single feature of the case that does the real work, and explain what happens to the argument if that feature is removed — in particular, whether the case collapses back into a generic zombie intuition pump. Next, give the epiphenomenalist's best reply to the case as you have built it, and say honestly whether that reply converts the paradox into a mere theoretical cost (one the view might reasonably pay) or leaves it looking like a genuine incoherence. Finally, state briefly what an interactionist dualist and a physicalist who accepts the explanatory gap but denies epiphenomenalism would each make of the scenario, so that I can see which positions the case actually discriminates among. Please write your answer in American English.
