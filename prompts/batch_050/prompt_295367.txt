So I'm in an undergrad metaphysics course and we just did the statue and the lump of clay -- statue and clay coincide in space, the clay can survive being squashed but the statue can't, so they can't be one and the same thing, etc. It all felt pretty airless until it occured to me that museums basically deal with this problem for a living, and now I can't figure out what their actual practice is committed to. Help me reconstruct it.

Here's what I take the practice to look like (correct me if I'm off on the facts, but this is my understanding). First: conservators swap out material all the time. They strip yellowed varnish off paintings, reline old canvases onto new backing, fill in paint losses, cut out rotted sections of wooden sculpture and graft in new wood. And afterwards everyone -- the label, the catalog, the insurance valuation -- treats it as the *same* Vermeer, not a new object that's part Vermeer. Second: at the same time, the whole institution treats the *original stuff* as weirdly sacred. Conservation ethics demands reversibility and minimal intervention, they keep the fragments they remove in little bags, and the market pays millions for the original while an atom-for-atom perfect copy is worth basically nothing. Third: with newer works -- installations made of off-the-shelf lightbulbs that burn out, pieces made of perishable stuff -- conservators have to actually decide, case by case, whether replacing the material preserves the work or kills it, and they don't all agree.

So my questions. What view of material constitution and identity over time does this practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that the artwork is not identical to the matter it's made of -- and could someone who thinks the statue just *is* the clay still make sense of conservation, or are they stuck saying the conservator destroys one object and creates another every time they replace a plank? And is there a real tension between the first and second things: how can you both freely replace the stuff *and* insist the original stuff is the only thing that matters? Which theory of persistence (if any) makes those two cohere?

Also, where is the practice actually underdetermined? Like, which parts strictly require some particular metaphysics and which parts would be licensed by several rival theories, so that the conservators are maybe just being pragmatic and don't need a theory at all. And one last thing: imagine a painting restored bit by bit over 400 years until zero original material remains, museum still sells tickets to see the original. Does the practice as it exists have a principled answer to whether it's still the same painting, or is that a point where the implicit theory just runs out? If the practice has no answer, is that a problem for the practice or for metaphysics?
