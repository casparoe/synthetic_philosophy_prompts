I would like a case analysis that runs in both directions: read the collapse of the northern cod fishery off Newfoundland through the tragedy of the commons, and then use the episode to test the theory.

Here are the facts as I understand them, in case they matter for the analysis. Cod had been fished off the Grand Banks for centuries, largely by small inshore boats. After the Second World War, industrial factory trawlers — many of them foreign — took enormous catches offshore. When Canada extended its fisheries jurisdiction to two hundred nautical miles in 1977, foreign fleets were pushed out of most of the banks, but the federal government then encouraged the expansion of a domestic offshore fleet and set annual quotas on the basis of scientific stock assessments. Those assessments persistently estimated the stock as far healthier than it was; inshore fishers, who reported smaller and scarcer fish through the 1980s, were largely discounted. In July 1992 the government declared a moratorium, throwing tens of thousands of people out of work in what was called at the time the largest layoff in Canadian history. More than three decades later, the stock has not returned to anything like its historic abundance.

What I want is a disciplined mapping first. Who exactly are the players in the commons model here — the inshore fishers, the offshore fleets, the federal department, the foreign trawlers before 1977 — and does treating them as one homogeneous set of herders flatten anything important? What is the resource, what are the options and payoffs each party faced, and where does the actual mechanism of depletion match the model's logic of individually rational, collectively ruinous extraction?

Second, the harder question. The classic statement of the tragedy concerns an unmanaged commons, and its famous remedies are privatisation or coercive regulation. But the Grand Banks after 1977 was precisely a regulated fishery: exclusive national jurisdiction, a scientific establishment, quotas enforced by the state. So does Newfoundland confirm the theory — the incentives operated as predicted and merely needed firmer constraint — or does it embarrass the theory's own preferred remedy, since the leviathan was present, active, and presided over the collapse? I would like you to take a position on this rather than split the difference.

Third, bring in the critique of the tragedy framework associated with work on commons governance: the claim that the model neglects the possibility that users can devise their own rules. Does that critique find any purchase here — was there ever a plausible route to self-governance among Newfoundland fishers, and if not, does the case show that the critique has limits of its own (scale, heterogeneity, the state having already displaced local institutions)?

Finally, say honestly what the tragedy of the commons cannot explain about this episode: the failure of official science, the political economy of quota-setting, the treatment of local fishers' knowledge, and the question of whether the model even has the conceptual resources to describe a collapse driven as much by bad estimates and institutional incentives as by over-extraction. If some element of the case is simply outside the theory's scope, say so rather than forcing the theory to speak.
