I teach an upper-level seminar in the philosophy of social science on long-run prediction, and I keep encountering the same defensive move, both in the literature and over dinner with economist colleagues: when a grand prediction fails on schedule, its defenders insist it was not wrong but early. I want to know whether that move can be disciplined by a principle, so I have assembled my gut verdicts on a set of cases, and I would like you to extract the principle behind them — or tell me which verdicts have to go. The cases: Malthus's 1798 argument that population growth must outrun subsistence strikes me as the most interesting failure of the lot, because the trap he described arguably did govern most of human history and the industrial regime broke it just as he wrote — so my verdict is that he was not early about a coming doom but late about a vanishing one, right about a regime rather than wrong about the future. Ehrlich's 1968 forecast of mass famine within two decades I count as straightforwardly refuted, and the date-shifting in later editions strikes me as a rescue that makes things worse, not better. The Simon–Ehrlich wager I treat as a uniquely clean refutation, because a bet has a deadline and no plea of earliness is available once it passes. The 1972 Limits to Growth projections leave me saying too early to tell, and I distrust that reflex in myself: a prediction for which early is always available and wrong never is looks unfalsifiable in practice. Hubbert's peak-oil work complicates that, since he called the American peak almost exactly and successive global peak forecasts have failed and been reissued with new dates — one genuine success, then perpetual earliness, and I find myself more forgiving here than with Limits to Growth and cannot fully say why. Finally, Keynes's 1930 forecast of a fifteen-hour workweek within a century has an open deadline, yet I am inclined to call it wrong rather than early, on the grounds that he misjudged the appetite for consumption relative to leisure. That last case and the Club of Rome case are the pair I cannot reconcile: same open deadline, opposite verdicts.

My tentative principle, which I would like tested against the cases and repaired rather than merely endorsed, is this: a failed prediction counts as early rather than refuted only if its mechanism has been confirmed on independent evidence and what failed is a timing parameter the theory itself treats as a variable, not a load-bearing assumption it holds fixed. This seems to acquit Malthus in a surprising way (his mechanism is historically confirmed, but what failed was a boundary condition he treated as constant, which on my principle makes him neither early nor refuted but right-within-a-regime), and it seems to convict the reissued peak-oil forecasts, but I am not confident it survives contact with the Keynes and Limits to Growth pair. Please state the most precise principle you can construct that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, motivate each clause by naming the case that forces it, give its verdicts on two cases I have not considered — say, a forecast of widespread water wars by 2040 and a forecast of commercial fusion power by 2050 — and be explicit about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, since I would rather lose a verdict than keep an incoherent set.

After the analysis, summarize your results in a table with exactly these columns: Case (the prediction, briefly identified); Core claim (what the theory actually asserted); Locus of failure (one of: mechanism, timing, magnitude, boundary conditions, or none); My verdict (as I reported it); Verdict under your principle; and Keep or revise (whether I may retain my verdict or must abandon it, with a few words on why). Aim for rigor over charity: the point of the exercise is to find out whether my instinct that some theories are right but early marks a real distinction or merely registers which predictors I happen to like.
