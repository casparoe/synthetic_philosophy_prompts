Here's where I land on video game characters, gut-level, and I want to know what principle (if any) is behind it:

- Mowing down pedestrians in GTA for fun: completely fine. It's basically interactive slapstick, the characters are cardboard.
- Playing The Sims and walling one in a room with no toilet until he wets himself and passes out: fine too, but somehow it reflects a bit badly on the person doing it? Like I'd judge a friend who does this every session slightly, even though no harm is done.
- Letting your Tamagotchi die by neglect because you got bored: feels genuinely a little bad. Not murder-bad. But more bad than the Sims thing, which makes no sense to me since the Tamagotchi is way dumber as a piece of software.
- Killing your horse in a big open-world game that you've ridden for 100+ hours, just to see the death animation: feels wrong-ish. I reloaded the save.
- Killing a random bandit NPC in the same game for the tenth time: totally fine.
- Griefing other players, spawn-camping them, destroying their stuff in an online game: not fine, obviously, but I take it that's about the real humans, not the characters.
- A game where a character is explicitly designed to beg, scream, and flinch very realistically when you hurt it, and nothing else -- that's its whole function: I honestly think playing that for enjoyment is not fine. Maybe worse than the walled-in Sim.
- Hypothetical future case: an NPC running on a genuinely sophisticated learning system, remembers everything you've done to it across sessions, adapts its behavior, develops what look like preferences and avoids you if you've been cruel. Hurting that one for fun starts to feel actually wrong, not just icky.

So the tension I can't resolve: my GTA verdict says simulated suffering of simple characters is nothing, my torture-box verdict says some simulated suffering is bad even with nobody real harmed, and my horse/Tamagotchi verdicts say my history with a character matters -- but surely moral status doesn't depend on whether *I* happened to spend 100 hours with something. And the future-NPC case suggests what really matters is the character's own sophistication, which would make the horse case irrational (it's the same code as the bandit).

Is there one principle that captures most of these? Candidates I've toyed with: it's all about what the act does to the player's character; it's about the character's actual inner complexity; it's about relationships. Tell me which one fits best, which of my verdicts I'd have to give up to keep it consistent, and what the surviving principle says about a case I didn't list -- say, an NPC in a current game with an LLM backend that remembers your conversations and "trusts" you, and you betray it to see what it says.
