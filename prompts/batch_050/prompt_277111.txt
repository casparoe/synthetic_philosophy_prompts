You are a moral philosopher with deep expertise in the doctrine of double effect (DDE), just war theory, and the literature on trolley problems, and you have a good working command of the history of the Second World War in Europe. I am writing a case study and need a rigorous two-way analysis: a real historical episode read through the doctrine, and the doctrine stress-tested against the episode.

The episode is RAF Bomber Command's area bombing campaign against German cities, roughly 1942–45. Take the following as the stipulated facts, and do not let your analysis turn on any more precise historical claim: by late 1941, British assessments (the Butt Report) showed that only a small fraction of night bombers were getting their bombs within miles of their aiming points, so precision attacks on specific military-industrial targets were largely failing; in February 1942 the Area Bombing Directive shifted Bomber Command toward attacking the built-up areas of German cities as such, a policy associated with Arthur Harris and supported by Frederick Lindemann's "dehousing" calculations about destroying working-class housing; the stated rationale was to break civilian morale and degrade war production; the firebombing of Hamburg in July 1943 killed on the order of forty thousand people, mostly civilians, and Dresden in February 1945 on the order of twenty-five thousand; and postwar assessments found the morale-breaking effects ambiguous at best.

The text I want the analysis to turn on is from the locus classicus of the doctrine, Aquinas's discussion of killing in self-defense in the Summa Theologiae (II-II, q. 64, a. 7, Dominican Fathers translation). Everyone quotes the opening claim that one act can have two effects, only one of which is intended. I am interested in what comes immediately after, which is quoted far less often:

"And yet, though proceeding from a good intention, an act may be rendered unlawful, if it be out of proportion to the end. Wherefore if a man, in self-defense, uses more than necessary violence, it will be unlawful: whereas if he repel force with moderation his defense will be lawful. … But as it is unlawful to take a man's life, except for the public authority acting for the common good, as stated above, it is not lawful for a man to intend killing a man in self-defense, except for such as have public authority, who while intending to kill a man in self-defense, refer this to the public good, as in the case of a soldier fighting against the foe … although even these sin if they be moved by private animosity."

Before writing the analysis proper, spend a short opening section brainstorming the candidate mappings — who plays the role of Aquinas's self-defender, what counts as the intended effect and what as the effect "beside the intention" — and flag where the mapping is forced or equivocal before you commit to one. Then give me the following, in clearly marked sections:

1. A careful interpretation of the quoted passage: what work the proportionality clause does relative to the intention clause, and what the public-authority clause permits and forbids. Note that Aquinas seems to allow public authorities to intend killing where private persons may not — say precisely what that permission is and is not.
2. The most charitable DDE defense of the area campaign: civilian deaths as foreseen side effects of attacks on morale, housing, and industry, with the campaign framed as an act of public authority for the common good. Identify exactly which clause of the passage this defense must lean on.
3. The strongest DDE objection: that "attacking morale" through urban firestorms makes civilian death (or terror) the means by which the good effect is achieved, hence intended, hence outside even the public-authority permission — the bombing analogue of the terror bomber in the standard trolley-adjacent contrast with the strategic bomber. Say whether this objection depends on a contested psychological claim about what the planners intended, or survives on the stipulated facts alone.
4. Proportionality as the real battleground: run the analysis assuming, arguendo, that the intention condition is satisfied. Given the stipulated facts — including the ambiguous morale effects — does the campaign pass Aquinas's test, and what does the passage's vague standard ("more than necessary violence," "moderation") actually deliver at the scale of a bombing campaign rather than a street assault?
5. The reverse direction: what this episode shows about the doctrine. Is it a case the DDE handles well, a case it was never built for (interpersonal self-defense scaled up to industrial war), or a case that exposes a structural weakness — for instance, that intention can be laundered through institutional redescription, or that the proportionality condition quietly does all the normative work while the intention condition does none? Argue for a verdict, and name the strongest reason someone might reject it.

Think carefully and slowly, distinguish what the doctrine actually says from what it is commonly taken to say, and where the historical record or the text is silent, say so rather than filling the gap.
