Here's a construction challenge. Build me a thought experiment where the principle of procreative beneficence -- the idea that couples using IVF should select, out of the embryos they could implant, the one expected to have the best life -- ends up telling a couple to pick an embryo with a serious impairment over a healthy one. So basically the reverse of how the principle usually gets deployed.

Constraints: keep it realistic, meaning ordinary present-day embryo selection with the kind of genetic info clinics actually have, no sci-fi. The scenario itself should be one paragraph. And no cheating: the verdict can't come from the "healthy" embryo secretly having some hidden problem, and it can't come from a lucky side-benefit the impairment just happens to come packaged with -- no tight-knit community the kid gets inducted into, no insurance payout, no dodging some external threat. The impairment has to do the work as an impairment, whatever that turns out to mean.

After the scenario, do three things: (1) walk through why the principle actually delivers that verdict rather than you just stipulating that it does; (2) say honestly which feature of the case is load-bearing, and whether removing it collapses the whole thing back into the boring pick-the-healthy-embyro verdict; (3) show how someone who thinks selection decisions should be left entirely to parents, and who is queasy about ranking lives from the outside, would attack the case without simply abandoning the principle.

Also, tell me upfront whether you think these constraints are even jointly satisfiable. I have a suspicion about the only route that can work here, and I want to see if you land on it too -- and if you think the route fails, say so and explain what that failure shows about the principle.
