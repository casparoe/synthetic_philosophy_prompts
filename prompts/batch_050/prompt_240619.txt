In my philosophy of mind reading group we have spent most of the term on the conceivability argument against physicalism, and I have noticed something that bothers me: the debate appears to be completely frozen. Roughly, one side holds that a perfect physical duplicate of a human being lacking phenomenal consciousness is conceivable, and that this conceivability is evidence for its metaphysical possibility, which in turn would show that the physical facts do not necessitate the phenomenal facts. The other side responds in a cluster of ways — that we are not really conceiving what we take ourselves to be conceiving, that conceivability of this kind is not a reliable guide to possibility at all, or that our current ignorance of how consciousness arises guarantees we will "find" zombies conceivable regardless of the underlying metaphysics. These positions have been in print, largely unchanged, for decades, and the people in my group who started out on one side have never, to my knowledge, been moved by anything the other side has said — not by sharpened formulations, not by thought experiments, not by analogies to other conceivability-possibility cases. Both sides seem to agree on what the argument says, and nobody is disputing any empirical result, so the standstill cannot be chalked up to missing neuroscience or sloppy exposition.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is at bottom about the status of conceivability itself — whether it is a fallible but informative faculty for detecting genuine possibility, or merely a report on the state of our concepts and background knowledge — and that this deeper disagreement is not itself arguable, because each side can only evaluate arguments by the very standard the other side rejects. But I am not confident in this, and I can imagine rival diagnoses: perhaps the two sides are equivocating on "conceivable" or on "metaphysically possible" and the dispute is partly verbal; perhaps there is a hidden empirical question (about, say, what a completed physics or cognitive science would reveal about phenomenal concepts) that, if answered, would settle things; perhaps the standstill is actually evidence that one side's intuitions about the zombie case are being generated by a cognitive mechanism we could investigate directly, the way one might explain away a perceptual illusion.

So my question is diagnostic rather than adjudicative: what kind of disagreement is this, really, and what would follow about the prospects for progress? If it is a dispute over fundamental epistemic standards, is there any way to argue for one standard over the other without begging the question, or should we expect permanent impasse and treat that as itself a philosophically interesting result? If it is partly verbal, what would the cleaned-up, non-verbal residue be? And whichever diagnosis is right, is there anything in principle — a distinction to be drawn, a formal result, an empirical finding, a reframing of the question — that would count as moving the debate forward, or is the honest conclusion that this is a bedrock collision of intuitions about which nothing more can be said?
