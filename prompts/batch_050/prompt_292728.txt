Option B: a new "climate resilience infrastructure" fund with a novel structure. No track record. My position: there's a real difference between a risk you can quantify and a risk you can't even quantify the quantifiability of, and a committee stewarding other people's money is allowed to treat those differently. He says I'm dressing up loss aversion in fancy language. I say he's pretending a guess is the same thing as a measurement.

Write me a memo I can actually bring to the committee that recommends a policy for how we should treat investments where the probability distribution itself is ambiguous, not just the outcomes. Specifically:

- Make a real recommendation we could vote on. Not a both-sides overview — an actual policy with a bottom line.
- Give the strongest honest version of Dan's side and explain where it goes wrong (or, if you actually think he's right, tell me and convince me, I'd rather lose the argument than bring a bad memo).
- Deal with this wrinkle that Dan raised and I didn't have a great answer to: if it's okay to discount options whose odds you can't pin down, that seems to justify never touching anything genuinely new, which can't be right, since everything was new once. A usable policy has to say when ambiguity-based hesitation is legit and when it's just conservatism in a trenchcoat.
