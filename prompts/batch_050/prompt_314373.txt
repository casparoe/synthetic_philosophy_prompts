I am a TA grading answers to the following exam question for a political philosophy course. Please give the answer a mark out of 20, and justify the mark with the specific comments you would write on the script -- what earns credit, what is wrong or imprecise, and whether the conclusion follows from the student's own premises. The question was:

"Some philosophers have argued that the state should require a license before people are permitted to raise children. Set out the strongest version of the case for parental licensing, and critically evaluate it in light of the claim that children have a right to an open future. Does that right strengthen or weaken the case for licensing?"

Here is the student's answer:

Hugh LaFollette's argument proceeds by analogy. Society licenses activities such as driving and practicing medicine because incompetent performance poses serious risks of harm to others, and we accept competence testing as a fair gatekeeper even though such tests are imperfect. Parenting, LaFollette argues, fits this pattern: it is an activity in which incompetence predictably causes grave harm to a vulnerable third party, so consistency requires that we license it as well. He concludes that there is no right to rear children in the way there is a right to free speech; parenting is instead a privilege to be granted upon demonstrated competence.

Joel Feinberg's right to an open future holds that parents have wide discretion over a child's upbringing and that the state should interfere only where parents would otherwise close off the child's options. Feinberg introduced this right to explain why parental authority deserves protection against paternalistic state interference in family life. Understood this way, the open-future right seems to weaken the case for licensing: if the point of the right is to shield childrearing from the state, then subjecting would-be parents to a state competence test inverts the proper relationship between family and state.

A defender of licensing might reply that the children of grossly incompetent parents are precisely those whose futures are most constricted, so licensing could itself be justified as a protection of open futures. But this reply fails, because licensing tests are imperfect: they will exclude some competent parents (false positives), and any test that produces false positives is an unjustifiable violation of rights. Licensing therefore cannot be justified on open-future grounds.

On balance, the analogy argument is the strongest element of the licensing case, but the right to an open future counts against it, and the imperfection of any testing scheme defeats the proposal.
