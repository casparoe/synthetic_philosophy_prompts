So here's something that's been bugging me since I started reading about how appelate panels actually decide cases. Say a three-judge panel hears a case where the plaintiff wins only if both issue A (the contract is valid) and issue B (the statute doesn't bar the claim) go his way. Judge 1 says A yes, B no. Judge 2 says A no, B yes. Judge 3 says A yes, B yes. So a majority of the panel thinks A holds, and a majority thinks B holds -- but two of the three judges think the plaintiff loses. As I understand it, real courts basically always vote on the outcome, so the defendant wins, and the "court's view" on each issue is just a tally you do afterwards by reading the individual opinions.

Here's what confuses me though. First, the same courts make a huge deal of each judge giving reasons, and the written opinions matter way beyond the case -- precedent supposedly lives in the reasoning, not the vote count. Second, when a court fragments so badly that no single rationale commands a majority (the US Supreme Court does this all the time), lower courts get told to follow the narrowest rationale that supported the result, as if the holding were hiding inside one judge's reasoning rather than in anything the court collectively did. So: outcomes decided by voting, precedent carried by reasoning, and when the reasoning fails to add up we pretend one opinion speaks for the whole court.

What does this mess presuppose about what an appellate court even is? If the court were in the business of finding out what the law is, you'd think premise-by-premise voting would be the honest procedure, since the court has after all "found" both A and B. If it's in the business of settling disputes between parties, outcome voting makes sense -- but then why does all the reason-giving and precedent machinery matter so much, and why would anyone care which rationale was narrowest? Reconstruct the implicit theory (or tell me there isn't one): which pieces of the practice strictly need which commitments about adjudication, which pieces are compatible with several rival views, and whether the three pieces can be made to presuppose one coherent picture at all. And tell me what would concretely have to change if courts switched to voting on the issues instead of the outcome -- does precedent as we know it even survive that?

One more thing: tell me how confident you are in the reconstruction overall, and single out the one claim in your answer you're least sure of.
