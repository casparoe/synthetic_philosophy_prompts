I work as a software engineer at a mid-sized company, and our team has spent the past year building a formal verification pipeline: we specify properties our systems should satisfy (no deadlocks in the scheduler, no integer overflow in the payment path, that sort of thing) and use automated tools to prove the code meets them. It has been slow and expensive but genuinely useful, and we're about to ask management for budget to expand it. At a recent design review, though, one of our senior engineers argued the whole effort is fundamentally misguided. His reasoning, as I understand it, comes from a popular science book he read about a famous result in mathematical logic: roughly, that any consistent formal system powerful enough to do basic arithmetic will contain true statements it cannot prove, and that adding more axioms never fixes this, because the new system just generates new unprovable truths. From this he concludes that verification tools are guaranteed to have permanent blind spots, that some true properties of our code are in principle unprovable, and that we're selling management a false sense of certainty that no expansion of the pipeline can ever deliver.

He went a step further and claimed this is also why human code review can never be automated away: a human reviewer can, in his words, step outside the system and simply see certain truths that any mechanical procedure is barred from reaching, which he takes to show the human mind isn't itself a formal system. This is the part that really loses me. I have no formal training in logic or philosophy, but my intuition is that a theorem about axiomatic theories of arithmetic is being asked to do a lot of work here. Our verifiers don't seem to be in the business of enumerating all truths; they check whether a specific, finite piece of code satisfies a specific property, and when they fail, it's usually a timeout or a tooling limitation, not some deep in-principle barrier. But I can't articulate exactly where his inference breaks down, and he's senior enough that his argument is gaining traction with our director.

Am I right to push back, and if so, on what grounds? Does the logical result he's invoking actually have implications for verifying concrete properties of actual programs, or is he misapplying it -- and if he is misapplying it, what is the cleanest way to explain the mistake without myself misrepresenting the theorem? And on the human-review point: is there a coherent sense in which people can "see" truths no machine can, or does that argument smuggle in an assumption about the mind that we have no reason to grant? I'd like to respond to him precisely rather than just outranking the objection with skepticism, so an explanation of what the result does and doesn't show, aimed at someone comfortable with math but new to logic, would be most useful.
