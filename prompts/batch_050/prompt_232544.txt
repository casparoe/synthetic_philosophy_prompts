I am a PhD student in moral philosophy applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship, and I need to turn a half-formed idea into a proper proposal document. My background is population ethics, but the project sits at the intersection of population ethics and the ethics of intervening in nature, so treat the framing as the hard part.

Here is the half-formed idea. The standard debate about whether we should reduce suffering among wild animals tends to evaluate interventions -- say, vaccination programs, supplemental feeding, habitat alteration -- by asking how they affect the aggregate welfare of the affected population, where population welfare is understood as something like total or average welfare per animal summed over animals and time. But the population structure of most wild species is very different from the human case this framework was built for: the overwhelming majority of individuals in most wild populations die extremely young, reproduction is prolific precisely to compensate for this, and population sizes are governed by resource constraints that refill vacancies quickly. This suggests to me that many interventions do not so much improve the lot of a fixed set of animals as change which animals come to exist and how long the vacancies they leave behind persist. If that is right, then a proposal to, say, reduce juvenile mortality in some species may barely change lifetime suffering per capita at all -- the niche refills, the total count of short bad lives stays roughly constant, and the intervention's real effect is on the identity and number of lives rather than on their quality. I suspect this undermines some arguments both for and against intervention, but I have not worked out which, and the literature I know does not cleanly separate this "turnover" structure from the familiar total-versus-average disputes.
