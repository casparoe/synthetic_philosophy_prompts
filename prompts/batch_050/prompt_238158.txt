I have no formal background in philosophy or medicine, but I have been turning an idea over in my mind for some time and would like help exploring its comic side through satire.

The idea is this. When scientists classify rocks or chemical elements, the things classified do not react to the classification: granite does not behave differently because geologists drew a boundary where they did, and no element has ever changed its properties after being renamed. But when doctors classify people, the people sometimes do react. They read the diagnostic criteria and recognize themselves in them (or learn to recognize themselves), they join communities organized around the label, they present themselves to clinicians in the vocabulary the label supplies, and all of this then feeds back into how the category is described and revised in the next edition of the manual. I understand the philosopher Ian Hacking called this a looping effect. I find it genuinely interesting and also faintly absurd, and it is the absurdity I want drawn out.

Please write a piece of satire in the form of deadpan software release notes — a version history, as on a tech company's changelog page — for an invented psychiatric diagnosis. Invent the diagnosis yourself; it should sound plausible but must not be a real condition, and the satire should target the practice and discourse of classification, never people who suffer. Around 600 words, structured as a sequence of versions from the original description through several revisions, each entry listing "new features" (criteria added), "fixes" (criteria removed or narrowed), and "known issues," all in the flat, euphemistic voice of technical documentation.

Some requirements:

- The loop should be the engine of every entry. Each version's changes must be caused, explicitly or implicitly, by how people classified under earlier versions reacted to being classified: self-diagnosis via online checklists, advocacy groups reshaping the criteria, symptom profiles migrating to match whatever the previous edition described, prevalence shifting after awareness campaigns, and so on.
- Stay deadpan throughout. Do not wink at the reader or step outside the format to explain the joke; a reader should be able to tell what is being satirized precisely because the document takes its own absurdity seriously.
- At least once, let the loop catch the classifiers themselves — the committee, the clinicians, or the authors of the notes.
- Somewhere in the document, include a brief contrast with a classification whose members cannot react to it (a mineral scheme or a table of elements), treated with the same bureaucratic seriousness, whose changelog is conspicuously, almost disappointingly stable.
- End on a version that is announced but never fully described, as if the document had to go to press before the population could finish changing.

After the piece, add a table with four columns and one row per version you wrote:

1. Version — the version number or label as it appears in the piece.
2. Change — a one-sentence summary of what that version altered.
3. Real practice exaggerated — the genuine feature of psychiatric classification or mental-health discourse the entry exaggerates (diagnostic inflation, checklist self-diagnosis, advocacy influence on criteria, the migration of symptom vocabulary, etc.).
4. Looping mechanism — the specific way in which the classified people's reaction to the earlier classification produced this change; write "none" for any row that works purely by contrast, as the mineral or element entry may.

If anything in my description of the looping idea strikes you as mistaken or oversimplified, please correct it in a sentence or two before the piece.
