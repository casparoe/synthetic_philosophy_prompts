A guy I know from an old reading group posted his summary of John Taurek's argument in "Should the Numbers Count?" and I want a second opinion before I argue with him about it. His version goes like this:

"Taurek says that if you can save either one person or five other people but not all six, the fact that five is more than one shouldn't decide it for you. His reason is that harms don't add up across people: the death of five isn't a worse event than the death of one, because there's nobody who suffers the five deaths the way the one person suffers his own. Each person only loses their own life, and that loss is the same size for everyone involved. So the only fair thing to do is flip a coin, heads the one, tails the five, giving everyone an equal chance. Taurek takes this to be a knock-down arguement against consequentialism in general, since consequentialists treat people as containers of value whose contents you're supposed to maximize, which is obviously dehumanizing."

Something about this smells off to me. I could swear I read somewhere that Taurek actually says you'd be perfectly within your rights to save your own friend in a case like this instead of leaving it to chance, which doesn't fit with the coin flip being "the only fair thing." But the guy is usually careful, so maybe I'm misremembering or thinking of a different philosopher.

So: how fair is this as a summary? Which parts would Taurek himself sign off on, which parts distort what he actually claims (even subtly), and where does the summary slide from Taurek's view into something stronger or just different? Also, setting aside what Taurek said — does the anti-aggregation reasoning in the middle of the summary, the part that seems genuinely right, actually support the conclusions drawn at the end? And if the coin flip part is a misrepresentation, what's the real view and is it any less weird?
