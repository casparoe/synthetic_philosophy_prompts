So I got laid off back in March, had a pretty rough stretch with it, and a friend reccomended I read Meditations (the Marcus Aurelius one). I did, and then fell down the rabbit hole of the whole modern Stoicism thing — the podcasts, the journaling apps, all of it. I mentioned this at dinner last week and my friends Chris and Dana immediately got into it, and the argument is STILL going in our group chat three days later. I keep getting tagged on every message so I need a referee.

Chris's side: he says the whole thing rests on the dichotomy of control — some things are up to us, some aren't, focus only on the first — and that this is either trivial or false. Trivial version: obviously you can't control the weather, congrats, nobody needed a philosophy for that. Serious version: only your own judgments are fully up to you, but then it's useless, because everything people actually care about — your health, whether you keep your job, your kids being okay — sits in this huge middle zone where effort matters but doesn't guarantee anything, and the dichotomy has nothing to say about the middle. He also went after the "preferred indifferents" thing: either health and money matter to a good life or they don't, and telling people to pursue them full-force while officially regarding them as nothing is just double-think nobody can actually pull off. And his finishing move was that modern Stoicism stole the ethics and threw out the physics — for the ancients the universe was rational and providential and THAT'S why accepting whatever happens made sense, so once you drop that, living in agreement with nature is an empty slogan and what's left is just detachment with better branding.

Dana pushed back on all three. She said Chris is misreading the dichotomy — it's not a claim about what you can influence, it's about where you stake your worth and what you can actually be praised or blamed for. The Stoic archer does literally everything to shoot well, and the gust of wind isn't his failure; the middle zone is exactly who the advice is FOR. On indifferents she said Chris has clearly never competed at anything — serious athletes leave everything on the field and are genuinly okay with losing, that's a real psychological state, not bookkeeping fraud — and the point about health was that someone who loses everything can still live decently, which is a claim about dignity, not accounting. On the physics she said the ethics doesn't need providence: it stands on a secular observation about what pinning your happiness on externals does to people, and the thousands of readers getting something out of this stuff without believing in a rational cosmos are kind of the proof.

Okay so: who's right about what? Go point by point — the dichotomy, the preferred indifferents, the physics objection — and then tell me who's more right overall, and whether there's something they're both missing.
