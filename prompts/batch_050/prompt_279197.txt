I am a second-year undergraduate taking a course on ancient ethics and historiography, and we have spent the last few weeks on Plutarch's Parallel Lives. I would like your help auditing an analogy that has come to structure our seminar, and I would like you to work through it carefully rather than give me a quick verdict.

In the preface to his Life of Aemilius Paulus, Plutarch explains his motives for writing the Lives, and I will paraphrase the key passage rather than quote it, since translations vary: he says that he first began writing biographies for the sake of others, but came to continue the work for his own sake as well, because he found himself using the lives of these men as a kind of mirror, in which he could see how to order and improve his own life, and he describes receiving each of his subjects in turn, one after another, like a guest he lives with and observes, considering what sort of man he was and taking from his life what is worth imitating. Our professor leaned heavily on this mirror image in a recent lecture. Her argument was that biography, and Plutarch's biography in particular, does moral work that abstract ethical theory cannot: a life shows you yourself, as a mirror does, and so reading lives is a uniquely effective instrument of moral self-correction -- more effective, she suggested, than studying virtue in the abstract, which is why moral education should be built around exemplary lives rather than around principles. She has assigned a short essay responding to this claim, and I want to interrogate the analogy itself before taking a position.

Please approach this as a scholar of ancient philosophy who is also practiced at analyzing arguments by analogy. I would like you to do the following, in roughly this order. First, before drafting your analysis, brainstorm a list of candidate correspondences between the two sides of the analogy: what, in the reading of a biography, plays the role of the mirror, of the reflection, of the act of looking, of the adjustments one makes after looking, and of Plutarch's additional image of hosting each subject as a guest. Then settle on the correspondence table you find most defensible and present it explicitly. Second, identify the disanalogies, and -- this is the part I care most about -- separate the disanalogies that matter for my professor's intended conclusion from the ones that are merely interesting. Here are the sorts of tensions I have noticed, though you should feel free to reframe, reject, or add to them: a mirror shows you your own face, but a biography shows you someone else's, and indeed someone else's face as curated by a biographer who openly selects material for its illustrative value; a mirror is passively accurate, while Plutarch's portraits are shaped by moral and literary aims that sit uneasily with neutrality; a mirror reveals defects as readily as virtues, while the Lives foreground what is worth imitating; and the mirror image seems to presuppose that the reader already possesses the evaluative standards needed to know what in themselves to adjust, which is arguably the very thing moral education is supposed to supply. Third, assess whether my professor's conclusion -- that reading lives is morally corrective in a way abstract theory is not, because it functions as self-confrontation -- survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the mirror metaphor was carrying the argument the whole way. In doing this, distinguish carefully between two claims she may be conflating: that biography is an effective tool of moral formation, and that it works in the specifically mirror-like way she describes. Fourth, if the analogy cannot bear the weight, propose a better one drawn from the same passage or elsewhere -- Plutarch's own image of receiving each subject as a guest, for instance, seems to point in a rather different direction than the mirror image does -- and say what conclusion that better analogy would actually license.

Two constraints. Do not simply give me intellectual history or a survey of scholarship; I want the argument audited on its merits, with the mapping made explicit. And where you find the analogy doing quiet work -- importing features of mirrors that reading lacks, or vice versa -- name that work directly rather than letting it pass. The essay is due in ten days, so a clear, well-structured analysis I can build an argument on top of is what I need.
