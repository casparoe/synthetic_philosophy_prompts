I have been reading Darwin's correspondence and keep circling back to a passage from a letter he wrote to the Harvard botanist Asa Gray on May 22, 1860, roughly six months after On the Origin of Species appeared. Gray was a devout Christian who had become Darwin's most important advocate in America and was trying to reconcile natural selection with divine design. Darwin writes:

"With respect to the theological view of the question; this is always painful to me.— I am bewildered.— I had no intention to write atheistically. But I own that I cannot see, as plainly as others do, & as I shd wish to do, evidence of design & beneficence on all sides of us. There seems to me too much misery in the world. I cannot persuade myself that a beneficent & omnipotent God would have designedly created the Ichneumonidæ with the express intention of their feeding within the living bodies of caterpillars, or that a cat should play with mice. Not believing this, I see no necessity in the belief that the eye was expressly designed. On the other hand I cannot anyhow be contented to view this wonderful universe & especially the nature of man, & to conclude that everything is the result of brute force. I am inclined to look at everything as resulting from designed laws, with the details, whether good or bad, left to the working out of what we may call chance. Not that this notion at all satisfies me. I feel most deeply that the whole subject is too profound for the human intellect. A dog might as well speculate on the mind of Newton.— Let each man hope & believe what he can.—"

(The Ichneumonidæ are parasitoid wasps whose larvae consume living caterpillars from within. A few sentences later he adds: "Certainly I agree with you that my views are not at all necessarily atheistical.")

Here is what I need help understanding.

First, the structure. Map the positions the letter entertains in turn: the recoil from particular design in the face of misery; the refusal to accept "brute force" as an account of the whole; the compromise of designed laws with chance details; the admission that even this compromise does not satisfy him; and the closing retreat into cognitive modesty. Does the letter arrive at a stable resting point, or is the oscillation itself the position?

Second, the status of the misery material. It is often cited as a textbook argument from evil, but Darwin's declared target is "evidence of design & beneficence on all sides of us," and his idiom is psychological ("I cannot persuade myself") rather than demonstrative. Is this an evidential problem of evil, an expression of moral recoil, or a reductio of one specific doctrine — that each structure was expressly contrived for its observed effect — rather than of theism as such? Note that he retains "designed laws" a sentence later.

Third, the language of involuntariness: "I cannot persuade myself," "I cannot anyhow be contented," "Let each man hope & believe what he can." Does the letter treat religious belief as settled by one's constitution rather than chosen on the evidence? If so, how does that bear on the final sentence, which is usually read as a gesture of tolerance but might equally be read as resignation?

Fourth, the dog-and-Newton line. What is the scope of the cognitive limitation Darwin asserts? If the subject is too profound for the human intellect, does that undercut his own confident judgment that there is "too much misery in the world" for particular beneficent design? Or is the modesty meant to attach only to ultimate questions — the origin of the laws themselves — while leaving local inferences about nature standing? Is there a risk that the move is self-undermining, invoked where it costs his opponents and suspended where it would cost him?

Fifth, the connection to morality. In The Descent of Man, Darwin would later argue that the moral sense grew out of social instincts sharpened by intellect and habit — a product of the same natural history that produced the wasp. Does that account underwrite or undermine the moral judgment doing the work in this letter? If our sense of beneficence and cruelty is itself an outcome of "the working out of what we may call chance," what standing does it have to arraign the character of a creator? And notice the asymmetry he relies on: he cannot see beneficence in the wasp, yet cannot be content with brute force in the universe. Is that asymmetry principled, or does it merely register which of his instincts rebel in each case?

Throughout, please distinguish what is plain on the face of the text from what is genuinely disputed among Darwin scholars — for instance, whether his position in 1860 is best labeled a residual theism, a deism, or a fluctuating agnosticism, and how continuous it is with the agnosticism he professed later in life. Do not present any contested scholarly reading as the settled consensus.

Finally, end your answer with a single line beginning "In one sentence:" that gives your best compact characterization of the position this letter stakes out.
