Two colleagues of mine in a game theory reading group had a drawn-out argument last week about a variant of the centipede game, and I'd like you to referee it. Here is the variant. Two players, R and C, alternately choose Take or Pass over at most ten decision nodes, with R moving first. The pot starts at $2 and doubles with each Pass. A player who Takes ends the game and receives 60% of the current pot; the other player receives 40%. If all ten nodes are Passed, the final pot is split equally. So the only structural differences from the textbook game are that the non-taker always receives a substantial share, and that the last mover's gain from Taking rather than Passing (60% instead of 50%) is small as a fraction of the pot.

(1) Daniel argued that the variant changes nothing of substance. Backward induction still applies: at the last node the mover prefers 60% to 50%, so they Take; the player at the penultimate node anticipates this and Takes rather than hand over the decision; and the reasoning unrolls all the way back to the first node, where R should Take immediately. The equilibrium has the same structure as in the standard game, and any talk of sustained cooperation is just noise about how real people behave.

(2) Mara replied that the smallness of the last mover's temptation is exactly what breaks the argument. Because the final Take yields only slightly more than the equal split, the penultimate player needs only a sliver of doubt that the last mover will actually maximize -- a rounding heuristic, a flicker of generosity, sheer indifference -- for Passing to be a worthwhile gamble. And once Passing at the penultimate node is reasonable, the same logic cascades backward: small doubts compound over ten nodes, so Passing early can be rational too. Backward induction, she said, is only as strong as the certainty it assumes at the final step, and here that certainty is doing all the work for almost no stakes.

(3) Daniel countered that this proves too much. Rationality assumptions are idealizations in every game-theoretic model; if a sliver of doubt at the end unravels the centipede, the same move unravels backward induction in every finite game of perfect information, including ones where Mara would happily accept the classical conclusion. She must either abandon backward induction wholesale or state a principled threshold for how much doubt is enough to license a Pass -- and he didn't see one on offer.

(4) Mara said there is a principled line, and the variant makes it visible: what matters is whether the last mover's gain from Taking is large relative to what earlier players risk by Passing. Where the final temptation is small, Passing is cheap to attempt and chains of doubt are rational; where it is large, they are not. The standard game conceals this because its numbers make the final Take tempting; the variant's numbers do not.

(5) Daniel's final move was that Mara had been equivocating throughout between two claims: that fully rational players with common knowledge of rationality might Pass (which he denied -- under common knowledge of rationality, Taking at the first node remains mandatory) and that real players, who doubt one another's rationality, might Pass (which he conceded but called trivial). Oscillating between a theorem and a psychology, he said, lets her refute the psychology while leaving the theorem untouched.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the five numbered moves, say whether it is sound, partially sound, or mistaken, and identify precisely what it gets right or wrong -- including whether Mara's proposed principled line in (4) actually delivers one, and whether Daniel's final distinction in (5) lands. Then say who is more nearly right overall, and what, if anything, both parties are missing.

After your analysis, give me a JSON object with these fields: "moves" -- an array of five objects, each with "move" (the move number), "speaker", "verdict" (one of "sound", "partially sound", or "mistaken"), and "key_point" (a one- to two-sentence justification); "missing_considerations" -- an array of strings naming considerations neither party raised; "overall_winner" -- the name of the colleague who is more nearly right, or "tie"; and "crux" -- one sentence stating what the disagreement ultimately turns on.
