A friend of mine recently finished Gödel, Escher, Bach and posted the following summary of Hofstadter's account of where the meaning of a message lives. Our reading group meets next week, and before I rely on this I want to know how much of it to trust.

His summary: According to Hofstadter, every message contains three nested layers. The frame message is the physical carrier itself — the grooves in a record, the ink on the page — whose presence signals that a message is there at all. The outer message is the set of symbols in which the message is written. The inner message is the meaning the sender intended, and because recovering it requires the receiver to supply the interpretation, meaning is never genuinely in the message: it exists only in the mind of whoever decodes it. Hofstadter makes this vivid with a jukebox — the very same record produces different music in different jukeboxes, which shows the record contains no music in itself. From this he concludes that a message written in a completely unknown language carries no meaning until someone happens to decode it, and that an alien civilization intercepting one of our radio transmissions could extract nothing from it unless we first taught them our conventions.

I have a nagging suspicion that the jukebox is being used here for nearly the opposite of Hofstadter's point, but I cannot reconstruct his actual argument well enough to be sure. Please audit the summary: separate what is accurate from what is a defensible simplification and from what misrepresents his position; correct his account of the three layers where needed; explain how Hofstadter himself deploys the jukebox example and what he actually claims about unknown languages and messages sent to alien intelligences; and finally, tell me whether the summary's closing conclusion — that meaning is wholly receiver-dependent — follows from anything correct in it, or whether it inverts the view it purports to describe.
