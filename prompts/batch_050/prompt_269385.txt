So here's something that's been bugging me. Legal systems lean really hard on translation being a determinate, right-or-wrong affair. If you submit foreign documents to a court or an immigration office, you need a certified translation where a sworn translator attests that the translation is accurate and complete -- and they can be professionally liable, sometimes even criminally, if they get it wrong. Courts will genuinely rule that translation X of a contract clause is correct and translation Y is a mistranslation, full stop. Meanwhile, with multilingual treaties (like EU legislation or UN conventions), all the language versions are equally authoritative, and when the French and English versions diverge, lawyers talk about what the treaty "really says" across both versions, as if there's one shared content that the versions are both trying to express.

But I keep thinking about Quine's indeterminacy of translation -- the gavagai thing, where a linguist in the field can't pin down whether a native expression refers to rabbits, undetached rabbit parts, rabbit-stages, or whatever, because any behavioral evidence fits multiple translation manuals, and Quine's conclusion that there's just no fact of the matter about reference, only translation manuals that work better or worse relative to each other. And Quine himself seemed to think this was totally compatible with translation being perfectly fine for practical purposes.

So what I want to know: what theory of meaning does the legal practice actually presuppose? Does treating translations as determinately correct or incorrect only make sense if there's a fact of the matter about what the source text means that Quine says doesn't exist? Or does the practice only need the weaker thing Quine concedes -- like, determinacy at the level of whole occasion sentences under practical constraints -- in which case a court ruling that translation Y is wrong isn't presupposing anything Quine would deny? The treaty case seems harder for Quine than the certified translation case, or maybe I have that backwards, because it seems to reify a single meaning shared across languages in a way certified translation doesn't.

Reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) of the practice, and be careful about the distinction between what the practice strictly presupposes vs what it just fits nicely with -- I suspect a lot of what looks like commitment to determinate meaning is really just commitment to convergence among competent translators, which is a different thing. And then the flip side: if you took the Quinean view seriously inside the institution, what would actually have to change? Would translator liability be incoherent, or just re-groundable as liability for deviation from professional consensus? Would the equal-authority rule for treaty versions need a different justification? I want to know whether the law here is resting on a philosophy of language that's been dead since 1960, or whether it never needed that philosophy in the first place.
