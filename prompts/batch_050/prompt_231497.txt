A friend and I have just spent the better part of a weekend arguing about autonomous weapons, and I would like to force the disagreement onto firmer ground. Her position is that "meaningful human control" — the property everyone seems to agree weapons ought to retain — is an empty slogan. No gunner, she says, has ever had meaningful control over a shell once fired; artillery is aimed at map references the crew cannot see, bomber crews have released through cloud on a pathfinder's marker, and every officer delegates to subordinates whose split-second decisions he cannot preview. Demanding of a machine a kind of control we have never demanded of human combatants is, on her view, special pleading dressed up as a principle. I suspect the concept can be made rigorous, but when pressed I could not say what exactly a theory of meaningful human control would have to deliver in order to answer her. So: write the desiderata.

The purpose is fixed: the theory should be good enough to advise a government on which weapon systems it may permissibly field, given that it already accepts the ordinary laws of armed conflict. What must any adequate account of meaningful human control specify, deliver, or respect? For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and mark whether it is genuinely non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp. The following cases came up in our argument, and any list should be tested against them:

1. A shipboard point-defence gun that tracks and fires on incoming missiles in under a second — far faster than any human could intervene — and is generally agreed to have saved lives. An account that condemns it for want of control seems to prove too much; one that accepts it must say where the control is located.
2. A loitering munition that searches a designated area for several hours and attacks any vehicle matching a signature profile, with a single operator nominally supervising forty such munitions and able, in principle, to abort any one of them.
3. A swarm of fifty drones that re-allocates targets among itself in response to losses and jamming; a human chose the mission and its boundaries, the swarm chose everything else.
4. The human baselines my friend pressed: an artillery crew firing on grid coordinates they cannot see; a bomber crew releasing on a marker; a military working dog sent in pursuit. If these count as under meaningful human control and the swarm does not, the theory must state the difference without simply pointing at the silicon.

Beyond the cases, the list should address, at minimum: the level at which control must be exercised — over each individual engagement, over the mission, over the system's design and training, or over the decision to deploy — together with an argument for why the "meaning" attaches at that level rather than equivocating between levels; the difference between nominal and real control, given that humans in the loop reliably rubber-stamp machine recommendations, which presumably means stating the epistemic, temporal, and authority conditions under which a human's involvement is genuine rather than decorative; and the relationship between control and responsibility — whether an account of meaningful control must guarantee that someone is answerable for each killing, or whether control and answerability can come apart without the concept collapsing.

If some subset of plausible desiderata turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable — I suspect, for instance, that per-engagement human comprehension, permission for defensive systems whose entire value lies in superhuman speed, and a one-human-one-decision model of control cannot all be held at once — say so explicitly; that is the part I most want to see. Please also identify which single desideratum excludes the largest number of accounts currently in circulation.

Finally, and briefly: how do three crude candidates fare against the list — "a human approves each engagement", "a human sets the mission and can terminate it", and "control is meaningful if and only if a specific human can be held to account for the outcome"? Keep the scoring short; the criteria are the deliverable. And tell me, on the strength of the list, whether my friend's scepticism is really an attack on one particular desideratum, and whether that desideratum survives.
