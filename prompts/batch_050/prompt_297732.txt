I work professionally in functional programming and have spent the last couple of years writing dependent type theory in Agda and Lean, so the Curry–Howard correspondence is second nature to me: propositions as types, proofs as programs, a proof of a disjunction as a tagged union carrying evidence for one side, a proof of an existential as a dependent pair of a witness and its certificate, negation as a function into the empty type. From this vantage point, classical logic looks like the assumption of a global oracle of type (A : Type) → A + (A → ⊥), which is exactly the kind of unprincipled axiom you can't implement. So when I recently started reading about Brouwer's intuitionism and mathematical constructivism more broadly, I expected to find my own vocabulary already there, just older. Instead I found something that sometimes matches and sometimes seems to be talking about entirely different things: proofs as mental constructions rather than terms, mathematics as an activity of a "creating subject" unfolding in time, choice sequences that are never finished and governed by no law, and the striking claim that classical mathematics is not merely unjustified but literally meaningless, since its statements lack construction-based meaning conditions.

Here is what I'd like from you. Restate the core of intuitionism — its rejection of the law of excluded middle, its account of what mathematical statements mean, its picture of what a proof is, and its more exotic commitments like choice sequences and the creating subject — in the vocabulary I actually work in: types, terms, elimination rules, canonicity, decidable versus semidecidable propositions, axioms versus definable principles. Do this as faithfully as you can, and don't smuggle in type theory's own assumptions and present them as part of the original doctrine; I want the translation, not a rational reconstruction that conveniently rounds off the weird parts. Then give me a residue report: which intuitionist commitments translate cleanly (I suspect the constructive reading of disjunction and existence will, and that continuity principles are where things start getting strained), which you had to distort to fit, and which have no counterpart at all in type-theoretic vocabulary. My hunch is that the mental-construction and temporal elements — the idea that a proof is something a subject does in time, that a real number can be a forever-unfinished process rather than a completed object — simply cannot be expressed as types, and that type theory quietly replaces the creating subject with the programmer, which is not the same thing.

Most importantly, I want you to commit to a verdict, not just lay out options. When something fails to translate, say whose fault it is: is the residue a genuine defect of type theory as a framework for capturing what intuitionists meant, or does the failure instead reveal that the non-translatable parts of intuitionism were never doing real mathematical or philosophical work — that they are, in effect, the parts a precise formalism forces you to discard because they were unclear to begin with? Take a definite position on whether intuitionism minus its residue is just constructive type theory with extra rhetoric, or whether something substantive is genuinely lost, and give me your reasons. I'd rather read an answer I disagree with than a balanced survey that declines to choose.
