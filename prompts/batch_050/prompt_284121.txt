Essay question: In 1894, C. Lloyd Morgan introduced what came to be known as Morgan's canon, according to which an animal's behaviour should not be interpreted as the outcome of a higher psychological faculty if it can be adequately interpreted as the outcome of a lower one. Explain what problem the canon was intended to solve, how it relates to a general principle of parsimony such as Occam's razor, and whether it should still guide research in comparative cognition today, illustrating your answer with at least one contemporary case study.

Student's answer: Morgan's canon was a response to the anecdotal method of earlier comparative psychologists such as George Romanes, whose Animal Intelligence (1882) gathered informal reports of clever animal behaviour and interpreted them in richly mentalistic terms. Morgan, working in the Darwinian tradition, wanted to place the study of animal minds on a properly scientific footing. His canon holds that a behaviour should never be attributed to a higher psychological faculty when it can be explained by a lower one, where higher and lower refer to positions on the phylogenetic scale, so that, for instance, explanations appealing to reasoning should not be used where associative learning will do. The canon is often presented as a special case of Occam's razor, and this seems correct: both principles tell us to prefer the simplest adequate hypothesis, and a lower explanation simply is the simpler one. So understood, the canon inherits the general justification of parsimony in science and requires no further defence. Morgan himself used the principle to argue that non-human animals are not capable of genuine thought, and in doing so he paved the way for behaviourism, which extended the same scepticism to all mentalistic explanation. As for whether the canon should still guide research, I believe it should. In the scrub-jay caching experiments of Clayton and colleagues, jays re-cache food when another bird has watched them hide it, which appears to involve attributing knowledge to the observer's mind. However, the behaviour can be explained by simpler mechanisms, such as learned associations between being watched and losing food, so the canon instructs us to reject the mentalistic interpretation. This shows the canon still does useful work in curbing researchers' tendency to romanticise animal behaviour, and I conclude that it should be retained essentially unchanged.
