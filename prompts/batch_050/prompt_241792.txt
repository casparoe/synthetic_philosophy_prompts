Forecasting tournaments, meteorological agencies, and many intelligence-analysis shops all run the same basic apparatus. Forecasters must express their uncertainty about each event as a single number — one probability, not a range and not a hedge — and that number is later graded two ways. First, by a strictly proper scoring rule such as the Brier score, whose defining property is that a forecaster maximizes her expected score by reporting exactly what she believes; propriety is routinely cited as the reason the scheme "rewards honesty." Second, by calibration: if, across all the occasions where a forecaster said 70 percent, roughly 70 percent of the events came to pass, she is deemed reliable, and systematic miscalibration is treated as a defect in her, not in the metric. Practitioners, meanwhile, often chafe at the single-number requirement when they feel genuinely ignorant — asked for the probability that a novel technology is regulated within five years, or that an unprecedented geopolitical event occurs, many want to answer with an interval or to decline, and the rules forbid this.

What view of credence does this apparatus, taken as a whole, presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: what must a credence be such that (a) there is a fact of the matter about which number the forecaster "really believes," such that a scoring rule can be designed to elicit exactly that number, and (b) conformity to long-run frequencies counts as evidence that her numbers were good ones? Note that these two grading practices seem to pull in different directions — one treats the forecast as a private state to be extracted, the other as a claim answerable to the world's statistics — so say whether the apparatus presupposes a consistent theory of probability at all, or two different theories stapled together. Then press on the single-number requirement: if a forecaster's actual state of uncertainty is better modeled as an imprecise credence — a set of probability functions she cannot rule between, or an interval she refuses to narrow — does propriety still guarantee that reporting her state is optimal, or does the requirement quietly force her to manufacture a precision she does not have, so that the score rewards something other than honesty? Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits comfortably with: could the whole apparatus be retained, scoring and calibration alike, by a subjectivist who denies that credences answer to frequencies, or by a frequentist who denies that there is any inner state to elicit? Finally, if we took imprecise credences seriously, what would a reformed practice look like — could scoring rules and calibration tests be redesigned for interval or set-valued forecasts, and would anything of epistemic value that the current practice captures be lost in the redesign?
