I teach a graduate seminar on the metaphysics and value of pain, and our final unit concerns pain asymbolia: patients who, typically following damage to the insular cortex or anterior cingulate, continue to feel and localize pain, describe it with ordinary intensity vocabulary, and identify it as pain — yet report that it no longer bothers them, and show no withdrawal, avoidance, or distress. I want my students to see how much philosophical weight this single syndrome has been asked to bear. One camp treats it as proof that the badness of pain resides in a distinctive phenomenal quality of unpleasantness, dissociable from pain's sensory dimension. The opposing camp treats it as proof that badness resides instead in aversion — in pain's being disliked, resisted, or motivationally shunned — so that what the asymbolic patient has lost is not an experience but a stance. Each side tends to write as though the case straightforwardly vindicates it, and the exchange has stalled.

Draft the joint statement that a committed phenomenal-quality theorist and a committed motivational-aversion theorist could both sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Structure it as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover, at minimum: the clinical profile both sides accept as datum; the point that ordinary intact pain is bad and that the dispute concerns what constitutes that badness, not whether it exists; the concession that asymbolia alone refutes neither view, since each view has an internally consistent description of what the patient has lost; and a shared standard for what would count as a fair test of the two positions rather than an intuition pump built for one side.

Second, a disagreements table in which the dispute is stated precisely enough that each party would endorse the wording of its own row. I expect at least: (i) a disagreement about what remains in the asymbolic's experience — whether a residue of negative hedonic tone persists but fails to move the patient, or whether the hedonic dimension is simply absent while sensory pain continues; (ii) a disagreement about constitution — whether the badness of ordinary pain is grounded in how it feels or in the subject's conative response to it; and (iii) a disagreement about what the lesion removes — an experience, a desire, or an evaluative representation — with each side committing to its answer.

Third, a cruxes section. For each crux, identify a case or finding both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. You should consider at least: the well-documented reports of patients on morphine who say their pain persists but no longer troubles them, and whether those reports pattern with asymbolia or diverge from it in ways that matter; the hypothetical intervention that restores an asymbolic patient's aversion while leaving their self-described experience untouched, and what each side predicts about whether the patient's condition would again be bad for them; and the converse hypothetical of a whole life in which the felt quality is preserved while the aversion is permanently abolished.

Fourth, a closing editorial note in your own voice. Do not split the difference. State which disagreements you judge genuine and which terminological — I suspect the word "unpleasant" is doing double duty, naming a felt quality for one side and a being-disliked for the other, and I want you to commit on whether that suspicion is correct and on what follows if it is. Commit to a single definite formulation of each crux rather than offering a menu of alternatives, and say plainly which one empirical result or thought experiment would, in your judgment, do the most to move the literature off its current impasse. Where your drafting leans on a contested reading of the clinical facts, flag it rather than smoothing it over.
