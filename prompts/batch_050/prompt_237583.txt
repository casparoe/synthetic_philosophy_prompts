I am a high school junior, and for our school's debate society I have been assigned the abolitionist side in a round on capital punishment. Our faculty advisor, instead of letting us rely on the usual statistics, gave us Albert Camus's 1957 essay Reflections on the Guillotine and asked us to anchor our case in it. One passage has stayed with me: "Capital punishment is the most premeditated of murders, to which no criminal's deed, however calculated, can be compared. For there to be an equivalency, the death penalty would have to punish a criminal who had warned his victim of the date on which he would inflict a horrible death on him and who, from that moment onward, had confined him at his mercy for months. Such a monster is not to be encountered in private life." While preparing, I came across the case of Caryl Chessman, executed in California in 1960, and I want to test Camus's passage against it. Here are the facts as I understand them: Chessman was convicted in 1948 as the so-called Red Light Bandit, for robberies and sexual assaults in which no one was killed, but which were prosecuted as kidnapping with bodily harm under a law that permitted a death sentence. He maintained his innocence throughout, taught himself law in prison, wrote four books, and spent nearly twelve years on death row, during which he received eight stays of execution, at least one of them arriving only hours before he was to enter the gas chamber. His case drew international attention and pleas for clemency from writers and public figures around the world before he was finally executed.

What I would like is an analysis that runs in both directions. First, use the passage to read the episode: map Camus's conditional equivalency onto Chessman's twelve years of confinement, his fixed and then repeatedly postponed execution dates, and the stays that arrived at the last moment, and say precisely where the case fits Camus's description and where it seems to exceed it, since Chessman was warned of the date more than once and then un-warned, which Camus's hypothetical monster never does. Second, let the episode push back on the passage: Camus builds the equivalence on the premise that the condemned man is guilty of some terrible deed, so what happens to the argument when, as here, the prisoner insists he is innocent, when no victim of his crimes was killed at all, and when the state is arguably punishing him partly for the embarrassment of his self-taught legal campaign? Does the case confirm Camus, expose a gap in the equivalency argument, or show that the argument was never really about the comparison to private murder but about something else, such as the state's claim to absolute and irreversible judgment?

I would also be grateful if you would end with a judgment I could actually use in the debate: whether this episode, analyzed this way, strengthens Camus's position, complicates it, or both.
