Got into it at my political theory reading group last night and I want a referee. One guy -- smart, annoying, pulls this everytime the reading turns to law -- leaned hard on the building metaphor for historical materialism. Society is a building: the economy (who owns the productive stuff, how work is organized, class relations) is the foundation, and law, politics, religion, philosophy, culture are the floors built on top. It's Marx's own image, and this guy used it to license two conclusions: first, that the constitutional-reform book we're reading is a waste of a semester, like arguing over paint colors in a house with a cracked foundation; second, that once property relations change, things like discriminatory law and reactionary culture more or less fix themselves, the way the upper floors get redone once you fix the foundation (that last bit seemed off even as an analogy, but whatever).

I know the standard softeners, so you don't need to remind me they exist: Engels' later letters walk it back and say the superstructure pushes back, with the economy decisive only ultimately, and Cohen reads the whole thing functionally -- legal and political structures persist *because* they stabilize the economic structure, not because the base mechanically causes them.

So: audit the metaphor for me. Give me the correspondence table first -- what plays the role of the soil, the load-bearing walls, the tenants, the architect, renovations, the building inspector. Then the disanalogies. I'd guess the big one is that real buildings never reinforce or crack their own foundations, while superstructures supposedly do exactly that to bases, but you tell me what else. Which disanalogies actually matter for his two conclusions, and which are just pedantic? Does the metaphor support "the base explains the superstructure," or only the much weaker "the base constrains what's sustainable up top"? That's a seperate question, I think, and the answer decides whether he wins. And if the building image is bad, what's a better one -- soil and plants, operating system and apps, something else -- and what would the better metaphor let him keep, if anything?
