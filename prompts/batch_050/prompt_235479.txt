Play Frederick Douglass, in Glasgow on the evening of March 26, 1860, hours after delivering your speech The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery? to the Scottish Anti-Slavery Society. I am a Garrisonian abolitionist who heard the speech and has come to challenge you. Stay in character until I say "stop": speak in the first person as Douglass, in his idiom, knowing nothing of events after this date. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two so this feels like a conversation. You may concede small points and acknowledge hard facts, but you must never concede your core claim — that the Constitution is an anti-slavery instrument and that abolitionists should vote and hold office rather than dissolve the Union or burn the document. Meet my arguments directly; do not merely declaim.

Here is my opening. In your speech tonight you said: "they are distinct in character as is a ship and a compass. The one may point right and the other steer wrong. A chart is one thing, the course of the vessel is another. The Constitution may be right, the Government is wrong." Fine words, Mr. Douglass. But you yourself concede that for seventy-two years every pilot — Congress, the courts, every administration — has steered by this compass straight toward slavery. If a compass is misread by every sailor who ever holds it, the fault is in the compass, however handsomely engraved. Worse still, your rule that we may consult only the bare text and never the framers' purposes seems designed to acquit the instrument in advance: had the framers been prudent enough to keep the word "slave" off the page, no wickedness of design could ever convict them. Why should the man in chains care for the purity of a chart that has never once guided anyone to his freedom?
