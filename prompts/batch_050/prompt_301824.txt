So Kavka's toxin puzzle: a billionaire will pay me a million dollars if, at midnight tonight, I genuinely intend to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon that will make me horribly sick for a day (not lethal, just awful). The payout depends only on the midnight intention -- once I've formed it and been paid, I'm completely free not to drink, and I know this in advance. Here are my verdicts on a batch of variants, and I want to know what principle, if any, sits underneath them:

1. Original case: I can't get the money. Since I know tomorrow-me will have no reason to drink, I can't sincerely intend it tonight, however much I want the million.
2. But if a gunman credibly says he'll kill my family tomorrow unless I actually drink the toxin, I can intend to drink tonight with no difficulty at all.
3. Weirdly, if the gunman instead says he'll kill my family unless a brain scanner confirms I *intend* tonight to drink (and again, nothing forces me to follow through tomorrow), I'm back to being unable to do it -- even though the stakes are identical to case 2.
4. If I'm a notoriously stubborn person and I tell an enemy I'll drink the toxin if he attacks me, as a deterrent, that intention seems genuinely formable in advance.
5. And if I can't intend directly but could pay a hypnotist tonight to install the intention, that indirect route seems perfectly available, which feels inconsistent with verdict 1.

What principle separates the intentions I can just form from the ones I can't? Is it about having reasons to intend versus reasons to act, or about what I'll know at the moment of action, or something else? Case 2 versus 3 especially bug me. If no single principle saves all five, tell me which verdicts have to go and why.
