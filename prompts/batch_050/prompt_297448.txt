Last week a friend and I argued late into the night over whether self-deception is even possible. She held that it is not: to deceive oneself, one would have to believe a proposition and its negation at once, and what we call self-deception is really just motivated carelessness. I held that it is the most ordinary thing in the world. The argument has become uncomfortably relevant, because I chair my university's research integrity panel, and we must rule next week on a case that turns on the question.

The respondent, an associate professor of psychology, excluded outlying observations across three published papers, in each instance just enough to push the result across the significance threshold. She does not contest the record. Her defence is that at the time she sincerely judged every exclusion warranted — that she had, as she puts it, managed to fool herself. Our investigator's report notes that the pattern is too systematic for coincidence but cannot identify any moment of conscious decision. Our sanction guidelines recognize two categories: knowing misconduct, which carries dismissal, and reckless negligence, which carries retraction and supervised practice. Half the panel reads the case as knowing misconduct, on the grounds that an expert could not have failed to see what she was doing; the other half wants to classify it as self-deception and place it below recklessness, since someone who genuinely did not know seems less culpable than someone who merely failed to look.

Draft the panel's decision memo. It should state which of the two guideline categories the conduct falls under — or argue that self-deception is a genuine third thing requiring its own treatment — recommend a concrete sanction, and take seriously the strongest argument of whichever side loses: in particular, either my friend's impossibility argument, or the worry that a respondent who frankly admits fault would be punished more harshly than one who claims to have fooled herself, which appears to reward the more successful deceiver.
