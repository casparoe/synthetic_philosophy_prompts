In the mid-1980s, the German Historikerstreit erupted when Ernst Nolte argued that Nazi mass murder should be understood as standing in a causal sequence after, and partly in response to, Bolshevik terror, and Jürgen Habermas accused him of apologetically relativizing the Holocaust. Nearly forty years later, the same dispute keeps reassembling itself in new costumes: the "double genocide" debates in post-communist Eastern Europe, the controversies over whether colonial massacres belong in the same frame as the Shoah, the 2020 affair in which Achille Mbembe lost a German festival invitation over comparisons between Israeli policy and apartheid, and the endless online skirmishes over which analogies to Nazi crimes are legitimate and which are obscene. What strikes me is that all the moves are by now common property. One side insists that comparison is not equation and that uniqueness claims function to exempt other victims from recognition; the other insists that contextualization slides into normalization and that certain crimes lose their warning function when absorbed into a general ledger of atrocity. Everyone knows both scripts. Nobody has moved in decades.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really a historical disagreement — nobody in these disputes is actually quarreling over archival facts — but a disagreement about what kind of act a comparison is. One side treats comparison as a cognitive operation with truth conditions: an analogy is apt or inapt, and its political afterlife is a separate question. The other treats comparison as a speech act whose identity is fixed by its foreseeable rhetorical effects, so that a historically defensible analogy can still be the wrong thing to say. If both sides are answering different questions under one heading, the stall is guaranteed. But I may be wrong, and I'd like the diagnosis tested rather than assumed.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it empirical (disputed degrees of structural similarity), conceptual (no shared account of what "compare" and "unique" even mean), moral (conflicting duties to different dead), or political (a contest over the allocation of institutional memory and recognition, with historiography as proxy)? Engage what each side actually argues, not caricatures. Then consider a distinction I have not seen drawn in these debates, between forensic comparison — ranking events against each other to allocate blame, guilt, or reparative priority — and constitutive comparison — using one event as the interpretive lens through which another is understood at all. Does the dispute dissolve once these are separated, or do both sides have reasons to resist the separation?

Finally, given your diagnosis: what would count as progress? A taxonomy of comparison types with different norms for each? A criterion of analogical aptness? Or should we conclude that the debate is a permanent proxy war over recognition, and that conceptual progress is neither possible nor the point?
