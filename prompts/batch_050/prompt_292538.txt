I coordinate a professional-development workshop series for mid-career engineers who have recently been passed over for promotion or had long projects cancelled, and the theme for the next session is the modern revival of Stoicism and whether its central discipline of desire can survive contact with genuine career disappointment. I would like to anchor the session in a passage I have chosen precisely because it is not one of the anthology favorites. In Book IV, chapter 1 of the Meditations, Marcus Aurelius writes (in A. S. L. Farquharson's translation): "The sovereign power within, in its natural state, so confronts what comes to pass as always to adapt itself readily to what is feasible and is presented to it. This is because it puts its affection upon no material of its own choice; rather it sets itself upon its objects with a reservation, and then makes the opposition which encounters it into material for itself. It is like a fire, when it masters what falls into it, whereby a little taper would have been put out, but a bright fire very quickly appropriates and devours what is heaped upon it, and leaps up higher out of those very obstacles."

Please describe what you take to be the most important implications of this passage for how I should coach my participants to apply the dichotomy of control in practice. In particular, I would like your response to engage three points of tension. First, the passage seems to insist on pursuing one's objects only "with a reservation" (the Stoics' reserve clause): what concretely distinguishes pursuing a promotion with reservation from pursuing it half-heartedly, and how should I respond to participants who suspect the reservation is just a preemptive hedge against disappointment that quietly drains motivation? Second, the fire metaphor suggests that obstacles are not merely tolerated but positively appropriated as fuel; does this imply that a good Stoic should in some sense welcome setbacks, and if so, how does that sit with the doctrine that externals are genuinely indifferent rather than disguised goods — or is the metaphor better read as being about the conversion of material that one would still prefer not to receive? Third, critics of contemporary popular Stoicism charge that it degenerates into a reframing technique that smuggles outcomes back in through the back door (I failed, but the failure was useful, so really I succeeded); does this passage, taken seriously, provide the resources to resist that charge, and how should I present the difference between appropriating an obstacle and merely rationalizing a defeat?
