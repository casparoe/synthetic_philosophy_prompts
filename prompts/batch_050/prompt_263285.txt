A colleague of mine—an economic theorist who has spent her career on the socialist calculation debate—has a standing exercise: describe to her any actual or proposed economic arrangement, and she will tell you whether it "has an answer" to Mises and Hayek, and if so, whose. Her verdicts so far:

- Soviet War Communism, with its moneyless, in-kind allocation of goods across an entire national economy: no answer. There were no prices anywhere in the system, and one cannot steer a large economy by bookkeeping in tons and kilowatt-hours.
- British economic planning during the Second World War: an answer, but a borrowed and perishable one. The planners could coast on the price ratios the pre-war market had left behind, the way one can navigate for a while on an old map; the longer the planning ran, the staler the map.
- Transfer pricing inside a large, vertically integrated corporation, where intermediate goods move between divisions without being bought and sold: an answer. The firm is an island of conscious planning, but it is surrounded by markets, and it can consult external prices at every edge of the island.
- A self-sufficient commune of two hundred people with no internal money: an answer. At that scale the planners and the planned are the same people, and the relevant knowledge fits inside a few heads around one table.
- Lange's market socialism, in which a central board posts prices for capital goods and adjusts them by trial and error in response to shortages and surpluses: no answer. In her view, the board's prices are decrees rather than the outcome of owners staking their own property on rival judgments, and even if the arithmetic converged, nothing in the scheme forces the shop-floor knowledge of plant managers into the numbers.

Here is the case I intend to put to her next. An archipelago federation of some sixty thousand people abolishes money internally and allocates labor, investment, and output through elected planning councils. It is not autarkic: it trades with the outside world, and its councils consult world market prices for everything traded internationally. Internally, however, no one buys or sells anything, and the councils rely on the external price lists, engineering data, and their own deliberations.

Two requests. First, predict her verdict, and say which argument she is implicitly running—the Misesian one, on which the archipelago has an answer because genuine market prices for the means of production exist somewhere and can be borrowed, or the Hayekian one, on which sixty thousand people is far past the commune's kitchen table, and external price lists report the scarcity of traded goods without transmitting the local, tacit knowledge of the islanders themselves. Note that all five earlier verdicts are consistent with either rule, so explain which considerations break the tie. Second, identify which of her five verdicts is the closest analog to the new case, and describe the further hypothetical case that would settle which of the two rules she actually holds.
