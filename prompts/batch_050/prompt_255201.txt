I am a novelist at work on a book whose plot turns on a version of the old Damascus story: my protagonist, an actuary, receives from a source she has overwhelming reason to trust a prediction about where and when she will die, and every precaution she takes is one the prediction has already accounted for. In drafting the middle chapters I discovered I could not write her deliberations convincingly, because I realized I do not actually know what the *rational* response to a reliable prediction of one's own choice is supposed to be. The philosophers I have asked tell me this case sits at the fault line between evidential and causal decision theory, and that it is contested territory. Before I commit to a rendering of her reasoning, I want the specification, not a verdict: what must an adequate theory of rational choice deliver in cases where the agent's own decision is reliably foreseen or foretold, and where the content of the prediction is about the very act of deciding?

Please write the desiderata list. For each requirement, give the case or intuition that motivates it: for instance, must the theory distinguish a merely statistical correlation between choice and outcome from a genuine causal leash? Must it make room for the agent to reason *from* the prediction without treating it as an excuse to stop deliberating, or is deliberation under perfect foreknowledge simply incoherent, in which case the theory should say so? Must it say something unified about Damascus-style cases, Newcomb-style cases, and cases where the predictor is imperfect, or is a theory permitted to fragment them? I would also like to know which desiderata strike you as genuine constraints any theory must meet and which are merely the housekeeping of one camp -- the way causalists and evidentialists each tend to build their favored verdicts into the entry requirements. If some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, that is the part I most want to see, since an impossibility result would itself give me the shape of my protagonist's tragedy.

Two practical constraints. First, do not cite particular papers or books; I will track down the literature afterward and only need the positions described well enough to recognize them. Second, close with a short paragraph addressed to me as a novelist rather than as a philosopher: given your desiderata, which rendering of the actuary's reasoning -- fatalistic collapse, defiant two-boxing-style independence, or some third posture -- would a careful decision theorist find least naive, and which rendering would be most dramatically interesting even if theoretically suspect? I am willing to sacrifice a little correctness for a good scene, but I want to know exactly how much I am sacrificing.
