I'm presenting my "position statement" at a welfare economics / political philosophy reading group next month and I want it stress tested before I have to defend it in person. Here's roughly what I believe:

1. Expected utility theory is the right account of individual rationality under risk, and collective evaluation should be held to the same standard. A social preference ordering over lotteries that violates the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms (completeness, transitivity, continuity, independence) is just incoherent, the same way an individual with those preferences would be.

2. The Pareto principle is close to sacred for me: if a policy leaves everyone at least as well off ex ante, in prospect, and at least one person strictly better off, then society should prefer it. I don't think there are interesting counterexamples to this.

3. Since I accept 1 and 2, I take Harsanyi's aggregation theorem to more or less force the conclusion that social welfare is a weighted sum of individual utilities. So I'm a utilitarian at least in the thin formal sense, whatever my gut says, and I don't buy the line that the theorem is "just a representation result" with no normative teeth -- if the premises are compelling and the conclusion follows mathematically, I don't see what's left to complain about.

4. That said, my gut is prioritarian. I accept something like the Pigou-Dalton principle at the level of well-being itself: if you could costlessly move a unit of welfare from someone doing well to someone doing badly, leaving the total exactly unchanged, that's a strict improvement, full stop.

5. In Diamond's case -- one kidney, two compatible patients, where Alice would get somewhat more out of the transplant than Bob -- I'd flip the fair coin rather than just hand it to Alice. Equal chances have value that expected totals don't capture, and I suspect whatever principle justifies the coin flip here will generalize to a lot of other cases.

6. I'm also unbothered by the seperability built into 3: when we compare policies that differ only in how they affect some subset of people, how the unaffected people are doing shouldn't matter to the comparison.

Do these hang together? For each pair that's in genuine tension, tell me how bad it is and what the cheapest fix would be -- and flag any pairs that look like they clash but actually don't, because I have a feeling at least one of these is a fake conflict. If some fix only works by quietly reinterpreting one of my commitments into something weaker than what I wrote, say so explicitly rather than letting it slide. And at the end: which single revision cleans up the most, and what does it cost me?
