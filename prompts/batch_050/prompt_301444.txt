So I TA a masters-level course in decision theory for public policy students, and for one seminar I have to teach Keynes's treatment of probability in the Treatise on Probability -- specifically his claim that probabilities aren't always numerical or even comparable. In chapter 3 he gives an example along the lines of: the chance that a coin comes up heads, and the chance that it will rain tomorrow given what the barometer says, can't necessarily be lined up on a single scale, so saying one is "more probable" than the other may be meaningless rather than merely hard to determine. I want the seminar to turn on whether that example actually works, because my students arrive fluent in Bayesian updating and will instinctively say you can always assign a number if you price bets right.

Plan the seminar for me, don't just explain Keynes at me. Concretely:

- The order of ideas. What has to be in place before the non-comparability claim is even intelligible to them? I'm guessing they need the idea of probability as a relation between evidence and conclusion rather than a property of the world, but maybe something has to come before that too. Tell me the dependency chain and why each step needs its predecessor.
- The carrying example. The coin-and-barometer pair is abstract; give me one concrete, modern case (a policy-relevant one would be great) that plays the same role -- where the students will feel the pull of assigning a number AND the pull of resisting it. Say what the example needs to have and what it must not have (e.g., if both sides of the case are about repeatable events, theyll just reach for frequencies and the lesson dies).
- The misconceptions, in the order they'll form them. I expect: (a) "unmeasurable" just means we lack data, so this is a practical not a conceptual point; (b) non-comparability is the same as a 50/50 credence; (c) Keynes is really talking about Knightian risk vs uncertainty, which they half-remember from an undergrad lecture. For each, tell me where in the sequence to head it off and how, including whether (c) is even a misconception or a conflation I should let stand for now.
- The passage as a stress test. Give me two or three questions to pose about the barometer example itself -- e.g., does Keynes need the stronger claim that NO ordering exists, or only that we can't know it? Can a Bayesian absorb the example by treating it as an interval-valued or imprecise credence, and if so does Keynes lose anything he cared about? I want the students to leave unsure whether Keynes wins, not sure that he does.
- One closing check for understanding: a single prompt I can write on the board where the right response shows they got the distinction rather than memorized the vocab.
- What to openly refuse to cover in 90 minutes (weight of evidence? the logical-interpretation baggage?) and how to say we're skipping it without making it sound like a dodge.

Ninety minutes, about fifteen students, whiteboard, no readings assigned beforehand. Be opinionated about the sequence -- if you think I'm wrong that the relational idea comes first, say so and reorder it.
