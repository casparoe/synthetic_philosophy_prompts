I teach a 200-level ethics seminar, about 14 students, one 75-minute session. Design an activity that makes them feel the force of Bernard Williams's one thought too many objection -- the idea that in some cases (famously, rescuing your spouse rather than a stranger when you can only save one) the problem isn't the answer but the fact that a justification had to run through your deliberation at all. Every time I've tried to teach this with the drowning case alone, half the room just says obviously save your spouse, where's the dilemma, and the interesting part -- that even the right rescuer can be alienated from their own motive if the thought "and this is permissible because..." had to do work -- goes completely flat.

So I want something staged, with real (small) stakes and time pressure, where participants actually catch themselves or each other reaching for the justificatory thought and feel what's weird about it, before anyone names Williams. Maybe structured rounds with different roles and a version where the facilitator quietly manipulates whether deliberation time is available. Include: the setup and rules, a timeline, what the debrief should map each moment onto, and one genuinely hard bit to get across -- the distinction between a justification existing in the background versus it being the thing that moved you, which is where students always get stuck. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (I suspect someone will treat it as a trolley problem and try to count lives) and which feature of the objection the activity just can't reproduce and I'll have to supply by talking.
