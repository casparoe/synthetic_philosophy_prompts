For a philosophy of mind seminar handout, write a decision tree that takes students from an agreed starting point — that in the standard Otto-and-the-notebook case, the notebook is as functionally integrated into Otto's behavior as biological memory is into Inga's — to a named position on where cognition ends. Build the tree from the logical space of the debate rather than from a roster of authors: each internal node should be a single diagnostic question, phrased so that a student can answer it without knowing which camp the answer lands them in, and each leaf a position located by its combination of answers. The nodes should cover at least: whether functional parity between inner and outer resources is sufficient for parity of cognitive status, or whether there is a further "mark of the cognitive" (such as non-derived content) that notebook entries lack; whether moving from causal coupling to constitution is a fallacy or a fair diagnosis of how cognition actually works; whether the mind should be individuated by persisting internal machinery rather than by transient coupled systems; and whether the threat of cognitive bloat is a principled objection or merely an affront to intuition. The leaves must include Clark and Chalmers's active externalism, Adams and Aizawa's content-based internalism, a Rupert-style position on which cognition is embedded but remains inside the skull, and a scaffolded-mind view. For every combination of answers that no named position occupies, add a leaf marked unoccupied and say briefly whether the gap looks incoherent, unmotivated, or simply unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves — for instance, active externalism and a Menary-style cognitive integration view — that your tree cannot cleanly separate, and say what extra question would be needed to split them.
