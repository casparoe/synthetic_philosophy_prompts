A colleague of mine has strong and remarkably stable intuitions about who deserves what in hiring, grading, and pay. I have been collecting her verdicts, and I now want to predict her judgment on a case she has not yet considered. Her record so far:

1. Two interns of roughly equal natural ability competed for a single job offer. One worked late most nights and mastered the relevant tools; the other coasted. She says the first clearly deserved the offer.

2. Two analysts put in genuinely equal effort, but one is simply quicker and produces twice as much useful work. She says the quicker analyst deserves higher pay, and that this is not unfair to the slower one.

3. Two employees delivered reports of identical quality. One reached hers through disciplined expertise; the other stumbled onto a shortcut that happened to work and could easily have failed. She says the expert deserves more praise and more trust with future projects, though she hesitates about whether their pay for this particular deliverable should differ.

4. A worker with a chronic illness outworks everyone in the building but, through no fault of his own, produces below-average output. She says he deserves great admiration and generous accommodation, but not the highest salary, and she sees no tension in holding those together.

5. A contractor delivered genuinely excellent work but was discovered to have padded his hours in billing. She says he fully deserved payment for the work itself but deserved to lose all future contracts, and that these two verdicts do not pull against each other.

Now the new case. Two mid-level employees, matched in talent and in effort, each spend a year pursuing major clients. One lands a transformative account — largely because the client's longtime contact at a rival firm retired at just the right moment, leaving the field open. The other, through no deficiency of her own, lands nothing comparable. The firm must decide whether the first employee deserves a substantially larger bonus than the second.

What will my colleague say, and what should she say if she wants to be consistent with her own record? In answering, please identify the distinct principles that each fit all five prior verdicts — for example, desert as tracking actual contribution, desert as tracking effort and skill with results serving merely as a proxy, or a pluralism on which different goods (pay, praise, opportunities) track different desert bases — and show where at least two of these principles diverge on the bonus case. Give your best prediction with reasons, name which prior verdict is the closest analog and why it nonetheless underdetermines the answer, and state what principle she would be committing herself to under each possible verdict. Finally, describe one further test case that would most efficiently discriminate between the surviving principles.
