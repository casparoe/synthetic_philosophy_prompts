Amartya Sen's liberal paradox is usually stated as a proof that no collective decision procedure can satisfy both the Pareto principle and even a minimal form of individual rights. What I can't get straight is the practical consequence. Economists advising liberal governments routinely recommend Pareto improvements, and those same governments treat certain personal choices as protected against majority preference. On Sen's result, one of these practices has to give: either a consistent liberal must accept that some mutually beneficial changes ought to be blocked, or welfare economists are quietly ignoring a theorem from their own field every time they recommend an efficiency gain. Which is it? And if the answer is that the two practices coexist without contradiction because the theorem doesn't actually bear on them, then what work is the theorem doing -- what would have to change about a society's institutions or its members' preferences for the impossibility to bite in practice rather than only on paper?
