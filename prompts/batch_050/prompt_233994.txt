I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether situationist psychology (the finding that whether people help, lie, etc. swings with trivial stuff like whether they just found a coin or whether theres a nice smell nearby) actually touches Aristotle, or only a cartoon of him. The fight kept coming back to this passage from Book II of the Nicomachean Ethics (Rackham translation), specifically the third condition:

"acts done in conformity with the virtues are not done justly or temperately if they themselves are of a certain sort, but only if the agent also is in a certain state of mind when he does them: first he must act with knowledge; secondly he must deliberately choose the act, and choose it for its own sake; and thirdly the act must spring from a fixed and permanent disposition of character."

Here's what I want you to do. Construct a thought experiment that puts maximal pressure on that third condition while leaving the first two completely untouched. Specs:

- One paragraph, contemporary and realistic: an office, a neighbourhood, a family. No psych-lab recreations, no hypothetical drugs, no brain science fiction, and the character should be an ordinary decent person, not a saint or a monster.
- It must be uncontroversial in the story that the person knows what they're doing and deliberately chooses the right act for its own sake (no prompting, no fear of punishment, no reputational payoff).
- The case has to be built so that the verdict on whether the person counts as having the virtue hinges entirely on how you read "fixed and permanent disposition of character." That is: on one defensible reading of that phrase the person clearly has the virtue, on another defensible reading they clearly don't, and the first two conditions shouldn't be what tips it either way.

Then show the case works: walk through both readings of the third condition, state the verdict each delivers on your character, and identify which single feature of the scenario is doing the work (and what happens to the case if you take that feature out).

After that I want you to actually commit, not just lay out options. Two verdicts, with reasons: (1) which reading of "fixed and permanent disposition" is the better interpretation of the passage as it stands, and (2) given that reading, whether the situationist results land on Aristotle himself or only on a weaker straw version of virtue ethics. If your honest answer to (2) is "it depends on empirical facts the thought experiment can't settle," say so, but still pick the side you think the evidence we have favors.
