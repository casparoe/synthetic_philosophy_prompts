Okay so I keep running into this thing and I want to know if philosophy has a name for it, because "guilt" and "regret" both seem wrong.

Two cases from actual life. First: a guy in my building was driving home, going under the speed limit, totally sober, and a cyclist swerved out from between parked cars right in front of him. Cyclist died. The investigation cleared him completely -- no negligence, nothing he could have done, everyone agrees including the dead guy's family. But here's the thing: he sold his car. He goes to see the family every few months. He contributes to some fund they set up. And nobody -- not him, not the family, not me -- experiences this as him being generous. If I gave money to that fund it would be charity; from him it's somehow owed. Second: my sister's a surgeon, lost a patient to a freak reaction nobody could have predicted, review board found zero error. Her attending told her "you did nothing wrong" and she said she knows that, but she still went to the widow and apologized, and the widow's anger at her specifically (not at the hospital, not at fate) struck everyone as somehow intelligible even though, pressed, nobody will say she DID anything.

So the pattern: someone causes a harm with literally no fault, and they end up in this weird normative zone -- expected to atone, legitimately a target of some kind of bad feeling, treating themselves as indebted -- while a bystander who knows all the same facts is in no such zone. And it's not sadness. Sad is what the bystander feels.

I found a line in Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments (Part II, Section I, the part on merit and demerit) that I think is pointing at exactly this. He's talking about how even a man consumed with hatred toward someone couldn't bear to cause that person's death himself, and he says:

"...it would hurt him excessively to have been himself, even without design, the occasion of this misfortune."

"Even without design" -- so Smith sees that mere causal authorship, absent any fault, does something to the causer. But he just notes it and moves on.

Is there an established term for this whole configuration? Not just for the feeling the causer has (that might be one name) but for the position: faultless causer of harm who owes something anyway, and whom the victim's side may properly resent-ish. My roommate took an ethics class and says this is basically moral luck, but from how she explained it moral luck is about outcomes making blameworthy conduct worse -- like two equally reckless drivers where only one hits somebody. Here there's no reckless conduct at all, and the interesting part isn't blame anyway, it's the obligation and the social expectation. So I suspect it's a different thing, or at most a cousin.

Please don't just hand me a label though. What I want: the nearest established concepts, how this thing differs from each, and which fits best (or whether nothing does and I should coin something). And where are the edges? Does it still apply when the harm is to property, not a person? When the causer is a company rather than an individual? Does the victim instantly forgiving the causer dissolve it, or is the debt still there? Where does it shade into regular guilt on one side and mere sympathy on the other?
