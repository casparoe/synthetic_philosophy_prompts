Ok so I'm putting together the discussion question for my jurisprudence seminar next week (we're mid-way through the rule of law unit — the students have done Fuller, Raz, and the Waldron piece) and I want something better than the usual "assess Fuller's internal morality" snoozefest. Put on your legal-philosopher hat for this one and actually think carefully rather than giving me the textbook summary. Maybe brainstorm a bit before committing to an answer.

Here's the setup. Take Fuller's eight desiderata of legality from The Morality of Law — generality, promulgation, prospectivity (no retroactive law), clarity, non-contradiction, possibility of compliance, constancy through time, and congruence between official action and the declared rules. Fuller's famous wager, roughly put, is that these aren't just a checklist for good law but partly constitutive of law at all: fail totally enough in any one direction and you don't get bad law, you get something that isn't a legal system.

I want you to stress-test that wager with a ranking exercise, but on a specific dimension I've cooked up, which I'll call the guidance metric. The idea: the deepest point of the eight principles, on one reading of Fuller, is that law is supposed to give subjects a basis for planning their own conduct — you can't guide yourself by rules you can't know, can't understand, can't satisfy, or that officials ignore anyway. So:

1. Sort all eight desiderata into a tier list ranked by how much damage a catastrophic-but-localized failure of that desideratum does to law's guidance function, assuming the other seven are in decent shape. So: a system that is perfectly clear, public, prospective etc. but where officials routinely disregard their own published rules (congruence failure) vs. a system identical except that the rules constantly change (constancy failure), and so on through all eight. One short justification per placement — no essays, just the crux of why it lands where it does.

2. Then the interesting part: tell me where your ranking is least stable — i.e., which two or three desiderata could plausibly swap tiers under a defensible shift in background assumptions (say, if you weight planning by ordinary citizens vs. planning by officials, or if you assume a system with strong courts vs. one without), and what that instability shows about whether Fuller's eight are really one idea or a bundle of several.

3. Finally, bring in the dissenters briefly: does Raz's line of objection — that legality is a virtue of the instrument, like sharpness in a knife, and tells you nothing about what the knife is used for — suggest that my guidance metric is smuggling in moral content Fuller never earned? And does Waldron's complaint that the list oddly ignores courts, hearings, and the opportunity to argue mean the list is missing a ninth desideratum that should have ranked near the top on my metric?

Don't just regurgitate the standard Fuller-Hart debate stuff, I've graded that essay a hundred times. I want the ranking to be genuinely yours and the justification to track the guidance metric specifically, not legality-in-general. If you think the whole metric is a bad way to read Fuller, you can say so, but you have to rank first and complain after.
