I'm setting the final examination for a graduate seminar in social choice and normative economics, and I need one essay question on Sen's liberal paradox (the impossibility of a Paretian liberal). The question must be answerable in 75 minutes and must resist being answered by reciting the textbook statement of the theorem and Sen's own illustrative example. I want the question to test three things.

First, whether students can reproduce the *structure* of the impossibility rather than its letter: they should be required to construct the preference cycle in a scenario of their own design, using the three conditions (unrestricted domain, the weak Pareto principle, and minimal liberalism understood as at least two individuals each decisive over at least one pair of alternatives within their personal sphere), and to state explicitly which condition does the work at each step of the cycle.

Second, whether they can adjudicate among competing diagnoses of where the trouble actually lies: (a) that the paradox is a defect in how rights are modeled, since rights attach to actions or strategies rather than to social states, so that the right framework is choice over game forms rather than choice over outcomes; (b) that the paradox indicts the Pareto principle's reliance on purely preference-based evaluation of outcomes, and so points beyond welfarism rather than beyond liberalism; (c) that the paradox arises only because of "meddlesome" preferences, so that a restriction on admissible preference profiles dissolves it. Students should have to argue for one diagnosis and explain what the other two get right and wrong — in particular, whether a domain restriction solves the paradox or merely fences off the cases in which it bites.

Third, whether they can carry the theorem into unfamiliar territory. The question should present a short novel scenario: a content-recommendation platform whose ranking function aggregates users' reported preferences, where each user has a personal sphere (settings of their own feed) but also holds preferences about what appears in other users' feeds. Students should be asked whether the impossibility applies to the ranking algorithm; if they say it does not apply, they must identify precisely which condition fails and argue whether that failure is a genuine escape or merely a relabeling of the conflict.

Please deliver: (1) the question exactly as it would appear on the exam paper, including any scenario text, phrased as a single integrated prompt; (2) a model answer of roughly 700 words; and (3) a marking scheme totaling 30 marks, built around six must-hit points. For each point, give the marks it is worth, a one-line description of the specific move a top answer must make, and a one-line note on the mistake candidates most commonly make instead — for example, presenting the cycle as a failure of individual preference transitivity rather than of social preference, asserting that restricting meddlesome preferences "solves" the result without noting that this only narrows its scope, or claiming the recommender escapes the theorem simply because an algorithm is not a society.
