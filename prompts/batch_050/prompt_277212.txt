I'd like your help as an expert in the philosophy of legal evidence — someone who can move comfortably between doctrine, epistemology, and probability theory — with a case I've been turning over in my head since I read a long magazine piece about it. I have no academic background in law or statistics, so please be patient with my framing, but I want rigor in the answer. Take your time, think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the different angles before settling into a structured analysis. The case is that of Sally Clark, the British solicitor whose two infant sons died in their cots in the late 1990s, in separate incidents about a year apart. At her 1999 trial, the pediatrician Sir Roy Meadow testified that the probability of two natural cot deaths occurring in a single affluent, non-smoking family was roughly one in 73 million. That number did enormous work: she was convicted of murdering both children, lost her first appeal, and was only freed in 2003 when it emerged that pathological evidence pointing to a natural infection in the second child had been withheld, and when statisticians had publicly demolished Meadow's figure. I know the broad facts; what I want is the philosophical anatomy.

Here is the lens I want applied. In the literature on so-called naked statistical evidence, there are two famous thought experiments I've seen described: in the blue bus case, a driver is run off the road by a bus at night, and the only evidence against the Blue Bus Company is that it operates the large majority of buses on that route; in the gatecrasher case, the organizers of a rodeo or concert sue an attendee, and the only evidence is that most people present that day climbed the fence without paying, so it is statistically likely this attendee did too. Courts and theorists tend to say such purely statistical proof, however strong numerically, cannot by itself ground a finding of liability or guilt. Now run the mapping in both directions. First, reconstruct exactly what Meadow's one-in-73-million was the probability of, and distinguish the two quite different errors it involved: the assumption of independence between the two deaths (which licensed squaring the figure for one cot death), and the prosecutor's fallacy of sliding from the probability of the evidence given innocence to the probability of innocence given the evidence. Second, place the case against the blue bus and gatecrasher paradigm: was Meadow's statistic naked evidence in the relevant sense, or something worse — dressed-up evidence, since it arrived wearing the authority of expert medical testimony rather than as an acknowledged base rate? Does the standard doctrine against naked statistics, designed for cases where the number is openly all there is, even get a grip here, or does the case expose a blind spot in it?

Third, and this is where I most want your judgment: what does the episode show about the doctrine itself? One reading says Sally Clark vindicates the suspicion of naked statistics — look what happens when a bare number carries a jury. Another says it shows the suspicion is misplaced, because the catastrophe came not from the evidence being statistical but from the statistics being incompetent and the inference fallacious; a correct Bayesian treatment, properly combining the base rates with the rest of the evidence, might have been the most honest way to think about her guilt at all. Tell me which reading you find more defensible, and what, in a case like hers, would have had to be true for statistical evidence to be legitimately non-naked — what kind of individualized, case-specific fact would have had to join the number before the number could properly contribute to proof beyond reasonable doubt. Where the theory is simply silent on any of this, say so plainly rather than forcing it to speak.
