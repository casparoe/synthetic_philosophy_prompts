Last night a friend and I got into a long, unresolved argument about why anyone willingly sits through sad films and bleak novels. Her position was the distancing one: we can enjoy tragic art precisely because some part of us never stops registering that it is not real, and that background awareness takes the sting out of the distress and leaves behind something pleasurable. I left the argument unconvinced, and I would like an essay that works the dispute out properly rather than just restating both sides.

Her view has a venerable pedigree. In his essay "Of Tragedy," David Hume presents Bernard le Bovier de Fontenelle's version of it as follows:

"there still lurks at the bottom a certain idea of falsehood in the whole of what we see. This idea, though weak and disguised, suffices to diminish the pain which we suffer from the misfortunes of those whom we love, and to reduce that affliction to such a pitch as converts it into a pleasure. We weep for the misfortune of a hero, to whom we are attached. In the same instant we comfort ourselves, by reflecting, that it is nothing but a fiction: And it is precisely that mixture of sentiments, which composes an agreeable sorrow, and tears that delight us."

Write an essay of roughly 900 to 1,200 words (the passage above does not count toward the length) assessing whether this account solves the puzzle of why we enjoy tragic art. The essay should do the following:

First, state the theory in the passage as precisely as you can: what exactly the "idea of falsehood" is supposed to do, whether the resulting pleasure is a distinct ingredient mixed with the pain or the very same pain weakened, and what the view predicts about how our enjoyment should vary with how convincing or immersive a work is.

Second, test it. At minimum, address these pressure points: that Hume himself, immediately after calling this solution just and convincing, says it needs some further addition to fully explain the phenomenon, so the essay should say what gap he might have had in mind; that we are often more pleased by tragedies the more they afflict us, whereas the passage seems to predict that maximal immersion should tip the balance back toward pure pain; and that audiences have historically preferred the devastating ending of King Lear to the happy-ending revision, which is hard to explain if the fiction-reflection is doing all the comforting. Consider also whether the view explains why we do not take the same pleasure in comparable real suffering, since it is designed to do exactly that.

Third, weigh at least one rival account against it — for instance the arousal-based idea Hume attributes to Dubos, that any strong passion is preferable to languid indolence, or a catharsis-style view on which the painful emotions are purged or relieved — and say where each does better and worse than the theory in the passage.

Finally, reach a verdict and defend it. You may conclude that the fiction-softening account is essentially right with amendments, that it explains a real phenomenon but not the paradox itself, or that it fails outright; but do not end by merely listing pros and cons. Say which side of my argument with my friend you would take, and give the single consideration you find decisive.
