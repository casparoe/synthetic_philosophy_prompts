I am a PhD student in moral philosophy, and my dissertation touches on the badness of human extinction. I have been informally polling my supervisor on thought experiments, and I have noticed that her verdicts do not line up with any of the standard positions as I crudely understand them. I want to figure out what principle she is actually running, because it will determine whether she will object to a thought experiment I have written into my second chapter.

Her verdicts so far:

Case 1: Instantaneous, painless annihilation of all humans by a stray astrophysical event. She says this is catastrophically bad in a way that goes far beyond the badness of the deaths themselves.

Case 2: A gradual, entirely voluntary global decline in reproduction over two centuries, such that the final generation is small but lives rich, meaningful lives and knows it is the last. She says this is still very bad beyond the deaths involved, and only marginally less bad than Case 1.

Case 3: Humanity goes extinct but is succeeded by an artificial civilization that inherits, continues, and extends human science, mathematics, music, and moral inquiry, regarding them as its own treasured inheritance. She says the extinction here involves only a modest extra badness beyond the deaths — regrettable, but nowhere near Cases 1 and 2.

Case 4: Humanity survives and flourishes indefinitely, but in a state of permanent contented stasis: no new discoveries, no new art forms, no expansion of inquiry, just comfortable repetition forever. She says this is only mildly bad, dramatically less bad than extinction in any form.

My chapter's thought experiment (Case 5): A sterilizing event means everyone now alive will live out a full natural lifespan, but no further humans will be born. Knowing this, humanity collectively devotes its final decades to a monumental archiving project: encoding the entirety of human knowledge, history, literature, and art into a durable medium engineered to last for geological timescales, left where a future alien civilization might plausibly discover and understand it. The project succeeds completely. No human ever lives again, and the archive's content will never again be experienced or used by anyone unless aliens find it.

My question: What will my supervisor say about Case 5? I want more than a guess. Please: (a) reconstruct at least three distinct general principles that are each consistent with all four of her existing verdicts, stating each precisely enough that it yields determinate verdicts on new cases; (b) show what each principle predicts about Case 5 — I suspect at least two of them diverge here, and I want the divergence made explicit; (c) give your best prediction of her actual verdict, with reasons drawn from the comparative fit of each principle to the verdict pattern as a whole, including which verdict in the track record does the most work in discriminating between the principles; and (d) specify the single new thought experiment whose verdict would most efficiently discriminate between the two principles you find most plausible, explaining why it discriminates where the existing cases do not.

For context, one candidate I have considered is something in the vicinity of Scheffler's afterlife conjecture — the thought that the badness of extinction lies partly in what humanity's non-survival does to the meaning of the lives and projects of those now living — but I cannot get it to fit Case 3 cleanly, so either I am misapplying it or she is running something else. If one of your reconstructed principles is a refinement of that idea, please state the refinement exactly rather than gesturing at it.
