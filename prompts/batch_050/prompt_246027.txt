Two friends of mine, Maya and Chris, got into it at dinner last week and they're still texting about it, so I'm outsourcing the refereeing.

The case that started it: a software company has to ship one of two flawed versions of an update. Version A has a bug that will corrupt one random user's files and cost them a miserable full day of recovery work. Version B just adds a 0.4-second delay, once, for 50 million users. Maya said B is obviously the worse outcome overall: any amount of badness, multiplied enough times, outweighs any fixed amount, and anyone who denies this gets walked down a continuum of pairwise comparisons into absurdity (each step clearly better, last step insane, so something's wrong with your transitivity). Chris said no: zero times anything is zero. A 0.4-second delay isn't even experienced as bad. His test: would anyone pay a penny to get rid of it? Would anyone remember it an hour later? An event that leaves no trace in anyone's experience isn't a harm, and a non-harm times 50 million is still nothing, whereas the corrupted files actually ruin someone's week.

Maya said the penny test proves too much. People don't pay to remove tiny irritations because *attending to the decision* costs more than the irritation -- that's a fact about attention being expensive, not about the irritation being valueless. And she cranked the dial: make it 3 seconds of consciously noticed annoyance per person. Now it's clearly bad for each of them, and nothing changed but degree. So Chris needs a line somewhere, and any line between "fine" and "catastrophe" is going to be arbitrary.

Chris said lines can be vague without being arbitrary (he gave the bald/not-bald thing), and then made what I think was his strongest move: nobody experiences the sum. Fifty million separate 0.4-second delays is not a giant suffering event located anywhere. Suffering only exists where someone suffers it, and the worst-off person in world B is doing vastly better than the worst-off person in world A. He also pointed out that we clearly think concentration matters in the one-person case: the same total seconds of pain spread thinly across thousands of people is better than all of it landing on one person, which shows totals aren't the whole story.

Maya's reply was that we happily aggregate across moments *within* one life -- everyone agrees a dull ache every day for thirty years can be worse than one awful afternoon, even though the "you" of any given Tuesday doesn't experience the sum of your life's aches either. If spreading across time inside a person doesn't neutralize badness, why does spreading across persons? Chris's answer: because within a life, moments compound -- chronic pain reshapes what you can do, how you sleep, who you become -- while across persons each speck just vanishes seperately. Aggregation, he said, requires compounding.

Maya's last text: "fine, then what about tiny harms that DO compound -- a factory hum that slightly degrades a million people's sleep for a year?"

I honestly can't tell who's winning. Who's right about which specific points here -- the penny test, the continuum argument, the "nobody experiences the sum" move, and the compounding distinction? And is there anything they're both just assuming that maybe they shouldn't be?
