I am in Year 12 and preparing a presentation for my school’s philosophy society. Our starting point is a dilemma involving three pupils organising a charity concert: one is broadly consequentialist and wants to maximise the total money raised; another believes that lying to sponsors would be wrong even if it raised more; and the third is mainly concerned that disabled pupils should have equal access to every part of the event. They cannot simply vote, because they disagree about what the vote should measure, but they could make a moral trade: each might accept a smaller setback in what they most value in exchange for a concession from the others. I want to distinguish this from ordinary political bargaining, mere compromise, and blackmail. Could you therefore set out the desiderata that an adequate theory of moral trade would have to meet? By moral trade, I mean cooperation in which people with different moral commitments exchange concessions or divide control over decisions so that the arrangement can be justified to each party without pretending that they all accept the same moral theory.

Please present the requirements in a table, with the case or consideration motivating each one. The list should cover at least the following: how to compare gains and losses when the parties use different measures of moral value; when a person may concede something they regard as intrinsically wrong; whether rights or basic interests set limits on what can be traded; how to deal with strategic misrepresentation and unequal bargaining power; whether future people, animals, or absent groups can be represented in the trade; how the theory should handle uncertainty about both facts and moral principles; and what makes a trade stable rather than merely a temporary tactical agreement. It should also distinguish morally valuable compromise from surrendering under pressure, aggregating preferences, and buying someone’s compliance. For every requirement, explain why it belongs on the list rather than simply stating it.

Finally, mark which requirements seem non-negotiable, which depend on the purpose of the theory, and which may reflect the assumptions of one moral outlook. If some of the desiderata cannot plausibly be satisfied together, identify the conflict and explain what would have to be abandoned. I would also like a brief assessment of which single requirement rules out the most naïve accounts of moral trade. You may finish by testing a few broad approaches against the list—such as mutual-advantage bargaining, compromise measured by each person’s own values, an impartial common-good procedure, and a rights-constrained hybrid—but please keep the emphasis on the criteria rather than producing an elaborate scorecard.
