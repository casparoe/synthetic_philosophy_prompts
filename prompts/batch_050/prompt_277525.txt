I was recently rereading Joseph Butler's ninth sermon, on the forgiveness of injuries, in his Fifteen Sermons (1726), in which he distinguishes between a deliberate, settled anger directed at a person and a steady indignation directed at the vice itself, suggesting that one can forgive an offender while continuing to oppose what the offender did. A friend and I disagreed about what follows from that distinction: she took it to show that forgiveness can be both obligatory and unconditional — owed even absent repentance — while I suspect Butler's distinction only shows that forgiveness is compatible with justice, not that it is required regardless of the wrongdoer's stance. Before we settle it, I want to stress-test my side by collecting considerations against treating forgiveness as an unconditional duty.

Here are the objections I already have and do not want repeated: (1) unconditional forgiveness risks condoning the wrong or signaling that it was not serious; (2) imposing a duty to forgive burdens victims a second time and can undermine their self-respect; (3) forgiveness partly involves affective change, and since feelings are not under direct voluntary control, they cannot be obligatory on ought-implies-can grounds; (4) much of the case for unconditional forgiveness trades on conflating forgiving with excusing, forgetting, or reconciling, which are separable; (5) forgiveness is often characterized as a gift, and a gift by its nature cannot be owed; (6) there are standing worries about forgiving on behalf of others or on behalf of the dead.

Give me one further consideration against obligatory, unconditional forgiveness that is not on this list and not a rewording, special case, or combination of items already on it. It may engage Butler's distinction if useful, but it need not. Then say which listed objection it comes closest to, how it differs, and honestly whether you think it is any good.
