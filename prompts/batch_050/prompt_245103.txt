I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and at our metaethics reading group last week two of my colleagues got into a dispute about the Euthyphro dilemma that neither could resolve. Daniel's position was that the dilemma is a parochial objection to divine command theory: its bite comes entirely from asking whether God's will is constrained by an independent standard, so once theology is abandoned it simply lapses. Rosa countered that the dilemma is really a template about grounding that recurs wherever a normative fact is explained by something else — for a hedonist, for instance, one can ask whether suffering is bad because it is disliked or disliked because it is bad — and that the same uncomfortable fork, arbitrary foundation versus ungrounded standard, arises either way. Daniel replied that the secular versions are benign: saying suffering is bad because of how it feels is a constitutive claim rather than a command, and there is no analogue of the worry that God could have commanded cruelty, since the felt character of suffering could not have been otherwise. Rosa answered that this misses the point: even if suffering could not have felt otherwise, it remains open whether its badness is grounded in our response to it or is a response-independent fact we are attuned to, and either answer inherits the costs the original dilemma pressed — value that bottoms out in something contingent and shallow, or value that floats free of anything we can explain. We ran out of time there. Please adjudicate the exchange: who is right about each specific move, is Rosa's generalization of the dilemma sound, and does Daniel's reply actually defuse it? Who is more right overall, and what, if anything, are both of them missing?
