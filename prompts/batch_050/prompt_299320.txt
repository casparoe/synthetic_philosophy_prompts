I write novels for a living, which mostly means I sit in cafes and eavesdrop, but this time the argument happened at my own writing group's after-dinner and I've been turning it over since. We were splitting the check -- six of us, messy shared plates, the usual. Dana and Marcus each did the math on their phones and got totals about eleven dollars apart. Both are sharp, both do this kind of arithmetic all the time, neither had been drinking much. What started as a bill dispute turned into an actual philosophical fight and I stayed out of it, but I genuinely can't decide who won, so I'm outsourcing.

Round by round it went: (1) Dana said the moment they got different answers, each should drop their confidence way down and treat the true split as probably somewhere in between, because they're equals at this and trusting your own calculation over an equally good calculator's is just favoring yourself. (2) Marcus said that's wrong because he can see his own reasoning -- the line items, the tax, the tip -- whereas all he has of hers is a bottom-line number, and treating her bare conclusion as worth the same as his actual worked-through reasoning throws away information; by her logic he'd have to be constantly abandoning things he's checked the moment anyone disagrees. (3) Dana shot back that he can see his reasoning but he can't see that it's *correct*, and other people independently checking is exactly the test for whether you've made an error -- plus his move is available to everyone always ("but I saw my reasoning!"), so it excuses him from ever being moved by anyone. (4) Marcus said fine, but if disagreement automatically drags him halfway, then any stubborn or confident person can steer him anywhere, and he'd have to go mushy on stuff he knows cold the moment some credentialed crank disputes it -- whether Dana counts as his equal is something he has to judge for himself, using his own judgment, so there's no escaping relying on yourself at some point. (5) Dana said the crank case is rigged because the crank isn't his peer, but by stipulation she and he are equals at dinner math, and if he won't move even here then "peer" just means "person who already agrees with me" and his principle does no work.

Please give me your verdict as a numbered list of exactly five items, a couple sentences each, no intro or wrap-up: items one through four should rate each of the four moves above from clearly right to clearly mistaken with brief justification, and item five should say who's more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
