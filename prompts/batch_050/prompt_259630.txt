Last week, my flatmate and I fell into a three-hour argument about Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility, and I came away convinced that the standard dialectic has a gap nobody at the table was exploiting. So I've started drafting a short essay that tries to thread the needle: it concedes Strawson's regress but attempts a novel escape that doesn't rely on compatibilism, quantum indeterminacy, or any of the usual maneuvers. Below is my current draft (still rough). What I'd like from you are comments on three things: (1) whether my reconstruction of the Basic Argument is accurate and fair, or whether I've smuggled in a strawman; (2) whether my proposed escape actually escapes, or merely relocates the regress one level up; and (3) whether the essay would be interesting to someone who already knows this debate, or whether it's a dressed-up version of a move that's been made before.

The draft: Strawson's argument, as I understand it, runs roughly as follows. What you do is a consequence of how you are — your character, motives, dispositions. So to be truly responsible for what you do, you'd have to be responsible for how you are. But to be responsible for how you are, you'd have had to do something, at some earlier point, for which you were responsible. And that earlier doing was itself a consequence of how you then were. The regress either runs back to childhood (where you're plainly not responsible for how you are) or requires an impossible act of self-creation. Hence no one is ultimately morally responsible for anything. My flatmate's response was the usual compatibilist shrug — responsibility doesn't require ultimate self-creation — but I think that shrugs past the argument rather than answering it, since Strawson's target is precisely the kind of desert-entailing responsibility we invoke when we say someone genuinely deserves blame.

My attempted escape goes like this. Grant that you cannot be responsible for how you are when you act. But consider a different relation: at the moment of acting, you may satisfy what I call the endorsement condition — had you been offered, in that moment, a fully informed choice between being exactly the way you are and being some specific way you are not, you would have chosen to remain as you are. A compulsive hand-washer fails this condition: she would have chosen to be rid of the compulsion. A calculating embezzler satisfies it: shown his own greed with perfect clarity, he keeps it. My claim is that endorsement is regress-proof in a way character-formation is not. Strawson's regress gains traction because character is backward-looking — it asks where your dispositions came from. Endorsement is present-tense and reflexive: it asks what you would affirm now, and that act of affirmation is not itself something you need to have authored, because its authority comes from its structure (an agent's current, informed relation to herself) rather than from its origins. Blame, I want to argue, tracks endorsement-failure and endorsement-success, not origin. The embezzler deserves blame not because he made himself greedy, but because greed is what he is and, given the mirror, he would not have it otherwise.

A few specific places where I'd especially value your scrutiny. First, my flatmate insists the endorsement condition is just Frankfurt-style hierarchical mesh theory in a trench coat — higher-order identification with first-order motives — and inherits the same problem, namely that your second-order attitudes are themselves part of how you are, so the regress simply restarts at the second order. Is he right, and if so, can my structural-versus-origins reply survive it? Second, in step one of my reconstruction I've stated the Basic Argument without the bells and whistles (I've deliberately omitted Strawson's framing in terms of heaven-and-hell responsibility and the causa sui requirement); does the bare-bones version I've given lose anything dialectically important? Third, the hand-washer/embezzler contrast is doing a lot of work — does it illicitly rely on intuitions about treatment versus blame that a skeptic could just deny? Please be blunt. I'd rather scrap the essay than publish a regress with extra steps.
