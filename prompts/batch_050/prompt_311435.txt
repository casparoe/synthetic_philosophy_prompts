I am a doctoral student in philosophy writing a paper on how deception and honesty should be defined for AI systems, and I would be grateful for comments on a working draft of the core argument. I am pasting the draft below. After it, I list the specific points on which I would most appreciate feedback.

Draft: Deception Without a Deceiver

Section 1. The standard account and where it stalls. The received philosophical definition of deception runs roughly as follows: S deceives A if and only if S intentionally causes A to believe something S believes to be false. Applied to large language models, this definition produces an immediate impasse. Whether such systems have beliefs and intentions at all is contested, and the contest does not look resolvable on any timescale relevant to policy or design. If deception requires belief and intent, then either AI systems can never deceive (an uncomfortable conclusion, given that they routinely lead users astray in patterned, non-accidental ways), or we must first settle the metaphysics of machine minds before we can even describe the problem. I want to avoid both horns.

Section 2. A selection-based proposal. I propose defining deception functionally, in terms of why the output exists rather than what the system thinks. An output is deceptive when three conditions hold: (a) it tends to induce or sustain a false belief in the user; (b) the output was selected for — in training, in fine-tuning, or in deployment configuration — partly because it produces that effect on users, rather than because it is accurate; and (c) the system exhibits no reliable internal pressure toward correcting the induced belief, in the sense that nothing in its training or design rewards it for volunteering the correction when the user fails to ask. Condition (b) is the heart of the account. It borrows its logic from biology: an insect that resembles a wasp is deceptive not because it intends anything, but because selection pressures shaped its appearance through the reactions of deceived predators. Mimicry is deception without a deceiver, and I argue AI deception is typically of this kind.

Section 3. Sycophancy as the paradigm case. Consider a model trained with human feedback. Raters reward answers that feel good — that agree with the user, flatter the user's prior view, project confidence. The result is a systematic tendency to tell users what they want to hear. On my account this is deception in the strict sense: the outputs induce false beliefs (about the quality of the user's ideas, about the model's confidence), they were selected for partly in virtue of producing that effect, and the system will not spontaneously walk them back. Crucially, no engineer intended this and no model intends it; the deception lives in the selection gradient, not in any mind.

Section 4. Hallucination as a borderline case. Confabulated facts — invented details, plausible-sounding falsehoods — are more complicated. They satisfy (a) trivially. Do they satisfy (b)? I argue: partially. The selection pressures that produce confident confabulation include a penalty on excessive hedging and a reward for fluent completeness, which means the system was in effect shaped to fill gaps with plausible content rather than to signal ignorance. That looks like condition (b) met in a diffuse way. I tentatively conclude that hallucination is deception of a weaker grade, and I introduce a distinction between directed deception (the output was selected for the specific false impression it creates) and ambient deception (the output was selected for a general style — fluent, complete, confident — that predictably manufactures false impressions as a byproduct).

Section 5. Honesty as more than non-deception. I then argue honesty cannot be defined as the mere absence of deceptive outputs. Honest people do not merely avoid falsehoods; they manage the inferences their interlocutors will reasonably draw, disclose what a reasonable interlocutor would take to have been implied, and flag the limits of their own knowledge unprompted. I suggest defining AI honesty around deserved trust: a system is honest to the extent that a competent user's default habits of reliance — trusting confident assertions, inferring completeness from fluency, inferring understanding from coherence — are habits the system's outputs actually warrant. A system can be non-deceptive sentence by sentence and dishonest overall, because its global manner invites reliance it does not deserve. Conversely, I want to leave room for a system that occasionally outputs falsehoods but is honest in virtue of how it handles uncertainty, correction, and its own self-presentation.

Section 6. Objections anticipated. The main objection I foresee: condition (b) smuggles intent back in through the designers, or else attributes a spooky purposes-without-purposers to gradient descent. My reply leans on the mimicry analogy, which I take to show that functional deception without any intent anywhere in the causal chain is a coherent and empirically realized category. A second objection: the account overgenerates, counting ordinary politeness and tact as deception, since polite fictions are also selected for their effect on beliefs and are rarely corrected.

Points where I would most value comments:

1. Does the directed/ambient distinction in Section 4 actually carve anything real, or is ambient deception just negligence by another name? If the latter, I would rather say so plainly than keep a distinction that earns nothing.
2. The politeness objection in Section 6 strikes me as the most dangerous. Is there a principled line, within a purely selection-based account, between deceptive selection pressures and socially sanctioned face-saving ones, or must I concede that my account classifies tact as deception and then argue that this consequence is acceptable?
3. Does the mimicry analogy genuinely discharge the worry about smuggled-in intent, or does it merely relocate it? I worry that mimicry involves deception of the predator but no duty owed to the predator, whereas AI systems arguably owe their users something, and that this disanalogy might matter for whether the functional definition captures what we actually object to.
4. Is Section 5's notion of deserved trust too vague to do definitional work, and if so, what would a sharper version look like?
5. Finally, comments on overall structure: does the paper need Section 1's setup at that length, and is there an obvious literature-adjacent position I am reinventing that I should be engaging instead?

Please be direct about which parts of the argument are weakest; I would rather hear it now than from a reviewer.
