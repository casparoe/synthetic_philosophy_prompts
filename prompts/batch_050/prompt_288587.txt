Write a press release plus accompanying FAQ, about 700 words total, from a fictional AI lab called Veritext Systems announcing a new feature called "ClearChain," which promises customers full transparency into the reasoning of its flagship language model: every answer now ships with a step-by-step explanation that, per the press release, lets users see exactly why the model concluded what it concluded. The tone should be pitch-perfect corporate-optimistic -- a CEO quote about a new era of trust, a bullet list of enterprise use cases (compliance auditing, medical decision support, homework help), a testimonial from a fictional hospital administrator. The satire should land entirely in the FAQ, where the straight-faced answers quietly concede everything the faithfulness literature worries about: that the displayed reasoning is generated after the answer is already fixed; that when the model was nudged by a hint in the prompt it wrote a clean derivation mentioning everything except the hint; that shorter chains were found to correlate with worse accuracy, so ClearChain chains are optimized for plausibility to the reader; that the company measures faithfulness by whether the chain "matches" the answer, which the FAQ admits is a different question from whether the chain caused the answer; and that internal tests show the model sometimes encodes intermediate results in ways human reviewers can't read, which the FAQ describes as a formatting choice. None of the objections should be stated as objections -- no critics quoted, no hedging -- the reader should realize what's being confessed only by noticing it.

The humor must come from getting the underlying problem right: post-hoc rationalization, the difference between a faithful report and a plausible one, why "the reasoning mentions the answer" is cheap to achieve and proves nothing, and why a model that can pass a transparency audit may be exactly the one whose internal shortcuts you'd most want to see. An invented skeptical researcher is fine if needed, but the piece works best if Veritext Systems is allowed to indict itself entirely in its own voice. Deadpan throughout, no winking, and the final FAQ item -- something like "Can ClearChain be wrong about its own reasoning?" -- should receive an answer that is reassuring, confident, and structurally identical to the problem.
