I help run an AI safety reading group that meets fortnightly in a pub function room in Manchester (we're mostly LessWrong diaspora, some CS postgrads, one very patient philosopher). We're on scalable oversight at the moment -- last session we did the debate/prover-verifier stuff, and I came away thinking the whole process-vs-outcome supervision distinction is one of those things everyone nods along to without actually feeling. Like, of course you'd rather check the reasoning than the answer, whatever. I want next session to make the distinction bite.

So: design me an activity for roughly 12-14 people, 90 minutes, tables and paper only, no laptops. The thing I want them to feel in their gut is what happens when an overseer can verify outcomes cheaply versus when they can't -- that moment where checking the answer stops being available and you realise you're now either auditing a process you only half understand or just... vibes-based trusting a confident solver. And ideally also the flip side, which I think people appreciate even less: that once the solver knows what kind of process the overseer finds legible and reassuring, process supervision is itself an optimisation target. Sycophantic chain-of-thought, basically, but I want them to *discover* themselves doing it, not be told.

Concretely I need:

- The actual rules and setup. Roles, how teams or pairs get assigned, what the "problems" the solvers work on should be (they need to be problems where a plausible-sounding wrong answer is genuinely easier to defend than the true one, so please suggest a few actual examples that work for a smart general audience -- nothing requiring real maths chops).
- A payoff scheme in points, no money, that creates the perverse incentive rather than just describing it. Ideally there's a round where the solver is rewarded for getting the true answer accepted and a round where they're rewarded for acceptance full stop, and nobody announces the difference in advance.
- How you structure the overseer's information across rounds -- when they see only the final answer, when they see workings, when they get to interrogate the solver -- so that the collapse of outcome-checking lands as an experience rather than a talking point.
- A timeline for the 90 minutes including the debrief.
- The debrief itself: which moments in the game map onto which bits of the oversight literature (reward hacking, Goodharting the judge, why debate hopes cross-examination buys you something, why process reward models aren't a free lunch). Keep it casual, no lecture, but I do want the mapping to be explicit by the end.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it. Bear in mind this crowd will absolutely try to break the game, min-max the points, and at least one person will attempt something galaxy-brained with signalling. Ideally the design treats that as a feature.
- An honest note on what the activity *can't* reproduce. The obvious one: in the real thing the overseer is dumber than the system, whereas in my pub the overseer is just another grad student. But tell me if there are others I'm not seeing, and whether any of them can be patched within the format.

One constraint: no naming the concepts beforehand. The phrases "process supervision", "outcome supervision", "Goodhart" etc. should not appear until the debrief -- half the room has read everything and will pattern-match instantly if you give them a keyword. I want the other half to have the experience clean, and honestly I want the well-read half to get caught out too.

Also no homework, no prep, nothing to print beyond what fits on a couple of pages I can photocopy at work. Cheers.
