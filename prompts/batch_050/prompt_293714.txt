I'm drafting the discussion section of a paper for a graduate seminar in philosophy of psychiatry, on whether psychopathic offenders possess moral knowledge, and I'd like a consistency audit of the position I've ended up with before I commit to it. The view grew out of an argument with a classmate who insisted that psychopaths "know the words but not the music" of morality, and I suspect my attempt to steer a middle course has left me holding commitments that don't cohere — but I can't see exactly where. Here is where I've landed. (a) Moral knowledge is a cognitive achievement: to know that an action is wrong is to grasp the considerations that make it wrong — that it harms someone, breaks an agreement, treats a person as a means — and affective responses like empathy, guilt, and revulsion are what normally motivate compliance with moral requirements, not constituents of the knowledge itself. (b) Clinically psychopathic adults of ordinary intelligence can typically state, accurately, which actions in a presented range are wrong, and can supply the right sorts of reasons for those verdicts; someone who says that unprovoked violence is wrong because it hurts the victim, and who can deploy that reasoning competently across novel cases, knows that such violence is wrong in the only sense that matters. (c) Accordingly, the calculating fraudster with psychopathic traits who fully anticipates that his scheme will ruin his victims and proceeds simply because he does not care is, on my view, a paradigm illustration that moral knowledge and moral motivation come apart. (d) An agent is fully blameworthy for wrongdoing only if, at the time of acting, they knew that what they were doing was wrong. (e) All else equal, psychopathic offenders merit somewhat less blame than non-psychopathic offenders who commit the same offenses, since their affective abnormalities appear to be innate, were never chosen, and could not have been reasoned or disciplined away. (f) I also accept the standard experimental finding that psychopaths perform atypically on moral/conventional distinction tasks — tending to sort transgressions by whether an authority prohibits them rather than by whether a victim is harmed — though I filed this away without deciding whether it sits comfortably with (b).

What I'd like from you is not a verdict on whether psychopaths are in fact morally responsible, but an audit of the set. Which pairs or triples among (a) through (f) are in genuine tension, and which only appear to be and dissolve once you are precise about what each commitment actually says? For each genuine tension, please identify the cheapest revision that restores consistency, what that revision costs me in terms of the overall position, and what further commitments the surviving view would take on. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, and be concrete about which numbered commitment has to go or be reworded in each case.
