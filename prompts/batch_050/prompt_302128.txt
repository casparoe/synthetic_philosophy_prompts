Yesterday evening a friend and I had a long and unresolved argument about rational belief, and we have agreed to suspend it until someone supplies what we discovered we lacked: a statement of what a theory of rational outright belief is even supposed to do. The occasion was the preface paradox. A meticulous historian believes every claim in her finished book, each on solid evidence, and yet writes in the preface that, given the track record of such undertakings, at least one claim in it is bound to be false. My friend holds that the historian is a model of rationality precisely because she believes each claim while expecting error somewhere; I hold that she is straightforwardly inconsistent and that any theory which certifies her pattern of belief has thereby refuted itself. When we turned to the lottery paradox we disagreed again, in the opposite configuration, which convinced me that we were both reasoning without a standard.

Please write that standard: the desiderata any adequate theory of rational outright belief — full, flat-out belief, as opposed to degrees of confidence — must satisfy, with the case or consideration motivating each requirement. The purpose is fixed in advance: the theory should be good enough to adjudicate disputes like ours, that is, to deliver principled verdicts about which of the belief patterns involved in the two paradoxes are rationally permitted, forbidden, or required. At a minimum your list must speak to the following, though you should add requirements I have overlooked:

- The lottery verdict. In a large fair lottery known to have exactly one winner, it seems rational to believe of each ticket that it will lose, yet it seems irrational to believe of every ticket that it will lose. Note that the puzzle depends on the guaranteed winner, since that is what makes the full set of ticket-beliefs inconsistent with something else the agent rationally believes. Say what a theory must deliver here, and whether it is permitted to take the escape route of denying that purely statistical grounds ever suffice for outright belief — leaving the agent with only high credence — or whether respecting the intuition that we do form such beliefs is a genuine constraint.

- The preface verdict. The theory must say whether the historian's combination — belief in each claim together with the belief that some claim is false — is rational, irrational, or not a case of outright belief at all.

- The asymmetry problem. The two paradoxes are structurally parallel, yet the intuitions pull apart: the historian looks rational while the person who believes of every ticket that it will lose does not. Require the theory either to give a unified treatment of both cases or to identify the feature that licenses divergent verdicts — candidate features include the track-record basis of the preface belief, the non-statistical character of the historian's evidence, and the fact that her beliefs concern different subject matters — and say whether any of these can bear the weight.

- Structural principles. The theory must take an explicit stand on which formal constraints rational outright belief obeys: consistency, closure under known consequence, closure under conjunction in particular. For each principle retained it must absorb the paradoxes; for each abandoned it must explain what replaces the principle's obvious appeal — for instance, what becomes of ordinary reasoning if one may rationally believe p and rationally believe q but not rationally believe their conjunction.

- The credence interface. The theory must say how flat-out belief relates to degrees of confidence, without collapsing belief into maximal credence, since that would make nearly all ordinary belief attributions false.

- Explanatory adequacy. The theory should not merely issue verdicts but explain the temptations: an account that convicts the lottery thinker of irrationality must diagnose why his pattern of belief feels rational, and likewise for whichever verdict it gives on the preface.

Then do three further things. First, mark which desiderata you consider non-negotiable and which could be traded away under pressure, since my friend and I evidently disagree most deeply at exactly this point. Second, flag any pair or subset of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied; if the specification itself harbors an inconsistency, that is a result we need before resuming. Third, briefly — and only briefly — score the main families of responses against the list: threshold views that tie outright belief to credence above some cutoff, views that preserve consistency by withholding outright belief from purely statistical conclusions, views that tolerate rational inconsistency, and views that divide the territory by distinguishing outright belief from some context-relative state of acceptance. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring; the criteria are where the argument actually lives.
