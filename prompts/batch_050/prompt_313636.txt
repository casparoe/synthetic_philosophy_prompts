A friend and I argued past midnight last week about a reform she supports; I invoked Chesterton's fence, and she countered that the principle is unfalsifiable -- any obsolete rule can be defended by inventing some plausible purpose it might serve. In the course of the argument I coined a distinction that I now want to build a lesson around: between a live fence, whose original function is still operative, and what I called a ghost fence, whose function has lapsed or migrated elsewhere, so that the fence now guards nothing. Next week I must teach this whole cluster -- the fence principle plus the ghost-fence complication -- to first-year undergraduates in a fifty-minute seminar. Plan my explanation rather than giving it: the order in which the components must come (I suspect epistemic humility about tacit, unintended functions has to precede the fence itself, but I want your judgment), the one carrying example to build the session around -- not a literal fence or gate, and please say what the example must contain to make the live/ghost distinction visible rather than asserted -- and the misconceptions to expect. I anticipate at least these: that the principle is an argument against ever removing anything, that 'understand the fence' means finding a documented intention behind it, and that producing any coherent just-so story about a rule's purpose settles that it should stay. Tell me where in the sequence to head each off, what one question to end with that would show students can apply the live/ghost distinction to a new case, and what I should explicitly decline to resolve at this level.
