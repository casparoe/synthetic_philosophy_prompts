I run a small discussion group that grew out of the rationalist blog scene — people who absorbed their decision theory from the Sequences and their moral arithmetic from forum arguments, and who tend to treat the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms as the last word on what "utility" could mean. After years of watching us argue about AI, charity, and policy, I've become convinced the word is doing at least four incompatible jobs in our conversations and that nobody notices the switches: sometimes utility is a bare redescription of what someone would choose, sometimes a cardinal quantity forced on us by the VNM axioms, sometimes a feeling of pleasure, sometimes whatever it is that makes a life go well. Arguments that look substantive dissolve once the senses are separated, and I want an instrument that forces the separation.

Act as a philosopher-economist who has taught both graduate decision theory and the philosophy of well-being, and who builds sorting instruments for fun. Before writing anything, think carefully about which concrete cases actually pull the positions apart — two theories can use different labels and still give identical verdicts on every case, and a good instrument ignores those pairs.

Then write a "what do you actually mean by utility?" quiz I can hand out at the next meeting. Requirements:

- Eight to ten questions. Each question presents a concrete case or choice situation on which the positions genuinely diverge — an Allais-style gamble, a preference-reversal experiment, a sour-grapes case of adaptive preference, a person who knowingly chooses what they predict will make them miserable, an agent with cyclic preferences being money-pumped, a case where someone's choices and their reported happiness come apart. At least three questions must draw on cases from that list; the rest are up to you.
- No question may ask about labels or doctrines. Each question must be answerable in ten seconds by someone who has never taken an economics class, with two or three plain-language answer options phrased as verdicts about the case.
- A scoring key mapping answer patterns onto at least these five positions: (1) revealed-preference ordinalism, where utility merely encodes choice and claims to add nothing; (2) VNM cardinal utility, a real measure but only of attitudes toward risk; (3) hedonic utility, a felt quantity of pleasure minus pain; (4) preference-satisfaction as welfare, where getting what you want is constitutive of your good; (5) coherence-constructed preference, where an agent's utility function is whatever a coherent reconstruction of their dispositions would yield, so a sufficiently incoherent agent has no determinate utility at all. If you need a sixth position to sort everyone sensibly, add it.
- Flag any pair of positions your quiz cannot reliably separate, and explain in one sentence why they collapse together in practice. Likewise, if some answer combination corresponds to no coherent position, mark it in the key as unsortable rather than forcing it somewhere.

The quiz itself — questions, options, and key — must fit in 1,000 words. Your preliminary reasoning about where the positions diverge does not count against the limit, so do that work out loud first, then give me the finished instrument.
