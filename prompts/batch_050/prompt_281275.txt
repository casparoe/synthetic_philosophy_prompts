Years of reading rationalist forums finally caught up with me: I joined an email-list game of Nomic, and within a month the players had used the rule governing amendments to amend the rule governing amendments, then spent a week arguing about which version of the rule had authorized the change. I'd like that experience, and the theory behind it, turned into comedy.

Write the changelog, in the deadpan voice of meticulous software release notes, for the first year of a self-amending constitution adopted by a small republic whose founding charter permits the amendment clause to be amended by its own procedure. Roughly 600 words. Each entry should treat a genuinely recognizable problem of legal self-reference as routine maintenance: an emergency hotfix issued after a motion to repeal the clause that defines valid repeal; a versioning dispute after the numbering rule is amended with retroactive effect; a deprecation notice for an entrenched clause once the entrenchment rule is transmuted into an ordinary one; a "known issues" section conceding that the authority of the very first amendment rests on a clause that a later amendment replaced.

The target is the quiet confidence, common to constitutional drafters and to designers of self-modifying systems alike, that sufficiently careful specification defuses self-reference. The jokes must exaggerate real features of self-amending rule systems rather than invent fake ones -- a reader who knows the self-amendment literature should recognize each entry -- and the tone must never wink.

Append a short postscript separating what is settled (that amendment procedures can in practice be turned on themselves) from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on this (whether self-applied repeal of the amendment power is valid, and whether the "paradox of self-amendment" is a real problem or a category error), flagging any joke that leans on one side of a live dispute.
