I cover science and tech for a general-audience magazine, and I'm drafting a piece on why "fair AI" keeps dissapointing people. The spine of the piece is a list of impossibility results -- cases where you can prove that several fairness properties everyone finds intuitive can't all hold at once. Here's what I have so far:

(1) Calibration vs. error rates: if a risk score means the same thing within each group (among everyone scored "30% risk," about 30% actually go on to reoffend, whatever their group) and the groups have different base rates, then the score cannot also equalize false-positive and false-negative rates across groups, except in trivial cases.

(2) The three-way version: you generally can't simultaneously have the score's distribution be the same across groups, have the score be independent of group membership among people with the same true outcome, and have the true outcome be independent of group among people with the same score -- unless base rates are equal or prediction is perfect.

(3) The individual-vs-group tension: treating "similar people similarly" in a fine-grained way tends to break group-level statistical parity, and enforcing parity means knowingly treating some similar individuals differently.

I'd like one more genuine impossibility or tension result to round out the piece. Constraints: it can't be a special case or restatement of (1) or (2), and it can't just be "the training data is biased" -- I want something structural, provable or at least rigorously statable, that bites even with clean data. It should also connect to a real deployment decision (hiring, lending, pretrial release, content moderation, whatever) so my editors don't kill it as too abstract. Finally, tell me honestly whether it deserves a spot alongside the other three or whether it's a weaker result I'm forcing into the piece.
