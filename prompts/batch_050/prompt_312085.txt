So I just spent most of a Saturday arguing with a friend about the murderer at the door and we got absolutely nowhere, and I want to understand why, not to get ammo for round two.

Her side: any moral theory that tells you not to lie to the murderer is refuted by that fact alone, end of story, and the only live question is what the right lying policy is. My side: that's way too quick. Kant's refusal isn't a blunder inside the theory, it's the theory doing its job -- the moral law doesn't get renegotiated becuase someone else manufactured a hostage situation, and if the worst happens the responsibility sits with the murderer, not the person who answered the door truthfully. She says I'm just refusing to count consequences. I say she's refusing to count anything else. And so on for three hours.

There was also an exegetical sub-fight: she's convinced even Kant's own system doesn't deliver the no-lie answer, because a maxim of lying specifically to murderers-at-the-door universalizes fine -- there's no standing practice of truth-telling-to-murderers that it would destroy. I think that move cheats by baking the exception into the act description, since you could do that for any maxim you like. Whether Kant's actual reply to Constant helps her or me is also contested.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the case itself was never what we were disagreeing about. She treats thought experiments as data a theory has to fit, so a theory that bites this bullet loses a point; I think cases this rigged can't adjudicate anything and the real dispute is methodological -- what moral philosophy is even allowed to appeal to. But I notice this hypothesis conveniently makes her the one who's confused, which makes me suspicious of it.
