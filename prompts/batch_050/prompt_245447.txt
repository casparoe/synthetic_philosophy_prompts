At my uni's journal club we keep having the same fight about evolutionary psychology and it's completely stalled. One side says hypotheses like cheater-detection modules, sex differences in jealousy, universal mate preferences are just-so stories: you observe a behaviour, cook up a plausible Pleistocene scenario where it would've been adaptive, and call that evidence. The other side points to actual methods — cross-cultural surveys, comparative data across species, task analyses, neuroimaging — and says this is ordinary hypothesis testing, no worse than plenty of biology. Twenty-plus years after the whole adaptationism fight, nobody has moved an inch, and I suspect both sides mostly agree on the facts and disagree about something else. So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it empirical (how well-evidenced specific hypotheses are, case by case), methodological (what even counts as evidence for a claim about selection pressures we can't observe), or bedrock (how much weight the sheer coherence of an adaptive story can bear at all)? I'd rather have a diagnosis with reasons than a survey of the debate, plus an honest answer on what progress would even look like — a particular kind of study, a formal test for adaptation, or admitting the question's ill-posed. And tell me what would change your mind: what finding or arguement would make you reclassify the disagreement, and where's your diagnosis most likely to fail?
