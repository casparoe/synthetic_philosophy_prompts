I'm an economist by training — my dissertation was on mechanism design, and I teach a graduate course in game theory — so my native vocabulary is extensive-form games, subgame perfection, commitment devices, time inconsistency, and equilibrium selection. A philosopher friend recently tried to explain Derek Parfit's "hitchhiker" case to me: a stranded driver is rescued by a motorist on the condition that the driver promise to pay upon reaching town; the motorist can reliably tell whether someone would actually pay once safely home; and it turns out to be rational, in some sense, to be the kind of person who would pay even when, at the moment of decision, paying is no longer instrumentally rational. My friend says Parfit uses this to argue that rationality can sometimes require being disposed to act irrationally — that rational irrationality is possible. My gut reaction was that this is just the value of commitment, something we've understood since Schelling, dressed up in confusing language, and that the motorist's lie-detection ability just collapses the game into one where the "commitment" is effectively enforceable. My friend insists I'm missing the point but couldn't say exactly which point.

Restate the hitchhiker case and Parfit's argument for rational irrationality in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can: set it up as an extensive-form game, identify where the standard solution concepts (subgame perfection, backward induction) deliver the "don't pay" result, and locate precisely what has to be added or modified — a commitment technology, a type-signaling structure, a restriction to dispositions rather than actions — for the "pay" outcome to become defensible. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of Parfit's argument translate cleanly into claims I already have theorems or intuitions about? Which parts only translate with distortion — for instance, is there a real difference between valuing a disposition ex ante and being rational to act on it ex post that game theory flattens? And is there anything left over that my framework simply cannot express: does the question of whether *rationality itself* can recommend irrational acts have a counterpart in a framework that treats payoffs and preferences as given and never passes judgment on the coherence of the agent's own deliberative standards, or is that leftover a sign that Parfit's puzzle trades on an equivocation between "rational" applied to acts, to dispositions, and to characters?

I want an honest verdict on my gut reaction at the end: am I right that this is commitment value all the way down, or is there a genuine philosophical problem here that game theory's machinery structurally cannot state? Please flag how confident you are in each part of the translation, and single out the single point in your own analysis where you are least sure you've gotten Parfit's actual position right rather than a convenient reconstruction of it.
