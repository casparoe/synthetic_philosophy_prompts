I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about how governments make decisions under deep uncertainty, using the 1976 swine flu affair as my main case. Quick recap of the facts as I understand them, since they're what I want the analysis to run on: in early 1976 a cluster of flu cases at Fort Dix, New Jersey -- including one death -- turned out to be a strain antigenically similar to the 1918 pandemic virus. Nobody knew whether it would spread; expert estimates of a major pandemic were all over the place, mostly low but nobody could rule out a 1918 repeat. The Ford administration, pushed hard by CDC leadership, chose to vaccinate essentially the entire country before flu season. Around 45 million Americans got the shot. The pandemic never came. Worse, the vaccine itself turned out to be associated with Guillain-Barré syndrome at roughly one case per hundred thousand vaccinees, and the program was suspended that December in a cloud of lawsuits and recrimination. The official postmortem made everyone look a little foolish.

Here's what I want from you, and I want it as a genuine analysis, not a summary. Read the episode through the decision theory of choice under ignorance: maximin, minimax regret, and the Rawls-versus-Harsanyi fight over which is rational. First the mapping. The decision looks maximin-ish -- protect against the worst case, a 1918-scale catastrophe, whatever the probabilities -- but it also looks like minimax regret operating on the officials rather than the public, since the regret of a pandemic you didn't vaccinate against is career-ending in a way that vaccine side effects, somehow, weren't. So whose regret matrix is the right one, and is that switcheroo itself a finding? Second, Rawls famously said maximin is only rational under specific conditions -- grave stakes, indifference to gains above the minimum, and probabilities so obscure that expected-utility reasoning is guesswork. Did those conditions actually hold at Fort Dix? There WAS a middle option nobody took seriously, by the way: manufacture the vaccine, stockpile it, and wait for surveillance data. What does the existence of that unchosen branch do to the claim that maximin was operating at all? Third, and this is the part that interests me most, the disaster arrived on the supposedly safe branch. The worst case wasn't the flu; it was the countermeasure. If maximin requires you to correctly identify the worst outcome of each option, and that identification is itself made under ignorance, does the theory even get off the ground for policy choices, or does 1976 show that worst-case reasoning just relocates the ignorance rather than taming it?

I don't want a balanced survey of positions here -- I have plenty of that in my notes already. Take a definite stand: which decision rule, if any, actually justifies what the administration did, and does the episode count as evidence for or against maximin-style reasoning as a default in public health? If you think the honest verdict is that the decision was defensible ex ante and just unlucky, argue that; if you think it fails under every rule on the table including the ones its defenders invoke, argue that instead. Just commit to something and show the work, because my editor is going to push back hard on whatever I write and I need to know where the argument is stronges.
