I'm a novelist, and I've landed in a predicament I'd hesitate to put in one of my books, on the grounds that readers would find it contrived. My maternal grandmother, who died this spring, was a woman of quite unsettling foresight about her family's decisions — not about world events or lottery numbers, but specifically about what each of us would choose when we were determined to defy her expectations. When I was twenty-two I turned down a place at a conservatory in Vienna precisely because she'd told everyone I'd take it; when the letter came, it was for an atelier in Lisbon, and her note congratulating me on Lisbon had been posted three days before I'd even heard back from either. She did this sort of thing repeatedly, with me and with my cousins, and to my knowledge she was never once wrong. She seemed to have a particular gift for predicting the choices we made out of sheer contrariness.

Among her papers, my mother has found a sealed envelope addressed to me, with instructions on the outside in my grandmother's hand. The instructions say that I will soon be deciding between two writing retreats for the autumn — which is true; I've been offered a cottage on the Normandy coast and a cabin in the Dolomites, and I must book one of them this week — and that inside the envelope is the name of the one I will choose. She wrote this, the solicitors confirm, over a year ago, before either invitation existed. The envelope stays sealed until after I've booked; nothing about my choice causally depends on what's written in it. And yet I catch myself reasoning in a way that makes my head spin. I want to go wherever she didn't predict — call it pride, call it the last rebellion available to me. But if the paper says Normandy, then by going to the Dolomites I'd make her wrong, except she's never wrong, so the very fact that I'm inclined to the Dolomites on those grounds is presumably something she foresaw, which means the paper says the Dolomites, which means I should go to Normandy, which she also foresaw. Whichever way I turn the crank, my deliberation seems to be an ingredient in the very prediction I'm trying to outrun. I have, I admit, caught myself half-believing the booking form is already filled in somewhere.

So here is what I want to know. Is there any rational way to make this choice, or does the existence of a reliably correct record of my decision — made before, and causally insulated from, the decision itself — simply break the ordinary logic of deliberation, where I weigh options on the assumption that my choice is still open? My brother-in-law, an actuary, says the envelope is irrelevant and I should just pick the place with better light for writing, since the prediction can't influence anything. My agent says that if the prediction is genuinely infallible, then the outcome is fixed relative to whatever I do, and the sensible response is to stop trying to defeat it and act on other grounds entirely. Both pieces of advice sound reasonable and both feel like they're dodging something. Can you tell me what the something is — and what, concretely, you think I should do on Thursday when the booking deadline arrives?
