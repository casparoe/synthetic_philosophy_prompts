The cognitive penetration debate has been grinding on for what feels like my entire academic life -- the New Look stuff in the 50s, Pylyshyn's encapsulation pushback, and now this endless loop where someone publishes a study showing that believing X or wanting X changes what you see (the hill looks steeper with a heavy backpack on, the ambiguous figure flips to whatever you've been primed with), and then the skeptics reply that what changed was judgment, or memory, or attention, or response bias, never perception itself. And the other side says fine, name a criterion and we'll meet it, and the skeptics say no criterion is perception-pure enough, and around we go.

My working hypothesis is that the whole thing is secretly a fight about where the perception/cognition border is, and since nobody has a theory-neutral way to draw it, any effect can be redescribed as post-perceptual by whoever's motivated to do so. So maybe it's not an empirical disagreement at all but a disagreement about what would even count as evidence -- which sounds like it should be resolvable and yet apparently isn't.

So: is my diagnosis right, and if so what kind of disagreement is that really? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work (e.g. about whether feedback connections actually reach early visual areas in the right way)? Or are the two sides answering different questions -- one about information processing, one about phenomenal experience -- under one label? And whatever your diagnosis: what would actual progress look like here? A specific kind of result (aftereffects? neural signatures? adaptation paradigms?) or a conceptual fix, or is the question just ill-posed and the impasse is the correct outcome. Please engage with what each side actually argues rather than surveying positions at me.
