For a piece I am writing on the vocabulary we reach for when AI systems go wrong, I have been following the dispute over how to characterize fluent falsehoods produced by large language models. Three positions keep recurring, and after several years the conversation seems to be circling. One camp, invoking Frankfurt's well-known account of bullshit as speech produced with indifference to its truth rather than with intent to deceive, argues that LLM output is a paradigm case: the systems are trained to produce plausible text, not to track truth, so calling their errors "bullshit" is not loose journalism but precise philosophy. A second camp objects that bullshitting, like lying, is something an agent does — it requires a speaker who could have cared about the truth and declined to — and since an LLM has no beliefs, intentions, or anything that could play the role of indifference, the correct term is "hallucination," "confabulation," or simply "error," borrowed from psychiatry or engineering. A third camp says the whole fight is terminological squeamishness: these are functional labels for a failure mode, and arguing about which word fits adds nothing to safety, evaluation, or policy.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the impasse is really a disagreement about what a correct diagnosis is for — that the first camp is doing conceptual analysis of public discourse, where what matters is how confident falsehood functions regardless of its source, while the second camp is doing philosophy of mind, where the absence of the relevant mental states is the whole point, and each side's term is correct relative to its own project. But I may be too quick to dismiss the possibility that something substantive is at stake, for instance in how responsibility gets assigned when these systems are deployed.

So: is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or several disagreements sharing a vocabulary? Diagnose it using what each position actually claims, and say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a sharper distinction between kinds of failure, an empirical finding about how these systems generate text, a stipulation about which purposes each term serves — or make the case that no resolution is available and explain why the question resists one.
