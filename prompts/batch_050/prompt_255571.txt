So I write a column covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about why karma still has such a grip on people who'd describe themselves as totally secular. Before I interview anyone I figured I should pin down what I actually think, so here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, is running underneath them.

- A guy who runs a scam preying on retirees loses everything to an even bigger Ponzi scheme. Deeply satisfying. That feels like karma doing exactly its job.
- A genuinely kind, scrupulously honest woman dies at 40 of some awful rare cancer. My gut says a karma theory has to say something here -- past life, unripened fruit, whatever -- it's not allowed to just shrug. Which is weird of me, because:
- A baby born with a painful genetic disease, and when someone floats that this is the fruit of deeds in a past life, I find that genuinely offensive rather than explanatory. But that's the exact same move I demanded in the previous case. I cannot articulate the difference and it bugs me.
- If karma just means character causation -- liars lose people's trust, cruel people end up isolated -- that feels too thin, like the theory's been explained away instead of defended.
- But if karma means some cosmic bookkeeper handing out rewards and punishments, that feels crude, it turns morality into accounting, and anyway it seems incompatible with the no-god, no-self Buddhism the idea actually comes out of.
- On that no-self point: if there's no continuing self across rebirths, then the being who suffers isn't strictly the one who did the deed. That stops looking like justice and starts looking like punishing the neighbors kid for something my kid did.
- The scam artist wins the lottery. Verdict: this does NOT disconfirm karma (past merit, the fruit hasn't ripened yet, whatever). But I notice I never extend that kind of flexibility to theories I dislike, so I'm suspicious of myself on this one.
- And the one that really gets me: if suffering is deserved, it seems like compassion for the sufferer gets undercut. My gut says no, compassion is still required, full stop -- but I can't see how the theory makes room for that.

What I want: is there one coherent account of karmic desert that captures most of these, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing which job? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop, and is there a more principled way to keep the cancer case and the baby case apart, or do I actually have to pick one? And tell me what the resulting account says about two cases I didn't list: someone who does good purely to rack up merit, and someone who completely reforms before any of the bad fruit has ripened.

Also please write the answer in American English -- my editors will make me change anything else anyway, so save me the pass.
