Quick backround: I'm in a reading group on medieval ethics, currently doing Abelard, and one guy keeps ending every discussion with the line 'the sin is in the consent, not the act.' He used it last week to dismiss my question about whether someone who fully consents to something terrible but gets interrupted before doing anything is just as bad as someone who actually goes through with it — he said Abelard already settled that, the whole thing happens on the inside, end of story. Then two weeks earlier he used the same line for a kind of opposite move, arguing that a soldier who kills in a just war with a clear conscience has done nothing wrong whatsoever and shouldn't feel a shred of guilt.

I don't see how one slogan can do both those jobs unless it's ambiguous, so: what are the different precise claims 'the sin is in the consent, not the act' could actually be making? Lay them out as a menu, not one reading explained in depth. Some dimensions I suspect matter but can't pin down myself: whether it's a claim about what makes anything sinful at all versus what makes a sin worse; whether 'consent' is doing real work as something stronger than desire or urge (Abelard seems to think you can feel a pull toward something without consenting to it, which is apparently why temptation itself isn't sin, but I'm fuzzy on where the line is); whether it's saying consequences are morally irrelevant full stop or just irrelevant to blameworthiness; and whether it cuts both ways — bad consent making an outwardly 'good' act sinful, as well as clean consent excusing an outwardly bad one.

For each reading on the menu: say whether it's a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or something probably false; whether it's plausibly Abelard's actual view, reconstructed as best you can — I know he leans on odd test cases, like the servant who kills his own master in self-defence and the mother who accidentally smothers her baby trying to keep it warm, and his thing about persecutors who sincerely believe they're pleasing God is in there somewhere; and whether the reading would actually license each of my reading-group guy's two uses. My suspicion is the first use needs a much stronger reading than the second, so check that.

Finally, put your money down: which reading do you think Abelard actually held, and what would change your mind? E.g. if one of those test cases works differently than I've remembered it, would that sink your preferred reading, and where in the disambiguation are you least confident?
