I'm preparing a lecture for a course on collective action problems, and I want to test whether the canonical models actually earn their keep when applied to a real case rather than to stylised parables about shepherds and pastures. The case I've settled on is the collapse of the northern cod fishery off Newfoundland, culminating in the 1992 moratorium. The salient facts, as I understand them: for centuries the inshore fishery was prosecuted by small local fleets at sustainable levels; from the 1960s onwards, industrial factory trawlers (Canadian and foreign, before the 1977 extension of Canada's exclusive economic zone) took enormous catches offshore; Canadian federal scientists and the Department of Fisheries and Oceans set quotas based on stock assessments that later proved far too optimistic; inshore fishers repeatedly reported that the fish were disappearing, and were largely disregarded; and when the moratorium finally came, tens of thousands lost their livelihoods and the stock has never properly recovered.

Analyse this episode through the lens of the tragedy of the commons, treated as a game-theoretic model: identify precisely who the players are (and note that there may be more than one natural way to carve them up -- individual fishers, fishing firms, the federal government, foreign fleets), what each player's options and payoffs look like, and in what sense the interaction approximates an n-person prisoner's dilemma rather than, say, an open-access race in which restraining oneself merely leaves fish for the next boat. Then tell me where the model breaks or goes silent. Some candidate pressure points I'd like you to assess, though you needn't limit yourself to them: the fishery was not in fact unmanaged -- quotas existed, so this was a regulated commons that failed through epistemic error and institutional incentives rather than through pure absence of rules; the collapse looks as much like a principal--agent problem between regulators, scientists, and industry as like a free-for-all; and Hardin's own prescribed remedies (privatisation or state control) were arguably present in part and did not prevent the outcome.

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: what does this episode show about the theory? Does it vindicate the tragedy framework with the caveat that governance can itself be captured or incompetent? Does it vindicate Ostrom's rival claim that commons tragedies are failures of particular institutional designs rather than of commons as such -- and if so, which of her design principles were missing here? Or is the honest conclusion that the tragedy model explains the incentive structure but is mute about the thing that actually killed the fishery, namely the interaction between scientific uncertainty, short political time horizons, and the discounting of local ecological knowledge? Please write in British English, and be disciplined about the mapping: where the model simply has no variable corresponding to a feature of the case, say so rather than stretching it to fit.
