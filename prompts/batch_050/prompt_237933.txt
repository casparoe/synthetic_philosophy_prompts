I'm TAing this term for a political philosophy seminar where the convenor does the week on luck egalitarianism entirely through cases — she reads out a little story, gives her verdict on whether the state owes the person compensation, and lets the theory emerge from the pattern. I've been keeping a tally because I suspect she's smuggling in more than one principle, and her rulings so far are: (1) someone born with a severe congenital disability, clearly owed compensation; (2) someone who blew a chunk of discretionary savings at the casino for fun and is otherwise comfortable, owed nothing; (3) someone who deliberately cultivated expensive tastes (the champagne-and-caviar sort of case) and now needs a higher income to feel as satisfied as everyone else, owed nothing; (4) someone hit by a freak, genuinely uninsurable accident and left unable to work, owed compensation; (5) a farmer who declined cheap, widely purchased crop insurance and then lost a season's profits to drought — note, profits, he's still well-off — owed nothing. So far so consistent, you'd think.

Next week she's unveiling a new case and I've got a pint riding on calling it in advance: a middle-class motorcycle enthusiast, always wears a helmet, never rides recklessly, crashes on a weekend leisure ride and ends up with a permanent disability plus medical bills that drag him below what anyone would call a decent minimum. Injury insurance was available but pricey, and he declined it. Here's what I want from you. First, lay out the candidate rules that fit all five of her existing verdicts — I'm assuming at least two survive, something like a strict brute-luck-versus-option-luck line on one hand and a sufficiency floor on the other, but don't let my guess constrain you. Second, say what each candidate predicts for the motorcyclist. Third, and this is the part I care about, commit: tell me which rule she's most plausibly running, given the whole track record, and why — I'd genuinely rather have a wrong definite answer than a menu. Also name the single earlier verdict that's doing the most diagnostic work, and describe the one follow-up case that would best seperate your favoured reading from its strongest rival.

Give me the candidate rules as a table with exactly these columns: Rule name | Core test it applies | Verdict on the motorcyclist (compensate / refuse) | One-sentence reasoning | Most diagnostic earlier verdict (case number). After the table, prose is fine for the committed pick and the follow-up case. Oh, and write the answer in American English — the seminar's shared doc is US-spelled and I'd like to paste it straight in.
