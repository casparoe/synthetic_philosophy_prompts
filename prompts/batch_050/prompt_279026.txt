Here's an argument I put together after reading up on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and I hate where it lands. Numbered so we can fight about it.

(1) Harsanyi's result, roughly: if social welfare respects unanimous individual preferences over risky prospects, and both individual and social preferences obey the standard expected-utility axioms, then social welfare has to be a weighted sum of individual utilities. Linearity is forced by the axioms, not bolted on as an extra moral assumption.

(2) Nothing in that argument essentially requires distinct people. Take one life and treat each time-slice of the person as an "individual," with momentary well-being as that individual's utility. Same axioms, same theorem: the value of a life is a weighted sum of the well-being of its stages. (The weights might let in time discounting, but nothing else.)

(3) So the *shape* of a life can't matter to how good it is. A life that starts badly and steadily improves, and one that starts great and slowly decays, are equally good if the totals match. A crushing year at 25 is exactly compensated by a good year at 60. Redemption arcs, regret, things finally coming together late — none of it registers except through the sum.

(4) That's nuts. Trajectory and timing are plainly part of how good a life is; almost nobody is actually indifferent between the uphill and downhill versions.

(5) So either the expected-utility axioms are wrong, or Harsanyi's theorem doesn't show what people take it to show.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd like to keep (1) -- the theorem seems real, and fine in the interpersonal case. So presumably the break is somewhere in (2) or (3), but I can't put my finger on what goes wrong when you move from people to time-slices. If anything aggregation within one subject seems *more* legit than across strangers. Also: does your answer change if the shape preferences in (4) are things I actually have, versus things I only think I should have?
