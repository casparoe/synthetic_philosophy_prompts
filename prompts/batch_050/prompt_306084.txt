I am a philosophy professor preparing a single 75-minute lecture for second-year undergraduates who have done some decision theory (they know payoff matrices and Nash equilibria) but have never read Lewis. My aim is to teach Lewis's account of convention in a way that also sets up, at the end, the idea that norms can evolve and stabilize without anyone designing or agreeing to them. I do not want the explanation itself written out for me; I want a plan for how to deliver it.

Please set out:

1. The dependency structure: which components of Lewis's account must be in place before later ones make sense, and why. My own sense is that I need to establish (a) the notion of a coordination problem with multiple proper coordination equilibria, (b) the higher-order knowledge condition, (c) salience and precedent as the non-rational glue, and only then (d) the definition of convention as a regularity sustained by everyone's conditional preference to conform given that others do. Tell me whether this order is right, where students will stumble if I get the dependencies wrong, and whether the higher-order knowledge condition should come before or after the concrete example.

2. The carrying example: one case that should run through the entire lecture. Lewis's own cases are driving on the right and the dropped telephone call; I am tempted to use a campus example, such as the emergence of a de facto meeting spot or of a shared convention for how to split a group dinner bill. Advise me on what the carrying example must contain and must not contain -- in particular, it must be arbitrary in the right way (an alternative equilibrium was available) but must not already involve explicit agreement, sanctions, or moral valence, since those are exactly the things I want to strip away. Tell me whether my candidates qualify and why or why not.

3. The misconception map: the confusions these students predictably bring or form, in the order they will surface, and the exact point in my sequence where I should head each one off. I anticipate at least these: that convention requires an actual act of agreement or a contract; that convention is just habit or statistical regularity (the sun rises regularly too); that if a convention is arbitrary it cannot be important or worth following; that salience means the rational or optimal option; and, most dangerously for my closing move, that a convention in Lewis's sense is the same thing as a social norm with attendant obligations and punishments. For each, tell me whether I am right that it will arise, what the student who holds it is half-correct about, and where in the lecture to defuse it rather than letting it fester.

4. How to land the closing move: with perhaps ten minutes left, I want to gesture from static convention to the evolution of norms -- how precedents compound, how a mere regularity can acquire normative weight, and how a settled convention can be displaced. Give me one question to pose that makes the students generate this dynamic themselves from the materials already on the board, rather than my asserting it.

5. A check for understanding: one exercise or question for the final minutes that would show the definition has actually landed -- something that would catch a student who memorized the definition but cannot use it. For instance, I could give them money, language, queuing, and tipping, and ask which are Lewis-conventions and what sustains each; tell me whether this discriminates well or suggest something better.

6. What to omit and say I am omitting: the technical apparatus (common knowledge defined rigorously, the full formal definition with its clauses) is available but presumably counterproductive at this level. Tell me what to cut, what to flag as a deliberate simplification, and where the simplification will come back to bite in a later course if I do not flag it.
