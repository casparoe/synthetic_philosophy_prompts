The question on the problem set was:

**Question 4 (25 pts).** In many cosmological models with an extremely long-lived vacuum, rare thermal or quantum fluctuations will occasionally assemble a "Boltzmann brain": a fleeting observer that comes into existence complete with apparent memories of a world like ours. In some of these models, Boltzmann brains end up vastly outnumbering "ordinary" observers who evolved on planets. (a) Explain why cosmologists take this to count *against* a model, rather than treating it as a harmless curiosity — and be precise about why the issue is not simply that it's improbable that we are Boltzmann brains. (b) Consider the following reply: "We know we aren't Boltzmann brains, because our experiences are coherent and our memories hang together, and a brain assembled by a random fluctuation wouldn't have coherent experiences like that." Assess it. (c) Describe one way a cosmologist could modify or constrain a model to avoid the problem, and say what the downside of that fix is.

And here's the student's answer:

*(a)* The worry goes back to Boltzmann's own suggestion that our low-entropy universe might be a rare statistical fluctuation away from equilibrium. In an equilibrium universe that lasts forever, fluctuations of every size eventually occur, and small ones are exponentially more frequent than big ones. A fluctuation just large enough to produce a single brain with memories of an orderly world is vastly more probable than one producing actual stars, planets, and evolved observers. So in any model where the vacuum persists long enough, fluctuation-produced observers will swamp ordinary observers by an enormous ratio. That means the model predicts that a generic observer with my evidence is almost certainly a fluctuation brain. So the problem isn't that it's unlikely that I'm a Boltzmann brain — given such a model, it isn't unlikely at all. The real problem is that the prediction is self-undermining: a fluctuation brain's memories and reasoning are products of chance, so if I took the model seriously I'd have to regard my own evidence and reasoning, including whatever led me to the model, as untrustworthy. A theory that tells me not to trust my grounds for believing it is in serious trouble even if it contains no strict inconsistency.

*(b)* I think the reply basically works. A fluctuation would have to assemble the brain into some particular state, and the overwhelming majority of physically possible brain-states do not contain coherent, mutually reinforcing memories of a lawful world. Since our actual mental lives are coherent in exactly that way, our experience is strong evidence that we are the products of an orderly process rather than of a fluctuation. So we can after all know that we're not Boltzmann brains.

*(c)* One fix is to favor models in which the vacuum eventually decays, or in which the universe has only a finite future lifetime, so there isn't enough time for fluctuations to dominate the observer count; cosmologists can also restrict themselves to probability measures that don't assign overwhelming weight to the eternally persisting de Sitter regime. The downside is that these moves can look ad hoc, chosen precisely to dodge the conclusion, and they may rule out otherwise attractive models. Alternatively, one might argue the scenario never gets going in the first place: a fluctuation that assembled a working brain out of a high-entropy vacuum would be such an extreme violation of the second law of thermodynamics that it can be ruled out physically, not merely assigned a low probability.
