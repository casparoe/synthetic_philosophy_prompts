I'm a software engineer with about a decade of experience building distributed systems — consensus protocols, replicated state machines, the usual — and no formal philosophy background. I recently came across Aumann's agreement theorem, the result often glossed as "rational agents with common priors cannot agree to disagree," and I have a strong suspicion that it is, at bottom, a statement about systems I already understand. I'd like you to test that suspicion rigorously rather than flatter it.

Act as a professional philosopher specialising in formal epistemology and the foundations of game theory who is also genuinely comfortable with distributed computing — someone who will not let me get away with a sloppy analogy.

Here is the task. First, restate the theorem as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: processes, local state, message passing, broadcast, failure models (crash and Byzantine), the Two Generals' Problem, common knowledge as it figures in the coordinated-attack impossibility results, and eventual consistency. Map the pieces explicitly: the agents, the common prior, each agent's private information and the partition model of knowledge, the announcement of posteriors, and the convergence result itself. Where no clean counterpart exists, say so rather than forcing one.

Second, the residue report — the part I care most about. Which components of the theorem translate cleanly? Which survive only with distortion (I suspect "common prior" is one: is a shared genesis configuration plus a deterministic update rule really the same thing, and does Bayesian updating have any real analogue in a state machine)? And which have no expression at all? Two candidates I'd like scrutinised: the sincerity of the agents (a Byzantine node "reporting its posterior" is presumably nothing like an honest agent stating a belief), and the normative sting of the theorem — in engineering, two replicas holding different values is just a state of the system, not a rational failing, so does the theorem's you-must-revise force evaporate in translation?

Third, the verdict, run in both directions. If something in the philosophers' debate — say, the plausibility of the common-prior assumption, or whether the theorem bears on real human disagreement at all — has no engineering counterpart, is that because my vocabulary is missing something real, or because that part of the debate was never as substantive as it looked? Conversely, does anything about how consensus protocols actually behave suggest the theorem is less surprising than philosophers take it to be?

Please begin with a short, explicit brainstorm listing the candidate mappings before committing to any of them, flag the mappings you are least confident in, and think carefully about where the translation breaks rather than smoothing over the breaks. No bibliography or citations are needed; I'll look up any sources myself. Please write your answer in British English.
