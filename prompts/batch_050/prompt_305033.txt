A driver is traveling along a road with exactly two exits. He knows the road layout in advance. If he takes the first exit, his payoff is 0; if he passes it and takes the second, his payoff is 4; if he passes both, his payoff is 1. Unfortunately, he suffers from complete short-term amnesia: whenever he reaches an exit, he cannot remember whether he has already passed one. His evidence at either exit is therefore exactly the same.

A behavioral policy is represented by a probability \(p\) of continuing at any reached exit, with the randomization performed independently on each occasion. Ex ante, his expected payoff is

\[
V(p)=4p(1-p)+p^2=4p-3p^2.
\]

This is maximized at \(p=2/3\), yielding \(V(2/3)=4/3\).

A friend argues that this optimization cannot really identify a rational policy:

1. A mixed plan is implementable only if, once the driver finds himself at an exit, his own expected-utility reasoning does not strictly favor departing from it. A plan that deliberation would overturn whenever it is used cannot guide action.

2. Once at an exit, the driver's evidence is exhausted by the fact that he is at either the first or the second exit. Since his evidence does not favor either possibility, he should assign probability \(1/2\) to each.

3. Taking the present exit then has expected value
   \[
   \tfrac12(0)+\tfrac12(4)=2.
   \]

4. Suppose instead that he continues on this occasion and that the \(p=2/3\) policy resumes thereafter. If he is at the first exit, he reaches the second, where the policy yields
   \[
   \tfrac13(4)+\tfrac23(1)=2.
   \]
   If he is already at the second exit, continuing ends the trip with payoff 1. Continuing therefore has expected value
   \[
   \tfrac12(2)+\tfrac12(1)=\tfrac32.
   \]

5. Thus, at the moment of decision, exiting is strictly better than following the supposedly optimal mixed plan.

6. Because the driver's evidence is identical at every exit he reaches, the same local verdict must arise every time. Rational deliberation therefore leads him to exit whenever possible, producing payoff 0.

7. Since the ex ante optimal policy has expected value \(4/3\), no policy is both ex ante optimal and stable under the driver's own deliberation. Expected-utility theory consequently gives contradictory guidance under imperfect recall.

The conclusion seems to prove too much: the setup assigns perfectly definite expected values to the available policies, and continuing with probability \(2/3\) is ex ante better than always exiting. Yet each step in the friend's reasoning has some plausibility.

Identify the first numbered step you reject, and explain why it initially seemed reasonable. Show how the argument changes once that step is corrected, including how the relevant self-locating probabilities should be calculated and whether the one-off deviation considered in step 4 is actually available to an agent who cannot distinguish the two occasions. If more than one step is defective, explain which defect is fundamental and which is merely downstream. Finally, state what must be given up under your diagnosis: ex ante optimization, moment-by-moment reoptimization, unrestricted commitment to a policy, or some principle connecting rational plans with the judgments an agent makes while executing them.
