Two distinct reasons are commonly given for why an agent's reward function cannot be uniquely recovered from its observed behavior, and I suspect they are often conflated. The first is that there exists at least one degenerate reward function -- such as the constant-zero reward -- under which literally every policy is optimal, so no amount of behavioral observation can rule out total indifference. The second is that even among non-degenerate rewards, whole families of reward functions (for instance, those related by potential-based shaping, where the reward is modified by a term of the form γΦ(s') − Φ(s)) induce identical optimal behavior.

Please explain: (a) why these are genuinely different obstacles to identifiability rather than one obstacle stated twice; (b) what kinds of additional assumption -- about the observer's model of the agent's rationality, about the structural form rewards are allowed to take, or about how the behavior was generated -- can break each kind of symmetry, and whether a single assumption can break both at once; and (c) what all of this implies for the project of inferring human preferences from choices. Does non-identifiability show that such inference is hopeless, or only that "the agent's preferences" must be understood as an equivalence class of reward functions -- and if the latter, is knowing the equivalence class enough for the purposes (predicting behavior, assisting the agent, auditing its goals) for which we wanted the inference in the first place?
