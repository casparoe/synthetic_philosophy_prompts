I'm a second-year undergraduate taking moral philosophy and intermediate microeconomics in the same semester, and I'd like to use the economics to stress-test the philosophy. In the philosophy course we've been working through demandingness objections to consequentialism: the complaint that if morality requires always producing the best available outcome, then it absorbs one's whole life -- every hour and dollar not spent relieving suffering is a moral failure -- and that this is grounds for rejecting the theory rather than accepting the conclusion. I'm fluent in the standard toolkit of constrained optimization: objective and social welfare functions, feasible sets, Lagrangians and shadow prices, comparative statics, corner versus interior solutions, lexicographic preferences, and the difference between a hard constraint and a cost entering the objective function.

Please translate the main positions in this debate, one by one, into that vocabulary. Include at least: the simple complaint that act consequentialism is excessively demanding; the strategy of carving out an agent-centered prerogative that permits (without requiring) giving some weight to one's own projects; satisficing rather than maximizing versions of consequentialism; the restriction of stringent duties to not harming, with aiding left optional; and rule consequentialism as an indirect theory. For each position, state precisely what it becomes when expressed as a claim about objective functions, constraints, or solution concepts; say what part of the original claim survives the translation intact, what has to be distorted or quietly altered to fit (for instance, whether an agent-centered prerogative is best modeled as a constraint on the feasible set, as extra weight on one's own welfare in the objective, or as something else, and what each modeling choice commits one to); and what, if anything, has no expression in this framework at all.

The part I care most about is the residue. Candidates I suspect may not survive translation: the doing/allowing distinction, the idea that a prerogative is permission rather than incentive, and the phenomenology of a moral requirement as opposed to a high price. For each untranslatable element, tell me whether its loss exposes a genuine limitation of the optimization vocabulary -- things morality can say that this framework structurally cannot -- or whether the translation failure instead reveals that the original moral claim was doing less work than it appeared to, and the economic rendering is actually a clarification. Where you think the answer is mixed, say so and explain which cases fall on which side.
