I serve on the board of trustees of a county historical society in a former mill town, and I need to circulate a written opinion before our vote at next month's meeting. The facts: in 1968 a fire at the town's textile mill killed thirty-one night-shift workers, and in the mid-1990s the society erected a granite memorial on the mill grounds engraved with all of their names; maintaining that memorial is one of our chartered obligations. We have now received a formal petition from the grandchildren of the shift supervisor, who died in the fire but was blamed for it in the state inquiry, which found locked exits and ignored complaints. They do not contest the findings. Their argument is that the memorial, whatever its original intent, now functions as a permanent public accusation, that their family has lived under the town's blame for two generations, and that even if a community has a right to remember, it does not follow that it must remember forever, in stone, in this form. Several other victims' families have counter-petitioned, arguing that the wall is the one place the town tells the full truth about what happened, that the supervisor's death in the fire makes him one of the dead however culpable he was in life, and that letting any name be struck turns a memorial into a public-relations exercise answerable to whoever lobbies hardest.

In preparing I have been reading around the ethics of memory, and two books have framed my thinking without settling it. Avishai Margalit's The Ethics of Memory defends the idea that shared memory is what binds people into thick relationships and genuine communities, and that such communities owe their dead remembrance as a matter of fidelity rather than sentiment. David Rieff's In Praise of Forgetting argues almost the opposite: that organized collective remembrance more often keeps grievance alive than heals it, that memory is routinely weaponized, and that a deliberate, sanctioned forgetting can be a real civic achievement rather than a failure. Both seem to me to capture something true, and the petition forces the question in a form my abstract reading never did. Does a memorial belong to the dead, to their descendants, or to the community that built and maintains it, and whose claim governs when they conflict? Is removing a name an act of forgetting, an act of mercy, or an act of falsification, and how much turns on which description is correct?

Please draft the opinion I will circulate, roughly 800 words, addressed to my fellow trustees in my voice. It should recommend a concrete disposition of this petition — remove the name, retain it, or some defined middle course such as recontextualization with an interpretive plaque — and, just as importantly, it should recommend a general policy the board can adopt for handling future requests to remove, add, or alter names, since this will not be the last one we receive. Whatever you recommend, the opinion must engage the strongest version of the losing side seriously rather than merely acknowledging it, because several trustees are genuinely undecided and will not be moved by a one-sided brief. End the document with a machine-readable line giving the bottom-line recommendation on the petition itself, in the form \answer{...}.
