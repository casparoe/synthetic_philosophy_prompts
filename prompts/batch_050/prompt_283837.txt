Ok so I'm on the yearbook committee at my school and me and my co-editor Maya got into this arguement I can't stop thinking about. We found a stack of yearbooks from the 90s in the storage room and our history teacher was going through them with us, pointing out which kids in the candid photos were notorious troublemakers back then and which ones went on to be semi-famous or whatever. Here's the weird part: after he told us, the photos genuinely looked different to me. One guy's smirk went from friendly to shifty and I swear his whole face seemed colder. Maya says that's impossible — the light hitting your eyeballs is exactly the same before and after, so the only thing that changed is your opinion about the photo, not what you actually see of it. But it doesn't feel like I changed my opinion. It feels like I'm looking at a different face.

We sort of tested it on ourselves. We printed candids from this year with no names attached and ranked them for the best-candid page, then ranked them again a week later with the names showing, and our rankings moved around a lot. Maya says that proves her point — our judgments shift once we know who's who, but the seeing part stays fixed the whole time. I said it could just as easily prove mine, because once you know someone got suspended you can't unsee it, and how would we even tell the difference from the inside? The thing neither of us can figure out is whether the line between seeing something and judging it is a real line at all — like is there an actual fact of the matter about where seeing stops and thinking starts, or is that distinction just something we assume?

This matters beyond settling the bet, because we're supposed to pick this year's candids by the end of the month and the current plan is for teachers to pre-sort the good ones first. If knowing stuff about the kids warps how the photos look to us and not just what we say about them, would covering up the names even help, or is it already too late once we recognize people? So two questions: who's more right, me or Maya, and what should we actually do about the picking process? Please don't just give me generic everyone-has-biases-try-to-be-objective advice, I want the actual substance on whether what you know can change what you see. Also be upfront about which parts of this are basically settled among the scientists and philosophers who study perception and which parts are still genuinely fought over — don't make one side sound like the consensus if it isn't. And at the end, put a short table with four columns: the claim or question at stake, whether it's settled / contested / just unknown, the strongest point on each side, and what it implies for how we should run the photo picks.
