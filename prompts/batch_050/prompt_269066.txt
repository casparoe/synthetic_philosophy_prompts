I keep running into a forecasting move and I can’t tell if it’s a named fallacy or just a few ordinary ones glued together. Someone wants to say how long a big state or empire has left. They start with all the obvious past cases, then prune: not Rome, no modern weapons; not the Ottomans, no internet or instant capital flight; not Britain, decolonisation was half-chosen; not the short-lived ones, they were never proper empires; not the unusually long-lived ones, they were just lucky with geography. Each exclusion sounds sensible in the pub, but by the end the comparison pile is basically twelve cases selected because they share the feature being predicted, so the “typical lifespan” is the conclusion wearing a wig.

It feels sharper than cherry-picking, because the pruning is done by rules rather than just grabbing favourite examples. It also isn’t quite survivorship bias, because sometimes they keep failures and throw out successes. And it isn’t only the general problem that any two things are similar in some respects and different in others; the weird bit is the feedback loop, where the set of past cases is tuned until the historical frequency says what the tuner already thought, while every single tuning step is locally defensible.

Is there an established name for that exact loop? If it’s near the reference-class problem, overfitting, the Texas sharpshooter thing, or No True Scotsman, can you say exactly where it matches and where it doesn’t? If there’s no clean name, coin one and define it with boundary cases. I’d especially like a practical diagnostic question you could ask mid-argument — something like, would that exclusion rule have been written down before seeing the outcome you want? — and what a non-rigged version of empire-forecasting would have to do differently.
