I'm TAing an undergrad bioethics seminar this spring and the unit on the definition of death always goes the same way: the students memorize "whole-brain death vs. cardiopulmonary death" as if it were a two-party system and then get totally lost when I mention the DCD protocols (donation after circulatory death) or the Shewmon cases. So I want to give them a decision tree instead of a lecture. Here's the angle I'd like you to build it around. In the 1981 President's Commission report Defining Death, the commissioners argue, roughly, that death is the collapse of the functioning of the organism as a whole, and that the brain earns its privileged place in the criteria because it is the body's central integrator -- not because consciousness is what we care about. (I'm paraphrasing from memory, so don't quote me on the wording, but that two-step -- death as loss of organismic integration first, brain as integrator second -- is the load-bearing move.) The problem is that the second step looks empirically shaky now: there are patients who meet the standard tests for whole-brain death whose bodies, with a ventilator and some hormone support, maintain homeostasis, fight infections, gestate pregnancies, even go through puberty for years (the famous case is a boy diagnosed brain dead at four whose body lived about two more decades). So the tree's first node should interrogate exactly that passage: does the respondent think organismic integration is the right concept of death at all, and if so, does the brain-dead body on life support still count as an integrated organism?

From there, build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names -- I want maybe six to eight branch questions, each phrased so a student can answer it without knowing where it leads (e.g., a question about whether a permanently comatose patient with a brainstem-level thermostat and no capacity for consciousness is dead yet, rather than "do you endorse higher-brain death?"). The leaves I definitely need covered: the traditional whole-brain position that keeps the Commission's two-step and bites the bullet on the chronic cases; a higher-brain or consciousness-based account; a circulation-centered view that takes the Shewmon cases to show the brain was never the integrator and relocates death in irreversible loss of the capacity for spontaneous circulation; and the deflationary corner (Miller and Truog territory) that says the metaphysics won't settle it, death is partly a moral decision, and we'd be more honest justifying organ retrieval directly instead of policing the line. If your logical space spits out a combination nobody defends -- say, someone who accepts integration as the concept but thinks a decapitated body on perfusion is still alive -- include the empty leaf and tell me in a sentence whether it's empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Two more constraints. Somewhere in the tree, force a branch on the DCD waiting period: after the heart stops, protocols wait somewhere between two and five minutes before procurement, and I want a node asking what would have to be true about autoresuscitation and brain perfusion for that pause to mark irreversible loss rather than just actual loss -- this is the node where my students' intuitions genuinely split, and it should route people toward different leaves rather than being a dead end. And flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree can't cleanly separate (I suspect whole-brain bullet-biters and a certain strain of circulation-focused view will land on the same answers to every question I can pose to undergrads, in which case say so and give me the one case or thought experiment that could prise them apart). Aim for something that fits on one printed page plus a half-page key explaining which real positions sit at which leaves.
