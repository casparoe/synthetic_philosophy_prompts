In my philosophy of probability seminar last week, a classmate shut down a debate by invoking what he called the Dutch book theorem: "if your degrees of belief don't conform to the probability axioms, you can be made a sure loser." His conclusion was blunt — anyone whose credences violate additivity is thereby irrational, and there is nothing more to discuss. I had been defending the possibility that a rational agent facing deep uncertainty might coherently hold, say, credence 0.45 in an event and 0.45 in its negation, refusing to force the two to sum to one, and he treated the slogan as a refutation of that position all by itself.

The exchange bothered me enough that I ran a small experiment. I constructed a friend with credence 0.6 in rain tomorrow and 0.6 in no rain, and I offered the two corresponding bets the standard argument prescribes: sell her a ticket paying one dollar if it rains for sixty cents, and a second ticket paying one dollar if it doesn't rain for sixty cents. Together the tickets cost her $1.20 and exactly one pays out one dollar, so she loses twenty cents no matter what happens. Except that she declined both bets, so nobody lost anything. When I reported this, my classmate replied that her refusal was irrelevant — the point stands whether or not she accepts. But then it is no longer clear what the slogan is even saying: in what sense *can* she be made a sure loser, and by whom, if the losing requires her cooperation and she withholds it?

I would like a careful disambiguation rather than a defense of either side. Please do the following:

1. Lay out, as a numbered list, the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing. I suspect they range from something nearly trivial (incoherent credences commit you, by your own lights, to valuing a package of bets that jointly guarantee a loss as if it were acceptable) through something substantive (any non-probabilistic credence function leaves you open to exploitation by a hypothetical adversary who knows your credences and acts only in their own interest) to something quite strong and probably false (a real exploiter exists, or rationality just is resistance to such exploitation). Include at least the readings that differ on these dimensions: whether the argument concerns the credences themselves or the agent's betting dispositions; whether the bookie must actually exist, be possible, or be merely a device for making an internal defect visible; and whether the sure loss is something that happens to the agent or something the agent's own valuations already endorse.

2. For each reading, give a verdict: is it a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false — and does it actually follow from the mathematics of the betting setup, or does it smuggle in extra premises (about what agents must accept, what fair prices commit one to, or what rationality consists in)?

3. Say which reading my classmate would need in order for the slogan to refute my original position — the claim that a rational agent may sometimes let credences in an event and its negation sum to less than one. If the only readings that get him his conclusion are among the weaker or more question-begging ones, say so explicitly.

4. Finally, address my experiment directly. Does my friend's refusal to bet undermine the argument, and does your answer change if the slogan is applied diachronically — to an agent's rule for revising credences over time, where the bookie allegedly needs only to know the agent's updating policy in advance — rather than to her credences at a single moment?

Keep the assessment at the level of the argument itself; I am not asking for a bibliography or for the history of the result.
