Okay so here's the situation. I'm a novelist, and the book I'm working on has a translator as one of the main characters -- she spends the whole book slowly realizing that a machine translation system is better than her at almost everything, except it keeps faceplanting on the same kinds of passages: jokes that depend on what a culture finds shameful, a line where the right word depends on knowing the narrator is lying, stuff like that. To write her well I need to actually know where I stand on the underlying fight, which is basically Hofstadter's old complaint: statistical/machine translation produces impressive output but doesn't *understand* anything, it's just surfing on gigantic patterns it hoovered up. A lot of people now say the newest systems have plainly defused that argument. I'm not so sure, but I'm also not sure my skepticism isn't just a novelist's professional vanity.

So: write me an essay, as a philosopher of mind who actually reads novels and doesn't treat language as just a data stream, on whether high-quality machine translation requires understanding -- and whether the success of modern systems shows that "understanding" was the wrong thing to demand all along. Think it through properly; sketch the main positions and their best arguments first before committing, and then commit -- I want a verdict, not a survey that ends with "it's complicated." To keep you honest, engage with a test I made up for my character, which I call the dedication-page test: take a novel whose dedication reads "for my sister, who never believed the ending." A translation system renders it flawlessly into twelve languages. My claim is that translating that dedication *well* requires knowing things no corpus statistics about the sentence itself contain -- e.g., whether the author actually had a sister, whether the book's ending is ambiguous in a way the dedication is winking at, what dedications are *for*. The system's defender says all of that is just more text, more context, more pattern, and my character is romanticizing. Who's right, and what does the answer imply about what understanding even is? If you think my test is rigged or confused -- say, that it smuggles in the conclusion, or that humans would fail my own test -- say that to my face rather than politely working around it.

One more constraint: don't just re-run the standard greatest hits (Chinese Room, "it's just autocomplete," etc.) as if they settle anything -- I know those; take them as read and spend your words on the harder question of what would actually count as evidence either way. And where you land, flag honestly how confident you are and what observation about future translation systems would prove you wrong. If it helps your argument to note where my novel's premise leans on a contested view of translation, do that too -- I'd rather fix the book than build it on a philosophical howler.
