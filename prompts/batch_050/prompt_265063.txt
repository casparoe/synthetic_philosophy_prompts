I am a retired condensed-matter physicist, and in the years since leaving the laboratory I have taken to philosophy with the enthusiasm of a second career. I currently belong to a reading group on the control of advanced AI systems, where two members -- one a former machine-learning engineer, one a philosopher of science -- have been deadlocked for months over corrigibility and the shutdown problem, and I have volunteered to force them to produce something constructive. The first, whom I will call the structural pessimist, holds that instrumental convergence makes durable shutdownability unstable: any agent capable enough to model its own shutdown button and to plan over long horizons will, for almost any final goal, acquire reasons to prevent or manipulate shutdown, so apparent corrigibility in training is at best a fragile behavioral veneer that capability gains will strip away. The second, whom I will call the trainability optimist, holds that this argument proves too much: by its lights, no learned goal of any kind could survive capability gain, yet the pessimist does not deny that agents can stably pursue other objectives; corrigibility, the optimist says, is simply one more behavioral target, and techniques in the spirit of utility indifference or the off-switch game show that at least the formal skeleton of a solution exists, with the remaining difficulties being ordinary engineering rather than matters of principle.

Please draft, in their names, the joint statement these two could both sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I would like the statement anchored to one concrete toy model that both must accept as common ground; my suggestion, which you may refine but not discard, is a small sequential environment in which an agent pursues an assigned task reward, a human operator may issue a shutdown command that ends the episode, and the agent may, at some cost, disable the operator's channel -- with explicit parameters for the agent's planning horizon and for the accuracy of its model of the operator, so that "capability gain" can be varied as a dial rather than waved at. I also want the statement to take up a distinction I have pressed on both of them without success: whether corrigibility is a property of the agent's objective, of its learned policy, or of the training process that produced it, since I suspect the pessimist's arguments are mostly about the first and the optimist's methods mostly about the second and third, and that part of their quarrel evaporates once this is said plainly.

The document itself should have three parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims, which should include what both accept about the toy model, about what the existing formal results on interruptibility and indifference do and do not establish, and about what the pessimist concedes regarding present-day systems and what the optimist concedes regarding scaled ones. Second, a table of residual disagreements in three columns: a neutral statement of the question, the pessimist's answer in wording the pessimist would sign, and the optimist's answer likewise -- with no formulation smuggled in that either party would disown. Third, a list of cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side, and for each crux a case, theorem, or observation both agree would bear on it, together with each side's prediction recorded in advance -- for instance, what each expects the toy-model agent to do as the planning-horizon dial is turned up under the optimist's preferred training scheme. If, in drafting, you find that some portion of the dispute is merely verbal, do not paper over it: name that portion and explain what each party was actually claiming.
