After years of reading about moral uncertainty and moral trade on rationalist blogs and forums, I assembled an argument out of ideas circulating there, and it seems to prove something I cannot accept. I want a diagnosis, not reassurance. (1) When two agents with different terminal values can each get more of what they value by coordinating on a compromise policy than by acting unilaterally on their own values, taking the compromise is simply rational: declining a Pareto improvement is leaving value on the table. (2) Each of us is morally uncertain, assigning less than unit credence to our own value system, so each individual is already a coalition of value systems, and the logic of (1) applies inside a single head just as it does between people. (3) Therefore moral compromise -- both interpersonal and intrapersonal -- is a rational requirement, not an optional courtesy extended to the likeminded. (4) Nothing in (1) through (3) refers to how the counterparty's values arose; bargaining theory takes preferences and outside options as given, so it cannot distinguish values held sincerely from values adopted strategically, precisely in order to extract concessions from compromisers. (5) Hence a rational compromiser must compromise on the same terms with an agent who deliberately made itself care intensely about something repugnant, because doing so improved its bargaining position against people like me. (6) But conceding to that agent rewards the manufacture of repugnant preferences, and a compromise norm that predictably incentivizes everyone to become more extreme in order to be bought off is self-undermining: you do not get a world of harmonious moral trade, you get an arms race of acquired fanaticism. (7) So at least one step is wrong, but I cannot identify the one I am entitled to drop. The step I keep circling is (4), yet rejecting it seems to require some principled audit of the provenance of other people's values before trading with them, and I cannot see what that test would be or how it would avoid collapsing into refusing to trade with anyone whose values I happen to dislike. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked compulsory in the ordinary cases that made the argument attractive, and what rejecting it costs. If you reject (4), I need a workable distinction between the sincere extremist and the strategic one; if you reject (1) or (2), I need to know what is left of the project of moral trade that drew me in; and if you think the argument is actually sound and the error is my squeamishness at (6), I need to know what I am being asked to swallow.
