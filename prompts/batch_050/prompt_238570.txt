I retired last spring after forty years in experimental physics, and at my leaving dinner two old friends — Denis, a particle theorist, and Margaret, a philosopher of science — fell into an argument that has stayed with me. I held my tongue at the table and would like an outside adjudication.

Denis opened: philosophy has given physics nothing in the past century. Philosophical questions either ripen into scientific ones, at which point scientists answer them, or else persist as irresolvable debate; the remainder is commentary after the event.

Margaret offered two counterexamples. Einstein's 1905 analysis of simultaneity was conceptual criticism of a recognisably philosophical kind, absorbed from Hume and Mach, and no experiment demanded it. And Bell's theorem grew directly out of the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen paper and the supposedly sterile Bohr–Einstein dispute; the entire experimental programme of testing entanglement followed from that "mere philosophy".

Denis answered twice over. First, survivorship: the same Machian positivism that inspired Einstein also kept Mach from accepting atoms long after the experimental case had convinced nearly everyone else, and a tradition that cannot distinguish its good counsel from its bad at the time is offering luck, not method. Second, rebranding: once conceptual work pays off we reclassify it as physics, so the claim that philosophy helps science is insulated from counterexamples by definition.

Margaret replied that neither move works. Science cannot identify its own good ideas in advance either, so that test would indict every heuristic scientists actually use. The rebranding point is a quarrel over labels rather than over what happened: the work was conceptual criticism, whatever we call it. And she noted that Hawking's proclamation of philosophy's death occurs inside a book defending a particular philosophy of science — the anti-philosophical stance is itself a philosophy, merely an unexamined one.
