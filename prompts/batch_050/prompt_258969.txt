EXAM QUESTION (100 points). Expected utility, risk, and rationality.

(a) Explain why, under expected utility theory, any aversion to risk must be captured entirely by the curvature of the utility function over outcomes. Using the Allais preferences (or a structurally similar example), explain why Buchak thinks there is a coherent and potentially rational attitude toward risk that no choice of utility function can represent. (25 points)

(b) Describe how risk-weighted expected utility theory (REU) evaluates a gamble. State precisely which axiom of the von Neumann–Morgenstern representation theorem the theory rejects, and explain why any theory that represents the preferences in (a) must reject it. (25 points)

(c) Let G be the gamble yielding $100 with probability 1/2 and $20 with probability 1/2. Compute the value that expected utility theory assigns to G under u(x) = x, and the value that REU assigns under u(x) = x with risk function r(p) = p². Interpret the comparison. (25 points)

(d) State the strongest objection to REU you can construct, and give Buchak's best reply. (25 points)

STUDENT ANSWER (transcribed in full).

(a) In expected utility theory, a gamble is evaluated as the probability-weighted sum of the utilities of its outcomes. Because probabilities enter linearly, there is nowhere for an attitude toward risk to live except inside the utility function: an agent who prefers $500 for certain to an even chance of $1000 must have u(500) > (u(0) + u(1000))/2, which is just diminishing marginal utility of money. But consider the Allais pattern: preferring $1 million for certain over a gamble offering $5 million with probability 0.10, $1 million with probability 0.89, and nothing with probability 0.01, while at the same time preferring a 0.10 chance of $5 million over a 0.11 chance of $1 million. Normalize u(0) = 0. The second preference says 0.10·u($5M) > 0.11·u($1M); adding 0.89·u($1M) to both sides yields 0.89·u($1M) + 0.10·u($5M) > u($1M), which contradicts the first preference. So no utility function represents both choices. Buchak's diagnosis is that the agent's preference reflects an attitude toward risk itself — toward how value is distributed across possibilities — rather than toward any outcome. The agent who takes the sure million is not irrationally undervaluing money; she assigns special weight to securing a good outcome and avoiding the worst one. Since probabilities enter EU only linearly, no reshaping of u can express this.

(b) REU supplements the utility function with a risk function r, which is non-decreasing with r(0) = 0 and r(1) = 1. Rather than taking a probability-weighted average of utilities, the theory transforms the probabilities themselves: each outcome contributes its utility weighted by r applied to its probability, so REU(g) = Σ r(p_i)·u(x_i). An agent is risk-avoidant when r is convex and lies below the identity, as r(p) = p² does, and risk-seeking when r is concave. The theory retains completeness, transitivity, and continuity; what it gives up is the independence axiom. It must, because the Allais pattern in (a) is precisely a violation of independence — the common 0.89 consequence cancels under independence, forcing the two choices to stand or fall together — so any representation of those preferences has to reject the axiom outright.

(c) Expected utility: EU(G) = 0.5·100 + 0.5·20 = 60. Risk-weighted expected utility: REU(G) = r(0.5)·100 + r(0.5)·20 = 0.25·100 + 0.25·20 = 30. The thirty-point gap between the risk-weighted value and the expected value measures the price this agent attaches to the gamble's riskiness: she is indifferent between G and $30 for certain. A risk-neutral agent, with r(p) = p, recovers the expected value of 60, as she should.

(d) The strongest objection is that REU purchases its flexibility at too high a price. Independence is not an arbitrary technicality; it underwrites the dominance reasoning in the von Neumann–Morgenstern theorem, and a theory that abandons it will sometimes rank a gamble above one that stochastically dominates it — preferring a lottery to another that pays at least as much in every state. Most people find that consequence very hard to accept. Buchak's reply is that independence is the very principle the Allais agent rejects, so wielding it against REU assumes precisely what is in dispute; the rationality of the Allais preferences shows that a competent agent can coherently decline to treat common consequences as canceling out.
