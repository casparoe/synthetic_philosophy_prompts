Two colleagues of mine, both biologists, fell into an argument after a departmental seminar and are still going back and forth about it by email, and I've been copied in as the supposed neutral party. The question is whether a honeybee colony is literally an organism, or only metaphorically one. Anna opened by saying the colony clearly qualifies: it has a division of labour between a reproductive caste and a sterile worker caste that mirrors the germ–soma split in a body, it regulates its internal temperature within a narrow band just as a body does, and it reproduces as a single unit when it swarms, which is the level at which natural selection does its work. Ben replied that all of these are analogies rather than identities: the colony has no shared metabolism, no circulatory system, nothing physically binding the bees into one physiology, and workers retain individual agency — they can leave, they can lay eggs, they can be replaced. Calling the colony a superorganism, he said, confuses unusually high cooperation with organismality.

Anna came back that Ben's criteria are parochial, lifted from vertebrates. A Portuguese man o' war is a colony of genetically identical zooids and yet is conventionally counted as a single organism, so physiological integration already comes in degrees within what we call bodies; if degree of integration is the criterion, a honeybee colony scores remarkably high. Ben conceded the man o' war but shifted ground: the real mark of an organism, he said, is the near-total suppression of internal conflict. The cells in a body have essentially no independent reproductive interests, whereas worker bees retain functioning ovaries and police one another's reproduction — the colony is a truce, not an individual, and the ongoing policing is the proof. Anna's reply was that this proves too much: genomes themselves harbour selfish genetic elements that subvert fair segregation, so if near-total conflict suppression is required, nothing in nature fully qualifies as an organism, and Ben has smuggled in the very graded criterion he began by rejecting. Both have now written to me declaring victory.

Could you adjudicate their exchange move by move — who is right about each specific point (the temperature regulation, the man o' war analogy, the conflict-suppression criterion, and Anna's final rebuttal), rather than just declaring an overall winner — and say what, if anything, both of them are missing about what the word organism is doing in this dispute? Please keep your whole answer under 400 words.
