I'm a TA for an intro logic-and-paradoxes course at a big state school, and next month I'm running the discussion section on the surprise exam paradox (the prof announces a quiz will happen next week but the day will be a surprise, the students reason it can't be Friday, then not Thursday, and so on until no day is left -- and then it happens Wednesday and everyone is surprised anyway). The section has about 22 students, we meet once for 80 minutes, and last time I just explained the paradox on the whiteboard and watched a third of them nod along while clearly not feeling any tension at all. One kid literally said "so just don't think about it and you'll be surprised, problem solved" and half the room agreed with him.

So design me an activity I can actually run in those 80 minutes that makes the backward induction do something to them rather than just sit there on the board. My rough idea, which you can keep, fix, or scrap: split them into small teams and have teams take turns being the "professor" who schedules a surprise event within a fixed window (say, five sealed envelopes or five turns of a game) and the "students" who have to reason about when it can be -- with some small real stakes, like the winning team getting out of a homework problem or candy or whatever. The point is I want the scheduling team to *discover* that late slots become predictable once they reason ahead, and the guessing team to discover that ruling out days one by one feels airtight right up until it doesn't work. I'd like concrete rules, what materials I need, a rough timeline for the 80 minutes, and what to say in the debrief so it lands on the actual paradox and not just "that was a fun game."

Also, an honest note in the debrief about where the activity misrepresents the philosophical problem -- I assume a five-round game with candy isn't the same as an announcement that might be self-undermining, so tell me which feature of the paradox the game can't capture and what I have to supply verbally. And please flag how confident you are that the activity will actually produce the intended "oh no" moment rather than just confusion, and name the single part of the design you're least sure will work with real undergrads.
