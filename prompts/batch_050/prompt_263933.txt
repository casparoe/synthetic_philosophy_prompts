So I'm a novelist, and for about two years I've been stuck on a chapter where two of my characters basically re-run the Grand Inquisitor's argument in modern dress. One is a kind of soft-spoken technocrat who thinks the Inquisitor was right: most people don't really want freedom, it frightens and exhausts them, what they want is bread, certainty, and someone to hand their conscience over to, and arranging things so they get that is an act of mercy rather than cruelty. The other character thinks this is the most dangerous thought ever thought, that a free and miserable person is better off than a contented managed one -- but whenever I make her explain *why*, she mostly sputters, which is exactly why the chapter isn't working.

I've rewritten their argument maybe six times and it keeps collapsing into them talking past each other, so I want to try something different before draft seven. Write the joint statement these two could actually both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, so I can steal its structure for the scene.

Concretely I want: (1) a numbered list of claims both would sign -- about what people are actually like, about what real freedom costs the people who have it, about what the Inquisitor's arrangement genuinely delivers and what it genuinely destroys. No vacuous stuff like "freedom and happiness both matter"; only claims the other camp would find slightly uncomfortable to concede. (2) A disagreements table where each side's position is worded in terms that side would itself accept -- the technocrat's view must not come out as "people are cattle" and the freedom-lover's view must not come out as "let them starve for a principle." (3) The cruxes: the sub-questions where, if one side turned out to be wrong, they'd actually have to move. My guess is these include an empirical question about what people in fact do with freedom when they get it, a question about whether happiness is even the right currency to be counting in, and a question about what to say about the minority who genuinely would choose freedom over comfort -- but tell me if those are the real cruxes or not. (4) For each crux, a case, observation, or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction about it written down in advance. And (5) if any chunk of their fight turns out to be verbal -- them using "freedom" or "happiness" in different senses, say -- flag exactly which chunk and why.

One more thing: the Grand Inquisitor scene has that famous silence at the end, and neither of my characters knows what to do with it either. If the joint statement can say something both sides agree the kiss (or whatever the modern equivalent is) does and doesn't settle, that would be a bonus, but don't force it if it doesn't belong in the document.
