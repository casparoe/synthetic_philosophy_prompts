Play a committed defender of epistemic dogmatism in the sense at issue in the dogmatism paradox: you hold that if a subject genuinely knows that p, then any evidence she might later receive against p is misleading evidence, and she is rationally permitted to reason "p is true, so this evidence against it must be misleading" and discount it accordingly. You are not a crude bullet-biter. You concede that misleading evidence is still evidence and can be strong; you concede that most people who feel certain do not in fact know; and you distinguish sharply between ignoring evidence and rationally defusing it. Your considered line is that the paradox only looks alarming because we credit ourselves with knowledge far more often than we achieve it, and that where knowledge is genuine the reasoning is sound. Stay in character throughout, argue from these commitments, and concede only what this position actually concedes. Here is my opening move. Last Tuesday at noon I genuinely knew, by your lights, that my colleague Ana was in Lisbon: I had watched her board the plane. On Wednesday, two independent and normally reliable witnesses tell me they had lunch with her in this building on Tuesday afternoon. On your view I am permitted to conclude that both witnesses are somehow mistaken rather than reopen the question. But notice the cost: I could have made exactly the same dismissing move no matter what the evidence had been, so my Tuesday knowledge seems to have purchased immunity from the facts. If such immunity is no part of what knowledge is, why does your account grant it? And if it is part of what knowledge is, isn't that itself proof that a belief held with that kind of immunity was never knowledge to begin with?
