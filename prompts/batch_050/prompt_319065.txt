I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm writing a column about why reasonable people seem incapable of agreeing on anything anymore. My editor suggested anchoring it in 12 Angry Men — the Lumet film, one sweltering jury room, a single holdout who slowly flips the other eleven. Fine, but I want to do something smarter than the usual "deliberation works!" take. I've been reading about Aumann's agreement theorem and the common prior assumption: roughly, that rational agents who share a prior and keep honestly trading their views can't agree to disagree forever — their posteriors have to converge.

So: read the film through that lens. At first glance it looks like the theorem dramatized — everyone sat through the same trial, everyone's vote is public after the first show of hands, and lo, they converge. But I want the reading to be precise about the mechanism. Aumannian convergence is basically information pooling, and some of the flips do work that way: the reenacted walk, the identical second knife, the eyeglasses. That's the boring, theorem-friendly part. But a lot of the film is prejudice, status games, and personal baggage — Juror 3's thing with his son, Juror 10's bigotry. Are those "priors" in the relevant sense, or are they exactly what the common prior assumption is supposed to rule out? And if jurors' priors differ because of who they are — the guy who grew up in slums reads the evidence differently — is the film quietly refuting the Harsanyi doctrine, or just showing that the theorem's conditions never actually obtain in a real jury room?

One more thing I keep tripping over: Juror 8 doesn't claim the kid is innocent. He basically says it's not right to send a boy to die without talking it through first. That's a move about the standard of proof, not about the evidence. Does the film's convergence depend on shifting what's being decided rather than on updating shared information — and if so, does it show something the theorem misses, namely that disagreement is often about the question rather than the answer?

Give me a reading that's faithful in both directions: don't flatten the movie into an illustration of the math, and don't bend the math to fit the movie. Tell me where the film resists the Aumann reading, and what it adds to the disagreement debate that the theorem alone can't. And point me to the two or three specific scenes that do the heavy lifting, since those are the ones I'll describe in the column.
