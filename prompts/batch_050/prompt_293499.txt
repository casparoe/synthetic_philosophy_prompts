I’m a novelist, and when I’m drafting I sometimes stop thinking in ordinary sentences altogether: I put characters, motives, secrets, and events on index cards, draw arrows between them, rearrange the arrows, and only later translate the result into prose. A friend in cognitive science said this raises a problem for the language of thought hypothesis, but I cant tell whether it really does. Write a short research proposal for a one-semester philosophy of mind paper, roughly 6000 words, on this narrower question: does the standard systematicity argument for a language of thought specifically support a sentence-like mental code, or would a graph- or map-like system of mental representation explain the same flexibility just as well? Define sentence-like representation in terms of discrete constituents, grammatical roles, and recombinability, and define graph-like representation in terms of nodes and typed links; then use simple variations such as the fox chases the goose versus the goose chases the fox to test what each format does and does not explain.

The proposal should make the central issue determinate rather than turning it into the enormous question of whether thought has any structure at all. Explain why the obvious answers are unstable: LOT defenders can redescribe every useful diagram as a weird notation, while critics can declare diagrams nonlinguistic without saying where compositionality ends. Set out an approach that compares a propositional model and a graph model on the same cases, and use one or two invented examples from writing or translation rather than relying on experimental findings. Say what the possible conclusions would be—for instance, that LOT collapses into a generic structured-representation thesis, that it carries a distinctive commitment graphs cannot capture, or that systematicity permits several representational formats—and what would count as the project failing. Keep the scope realistic: no neuroscience survey, no history of cognitive science, no bibliography or literature review by citation; I’ll fill in references to the usual positions myself.
