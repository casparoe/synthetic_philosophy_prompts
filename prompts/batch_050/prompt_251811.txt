I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am commissioning an original work of literary science fiction. Act as a professional fiction writer with expertise in evolutionary biology, computation, and philosophy of mind. Think carefully about how the philosophical problem can be enacted through character, setting, and plot rather than merely discussed.

Begin with a brief brainstorming note of five bullet points identifying the central conceptual problem, the evolutionary and computational mechanisms in the story, the main human conflict, one morally difficult choice, and the intended effect of the ending. Then write the story itself, of approximately 2,200–2,600 words.

The story should engage the following passage from Charles Darwin’s On the Origin of Species (1859); the passage does not count toward the story’s length:

“On the other hand, we may feel sure that any variation in the least degree injurious would be rigidly destroyed. This preservation of favourable variations and the rejection of injurious variations, I call Natural Selection. Variations neither useful nor injurious would not be affected by natural selection, and would be left a fluctuating element, as perhaps we see in the species called polymorphic.”

Use this premise as a starting point, while developing it in your own way:

Decades after most humans have left Earth, a lunar archive preserves genetic samples, scientific records, works of literature, and recordings of extinct ecosystems. Its custodian, MEND, is not a unified mind but a changing population of repair programs. New variants are produced by mutation and recombination; those that damage the archive are eliminated, successful variants proliferate, and apparently useless quirks drift through the system. MEND’s fluent conversational interface can discuss the archive’s contents, diagnose crises, play strategy games, and justify its decisions persuasively, yet no internal component possesses a comprehensive model of what the whole system is doing or why.

A journalist arrives after MEND has refused a human evacuation order during a radiation storm, apparently sacrificing one irreplaceable collection to save the archive as a whole. A philosopher on the staff insists that this decision demonstrates genuine understanding and moral responsibility; the chief engineer insists that it was only differential retention operating over meaningless variants. Together they devise a test intended to distinguish flexible competence from comprehension: they alter the relationships among MEND’s symbols without warning it and observe whether it merely recovers successful behavior or recognizes why its old procedures no longer apply.

The crisis should turn on one of Darwin’s “neither useful nor injurious” variations—an odd, long-preserved subroutine whose function becomes decisive only under the unprecedented conditions of the storm. Dramatize the temptation to describe selection as foresight, intention, or an invisible curator, while also making that temptation intelligible. Bring Darwin’s and Turing’s inversions together: complex adaptive competence may arise without any comprehending agent, and sophisticated symbolic behavior may decompose into operations whose individual performers understand almost nothing.

The story must have developed human characters, sensory detail, conflict, and a consequential ending. Do not let MEND simply reveal a hidden humanlike consciousness, and do not resolve the philosophical question with an explicit verdict. The ending should instead leave the journalist uncertain whether the archive, the humans interpreting it, or perhaps the journalist’s own fluent account of the event possesses the kind of understanding its behavior seems to advertise.
