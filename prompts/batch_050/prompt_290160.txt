Question: Explain Grice's distinction between what is said and what is implicated, using an example of your own or one discussed in class. Your answer should state at least two diagnostics that distinguish conversational implicatures from entailments, explain the difference between generalized and particularized conversational implicatures, and say what role the speaker's intentions play in implicature on Grice's account of speaker meaning.

Student answer: Grice distinguishes what is said, which is roughly the literal, conventionally determined content of the sentence uttered, from what is implicated, which is content the speaker conveys without the sentence literally saying it. Implicatures arise because conversation is governed by the Cooperative Principle and its maxims of Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner: speakers are expected to be appropriately informative, truthful, relevant, and clear, and hearers assume this unless given reason not to. For example, if I run out of fuel on the highway and you tell me there is a petrol station a mile up the road, you implicate that the station is open and selling fuel, since otherwise your remark would violate the maxim of Relation. This implicature is particularized: it depends on the specific circumstances of my breakdown. Conversational implicatures differ from entailments in at least two ways. First, they are cancelable: you could add that you have no idea whether the station is open without contradicting yourself, whereas an entailment cannot be canceled without contradiction. Second, they are calculable: a hearer can reconstruct the reasoning from what was said, the maxims, and the context, which is how the implicature gets conveyed at all. Implicatures of the familiar kind where an utterance of "some" conveys "not all" are particularized conversational implicatures, because they depend on contextual factors such as which alternatives happen to be relevant in the conversation; in a context where "all" is not a live option, the inference does not arise. Finally, because an implicature must be calculable by the hearer from the maxims and the context, the speaker's own intentions drop out of the picture: what the speaker meant is fixed by what a rational hearer would work out, so implicature is really a matter of the conversational circumstances rather than of speaker meaning.
