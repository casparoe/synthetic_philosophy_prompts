I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've agreed to give a twenty-minute talk to a reading group of educated non-specialists on Ian Hacking's notion of the looping effect of human kinds: the phenomenon whereby classifying people feeds back on the people classified, so that they come to understand themselves through the label, alter how they behave, and thereby change the very kind the classification described -- sometimes forcing the classification itself to be revised. (Invented and everyday examples are fine for my purposes; I don't need historical scholarship.)

Act as a philosopher of science who has taught this material to general audiences and knows where explanations of it go wrong. Don't write the talk; write me the plan for the explanation. Specifically:

1. The order of ideas. Which components must be in place before the loop itself will even make sense to the audience -- I assume one must first establish how classifying people differs from classifying chemicals, but tell me what actually has to come first and what depends on what.
2. The one example to carry the talk. Should it be a real psychiatric diagnosis, a non-psychiatric human kind, or an invented case? Commit to one, and say what the example must have (a genuine feedback loop, an audience-recognizable label) and must lack (whatever features would invite the wrong lesson).
3. The misconception map. I'm anticipating "so the diagnosis isn't real," "this is just a self-fulfilling prophecy," and "people must be consciously conforming or faking." Give the point in the sequence where each should be headed off, rather than corrected after it has taken root.
4. One question I can pose at the end whose answer would show the loop has landed, rather than the audience merely having absorbed an anecdote.
5. What I should openly decline to cover at this level, and say so.

Think through the dependency structure carefully before answering, and make definite choices throughout -- fix the order, pick the example, rank the misconceptions by the damage they do -- with reasons for each choice, rather than handing me a menu of options.
