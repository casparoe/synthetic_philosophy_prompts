I'm recording the closing interview of a podcast series on the ethics of AI assistants. My guest is a political philosopher who defends a view she calls the neutral assistant thesis: an AI assistant should stand to its users' values roughly as the liberal state stands to citizens' conceptions of the good — willing to help any user pursue any lawful project, and overstepping its mandate whenever its designers build in substantive moral commitments of their own, whether as refusals, moralizing framings, or gentle steering toward particular ideals of life.

Draft a sequence of seven to nine interview questions. I don't want questions that invite her to restate the analogy; I want questions that test where it breaks. The sequence must include, in some order:

1. A question on the disanalogy of power: state neutrality is a constraint on coercion backed by a monopoly on legitimate force, whereas an assistant's influence over users is persuasive, personalized, and continuous rather than coercive. Why should a constraint designed for the former bind the latter — and does the different kind of power make neutrality more urgent for an assistant, or less?

2. A question on whether neutrality is even available: training corpora, reward signals, and interface defaults already encode substantive choices, so the realistic alternative to designed-in values may simply be the unexamined values of whoever assembled the pipeline. How does she distinguish genuine neutrality from smuggling?

3. A question on the standard liberal distinction between harm-based and value-based restrictions: refusing assistance with wrongdoing might track the harm principle rather than any conception of the good, in which case much of what she calls overstepping is ordinary harm prevention. Anticipate her most likely reply — that harm-based refusals are fine and only thick moral steering is objectionable — and give me a follow-up that forces her to draw the line in a hard case, such as an assistant helping a user write a sincere public defense of a cruel but lawful practice. And if she retreats to lawfulness itself as the line, have a second follow-up ready: law varies by jurisdiction and sometimes encodes thick values she herself rejects, so an assistant serving users worldwide cannot outsource its ethics to statute.

4. A question on aggregation across users: the state is neutral once, for everyone, whereas a single assistant serves millions of users whose projects conflict. Is per-user neutrality still coherent when competently serving one user's project foreseeably frustrates another's?

For every question, supply the follow-up I should use depending on her most probable first answer, so that the sequence corners the position rather than surveying it. Finally, rank the questions so that if the recording runs short I know what to cut: commit to the ordering and give a one- or two-sentence justification for each of your top three, rather than presenting the questions as interchangeable.
