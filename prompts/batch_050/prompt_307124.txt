Peirce as the grown-up in the room: the guy who thought truth is real but that inquiry is never finished. Problem is, the only thing my editor has read is a pop-philosophy book that glosses him like this:

"Peirce, the founder of pragmatism, held that the meaning of any idea is just its practical effects, and so truth itself is whatever belief proves useful and gets accepted. On his view, truth is simply the opinion that all investigators would eventually converge on and agree to, which makes truth a matter of consensus rather than reality. And since Peirce was a fallibilist who held that any belief might turn out false, he concluded that we can never claim to know anything, only to hold opinions that haven't been refuted yet. His famous essay on the fixation of belief catalogs several methods for settling opinion -- sticking tenaciously to your views, deferring to authority, and science among them -- as options we pick between depending on temperament."

Act as a Peirce scholar who's also fair-minded about popularizations -- don't just dunk on the book, but don't let real errors slide either. Think carefully before answering; it may help to first list the seperate claims the paragraph makes and check each one on its own. Then tell me: (a) what's accurate, (b) what's a common simplification I could get away with in a magazine, (c) what Peirce explicitly rejected or would have hated, and (d) whether the book's overall picture -- Peirce as basically a consensus theorist who thought knowledge is impossible -- survives the corrections. 600 words max.
