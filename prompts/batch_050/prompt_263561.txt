Modern clinical medicine has a curious double attitude toward suffering, and I want the implicit philosophy excavated. On one side, the practice treats suffering as a pure evil to be driven down wherever found: pain is monitored as a routine vital sign, untreated pain is considered a scandal and a possible basis for malpractice, anesthesia for surgery is non-optional, and palliative care increasingly treats the relief of suffering as an end that justifies side effects, sedation, and in the limiting case even some shortening of life. On the other side, the same profession regards the capacity to suffer as something to be preserved and even restored: congenital insensitivity to pain is classified as a tragic disorder rather than a blessing, clinicians try to bring a depressed patient's dulled affect back toward a normal range that includes grief and distress, no medical body proposes eliminating the neural machinery of suffering outright even in principle, and the discipline treats a patient's ability to feel fear, regret, and loss as partly constitutive of the agency that informed consent exists to protect.

Take this practice as your object. First, reconstruct the view of suffering it presupposes: is suffering being treated as intrinsically bad, as bad only when it serves no function, as an alarm signal whose value is instrumental, or as something bound up with personhood in a way that makes its wholesale elimination a harm rather than a benefit? Be careful to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say where it is underdetermined -- where several rival theories of suffering's badness would license exactly the same clinical behavior. Second, assess the internal coherence of the implicit view: can the abolitionist impulse in pain management and the conservationist impulse toward the capacity for pain rest on a single consistent account, or is the profession running two incompatible theories in different departments? Third, tell me concretely what would have to change in clinical practice, research priorities, and medical ethics codes if the presupposed view were replaced by the position -- associated with transhumanist writers on the hedonistic imperative -- that suffering's badness is entirely intrinsic to the experience itself, that its biological substrates are dispensable, and that phasing out the capacity for involuntary suffering is a legitimate long-term medical goal. Which current practices would that view condemn that we now consider obligatory, and which would it mandate that we now consider impermissible? Finally, indicate how confident you are in your reconstruction overall, and single out the one claim in your analysis -- whether about what the practice presupposes, about its coherence, or about what the abolitionist replacement would entail -- that you are least sure of, saying why it is the weakest link.
