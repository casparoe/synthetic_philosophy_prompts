The topic: the heuristic probabilistic arguments number theorists use to guess at conjectures — treating events like "n is divisible by p" and "n is divisible by q" as independent, modeling primes as if they were random, and reading off densities for twin primes, prime gaps, and the like. My half-formed idea: the presumption of independence in these arguments is literally false (over any finite range the divisibility events are not independent, and the naive random model is known to mispredict the distribution of primes in short intervals), and at least one conjecture that heuristics of this style supported was later disproved. Yet the heuristics usually deliver the right answer up to a correction factor that experts have learned to expect, as with the constant in the twin prime conjecture, where raw independence gives the wrong number and the standard refinement fixes it. So the interesting question is not whether independence holds — it doesn't — but what makes pretending it holds reliable, and whether the corrections can be derived from a principled criterion for when the independence heuristic is trustworthy rather than from case-by-case folklore.
