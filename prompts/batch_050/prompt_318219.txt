I facilitate a first-year university seminar called The Grammar of Society (24 students, one 75-minute session per topic), and I want to design an in-class activity on the distinction between tight and loose cultures -- roughly, societies that demand strict norm compliance versus those that tolerate deviation. I've been a reader of rationalist blogs and forums for years, so coordination failures, incentive gradients, and norms-as-equilibria are familiar furniture to me, and that background shapes what I'm after: my students can already recite the claim that tightness is a functional response to chronic threat, but they privately file tight cultures under authoritarianism with extra steps, and loose cultures under freedom with a side of disorder. They treat the trade-off as a moral spectrum rather than an engineering one. I want them to feel, in their own decisions, why a community under pressure starts begging for rules, enforcement, and even punishment -- including from the bottom up, not just imposed by a leader -- and then to feel the bill come due when the pressure lifts and the rules don't.

Concretely: design a game or simulation, runnable by one facilitator without a TA, in which small groups (say, four to six societies of four to six students each) govern themselves across several rounds. The environment should have hidden or semi-hidden states so that threat can arrive as a shock mid-game rather than being announced in the setup -- I want the demand for tightness to emerge from experience, not instruction. Give me: the full rules; a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money but creates genuine small stakes (points, privileges, scarce roles, whatever works); a mechanism by which groups can propose, adopt, and enforce norms, including some form of costly punishment; a round-by-round timeline that fits 75 minutes including debrief; and a scripted threat event (a resource shock, an invasion-like inter-group contest, an epidemic-style cascade -- your choice, or better, recommend the one that most reliably produces the desired dynamics). The debrief should map specific moments of the game onto the concepts: threat-driven norm convergence, the coordination benefits of tightness under pressure, the ratchet effect by which emergency rules outlive the emergency, and the costs of tightness in a benign or rapidly changing environment -- each anchored to something that actually happened at a particular table.

Finally, be honest about failure modes and limits. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this -- I suspect at least one group will speedrun to theatrical tyranny for laughs, and at least one student who has read ahead will perform tightness ironically rather than discover it -- and how to handle each. And name explicitly which features of real cultural tightness a 75-minute game cannot reproduce (internalized shame, intergenerational transmission, the way threat perception itself gets socially constructed), so I know what the debrief has to supply by narration rather than by experience. If two design choices conflict -- say, verisimilitude of the shock versus keeping the game legible to students -- flag the trade-off and tell me which way to go.
