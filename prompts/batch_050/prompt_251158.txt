I'm in a political philosophy seminar this term (mostly distributive justice -- we've done Rawls, Nozick, a bit of luck egalitarianism) and I have to write a paper on inheritance and equality of opportunity, and I realized I don't actually know what I think. So I did the thing where you list your gut verdicts on cases and see if there's a principle underneath, and mine seem like a mess. Here they are. (1) A family farm that's been worked by three generations, passed to a daughter who's run it for a decade -- taxing this at 50% when the dad dies feels wrong, almost like confiscation. (2) A liquid trust fund of $40 million going to a great-grandchild who's never met the person who earned it and has never worked a day in their life -- tax the hell out of this, I'm basically fine with 80-90%. (3) My roommate's parents paying his full tuition and giving him the down payment on a condo -- this is totally fine, it would be creepy and totalitarian to tax parental support like that. (4) But wait, (3) is just an inheritance given early, and it advantages him over me exactly as much as (2) advantages the trust fund kid, so my own verdicts already seem inconsistent. (5) Someone sets up a dynasty trust specifically so their wealth skips taxes for generations -- this feels like cheating even if it's legal. (6) Lottery winnings: I'm fine taxing those heavily, but inheritance is also basically a windfall for the recipient, so why does (1) still tug at me? (7) The thing nobody can tax: my roommate's dad is a lawyer and basically handed him a career -- connections, knowing how to talk in interviews, which internships matter. That stuff confers way more advantage than the condo and I *don't* think the state should touch it, which makes me wonder why I care about the money at all.

So my question: is there a single principle that captures most of these verdicts, or close to most? The obvious candidate is something like "transfers are more objectionable the less the recipient did to earn them and the more they entrench advantage across generations" -- but that seems to condemn (3) as much as (2), and I'm not willing to condemn (3). Another candidate is "the state may tax wealth at death but not support given while alive," which saves (1) partially and (3) fully but seems completely arbitrary (why should the timing of the transfer matter morally?) and it makes (5) look fine when done early, which I hate. A third option is that the real principle is about *equal opportunity* rather than transfers per se, but then (7) becomes the biggest injustice on the list and the whole tax-focused project starts to look like it's aimed at the wrong target.

What I'd like from you: state the most precise principle (or two rival principles) you can find that fits my pattern, and be honest about which verdicts it forces me to give up -- don't just smooth everything over. For each clause of the principle, point at the case that motivates it. Then tell me what the principle says about two cases I haven't decided: a grandmother paying private school tuition directly to the school (so no money ever touches the parents), and someone who earned $5 million, already paid income tax on it, and leaves it to their spouse who helped build the business unpaid for twenty years. If the answer is genuinely "your intuitions in (1) and (2) can't be saved by any principle that also saves (3)," then say that and tell me which one you think should go and why, rather than inventing some gerrymandered hybrid principle with five clauses.
