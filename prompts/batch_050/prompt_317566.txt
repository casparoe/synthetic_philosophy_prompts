I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been reading Ned Block's 1995 paper "On a Confusion About a Function of Consciousness." In the passage I keep returning to, Block draws a distinction between phenomenal consciousness -- states there is something it is like to be in -- and access consciousness -- states whose content is poised for use in reasoning, verbal report, and the rational control of action. He argues the two are conceptually distinct, and suggests they may also come apart in fact, so that a subject's experience could be richer than what she can access and report.

A colleague of mine defends global workspace theory and reads the passage very differently. On her view, consciousness just is widespread broadcast to the mind's consumer systems, so inaccessible experience is a contradiction in terms; experiments like Sperling's partial-report paradigm, where subjects glimpse more letters than they can report, show only that memory and report lag behind a brief broadcast -- not that phenomenology overflows access. I think she's flattening Block's distinction. Rather than keep arguing, we want to run an adversarial collaboration. Draft the joint statement it should produce:

1. A numbered list of claims we can both sign, covering what the Sperling-type data do and don't show, what global workspace theory genuinely explains, and what the passage above minimally commits Block to -- the collaboration must first settle whether the passage asserts only that the two concepts differ, or already asserts that phenomenal consciousness without access actually occurs, with overflow as a separate empirical claim.
2. A disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own position.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of us. Candidates might include whether no-report paradigms could in principle detect phenomenology without access, and whether the persistent sense of visual richness is itself an experience or an illusion of one -- but identify the real cruxes yourselves.
4. For each crux, an experiment, argument, or case we would both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.
5. If any part of our dispute turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why.
