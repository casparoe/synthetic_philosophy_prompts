I'm on the board of a family foundation -- my great-aunt passed away two years ago and left roughly $400M with very specific instructions: the money is to exist in perpetuity and fund early-childhood literacy programs in our home state, forever, and only that. Her lawyers were apparently thorough, and the trust language tries hard to lock this in. But some of us on the board think we should push back while we still can and restructure: either spend the whole thing down over, say, 75-100 years, or at least build in a mandatory generational review where future boards can broaden the mission if the world stops looking like 2015. The family is genuinely split, and I've been voluntold to write the memo that goes into the December board packet.

Here's what's been nagging at me. Last weekend my brother-in-law (who reads a lot of pop history and never shuts up about it) was arguing that no institution should be trusted to stay on-mission for more than a couple generations -- his line was "just use the base rate, look at how long the average empire even lasted before it collapsed or turned into something its founders wouldnt recognize." And annoyingly, once I set aside a handful of churches and ancient universities, I struggled to name organizations that lasted even 200 years while still doing what their founders actually wanted. At the same time, the perpetuity camp has a real point: compound growth means a permanent endowment eventually commands staggering resources, literacy isn't going out of fashion, and it was her money and her call -- the dead deserve some grip on the wheel, surely.

So write the memo for me. Act as a philanthropic-governance expert who moonlights as a decision analyst, and think carefully rather than just splitting the difference. Before settling on a recommendation, explicitly work through the forecasting problem: what is the right reference class for estimating how long a legally restricted endowment stays faithful to donor intent (family firms? endowed charities? sovereign dynasties? empires, per my brother-in-law? some mix?), and show whether picking a different class actually changes the practical answer -- if it doesn't, say so and explain why. Also give the best version of the losing side's argument in terms strong enough that they could respond to it. The memo needs one concrete bottom line: perpetual-as-written, spend-down over a stated number of years, or perpetual with a defined review/escape mechanism. Keep it under 1,000 words so nobody on the board can claim they didn't read it.
