I help run a small reading group on classical Islamic theology (mostly grad students, a couple of us are philosophy people who wandered in), and for the whole term two members have been at each other's throats over what I gather is basically the Islamic version of the Euthyphro question. One of them is a convinced Mu'tazilite sympathiser — he thinks good and evil are knowable by reason independently of revelation, that justice is something real that God's commands track, and that God simply could not command cruelty or punish the innocent because that would violate justice itself. The other is firmly Ash'arite — she holds that nothing is just or unjust until God's command makes it so, that obligation only arises from a command addressed to a subject, and that since no one commands God, it makes no sense to hold Him under obligations or to call anything He does unjust. They've been arguing past each other for months, and our group facilitator has finally made them sit down and produce a joint statement in the style of an adversarial collaboration, rather than another shouting match. They've asked me to draft it (neutral third party, apparently), and I'd like your help.

Write the document they should come away with, roughly 800–1300 words. I want: (1) a numbered list of claims both can actually sign — things like God's justice, the badness of injustice, human accountability for action, the authority of revelation — but phrased carefully so neither side can object that the wording smuggles in the other camp's premises (this is the hard part, please don't just write vacuous stuff both agree with in name only); (2) a disagreements section where each residual dispute is stated precisely, with each side's position worded the way that side would itself put it — I'm fairly sure the central one is whether God commands what is just because it is just, versus justice being constituted by God's command, but there are sub-disagreements about whether reason can know moral truths before revelation, and whether terms like 'just' apply to God univocally or only by analogy; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one side to move — and for each crux, a concrete case or text both camps would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded. Candidate cases you might use: the story of Moses and al-Khidr in Sura 18, especially the killing of the boy who had committed no wrong; the hypothetical of whether God could command wanton cruelty or lying; the question of animal and infant suffering and whether God owes compensation for it; and the Quranic point that God is not questioned about what He does while His creatures are questioned. Where you suspect a disagreement is really about words rather than substance, say so explicitly and restate it in the non-verbal form, if there is one.

A few requests about how you frame the answer: don't adjudicate who wins — the whole point is that both of them could put their names to this document, so if you find yourself leaning Ash'arite or Mu'tazilite in the phrasing, flag and fix it. At the end, add a short note saying how confident you are overall that the statement is genuinely neutral, and single out the one formulation or crux-description you are least confident both parties would actually accept as fair, and why. And please write the whole thing in British English.
