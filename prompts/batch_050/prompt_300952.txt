Here's something I want my students to work through this term and I keep going back an forth on it myself. Take the Irish famine of the 1840s: the potato blight destroys the staple crop, yet Ireland continues exporting grain and livestock to Britain throughout, roughly a million people die and another million or so emigrate, and the British government mostly sticks to laissez-faire doctrine with limited and grudging relief. Analyze the episode through Sen's entitlement approach -- do the mapping properly: whose entitlements failed and how, in what sense there was or wasn't a food availability decline, and what the continued exports do to the analysis. Then run it in the other direction. A colleague of mine argues that the entitlement framework is a theory of mechanism, not of culpability: it tells you how people lost access to food, but it's structurally silent on whether anyone did anything wrong, which is exactly why the 'natural disaster vs. political crime' framing keeps talking past it. Is she right? Does the Irish case confirm the approach, expose a gap in it, or show that you need something beyond entitlements -- intentions, ideology, foreseeability -- to make the 'crime' charge stick, and if so, what?
