Two of my friends have been having the same fight for months and I want to end it. One's a psychiatry resident who thinks the looping effect stuff is massively oversold: sure, diagnostic labels change how people talk about their distress, but schizophrenia or melancholic depression are real breakdowns in brain function that would exist no matter what we called them, and all this talk of "making up people" confuses the illness itself with its cultural costume. The other's a sociologist who thinks the resident is hopelessly naive: diagnoses aren't just descriptions, they're interventions. Being told you have dissociative identity disorder or social anxiety gives you a script for how to be ill, clinics and pharma marketing spread the scripts, and the category and the condition co-produce each other — which she thinks explains things like the DID explosion in the 80s and 90s and the way anorexia changed its presentation when it showed up in Hong Kong.

I told them to do an adversarial collaboration instead of yelling, and they agreed on the condition that I draft the joint statement. So: write the document they'd both sign. Start with a numbered section of agreed claims — the stuff about the reality of suffering, about diagnostic fads, about what the 1973 removal of homosexuality from the DSM does and doesn't show, about the limits of "it's all social construction" on one side and "culture doesn't matter" on the other. Then the remaining disagreements as a table, each one worded neutrally enough that the person actually holding that view would accept the description of their own position. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions where a clear answer would genuinely move at least one of them. I figure one crux is roughly whether looping changes the underlying condition or only its expression and reporting, and another is whether the historical and cross-cultural instability of categories undermines their validity, but include any others that turn up. For every crux, give a concrete case or finding both would accept as bearing on it (the cross-cultural epidemiology of schizophrenia, including its wierdly variable outcomes across countries, might be one) and write down each side's prediction or reading in advance, so nobody gets to move the goalposts afterwards. Keep both of them honest — no strawmen in either direction.
