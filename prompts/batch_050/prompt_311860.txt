Please explain Wittgenstein's notion of family resemblance and its relation to his idea of language-games, focusing on the parts that are easy to get wrong rather than giving the standard overview. First, what exactly is the positive claim: that many everyday concepts lack necessary and sufficient conditions for application, that no concept has them, or merely that philosophers are wrong to treat the search for them as the central task — and does the difference between these readings matter for what the argument shows? Second, distinguish family resemblance from two things it is often conflated with: vagueness (a concept can have fuzzy borderline cases while still possessing precise sufficient conditions, so fuzziness alone is not family resemblance), and prototype theory in cognitive psychology (are Wittgenstein and Rosch making the same claim, or is one a thesis about word use and the other an empirical thesis about mental representation, and can one be true without the other?). Third, is the view compatible with disciplines such as mathematics, law, or taxonomy giving stipulative definitions — does family resemblance describe how concepts must work, how they in fact work, or only how they work prior to regimentation? Finally, the concept of a language-game is itself standardly treated as a family-resemblance concept; is that self-application a strength of the view, a problem for it, or neither? Where interpreters genuinely disagree — for instance, over whether family resemblance is a semantic thesis, a metaphysical one, or only therapeutic advice — please flag the disagreement rather than presenting one reading as the consensus.
