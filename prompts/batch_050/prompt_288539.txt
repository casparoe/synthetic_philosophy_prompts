You're a philosopher of mind who also happens to know a fair bit about financial markets and market microstructure. Think carefully before you write, and maybe jot down your mapping (players, rules, behaviors) before you commit to it.

Here's what I want analyzed: the Flash Crash of May 6, 2010, when the Dow dropped something like 600-1000 points in a matter of minutes and then mostly bounced back just as fast, with some stocks briefly trading at absurd prices (a penny, or $100,000). The rough facts everyone agrees on: a big sell order in E-mini futures hit the market, high-frequency trading algorithms responded, liquidity evaporated, the algos kept trading with each other in a kind of hot-potato cascade, and the whole thing reversed once trading pauses kicked in and humans and systems reset.

Analyze this through the lens of sphexishness -- Dennett's idea (via the digger wasp that mindlessly re-checks its burrow every time you move its prey a few inches, restarting the same loop forever) that apparently intelligent behavior can be rigid, context-blind rule-following, and that the test for it is whether the system keeps doing its thing when the environment stops matching the assumptions the behavior was built for.

But I don't want just "the algos were like the wasp, the end." Concretely:

1. First, do the honest mapping. Which behaviors in the crash were genuinely sphexish and which only look that way? Selling into a falling market because your rules say to reduce inventory is rule-following, but is it *sphexish* rule-following or is it locally rational behavior that's only collectively catastrophic? I think this distinction matters and people constantly blur it.

2. To make the analysis sharper, use this little framework I'm proposing (steal it, critique it, whatever): distinguish *first-order sphexishness* (the rules themselves are rigid and the agent has no way to notice the environment changed) from *second-order sphexishness* (the agent has meta-rules -- circuit breakers, kill switches, sanity checks -- but the meta-rules are themselves triggered by crude proxies and can fire wrongly or fail to fire). Where did the crash involve each? The trading pauses that ended the crash were presumably second-order -- did they work by being *less* sphexish or just by being sphexish in a lucky direction?

3. Then run it back the other way: what does the episode show about sphexishness as a concept? Does a market made of sphexish parts count as sphexish itself, and is that even a well-defined question? And the uncomfortable one: the human traders who pulled out and refused to quote prices were arguably the *flexible* ones -- but from a regulator's view their flexibility made things worse. Does that mean flexibility isn't unconditionally good, and that the right target is something like "sphexish in the right places"? If so, is there a principled way to say which places, or does the wasp analogy stop being useful exactly at that point?

Keep it under 800 words. I'd rather you be disciplined about what the analogy can't explain than force it to explain everything.
