I help run a weekly game theory reading group for advanced undergrads plus a couple of grad students, about 14 people, seminar room with a whiteboard and a big stack of index cards, no computers allowed in this room (department rule, long story). We've just spent two sessions on all the standard reasons cooperation shows up in the prisoner's dilemma -- iteration, reputation, punishment, kin selection, the whole tour. For the last session I want to do program equilibrium, and I definately don't want to just lecture it. The idea as I understand it: in a one-shot PD where each player submits a program that gets to read the other player's source code before outputting cooperate or defect, mutual cooperation becomes an equilibrium, because a program can cooperate iff it verifies that the other program cooperates with it, which makes it unexploitable. No trust, no shadow of the future, no enforcement -- cooperation falls out of mutual transparency alone.

What I want them to come away feeling, not just knowing: cooperation here rests on verifiability, not on niceness or reciprocity, and it's weirdly fragile -- it collapses the moment "inspection" turns into guessing at the other person's intentions instead of actually checking their procedure. So design me a runnable activity of about 60 minutes that makes this happen in the room. I need concrete rules for how "programs" get written and inspected (humans can't literally read source code, so what's the right proxy -- I've been imagining short decision procedures written on cards in some tightly constrained format, but if there's a better mechanism, use it), the payoff structure (small real stakes are fine, I can bring chocolate), a timeline, and at least one built-in wrinkle that forces the contrast between genuine verification and mere signaling or vibe-reading. One thing I'm unsure about that you should take a position on: should pairs be allowed to deliberately write identical procedures, or should the game push them toward the more general "cooperate with anything that provably cooperates with me" style? Which of those is the thing the activity should actually dramatize, and why?

Also the debrief. Map specific moments that will happen in play onto parts of the idea (which moment is "one-shot, no reputation," which moment is the equilibrium snapping into place), tell me what will predictably go wrong -- people writing vague strategies like "be nice if they seem nice," someone attempting an exploit dressed up as cooperation, the whole thing quietly turning into an iterated game because everyone knows each other -- and how to handle each. And be honest about what the activity structurally can't reproduce: my guess is the formal-verification part and the self-reference weirdness are exactly where the human proxy breaks down, but tell me what the facilitator has to just say out loud because no game mechanic will deliver it. One firm constraint: the penny should drop during play, not in my wrap-up. I don't want a lecture with a game stapled to the front of it.
