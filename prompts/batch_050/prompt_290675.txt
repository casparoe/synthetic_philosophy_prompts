I direct a graduate seminar on formal approaches to distributive justice, and each year, when we reach the axiomatic theory of bargaining, I find I have less to say than the year before. The situation, as I understand it, is this. Nash characterized his solution uniquely by efficiency, symmetry, invariance under positive affine transformations of utility, and independence of irrelevant alternatives. Kalai and Smorodinsky showed that dropping IIA in favor of an individual monotonicity requirement characterizes a different solution, which selects the maximal point at which each party's gain stands in the same proportion to the best they could feasibly obtain. Both results are sixty-odd years old; the mathematics is settled; and yet the normative dispute between the two camps has, so far as I can tell, not advanced in decades. Each side exhibits cases in which the other's axiom produces a counterintuitive result — the Nash partisans note that monotonicity makes the outcome depend on alternatives no one would choose, while the Kalai–Smorodinsky partisans note that under IIA a party can fail to benefit even when the feasible set expands strictly in their favor — and then nothing happens. The same examples are rehearsed; nobody converts.

My working hypothesis, which I offer only as a hypothesis, is that the axiomatic method has not resolved the normative question but merely relocated it. The program's promise was that we could stop haggling over solutions and instead argue over axioms, whose plausibility could be assessed independently. But if axioms are in practice chosen because they deliver an antecedently preferred solution — what one of my colleagues uncharitably calls axiom shopping — then the disagreement about outcomes simply reappears, one level up, as a disagreement about properties, with no independent leverage gained. On this picture the impasse is exactly what one would expect.

What I should like is a diagnosis, not a verdict on which solution is correct. Is this a substantive disagreement about what fairness requires of an arbitrator? A case of the two camps answering different questions under one heading — one asking what an impartial arbiter should impose, the other what rational parties will in fact agree to, with the strategic support for the Nash solution (via alternating-offers models of the Rubinstein type) blurring that distinction? Or is the dispute a symptom that the canonical bargaining problem is ill-posed, abstracting away outside options, information, and repeated play on which any sensible verdict would depend? Please ground the diagnosis in what each side actually appeals to, and tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress — a new argument, experimental evidence about what parties accept, a different formalization of the problem — or else make the case that no progress should be expected. Finally, I ask that you state what would change your own mind: which consideration or result would lead you to classify the disagreement differently than you have, and at what point in your reasoning you take the failure of your analysis to be most likely.
