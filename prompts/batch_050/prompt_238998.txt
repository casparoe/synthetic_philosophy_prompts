A congestion-charge referendum in my city passed with 51 percent of votes cast on 38 percent turnout, and both sides now invoke the same slogan: the mayor says implementing the charge respects "the will of the people," while her opponent says that very slogan forbids it, since most eligible voters never endorsed the charge and the question on the ballot bundled the charge with a transit plan many voters wanted for its own sake. I suspect the slogan is doing no work at all here, but I want to be shown rather than told. Lay out the distinct precise claims "the will of the people" could be making -- for instance: that there exists a single preference attributable to the electorate as such; that the majority of votes cast reveals it; that only a majority of eligible voters could; that it is what an adequately informed or deliberating public would choose; that it is a general will in something like Rousseau's sense, distinct from the mere aggregate of individual wills; or that it is whatever the outcome of a fair aggregation procedure is, even though such procedures can deliver inconsistent results on multi-option questions. For each reading, say whether it is coherent, whether any actual referendum could in principle establish it, and whether it would vindicate the mayor, her opponent, or neither. Then tell me which reading the slogan's everyday users most plausibly have in mind, and whether that reading is one of the defensible ones.
