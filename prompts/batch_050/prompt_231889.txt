So in my history class we've spent like three weeks going in circles on this question: was John Stuart Mill basically a mouthpiece for the British empire, or is that super unfair to him? Quick background we were given: Mill worked for the East India Company for most of his career, and in his political writing he says some peoples aren't ready for self-government and do better under a kind of tutelage, with India as the obvious case. Half the class says ok, case closed — his famous liberty stuff was only ever meant for Europeans, and liberalism was just the polite face of empire. The other half says his principles are universal, he attacked abuses of power everywhere, and it's pointless to dunk on a Victorian guy for sounding bad by today's standards. Nobody has budged an inch and the teacher keeps going "interesting points on both sides," which helps no one.

Then I actually read chapter 1 of Considerations on Representative Government (1861) and found this bit, which literally nobody in class has brought up. Mill is setting up two rival ways of thinking about institutions:

"They look upon a constitution in the same light (difference of scale being allowed for) as they would upon a steam plough, or a threshing machine. [...] The fundamental political institutions of a people are considered by this school as a sort of organic growth from the nature and life of that people: a product of their habits, instincts, and unconscious wants and desires, scarcely at all of their deliberate purposes."

My possibly dumb theory: our argument is stuck becuase these two versions of Mill don't fit together. If a constitution is a machine, then anyone can in principle just choose a good one, and withholding self-government from India is inexcusable. If institutions grow out of a people's character, then "not ready yet" is a real category and the India question becomes empirical, not moral. So which is he?

My actual question: what KIND of disagreement is my class even having? Is it factual (what did Mill actually say and do), interpretive (which strand in this passage is the real Mill), or moral (is it even legit to judge him)? Diagnose it based on what each side is actually saying — don't just survey both — and tell me what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves the fight, a textual test that settles which Mill wins, or is this just one of those arguments where someone has to lose?
