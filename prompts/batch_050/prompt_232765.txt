I chair the tenure commitee in my department and I've fallen into the habit of reading social choice theory after our meetings, which is how I noticed something I can't find a name for.

The setup: our rules say tenure requires excellence in three areas -- research, teaching, and pulling in funding -- and then we vote on the actual question, grant or don't. Last year, three of us on the committee, and each of us thought the candidate was genuinely excellent in two of the three areas and merely fine in the third. But a different third for each of us. All three of us voted to grant, honestly, since each of us figures two out of three is plenty. So far so boring. The problem showed up when I drafted the report. Taken area by area, each criterion had a majority behind it -- two of us vouched for the research, two for the teaching, two for the funding -- so the report reads: the candidate is excellent in all three areas, therefore tenure. And here's the thing: not one of the three of us believes that sentence. Each of us thinks exactly one of the report's three claims is false.

So the outcome is unanimous and nobody has a complaint about it, but the justification belongs to no one. It's not that a majority voted no while the report says yes. It's that the reasons the committee gives for its yes are, taken as a package, rejected by every single member. I've since noticed the same shape in an appeals court opinion a lawyer friend showed me, where each paragraph of the reasoning had a majority of judges behind it but no individual judge would sign the whole thing.

I know this is adjacent to the discursive dilemma / doctrinal paradox stuff, but as I understand it the drama there is that voting on the premises can force a conclusion the majority rejects. Here the conclusion is the one everyone wanted all along. The pathology is entirely on the justification side, and it seems to survive the standard fixes: voting only on the bottom line gives you the same outcome with no rationale at all, which feels worse, and voting on the premises gives you a rationale nobody actually holds. Does this specific configuration have a name of its own, separate from the discursive dilemma proper? If it doesn't, what would you call it, and where are its edges -- does it still count as the same phenomenon if one member does accept the full rationale but abstained on the final vote, or if the package of reasons is held by someone who wasn't in the room? And does it actually matter, or does it only bite in cases like judicial opinions where the stated reasons go on to be used as inputs to later decisions?
