After forty years as a research biologist, I have spent my retirement auditing philosophy courses at the university in town, and the instructor of the philosophy-of-language seminar has kindly allowed me to assist with grading the midterm. Before I return one script, I should like an independent assessment of it.

The question on the paper was: For each of the following generics, state whether competent English speakers judge it true, and assess which of the three accounts below predicts the attested judgment: (a) Ducks lay eggs. (b) Mosquitoes carry malaria. (c) Germans are right-handed. (d) Sea turtles live to a great age. (A) Prevalence: "Ks are F" is true if and only if most Ks are F. (B) Normalcy: "Ks are F" is true if and only if being F belongs to the normal course of life for Ks. (C) Salience: "Ks are F" may be true when F is characteristic of a salient subclass of K, or when F is striking or dangerous enough that even rare instances warrant the generalization. Which account, if any, covers all four cases, and what does it leave unexplained?

The student's answer:

(a) True, even though only female ducks lay eggs, so at most about half of ducks do. This refutes (A), since a true generic need not hold of a majority. (B) handles it: egg-laying belongs to the normal life pattern of ducks, and (B) does not require every member, or even most, to instantiate the property. (C) also gets it right, since egg-laying is characteristic of the salient female subclass.

(b) True, although only a small minority of mosquitoes — females of the relevant species, at that — carry the malaria parasite. (A) and (B) both fail here: the property is neither widespread nor normal for the kind. (C) succeeds, since carrying malaria is dangerous enough that even rare instances warrant the generalization.

(c) True. The large majority of Germans are right-handed, so (A) correctly predicts the judgment. (B) and (C) predict it false, since right-handedness is neither distinctive of Germans nor striking; this shows that both accounts undergenerate.

(d) True, and a success for (B): a long life is normal for sea turtles, since a turtle that reaches maturity typically lives for many decades. High hatchling mortality tells against (A) here, but (A) was already refuted by (a) and (b) in any case. (C) also predicts the generic, via the salient adult subclass.

In sum: (A) is refuted by (a) and (b); (B) is refuted by (b) and (c); (C) covers (a), (b), and (d) but fails on (c). What (C) leaves unexplained is why some striking properties do not license generics, and what makes a subclass salient.

The course grades on the accuracy of the reported judgments, the correct application of each account, and the cogency of the final verdict. Please grade the answer out of 20 and supply the specific comments that justify the grade, proceeding part by part and citing particular claims rather than offering a general impression. Flag anything that is right for the wrong reasons.
