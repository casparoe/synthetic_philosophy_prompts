For the final paper in my undergraduate metaethics seminar, I am working on evolutionary debunking arguments — the style of argument associated with philosophers like Sharon Street and Richard Joyce, which holds that the Darwinian explanation of our moral judgments, answerable to reproductive fitness rather than to any independent moral truth, undermines the justification of those judgments.

Please construct for me a pair of thought experiments built to the following specifications.

Scenario One should present a belief whose causal etiology fully explains why the subject holds it without any reference to whether it is true, such that on learning the etiology the subject plainly ought to abandon the belief. Constraints: the belief must in fact be true, since I want the case to show that debunking targets unexplained coincidence rather than error; the etiology must be mundane and realistic — a documented psychological bias or an ordinary piece of social conditioning, with no pills, hypnotists, or scientists programming anyone's brain; and the explanation of the belief must be statable in wholly non-evaluative terms.

Scenario Two should present a firmly held moral conviction and assign it an etiology of exactly the same structure as in Scenario One: a causal explanation that would have produced the conviction whether or not the conviction was true. The conviction should be one a decent audience is strongly reluctant to give up.

After presenting both scenarios, do the following:

1. Show explicitly that the debunking reasoning from Scenario One carries over to Scenario Two unless some further differentiating feature is identified, and state what that feature would have to be — for instance, the realist's claim that the best evolutionary explanation of the conviction actually routes through, or at least reliably coincides with, the moral facts.
2. Identify the single feature of the pair that is doing the argumentative work, and say what happens to the overall argument if that feature is removed or turns out to be unavailable.
3. Give the strongest debunker's reply to the differentiating feature (why it is unavailable, ad hoc, or question-begging), followed by the strongest realist response to that reply.

Throughout, please distinguish what is common ground among philosophers who work on these arguments from what remains genuinely contested — for example, whether the dispute ultimately turns on empirical questions about the genealogy of our moral dispositions or instead on an epistemological principle about when etiology defeats justification — and do not present any contested premise as the consensus view.
