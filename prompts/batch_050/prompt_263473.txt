Here's a position I've talked myself into, and I want to know what would actually count against it. The view: what gives a creature moral status is its total capacity for rich inner life, and the best available proxy for that is sheer number of cortical neurons -- not intelligence, not behavior, just raw information-processing matter. On this view the ordering of moral concern doesn't track how useful or relatable or human-like animals are; it tracks cortical hardware. So an elephant with its huge cerebrum should get way more moral weight than a mouse, and probably more than a crow, even if the crow outsmarts the elephant on some tasks.

Some things that already make me sweat: elephants actually have *fewer* cortical neurons than humans even though their brains are three times bigger, which I'm treating as fine (humans on top, but by less than we think) -- but then the view also implies that individual humans with bigger cortices (there's real variation) have slightly more moral status, and I hate that conclusion. Also corvids cram neurons at crazy density into tiny brains, which suggests the proxy breaks at the small end.

So: what would defeat this view? Which empirical findings (say, if it turned out neuron count correlates terribly with anything welfare-relevant), which thought experiments (a neuron-rich blob with no unified experience?), and which downstream implications would a defender actually concede as damaging rather than bite the bullet on? I'd also like to know whether the view has been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability -- every counterexample gets absorbed by tweaking the proxy. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely embarrassing, and be honest if the view is so vague it doesn't risk anything.
