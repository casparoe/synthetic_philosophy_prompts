Most of the students have some high-school physics but no real philosophy of science. Don't explain the argument to me — plan how I should explain it.

I want the section to turn on the bit where Newton walks through the stages of the experiment: the bucket gets twisted up and released, the water starts out flat while the bucket is already spinning, and only later, once the water has picked up the motion, does it climb the sides. The students are going to read that passage in exactly the wrong way, I think — as saying the water climbs because it's moving relative to the room, or relative to them. So tell me how to sequence the section so that by the time we read that passage closely, they can see the problem Newton thinks the stages create: the effect doesn't track the water's motion relative to the bucket (it's flat when the relative motion is greatest, concave when relative motion is roughly zero), so something else has to be the reference. What has to be in place first — I'm guessing the kinematics/dynamics distinction and the idea that rotation has dynamical effects — and in what order?

Also: which modern carrying example should I use alongside the bucket (spinning space station? skater pulling her arms in?) and what it needs to preserve from the original. What misconceptions will they bring — I expect "centrifugal force is fictitious so Newton made a physics mistake," "Einstein showed everything is relative," and "Mach obviously wins" — and where in the sequence do I head each off? Should I bring up Leibniz and Berkeley's replies or leave them out, and is the two-globes-in-empty-space experiment worth the time or a distraction at this level? Finally, give me one question to end with that shows whether they got that the argument is about what rotation is motion relative to, not about how we can tell we're moving.
