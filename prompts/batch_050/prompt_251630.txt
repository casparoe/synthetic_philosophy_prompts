I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I've gotten myself stuck. For the book I'm writing the main character is a logician who becomes convinced he can prove he isn't a machine, and the conviction slowly eats him alive. To write him properly I spent the fall in the Lucas–Penrose rabbit hole, and what I can't make sense of isn't the argument itself, its the state of the debate.

As far as I can tell it's been frozen for decades. Lucas published his version in 1961, Penrose gave it a much bigger stage in the nineties, and since then the same exchange just repeats. The Gödelians say: for any consistent formal system rich enough for arithmetic there's a sentence it can't prove but we can see to be true, so the human mind can't be such a system. The critics answer: you only "see" the sentence true if you're already assuming the system is consistent (really, sound), and no human can establish that about their own reasoning, so the argument at most shows we aren't a *sound* machine, which nobody needed proving. Penrose replies that mathematicians genuinely grasp mathematical truth rather than mechanically deriving it, and round it goes.

Here's my working theory, which I want tested rather than confirmed: the debate isn't really about logic at all. I think the two sides quietly swapped questions. Penrose is answering "could mathematical understanding be computation?" and the critics are answering "can we prove it isn't?" — and the first-person/third-person asymmetry is doing all the work, because from inside my own reasoning I can't coherently doubt its soundness without undermining the doubt itself, but from the outside the machine can't certify itself either, so every move looks question-begging to the other side forever.

So tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is. Verbal? A hidden empirical question about whether human mathematicians are in fact sound? A standoff of bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? Two questions wearing one coat, like I suspect? Commit to one diagnosis and defend it with reasons drawn from what the sides actually say, and then tell me what, if anything, would move it — a theorem, a cleaner account of what it would even mean for a person to be sound, an empirical result about human mathematical error, or nothing ever. Don't hand me a menu of options and wish me luck; I need a verdict I can build a character around.
