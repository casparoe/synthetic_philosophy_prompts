A colleague and I have been circling the same disagreement for months, and I would like to work out where it actually lives. The question is whether the quantum wavefunction is real: whether a system's quantum state is part of the physical furniture of the world (the psi-ontic view) or instead a representation of an observer's information, beliefs, or expectations about the system (the psi-epistemic view). My credence that some broadly psi-ontic view is correct is about 0.75; hers is about 0.3. We are both working physicists rather than philosophers, so neither of us entirely trusts our own decomposition of the question.

Please give me your own credence, but more importantly the decomposition behind it. The sub-questions I would expect to matter include: whether the assumptions of the PBR theorem, in particular preparation independence, are defensible or whether they quietly presuppose something close to the conclusion; whether any genuinely psi-epistemic model can reproduce the full phenomenology of interference and measurement without smuggling the wavefunction back in as part of the ontology; whether the ontological-models framework that structures this debate carves the space of possibilities at the right joints at all, or whether views such as QBism and certain relational interpretations show the carving to be inadequate; and whether the question even has a determinate answer, given that the word 'real' is doing unexamined work on both sides. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, justify each in a line or two, and show how they combine into your final number.

Then do three further things. First, tell me which term your overall credence is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would move it most. Second, adjudicate between us: I believe our disagreement centres on the preparation-independence assumption, whereas she believes it centres on whether epistemic states can survive the constraints that contextuality results place on any underlying model of quantum phenomena. Given a gap from 0.3 to 0.75, which of us is more plausibly right about where the disagreement lives, and what would each of us have to concede to test that? Third, state what would count as the question being resolved, since no single experiment can decide it directly — and if you think no resolution is possible even in principle, say so explicitly and represent that as one of the terms in your decomposition rather than an excuse to give no number.

Please answer in British English.
