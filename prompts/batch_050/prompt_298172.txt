News outlets, HR departments, and marketing firms all sort people into generations with hard birth-year cutoffs -- say Millennials born 1981 to 1996, Gen Z from 1997 on. Whole training seminars exist on "managing Gen Z in the workplace," and a person born in December 1996 and one born in January 1997 get put in different boxes with supposedly different values, work ethics, and relationships to technology. Meanwhile the same institutions track age itself as a smooth variable everywhere else -- no one writes trend pieces about 31-year-olds as a type.

What picture of what a generation *is* does this practice presuppose? Does it have to assume cohorts are real natural kinds -- that being born in a certain window causally stamps people with a shared character, the way a species boundary does? Or could it get by with a much weaker view, where generations are more like constellations: convenient groupings drawn for narrative purposes, real only in that we keep pointing at them? And there's something odd about the cutoffs specifically: what would have to be true about history for the boundary to fall exactly at a birth year rather than at an event (a war ending, a technology arriving)? Does treating the boundary as sharp presuppose that social change is punctuated rather than gradual, and if so, does the practice survive contact with people born on the edges?

Reconstruct the implicit theory, say which parts of it the practice strictly needs versus which rival views it would also be compatible with (maybe the whole thing works fine as acknowledged fiction?), and tell me what would have to change -- in the seminars, the trend pieces, the marketing -- if generations turned out to be nothing more than statistical noise dressed up with names.
