I am drafting the position statement for a graduate seminar on individual ethics and collective harm, and I want a consistency audit before I commit to it. The topic is the so-called inefficacy objection: the worry that in large-scale collective problems, no single person's contribution changes the outcome, so no single person has reason to refrain. Below is the full set of commitments I currently hold. Please do not treat it as an invitation to write an essay on the topic; treat it as an audit of this specific set.

My commitments:

(a) In large collective harms, an individual contribution typically makes no difference to the outcome. Emissions systems, food supply chains, and elections respond to thresholds and aggregates, not to my flight, my chicken purchase, or my ballot taken one at a time.

(b) Nonetheless, I am morally required to abstain from factory-farmed meat, and I am morally required to vote in national elections.

(c) Obligations to act must be grounded in the expected impact of the act on the harm itself. I reject appeals to complicity, participation in a collective wrong, or expressing the right commitments as independent sources of obligation; at most they redescribe the question.

(d) The expected impact of a single retail meat purchase is negligible: the probability that my purchase is the one that crosses a supply threshold, multiplied by the suffering thereby caused, is vanishingly small.

(e) The expected impact of my vote is also minuscule, since the probability of a tied national election is minuscule; but the stakes are so large that the expected-impact calculation still yields a duty to vote.

(f) Generalization reasoning (what if everyone did that) has no place in individual ethics: the permissibility of my act cannot depend on a counterfactual about how others behave.

What I want from you: identify which of these commitments are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and what the least costly revision would be. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious, and for each one state precisely what would have to give. In particular, check whether my reasoning in (d) and (e) applies the expected-impact standard symmetrically, since I have a nagging worry there but cannot locate it. Do not signal which commitment you think I should abandon by softening your analysis of it; state the costs of each option plainly.

One further request: in your verdicts, separate what is settled among people who work on this problem (for instance, that thresholds and lumpiness in supply chains are the standard setup, or that expected-value replies are the orthodox first move) from what is genuinely contested (for instance, whether the empirical premise in something like (d) actually holds in real meat markets, or whether participation-based accounts can be made to work). Do not present a contested view as the consensus.

Structure your reply with these sections, in this order:

1. Tension audit (roughly 400 words): the genuine tensions, ranked by severity, each with the commitments involved and what would have to give.
2. Apparent tensions (roughly 150 words): pairs that look inconsistent but dissolve on inspection, with the one-line reason each dissolves.
3. The revision menu (roughly 200 words): the two or three cheapest ways to restore consistency, what each costs, and which you would take.
4. Settled versus contested (roughly 150 words): the points above flagged as consensus versus open, as requested.
