In three weeks I'm giving the best-man toast at my friend Daniel's wedding. He and his fiancée met in a graduate game-theory reading group, and roughly half the guests will be economists and AI-governance researchers who will catch every reference and pounce on every sloppy one; the other half are aunts, uncles, and old college friends who think game theory is about chess. Here is the angle I want to build the toast around. There is a concept his crowd calls a commitment race: a situation in which two or more parties compete to be the first to make an irreversible, costly-to-reverse commitment, because locking yourself in first gives you leverage over everyone who hasn't yet committed. The textbook cases are disasters — rival firms each publicly staking their reputations on prices they can't sustain, rival states each burning their bridges to prove they won't back down — because both sides end up bound to positions nobody actually wanted. My thesis for the toast: a wedding is the one commitment race that works, where both parties sprint to bind themselves first, in public, before the largest possible audience, and win precisely by losing the ability to leave gracefully. You are a professional speechwriter with a serious sideline in game theory. Before drafting, think carefully about what a commitment race actually is, why such races typically end badly, and exactly where the analogy to a wedding holds and where it breaks — because the experts in the room will immediately notice the obvious objections (marriages can be dissolved; a commitment device raises the cost of exit rather than eliminating it; here both parties commit simultaneously rather than racing for first-mover advantage). Decide which disanalogies the toast should openly concede and get laughs from rather than paper over. Then write the toast: about five minutes, roughly 650 words, one good game-theory joke at most, warmth for the family half, rigor that survives the other half, and a landing that works for both at once.
