I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I was at a cognitive science conference last month working on a piece about language and thought. At the hotel bar afterwards I ended up sitting next to two people -- a linguist and a cognitive psychologist, both legit in their fields -- who got into a running argument about the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis that went like four rounds. I recorded it for possible use in the piece but honestly I can't tell who won, and I want a second opinion before I write it up wrong.

Round 1: The psychologist said Whorfianism is basically dead, that the strong version (language determines thought) is unfalsifiable mush and the weak version (language influences thought) has been reduced to tiny lab effects, like Russian speakers being a hair faster at distinguishing light vs dark blue because Russian has separate words for them. She said that's a speed-of-processing quirk, not a different way of seeing the world.

Round 2: The linguist fired back that "tiny lab effects" is cherry-picking. He brought up speakers of languages with absolute spatial reference -- languages with no "left" or "right" where you have to say things like "the cup is north of the plate" -- and how those speakers stay oriented with an accuracy that baffles English speakers, and even gesture in absolute directions when remembering stories. He said you can't constantly track cardinal directions your whole life and call that a quirk; that's language restructuring cognition.

Round 3: The psychologist said that's actually evidence *against* Whorf, because it shows speakers can learn a cognitive skill (dead reckoning) that their language *requires*, which just means thought is flexible and language rides on top of it. If language really determined thought, she said, the causality claim would be untestable -- but here's the thing she said that stuck with me: bilinguals should then be cognitively trapped, unable to take off one language's "worldview" like a pair of glasses. But bilinguals switch languages all the time and report that it feels like switching modes, which she said proves the effects are shallow and contexxt-dependent rather than constitutive.

Round 4: The linguist said she's got it backwards -- the mode-switching feeling is exactly what the hypothesis predicts, and the real problem is that both of them keep talking about "thought" as one thing. He made a distinction I hadn't heard before: language might not change what you *can* think (competence) but changes what you *habitually and effortlessly* think about (what he called "thinking for speaking" -- what your language forces you to encode every single time you open your mouth, like grammatical gender or evidentiality marking, so those categories become automatic). He said the psychologst keeps scoring the strong version and ignoring this middle position, which is where the actual evidence lives.

Round 5 (brief): She said the middle position is trivially true and nobody ever denied it, and he said if it's trivial why did the field spend fifty years saying Whorf was simply wrong, and it kind of devolved from there.

So: adjudicate this for me, round by round. For each move, tell me whether it lands, whiffs, or does something sneaky like changing the subject. And specifically: is the "mode-switching" observation in round 3 evidence for or against linguistic relativity, or is it actually neutral? Is the competence-vs-habit distinction in round 4 a genuine middle position that survives her triviality objection, or is he just redefining the hypothesis until it's unfalsifiable, which is the exact charge he opened with against her? Finally -- is there something both of them are missing that I should put in the article so I don't just regurgitate a 70-year-old debate?
