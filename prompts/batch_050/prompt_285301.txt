So I retired two years ago after four decades as a field biologist -- seabirds mostly, some work on corvid cognition toward the end -- and I've been using my free time to catch up on the philosophy I skipped. Right now I'm stuck on Morgan's canon, the old rule of thumb that you shouldn't explain an animal's behavior by appeal to some fancy higher mental faculty if a simpler, lower one will do the job. My birding group argued about it for a whole evening last week, because half of us think it's just good scientific hygiene and the other half think it's a straightjacket that let a century of researchers pretend animals are clockwork. I can see both sides and honestly both sides annoy me.

Here's what I'd like: write me the deadpan internal memo, around 600 words, from the director of an imaginary animal behavior institute -- call it something bland like the Whitcombe Institute for Comparative Cognition -- announcing a new institute-wide policy. After "recent incidents" (a grant reviewer accused the lab of anthropomorphizing its star scrub jay; a graduate student was caught saying her octopus was "in a mood"), the director has decided that Morgan's canon will now be applied rigorously and consistently to EVERYTHING under institute jurisdiction. The memo should walk through the consequences with a straight face: the cafeteria's new explanation for why staff queue (taxis and released behavior, not "knowing what a queue is"), the ruling that principal investigators may no longer be credited with "insight" in grant proposals when reinforcement history suffices, the incident form researchers must file whenever they catch themselves describing a rat as "curious," and a closing note that the canon's own author is, pending review, probably best explained as a creature of habit himself.

The humor should come from taking the canon completely seriously and letting the regress do the work -- every "higher" explanation gets replaced by a "lower" one until nothing, human or animal, survives the parsimony audit. But I want the satire to be fair: a reader who knows the debate should recognize the real tension (anthropomorphism on one side, reflexive stinginess about animal minds on the other, and the awkward fact that "lower" faculties aren't always simpler), and the memo should never wink or state the moral outright. No real people, no real insitutions. Keep the corporate-memo voice painfully earnest throughout, and if you can work in one genuinely plausible bit of behaviorist jargon being used somewhere it obviously doesn't belong, that's the dream.
