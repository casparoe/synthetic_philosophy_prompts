I belong to a small recreational mathematics circle that meets once a month, and at our last gathering a friend presented a puzzle he claims shows that rationality is self-defeating. A host takes two envelopes, places some amount of money in one and twice that amount in the other, seals and shuffles them, and lets you pick one. Before opening it, you may switch to the other. My friend argues as follows. (1) Let A be the amount in the envelope you are holding. (2) The other envelope contains either A/2 or 2A, and since the shuffling destroyed all information about which envelope is which, the two possibilities are equally likely. (3) So the expected amount in the other envelope is one half of A/2 plus one half of 2A, which comes to 5A/4, strictly more than A. (4) Therefore you should switch. (5) But after switching you face exactly the same situation as before, so the very same argument instructs you to switch back, and since it can be applied again after every switch, each switch multiplies your expected holding by 5/4; the rational strategy is therefore to keep switching forever and never open anything, driving your expected wealth toward infinity. (6) Yet the arrangement is perfectly symmetric, and a strategy that merely shuttles you back and forth, leaving you in expectation with the same envelope you started with, cannot have manufactured wealth out of thin air, let alone infinite expected wealth.

I have turned this over for a week and cannot locate the mistake, but I refuse to accept step (5)'s picture of the perfectly rational player as an oscillating money pump who never gets to open anything. If I have to give something up, I would far sooner abandon the principle that one should maximize expected monetary value than surrender the elementary arithmetic in step (3) or the symmetry observation in step (6). A variation makes my confusion worse: suppose you open your envelope first and see that it contains $40. Then the other contains $20 or $80; I want to say these are equally likely, their average is $50, which beats $40, so you should switch — and in this version no infinite regress ever arises, so it seems to survive whatever kills the original argument. Does it?

Where exactly does the original chain first go wrong? Please identify the specific numbered step I should reject, explain why it looks so innocent when stated, and tell me what rejecting it costs: must I deny that the two possibilities in step (2) are equally likely, deny that expected-value comparisons can rationalize action, or accept something subtler about how the quantity A is being used across the steps? Finally, does your diagnosis also defuse the opened-envelope version, or must I concede that switching really is correct there? Please write your answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a letter.
