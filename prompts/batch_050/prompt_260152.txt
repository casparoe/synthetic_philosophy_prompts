I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general audience, and I'm working on a piece about medieval philosophers "getting there first" -- you know the genre, some 12th-century thinker anticipating Hume or Darwin or whoever. Right now I'm stuck on al-Ghazali and causation, because I interviewed two academics who completely disagreed and I can't tell who landed the better punches. The background, as I understand it: in the Incoherence of the Philosophers, al-Ghazali argues that when fire touches cotton and the cotton burns, we observe the two events together but never observe the fire *making* the cotton burn, and he concludes the connection isn't necessary -- God creates the burning directly, on the occasion of the contact, and the regular order of the world is just divine habit.

Here's the fight. Lena (philosophy of science) said this is basically Hume 600 years early: same observation that we only ever see conjunction, never connection, and she said the lesson is that the "problem of induction" was on the table long before modernity. Yusuf (Islamic philosophy) shot back that this flattens everything -- Hume's point is epistemological, that we can't *know* any necessary connection, whereas al-Ghazali's is metaphysical: the connection really isn't there, God is the only true cause, and we can know *that*. So al-Ghazali isn't proto-Hume, he's Hume's mirror image: skeptical conclusion reached via confidence about God, not skepticism about everything. Lena then changed tack: fine, but occasionalism makes science impossible, because if fire doesn't burn cotton then medicine and engineering rest on nothing, and al-Ghazali's own answer -- that God also creates in us reliable knowledge of His habitual order -- is an ad hoc patch, faith dressed up as a foundation. Yusuf said that's unfair twice over. First, al-Ghazali explicitly says that once you grant God's uniform habit, rational inference and medicine work exactly as before; his whole target is just the philosophers' claim that fire burns *by its own nature, necessarily*. Second -- and this is where they got properly heated -- Yusuf said the proof is the Abraham passage: al-Ghazali says, look, even if we grant for argument's sake that fire is a genuine cause, the Quran tells us Abraham was thrown into the fire and didn't burn, so either fire's nature can be changed or God blocks the effect, and therefore denying *necessity* is all he needs; he never has to deny the orderliness of nature. Lena replied that this proves her underlying point: the argument exists to make room for miracles, so it's theology driving philosophy, and the "habit" business is just there to stop the miracle-doctrine from eating the world. Yusuf's last move was that Lena was overstating the occasionalism itself -- plenty of the concession-arguments in the text ("suppose we grant the philosophers their causes...") suggest al-Ghazali was perfectly happy letting secondary causes do real work, so calling him a flat-out occasionalist might be a mistake commentators make, not one he made.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? Specifically: (1) who's closer to right about the Hume comparison -- early version, mirror image, or is the comparison just hopeless; (2) does occasionalism of his sort actually undercut science, and is the divine-habit reply ad hoc or a coherent position; (3) does the Abraham/fire bit show the whole discussion is motivated by safeguarding miracles, or can the argument stand on its own legs; and (4) does the text really commit him to full occasionalism, or does Yusuf have a point about the concessive structure? Also tell me if both of them are missing something obvious, because I have to write this up for readers who've never heard of either al-Ghazali or occassionalism and I don't want to print something embarassing.
