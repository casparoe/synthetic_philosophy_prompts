I've been reading a lot of old LessWrong threads on institutional design and it struck me that Ireland's Citizens' Assembly (2016–2018) is the best real-world test case sortition advocates have. Roughly: 99 randomly selected citizens, meeting over a series of weekends with expert testimony and facilitation, deliberated on the Eighth Amendment and recommended liberalizing abortion law; the subsequent referendum passed by about two-to-one, closely tracking the assembly's own vote. Analyze this episode through the lens of the strongest version of the sortition case — the claim that a randomly selected, informed, deliberating body represents the considered judgment of the public better than elected legislatures do. Then tell me where the analysis breaks: Did the agenda-setting, expert selection, and facilitation do the real work, such that the random sample is window dressing? Does the assembly's success in predicting the referendum outcome actually vindicate sortition, or just show the country had already moved? And what does the episode imply for the stronger lottocratic proposal of giving such bodies binding power rather than advisory power — does the case support it, or does it quietly depend on the referendum as a legitimating backstop?
