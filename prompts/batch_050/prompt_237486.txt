I've been a reader of the rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade, and like many people there I picked up my understanding of algorithmic information theory secondhand -- enough to use the vocabulary, not enough to always catch my own mistakes. A friend and I have been arguing about whether the usual formal story actually vindicates Occam's razor, and he has an argument that I cannot refute but also cannot accept, because it seems to prove far too much. I'd like a careful diagnosis. (Please write your answer in American English.)

The argument, as he gave it to me:

(1) Fix a universal Turing machine U. Define the universal prior M, which assigns to each observation sequence the probability that U, fed a random program, outputs that sequence. For any computable environment μ, predicting with M rather than μ costs you only a bounded amount of total expected error, where the bound is governed by K(μ), the length of μ's shortest program on U.

(2) Therefore the universal predictor is biased toward simplicity in a provable sense: the simpler the true environment (the shorter its program), the tighter the guarantee on how badly the universal predictor can do relative to it. This, he says, is a formal vindication of Occam's razor -- not a pragmatic rule of thumb but a theorem.

(3) The choice of U is arbitrary. By the invariance theorem, for any two universal machines U and U', the complexity functions differ by at most an additive constant independent of the string being described, so nothing essential hinges on which machine we picked.

(4) But now consider any finite sequence of observations and any hypothesis H about what comes next -- even a bizarre, ad hoc one. We can always construct a universal machine U' under which H is the shortest available explanation of the data so far, hence the maximally simple hypothesis, hence the one the universal prior favors.

(5) So by (2), applied with U' in place of U, predicting H is the Occam-endorsed move, complete with the tightest possible error bound.

(6) Since H was arbitrary, every possible prediction about the unobserved data is, on some legitimate choice of universal machine, the one that "simplicity" favors and the one the formal guarantee covers. The invariance theorem (3) tells us these choices are all equally respectable.

(7) Therefore the formal story vindicates everything and nothing: at any finite horizon, "the simplest hypothesis" is a free variable, and the celebrated convergence result gives us no reason at all to expect the future to resemble the past in the way we actually expect it to. Solomonoff induction, far from grounding Occam's razor, dissolves it.

Now, I would very much like to keep (1) and (2) -- the convergence result is the one piece of the formalism I feel on solid ground about -- and (3) is a theorem, so it presumably stays too. My suspicion is that something goes wrong in the neighborhood of (4), or in the way (4) is used in (5) and (6), but when I try to say exactly what, I end up gesturing at "cheating" without being able to state the rule being broken. My friend, for his part, insists the argument is valid and that the right response is to accept (7) and give up on Occam's razor as anything more than aesthetic preference.

So: which step should actually be rejected? If it is (4) or (5), state precisely what construction is being smuggled in and why it does not deliver what the argument needs -- in particular, what happens to the complexity of the hypothesis when you relocate it into the machine, and what that does to the bound in (1). If instead the argument is valid all the way through, tell me which of (1)-(3) is the least costly premise to abandon, and what we lose by abandoning it. Either way, I'd like to know what survives of the Occam justification once the smoke clears: is it purely asymptotic, does it require privileging some class of "natural" reference machines, and if so, does needing that privilege mean the formal story was doing less work than the blogs led me to believe?
