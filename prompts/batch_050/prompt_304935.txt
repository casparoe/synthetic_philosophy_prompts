A bit of background, since it shapes what I'm after. I spent forty years as a research chemist (polymers, nothing remotely to do with physics) and since retiring I've joined a philosophy reading group at the local U3A. We've just finished Bostrom's Superintelligence, and at the last meeting I got into an argument about the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergance. My gripe was that these ideas always get tested against thought experiments — paperclip maximisers, stamp collectors — when there's a perfectly good real episode sitting in the history books: the building of the atomic bomb. The others reckoned human history is too messy to test anything. I want to see who's right.

So here's the task. Take the Manhattan Project, roughly 1942 to 1945, plus the decade or so after, and analyse it through the lens of those two theses. Map it properly first: who the agents are (say Szilard, Fermi, Oppenheimer, Teller, and their political masters in Washington) and what their final goals actually were — and these genuinely differed, from beating Germany to it, to pure scientific curiosity, to Teller's fixation on fusion, to simply finishing the job one had been given. Then: does the episode confirm instrumental convergence? Did agents with very different ends converge on the same instrumental subgoals — keeping the project alive, controlling information, protecting their own positions and access? And on the other side: the Franck Report in mid-1945 arguing against surprise use on Japanese cities, Szilard's petition that summer, Oppenheimer's 1954 security hearing where Teller testified against him — are these the points where the orthogonality thesis creaks, where intelligence turned out not to be independent of ends at all, because the cleverest people kept letting their values steer the work?

I don't want a retelling of the history — I know the history. I want the analysis to run in both directions: what Bostrom's framework illuminates about Los Alamos, and what Los Alamos says back about the framework — confirmation, anomaly, or a case the theory was never designed for, given that human minds aren't the kind of agent the theses are really about? Act as a professional philosopher who knows both the AI safety literature and the history of twentieth-century physics, and think carefully — sketch the mapping to yourself before you write, rather than winging it. Open with a one-sentence bottom line on whether the episode on balance supports or undermines the two theses, then give the analysis. Maximum 700 words.
