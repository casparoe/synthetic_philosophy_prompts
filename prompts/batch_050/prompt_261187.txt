Pascal's mugging is usually presented as a problem where the mugger offers some astronomically large payoff (3^^^3 happy lives or whatever) at a tiny probability, and expected value reasoning says you hand over your wallet. The standard fix people reach for first is some kind of truncation: just refuse to take seriously hypotheses whose probability is below some threshold, or apply a "leverage penalty" that discounts the probability the bigger the claimed payoff. Please explain, at a gut level, why both of these fixes don't really work. Specifically I want to understand (a) why a hard probability threshold either makes you exploitable in other ways or makes you dogmatically immune to evidence once the stakes scale up, and (b) why the leverage penalty basically amounts to smuggling in a bounded utility function without admitting it. If that's right, is the real takeaway of the mugging just that unbounded utility functions are the culprit, and everything else is window dressing? Or is there a sense in which the mugging is a problem even for an agent with bounded utility?
