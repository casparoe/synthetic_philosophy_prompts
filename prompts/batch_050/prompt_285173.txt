I recently finished a master's thesis on Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes, and I am now choosing between two research groups for my doctorate — one working squarely inside the dominant paradigm of my field, the other attached to a heterodox upstart. To keep the decision honest, I sat down and wrote out, as explicitly as I could, the methodological commitments I want to govern how I evaluate research fields, including my own. I think of the list as a kind of personal creed. When I showed it to a friend in the philosophy department, she read it twice and told me it does not hang together — but, being the sort of friend she is, refused to say more until I had located the problem myself. After a week of staring at it I still cannot, so I am asking for an outside audit.

Here is the creed. (1) The proper unit of appraisal is the research programme rather than the isolated theory: a hard core rendered irrefutable by methodological decision, surrounded by a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses revised under the guidance of a positive heuristic. A programme is progressive when successive revisions of the belt predict novel facts that are later corroborated, and degenerating when they merely accommodate anomalies after the fact. (2) No anomaly, however stubborn, by itself refutes a programme. Recalcitrant results are shelved or processed within the belt, and treating them as falsifications of the hard core is simply a methodological mistake. (3) Whether a fact counts as novel is an objective, time-indexed matter, settleable from the public historical record: a fact is novel relative to a theory if and only if it was not known at the time the theory predicting it was first proposed. Novelty is not in the eye of the beholder. (4) The anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion, which had been known for decades before general relativity, is among the strongest evidence for that theory — precisely because Einstein derived the precession from the theory rather than adjusting the theory to fit the precession. (5) There is no instant rationality. Since degenerating programmes sometimes recover — the atomic programme was widely written off around 1900, shortly before its spectacular revival — it is never rational to pronounce a programme dead at a time; the most one can honestly do is report its track record to date. (6) Funding bodies, hiring committees, and individual scientists choosing a research direction act rationally when they shift money and people away from long-degenerating programmes toward progressive rivals.

What I want is an audit, not a tutorial on Lakatos. Tell me which commitments genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to, ordering the genuine tensions from most to least damaging. For each genuine tension, give the cheapest available fix and state plainly what it would cost me. If some pair looks contradictory but is not once the commitments are read carefully, flag that explicitly too — I would rather have real discrimination than a flat list in which everything is treated as equally problematic. Finally, if I could revise only one item on the list, which revision would remove the most strain?
