Last week at our department's ethics reading group we ended up in a two and a half hour argument about the classic torture-versus-dust-specks case (50 years of horrible torture for one person, versus a dust speck in the eye for some astronomical number of people), and I defended a cluster of views that everyone agreed seemed "basically sensible" individually but that two people claimed was an inconsistent package. Nobody could actually tell me where the inconsistency was supposed to live, though, and I couldn't either by the end. So here's roughly where I've landed and I'd like a proper audit. I hold all five of these:

(a) I pick the torture. No finite number of dust specks, however large, outweighs 50 years of torture for one person. This isn't squeamishness, I'm willing to defend it as the actual verdict.
(b) Within a single life, tiny harms genuinely add up. A life of 80 years with a barely-noticeable headache every waking minute is, all else equal, worse than the same life with one week of severe pain in the middle. So I accept intrapersonal aggregation of very small bads into very large ones.
(c) Betterness is transitive and there are no sharp thresholds: if you take any two outcomes and slide one continuously -- making the per-person harm slightly milder while slightly increasing the number of people affected -- there's no point where a hair's-width change flips the ranking. Any view that says "one extra speck, or one second less of pain, tips it" strikes me as a reductio of itself.
(d) The boundaries between persons don't matter for how bad an outcome impersonally is. A hundred-year life containing a bad moment every minute is, from the point of view of outcome value, equivalent to a vast crowd of momentary lives each containing one such moment. What matters is the pattern of experiences, not how they're bundled into selves.
(e) Holding the total amount of harm fixed, it's better that fewer people bear it -- I'd rather one person suffer a given total of pain than that same total be divided equally among a million.

So: does this hang together? Please treat it as an audit, not a survey of the literature. I want the genuine tensions in the set identified and ordered by severity, with each one spelled out as an actual argument (which premises of mine generate the contradiction, step by step), and for each, what would have to give and what the cheapest repair costs me. I also want you to tell me which pairs among (a)-(e) only *look* like they conflict but actually don't once you state them carefully, because I suspect at least one of the objections I got at the reading group was of that kind. Please don't just flag everything and call it a day -- the discriminating between real and apparent tensions is the part I care about most, and I genuinely don't know where the rot is, if there is any.
