Two friends of mine who are both taking the same early modern seminar got into a long argument after class about Spinoza, and they asked me to weigh in, but I honestly can't decide and want an outside opinion. The fight started over the intellectual love of God in Part 5 of the Ethics. Maya's position: the whole thing falls apart once you take Spinoza's necessitarianism seriously. If everything follows from God's nature with absolute necessity and nothing could have been otherwise, then "loving" God is a weird response -- love seems to make sense only toward something that could have failed you but didn't, or at least toward something contingent. What Spinoza calls the intellectual love of God, she says, is just resignation dressed up in nicer language: you stop fighting the inevitable and call it joy. She also pressed a second point: Part 5 reads like advice -- do this, understand more, and you'll achieve peace of mind -- but if everything is necessitated, then whether you achieve that understanding is itself necessitated, so the exhortations are empty. Either you were going to get there or you weren't.

Daniel pushed back on both points. On the first, he said Maya is importing a notion of love that Spinoza explicitly rejects -- for Spinoza love is just joy accompanied by the idea of an external cause, and since adequate understanding shows you that God is the immanent cause of everything, including your own mind, understanding more quite literally *is* loving God more; the necessity of it all doesn't weaken the love, it's exactly what makes the love stable and immune to the jealousy and disappointment that come with loving contingent things that can change or leave. On the second point, he said the "empty advice" objection confuses a description with a command: Spinoza isn't commanding anyone, he's tracing how a mind that does come to understand more will in fact become more free and more joyful, the same way a doctor describing how exercise improves health isn't doing anything incoherent just because your exercising or not was determined. Maya's comeback: but then the ethical vocabulary of freedom and bondage is doing no real work -- a stone that rolls downhill necessarily isn't "free" in any interesting sense just because we describe its trajectory accurately, and if confused, passive, suffering minds are equally necessary expressions of God, it seems almost insulting to say the sage's necessity is somehow better than the fool's. Daniel said the better/worse is internal to a conatus-relative notion of perfection and power, not an absolute ranking, and Maya said that's exactly the kind of move that makes Spinoza's ethics circular. Then they both looked at me.

So: who's right about which specific moves here? I want a point-by-point verdict -- is Maya right that love requires contingency, or does Daniel's definitional reply actually work? Is the "empty advice" objection a real problem for Part 5, or does the description-not-command defense settle it? Is Maya's stone analogy fair or does it miss something about minds versus bodies? And is there anything both of them are missing about how necessitarianism and the intellectual love of God are supposed to fit together?
