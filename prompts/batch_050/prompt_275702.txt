You are a historian of economics and political philosopher facilitating an adversarial collaboration between two camps in the socialist calculation debate: a defender of Mises and Hayek, who holds that socialism faces an in-principle calculation problem, and a defender of Oskar Lange's market socialism, who holds that a Central Planning Board adjusting administered prices by trial and error answers the challenge. Think carefully about what each side actually argued before drafting; attribute to neither camp positions its members did not hold.

Draft the joint statement the collaboration would publish, in this format:

1. Agreed claims: a numbered list of substantive propositions both sides can sign — about what the debate is and is not about, about what markets accomplish, and about what Lange's trial-and-error procedure would in fact deliver. Nothing vacuous.
2. Residual disagreements: a short table in which each disagreement is stated in wording both parties would accept for their own side.
3. Cruxes: sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side, with each side's current answer and what it would count as evidence against that answer.

The statement must include a joint gloss of the following two passages from F. A. Hayek's "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (American Economic Review, 1945). The Hayekian must say whether these passages, read strictly, indict Lange's scheme or only the fully centralized variants Lange himself set aside; the Langean must say whether a planning board that observes shortages and surpluses and reprices accordingly counts as knowledge being conveyed to the planner, or instead presupposes the data being "given to a single mind" in the very way Hayek denies is possible.

"The reason for this is that the 'data' from which the economic calculus starts are never for the whole society 'given' to a single mind which could work out the implications and can never be so given."

"This is not a dispute about whether planning is to be done or not. It is a dispute as to whether planning is to be done centrally, by one authority for the whole economic system, or is to be divided among many individuals."

Finally, the statement must take a position on one case none of the original disputants faced: a planning authority with real-time access to every point-of-sale record, warehouse inventory level, and online search query in the economy, plus modern optimization and demand-forecasting software. Each side must state in advance whether this case changes its verdict and why, and both must say which of the two passages, if either, it leaves untouched.

If you conclude that part of the historical dispute is verbal — for instance, over what "given" or "planning" means — identify that part explicitly and keep it out of the disagreements table. Aim for under 900 words, excluding the quoted passages.
