Okay so I'm a novelist, and my brother-in-law (who works in advertising, which matters) cornered me at Christmas with an argument I've been picking at ever since. Here it is, cleaned up a bit.

(1) Influencing someone's beliefs or feelings counts as manipulation when it works by going around their capacity to weigh reasons, rather than by putting reasons in front of them to evaluate.
(2) Whether an influence is manipulation can't depend on the medium it arrives in. A novel, an ad, and a political speech stand or fall together if they work on the mind in the same way.
(3) Fiction doesn't work by putting forward reasons. It works by making you inhabit a character, by pacing and imagery, by withholding information and springing it on you at the right moment, by making the sympathetic view feel true before you've examined it. A novelist who does the job well is deliberately steering the reader's emotions, and the reader mostly can't see the machinery while it's working.
(4) So a novelist making readers grieve a made-up person, or come away sympathizing with a view they'd have rejected in an argument, is doing structurally the same thing as an ad that makes you feel inadequate so you'll buy the shampoo.
(5) So either both are manipulation or neither is.
(6) I'm not prepared to say I'm running the same con as a propaganda minister, and neither is anyone else who reads books. That's just off the table.
(7) Therefore advertising-style emotional influence isn't manipulation either. Which, he cheerfully concluded, means his job is fine and I should stop being precious.

Now, I refuse the conclusion — something definately slipped somewhere — but when I try to name the step, every candidate seems to cost me something. (3) is just... an accurate description of my actual craft, sadly. And I'm not sure I'm allowed to say the difference is that readers consent, because readers don't know in advance what's going to be done to them; that's half the point of a good book, and anyway you "consent" to watch TV in the same thin sense.

So: which premise or step do you think I should reject, why does it look so plausible given (1) through (4), and what do I have to give up by rejecting it? If I get to keep one thing, I'd rather keep (6) — that there's a real line between what I do and what he does — but I want to know the honest price of that.
