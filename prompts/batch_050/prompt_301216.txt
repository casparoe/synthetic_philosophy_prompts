So I'm running a small reading/viewing group at my department and next session we're doing Breaking Bad, specifically the run from late season 2 through season 3 (Jane's death, the plane crash, Walt poisoning Brock). I want to read it through the Knobe effect literature -- the finding that people say a foreseen side effect was brought about "intentionally" when its bad, but not when its good, even when the agent's knowledge and indifference are held fixed.

The show seems almost designed for this. Walt lets Jane die: he knows she'll aspirate, he doesnt want her dead exactly, but he foresees it and does nothing. Then the Wayfarer 515 crash happens as a downstream consequence -- Jane's father, distraught, goes back to work as an air traffic controller too soon. Nobody foresaw that. And then with Brock, Walt poisons a kid as a *means*, not a side effect, which seems like it should break the Knobe frame entirely since there the harm really is intended in any theory's sense.

Here's what I want from the reading:

1. Does the show itself track the Knobe asymmetry in how it gets us to judge characters? E.g. nobody in the show or the audience seems to give Walt credit for "intentionally" providing for his family (his stated aim from episode 1) the way we blame him for intending harms he only foresaw. Is that the asymmetry dramatized, or is something else going on (e.g. the credit side just never gets tested because the money keeps getting contaminated)?

2. The Jane scene is the interesting hard case because its an omission. Knobe's original chairman cases are all actions. Does the folk judgment about Walt "letting her die" pattern with the intentional-harm side of the effect, or does omission break the pattern? Point to how the scene is staged if it matters (he tries to save her and then stops, right?).

3. The plane crash: blame clearly cascades onto Walt in the audience's mind even though he couldnt have foreseen it. Is that the Knobe effect overextending -- people retrofitting intentionality onto an unforeseeable outcome because the valence is bad -- or is it a different phenomenon (moral luck, causal-responsibility tracking)? Does the show take a position?

4. Where does the show resist the reading? I don't want the Knobe effect used as a skeleton key. If there are scenes where the folk-intentionality frame just doesnt fit, say so.

End with what, if anything, the show adds that the vignette studies can't: my worry is the experimental cases are so thin (a chairman, the environment, one paragraph) that they can't test how judgments of intentionality accumulate over a whole arc of foreseen harms. Does the serial format let us see something about how intentionality attributions compound or revise that a one-shot vignette never could?

We're not film-studies people so keep it philosophically pointed, and no need to quote dialogue, just describe scenes well enough that we can find them.
