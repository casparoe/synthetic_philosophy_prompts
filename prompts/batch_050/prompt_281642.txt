I teach philosophy for a living, which makes it faintly embarrassing that the decision problem I cannot crack is waiting for me every day at my own bathroom cabinet. Here is the situation, and I would like your advice on how to reason about it.

I take a prescription medication once daily, and my routine affords me exactly two opportunities to take it: breakfast and dinner. Breakfast is the ideal time — the drug works best then — but an evening dose on its own is still clearly worthwhile, just a bit less effective. Two outcomes are genuinely bad. Taking the pill twice in one day risks a reaction serious enough to put me in the hospital. Taking it not at all lets the underlying condition flare — bad, though roughly an order of magnitude less bad than the hospital. For concreteness, score the harms this way: the double dose costs 10, the missed day costs 1, and a day on which I take it only at dinner costs 0.5, relative to a clean breakfast dose at 0.

The catch is my memory. When I find myself at the cabinet, I usually cannot recall whether I have already taken the day's dose, and experience has taught me that my felt sense of remembering is worthless as evidence either way. As far as I can tell, the two occasions are subjectively indistinguishable: whichever meal it is, the question confronts me in exactly the same form — take it now, or not.

Please do not tell me to buy a pill organizer or set a phone alarm. I know about those. What I want is the theory. There seem to be two standpoints available to me, and I suspect they do not speak with one voice. From the armchair — say, planning on a Sunday — I can ask what standing rule for occasions of this kind would minimize my expected harm over the long run. The answer there seems to be some rule that takes the pill only a certain fraction of the time, with the fraction tuned to the harms above, and I am willing to randomize at the cabinet if that is what the arithmetic demands. But from the cabinet the question is singular: this dose, now. And it seems I should answer that question by weighing my uncertainty about which occasion I am on against the same harms. If the two standpoints recommend different things, I cannot tell which has authority over me. Is the plan I would have endorsed in advance binding on the person I am at the cabinet? Or is that person entitled — rationally required, even — to second-guess it?

Structure your response in the following sections, in this order:

1. The Shape of My Problem (about 200 words): characterize the decision-theoretic structure of my situation in plain terms. Do not feel obliged to name the literature; I care about the analysis, not the pedigree.
2. The Sunday Plan (about 200 words): work out the best standing rule from the ex ante standpoint, showing the arithmetic with the harms as I scored them.
3. The View from the Cabinet (about 250 words): reconstruct the reasoning available to me in the moment, including what I ought to believe, standing there, about which of the two occasions this is.
4. Do the Two Standpoints Conflict? (about 250 words): tell me whether the two perspectives genuinely diverge in a case like mine. If they do, adjudicate — which should govern, and why? If they do not, explain what makes the conflict I sense illusory here, and what feature a situation would need in order for the conflict to be real.
5. Your Prescription (about 100 words): a bottom line I can actually follow at the cabinet tomorrow morning.
