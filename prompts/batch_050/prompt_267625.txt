I'm an applied statistician who works in causal inference. I think fluently in terms of potential outcomes, directed acyclic graphs, confounding, instrumental variables, natural experiments, mediation, and effect heterogeneity, and my professional reflex is to ask of any causal claim whether the effect is actually identified or merely asserted.

Lately I've been reading history written under the banner of "non-human agency": work that treats pathogens, climate shifts, and volcanic eruptions not as backdrop but as actors that do things in history — the way smallpox is said to have conquered empires more thoroughly than any army, or a single eruption is said to have chilled harvests, starved populations, and shaken governments continents away. Alongside this literature runs an older, still-potent accusation: that this style of explanation is environmental determinism, a charge historians deploy when they want to end an argument.

Translate this historiographical dispute into my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. In particular: what kind of causal claim is "the eruption caused the famine which caused the revolt," and what does its identification require? Is a slow-moving climate trend even a well-defined treatment, and if not, what happens to claims that climate "acted"? Does the determinism charge correspond to anything I would recognize — overreach from an instrument, attributing all variance to an exogenous shock, ignoring mediation through human choices?

Then give me the residue report. My own candidates for what won't translate: calling a microbe an "actor" seems to say something about agency rather than mere causation, and historians argue about contingency and narrative in ways that don't look like disagreements about identification. For each residue item, tell me whether it marks a gap in my framework or a claim the historians cannot make precise. And be honest wherever translation weakens the original — if "climate caused the collapse" survives only as a statement about shifted outcome distributions, say so plainly.
