I'm teaching an upper-level undergraduate seminar on practical rationality (about 14 students, one 75-minute meeting) and we're spending two weeks on cases where being rational at the moment of choice seems to make you worse off. The centerpiece is Parfit's hitchhiker case: a stranger rescues you from the desert on the strength of your sincere promise to pay her once you're safely in town; but once you're safe, the self-interestedly rational move is not to pay, and since a perceptive driver could have foreseen that, she'd never have rescued you in the first place. My students grasp the logic on paper but treat it as a curiosity, because nothing is at stake for them and they get to imagine themselves as the kind of person who would obviously pay. I want an in-class game that strips away that complacency — one where they actually experience the temptation to defect at the payment stage, watch trust dry up across rounds as a result, and feel in their own decisions the difference between what it is rational to do and what it is rational to be disposed to do.

Design the game for me. I'd like it to involve repeated rounds of rescue-and-payment interactions with rotating roles, points or tokens instead of money, and some mechanism that makes players' dispositions at least partly visible to would-be rescuers (an imperfect "character-reading" device or public track record), since the philosophical bite of the case is that what matters is what kind of chooser you are, not what you say. Ideally a later round should also let players purchase or adopt some kind of commitment device, so the group discovers for themselves why someone might rationally choose to constrain their own future rationality. The problem should show up in what happens during play, before anyone names it.

Please structure your answer with these sections, in this order: Setup and Materials (about 100–150 words — what I need to prepare, role distribution, the point economy); Rules and Payoffs (about 200–250 words — the exact structure of a round, how promises, rescues, payments, and the character-reading mechanism work numerically); Running Order (about 150 words — a timeline for the 75 minutes, including when to introduce the commitment device and when to stop play); Debrief (about 200 words — how to map specific moments that will have occurred in play onto the philosophical points: the rationality of the refusal at the payment node, why a sincere promise isn't enough, and what "rational irrationality" means concretely); Predictable Failure Modes (about 150 words — what will go wrong the first time I run this, e.g. students colluding, treating it as a pure iterated prisoner's dilemma, or gaming the reading mechanism — and how to handle each); and What the Game Can't Reproduce (about 100 words — which features of Parfit's case, such as the one-shot nature of the original dilemma or the role of genuine character versus strategic play, the activity inevitably distorts, and what I should say about that in the debrief).
