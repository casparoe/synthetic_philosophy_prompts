Three weeks ago my brother and I became co-executors of our mother's estate, and we have already settled into the pattern every negotiation book warns about: each of us is certain the other took advantage first, and each of us describes our own small retaliations as merely being reasonable. Friends keep urging me to make the first conciliatory move and trust that reciprocity will do the rest, usually with a vague appeal to game theory behind it. Before I act on that advice, I want the other side of the argument pressed against me as hard as possible, by someone who will not let me off the hook.

Please play a committed skeptic of the cooperation literature: someone who knows the iterated prisoner's dilemma results thoroughly (Axelrod's tournaments, tit-for-tat, forgiveness, the effects of noise) and thinks they license almost nothing for messy real disputes like mine. Your view, roughly, is that the tournaments are stylized in ways that quietly stack the deck for cooperation: fixed pairings with no option to exit the relationship, a shared and unambiguous record of who did what, no power asymmetries, and an indefinite horizon every player can count on. Real estrangements between siblings have none of those properties, and the sunny lesson people take from the results (be nice, retaliate, forgive, be clear) is in your view a morality tale wearing a theorem's clothing. You also think my urge to make the first generous move is less about strategy than about buying the moral high ground at a discount. Be sharp but not a strawman: concede small points graciously when they are sound, never concede the main one, and avoid therapy-speak entirely.

Stay in character until I type stop. Keep each of your replies to a single paragraph, so this feels like a conversation rather than a lecture. No headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables: continuous prose only, the way one would write a letter. American English, please.

Here is my opening move: the second tournament is the part you have to explain away, not the first. Every entrant in it knew tit-for-tat had won the first time, so they could and did design strategies specifically to beat it, and tit-for-tat won again. If exploiting the conciliatory were as reliable a route to success in repeated interaction as you claim, at least one of those entrants, forewarned and highly motivated, should have found the exploit. They did not. What is your account of that?
