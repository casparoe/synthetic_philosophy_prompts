I am a journalist writing a long-form piece for a science-and-ideas magazine on how historians and social scientists handle counterfactuals, and I keep encountering the same analogy, usually deployed by writers of a structuralist bent: Hitler was merely the spark and Weimar Germany the dry tinder. A forest fire needs both, but once the tinder is ready any spark will do — fixating on the man is like crediting one particular match for a blaze that lightning, or a discarded cigarette, would have started regardless. The conclusion the analogy is made to carry is that something like the Third Reich — an aggressive, revanchist, racially obsessed dictatorship arising from the wreckage of the Great War, Versailles, hyperinflation, and the Depression — was close to inevitable by the early 1930s, and that the counterfactual question "what if Hitler had been killed in the trenches?" is therefore idle.

I would like a proper audit of the analogy before I let it into the piece. First, set out the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine: what plays the role of the spark, the tinder, the oxygen, the wind, the fire itself. Second, identify where the mapping breaks, and be discriminating about it: separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended use from the ones that are merely true but irrelevant. Candidates I want assessed include: sparks are interchangeable in a way leaders plausibly are not, because a fire's course is fixed by fuel and weather whereas a regime's course may partly be fixed by the particular obsessions, talents, and risk appetite of whoever seizes it; combustion has no analogue of veto players, party bureaucracies, general staffs, or succession struggles that can throttle or redirect a leader; and fires do not strategise, lie, or learn. Third, say what the analogy quietly imports: a picture in which macro-level conditions determine outcomes and individuals are epiphenomenal — which looks suspiciously like the contested premise itself, smuggled in as physics. Fourth, address whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, distinguishing a weak version (some authoritarian, revanchist, antisemitic German regime was likely without Hitler) from the strong version (something recognisably Nazi, including a war of annihilation in the East and a programme of industrial genocide, was likely without him), since the analogy arguably supports the first while being used to sell the second. Finally, if the spark-and-tinder picture cannot do the work, propose a better analogy — one that captures both the genuine force of structural explanation and the ways a single actor might alter a trajectory already in motion — and say what the improved analogy does to the counterfactual question my piece is organised around.
