I'm a senior in high school, and in my practical ethics elective our teacher has spent the last month on a unit about comparing possible futures that contain different numbers of people. Each week she describes two futures for an isolated colony -- self-sufficient, no contact with the outside world -- and tells us which she judges better, without explaining her rule. She insists she has been perfectly consistent. Her verdicts so far:

1. Future A: 1,000 colonists, all leading excellent lives. Future B: the same 1,000 leading excellent lives, plus 2,000 additional colonists leading lives that are clearly good and well worth living, though not as good as the first group's. She judges B better.

2. Future C is identical to A. Future D: the same 1,000 leading excellent lives, plus 20,000 additional colonists whose lives are barely worth living -- no real suffering, but only slightly above the point where a life would not be worth living at all. She grants that the total well-being in D exceeds that of A or B, yet judges D worse than both.

3. Given a fixed surplus, the colony can either fund medicine that raises 500 existing colonists from good lives to excellent lives, or fund a settlement program resulting in 600 additional colonists with excellent lives. She concedes the settlement program adds more total well-being, but prefers the medicine.

Next week's case: Future E -- the colony stays at 1,000 people leading good (not excellent) lives. Future F -- those same 1,000 people have slightly worse lives than in E, but there are also 3,000 additional people living at the same level as the extra group in Future B, so total well-being in F far exceeds E's while the average is lower.

Please predict her verdict on E versus F, and walk me through your reasoning: which familiar rules for ranking such futures are ruled in or out by verdicts 1–3, whether a single rule or only a combination fits her record, and which further comparison would most efficiently settle between the candidates that remain.
