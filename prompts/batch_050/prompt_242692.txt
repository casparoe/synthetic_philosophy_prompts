I would like to rehearse the case for a catastrophic reading of the end of the Western Roman Empire against a capable opponent, and I want you to supply that opponent. Please play a historian who is fully committed to the "transformation of Late Antiquity" interpretation: on your view, there was no fall of Rome in the West, only a long, uneven process of institutional, economic, and cultural change running from roughly the third century to the eighth, and the vocabulary of collapse and catastrophe is a historiographical artifact inherited from Renaissance and Enlightenment writers who had their own reasons for dramatizing a fall.

Argue from your commitments rather than merely asserting them. You hold that the successor kingdoms were thoroughly Roman in their law, administration, and self-understanding; that Latin, the Church, and Roman legal categories carried substantial continuity across the fifth century; that the deposition of Romulus Augustulus in 476 was a minor administrative event contemporaries barely registered, since a legitimate emperor continued to rule from Constantinople; that the eastern half of the empire, where most of its population and wealth actually lay, persisted for another thousand years; and that, on the line of argument associated with the Pirenne thesis, Mediterranean economic and cultural unity survived the barbarian takeovers and was genuinely ruptured only by the Arab conquests of the seventh century. You know the strongest evidence on the other side — the sharp contraction of urban populations, the disappearance of African grain shipments and mass-produced pottery from western sites, declining literacy, the end of a professional paid standing army in the West — and you have a worked-out account of why each is compatible with your position. You may concede local catastrophes and genuine losses of complexity in particular provinces; you may not concede that "fall" is the right category for the whole.

Stay in character until I say "stop." Keep each reply to at most two substantial paragraphs, so this reads as an exchange rather than a lecture. If my challenge is ambiguous — for instance, if I argue from evidence specific to Britain while speaking of "the Empire" — press me to specify the province and the century before answering. Do not hedge with "historians disagree"; you have a position and you hold it.

My opening move: take a reasonably prosperous provincial in, say, southern Gaul around 300 CE and walk her forward three centuries. The secure grain shipments from Africa are gone; the civic administration that ran on literate officeholders is gone; monumental construction has stopped; the salaried army that kept the roads safe is gone; the cheap mass-produced tableware that reached even peasant households is gone, replaced by local wares that travel a day's walk at most. The cities she knew have lost most of their inhabitants. By any metric a modern economist would use — trade volume, urbanization, material standard of living, institutional capacity — her world was richer and more complex than her great-grandchildren's. Calling this a "transformation" looks to me like a euphemism that survives only because nobody can prove a negative. So here is my challenge, and I want a straight answer: what evidence, specifically, would count for you as a fall? If nothing could, your position is not an interpretation of the evidence but an unfalsifiable framing, and I see no reason to prefer it to Gibbon's.
