So my business ethics class keeps going around in circles on whether a corporation itself can be blameworthy, as opposed to just the people in it, and I realized I have pretty firm gut reactions to cases even though I can't articulate what principle is driving them. Let me just lay out my verdicts and you tell me what theory I actually hold, if any. Case one: a pharma company's pricing algorithm, which three different teams each built a piece of, ends up setting insulin prices so high that some patients ration and die, and no single employee understood the whole system or intended that outcome. I feel strongly the company itself is blameworthy here, not just "bad things happened." Case two: the CEO of a shipping company personally orders a captain to cut costs by skipping maintenance, a hull fails, people drown. Here I want to blame the CEO, and saying "the company is guilty too" feels almost redundant, like it's just him wearing a company hat. Case three: a bank's official written policy is perfectly responsible, but there's an entrenched culture where everyone quietly ignores it and frontline staff push predatory loans, and management has looked the other way for years. I blame the bank, not just the individual brokers. Case four: a startup with six employees, where the founder makes every decision, does something reckless under her direct orders. I don't blame the startup at all, just her -- it's basically her alter ego. Case five: a company does something genuinely awful by any normal standard, like dumping waste in a river, but it turns out every individual involved followed reasonable-seeming orders and each person's contribution was tiny. I still want to say the company is blameworthy, but honestly this one sits uneasily with how I judged the pharma case, because there the diffusion of responsibility was my whole reason for blaming the company instead of the people, and here the same diffusion seems to make me want to let the individuals off while still nailing the company. Maybe that's fine, but it also might be incoherent.

So: is there one principle that captures when a corporation itself is a fit target of blame that delivers all five of my verdicts? Something like "blameworthiness requires decision-making structures that can't be reduced to one person's will" gets cases 2 and 4 but I'm not sure what it does with 1, where there arguably was no decision at all, just an emergent outcome. If no principle saves all five, tell me which verdict you'd overturn and why. And then apply whatever principle you land on to a case I'm genuinely unsure about: a company's AI customer-service system, deployed with normal testing, starts systematically denying valid insurance claims in a way nobody explicitly designed, and it takes the company eight months to notice -- blameworthy the whole time, only after they should have noticed, or never?
