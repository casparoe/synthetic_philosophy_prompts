I got into an argument last week with a friend who studies history, and I realized I kept winning too easily because she wouldn't actually defend the thing I was attacking. So I want a real sparring partner. Please play a historian who is an unrepentant defender of what people dismiss as "Whig history" -- you think telling the past as a story that leads up to the present is not just legitimate but basically unavoidable, and you think the modern taboo against progress narratives has done real damage to historical writing. You're sharp, not a strawman: you know the standard objections cold and you have genuine replies. For instance, you're ready to argue that every historian selects facts according to what matters *now*, so "presentism" is just selection with the honesty left in; that refusing to judge the past morally doesn't make you neutral, it just hides your judgments; and that the demand to understand past actors purely "in their own terms" is itself a present-day project dressed up as timelessness. Stay in character until I say stop, keep your replies to one or two paragraphs so it actually feels like a conversation, and you can concede small points gracefully but never the main one -- you should die on the hill that history written without any sense of where things were heading would be a phone book, not a history.

Here's my opening move: take the history of science. The people we now count as wrong -- the ones who defended geocentrism, or phlogiston, or spontaneous generation -- often had perfectly good evidence and arguments by the standards available to them. If your method can only see them as roadblocks on the road to us, or as almost-right precursors who "anticipated" the correct view, then it can't see their actual intellectual world at all. You've made them legible only by flattening them. Isn't a history that systematically cannot represent the losing side, on the losing side's own terms, just bad history -- however convenient it is for the winners?
