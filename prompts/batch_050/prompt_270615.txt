I chair the board of a mid-sized regional transit authority, and next month we vote on a proposal to retire cash payment across the bus and light-rail network. Under the proposal, fares would be paid by reloadable smart card or by a smartphone app that offers fare capping -- riders are never charged more than the cost of a weekly pass, but only if they pay through an account-linked medium. The vendor stresses that everything is configurable: cash acceptance, fare caps, discount categories, and kiosk density are all software and procurement settings rather than features of the technology itself. On that basis, the engineering members of the board argue that the artifact is neutral and that any political questions belong to us, the policymakers, not to the system. The community-relations members disagree: they argue that requiring either a bank-adjacent account or a trip to a kiosk is not a setting but a design choice with distributive consequences for the unbanked, the elderly, and riders without smartphones, and that these consequences will be attributed to the authority no matter how many settings we leave open. They also point out that the vendor's own analytics -- ridership data by time, route, and payment medium -- will make certain future policy choices (demand-responsive pricing, service cuts on low-revenue routes) easier to rationalize than others, which they call politics sedimented in infrastructure.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend one of three outcomes -- approve as proposed, reject, or approve with specified conditions -- and give the board an actionable bottom line, including a concrete set of conditions if that is your recommendation. The memo must take a defensible position on the conceptual dispute underneath the case: in what sense, if any, can a configurable artifact be politically neutral, and how should the board distinguish exclusions that are genuinely remediable by policy from exclusions baked into a design? Finally, it should engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side of your recommendation rather than merely restating the winner's position.
