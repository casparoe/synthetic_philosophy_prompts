I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing a long feature on why the extinction of humanity would be bad beyond the deaths of the people alive at the time — a question I keep encountering when interviewing existential-risk researchers, since nearly all of them insist that the moral stakes of extinction dwarf the body count, and almost none of them can say precisely why. For the feature's sidebar I want a decision tree that lets a careful reader locate their own position on this question. Please build it for me.

The tree should start from the shared premise that the deaths involved in extinction are terrible, and sort readers on the further question of what, if anything, is additionally lost when the human story ends. The leaves must include at least the following positions, and you may add others if the logical space demands them: (1) the person-affecting view that nothing beyond the deaths is bad, because badness requires someone for whom things go worse, and never-existing people cannot be wronged; (2) the total-welfare view on which extinction is catastrophic because of the sheer quantity of good future lives foreclosed; (3) Samuel Scheffler's "afterlife conjecture" — the claim that much of what gives our current lives meaning (our projects, values, and sacrifices) depends on the assumption that others will outlive us, so that the prospect of extinction would drain present life of point even before anyone dies; (4) the view that humanity itself, as a continuing project or civilization, has value that is not reducible to the welfare of its members; and (5) the view that extinction is a great loss but not a wrong, because there is no one it wrongs — a tragedy rather than an injustice.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from the list of names: identify the questions on which these positions genuinely come apart — for instance, whether badness requires a subject who is worse off; whether the value of future people is impersonal or must matter to someone; whether the meaning of present activities depends on posterity, as Scheffler suggests; and whether collectives like civilizations can be bearers of value. Phrase each node as a question about a concrete case that a reader could answer without knowing which position it leads to. I would like the diagnostic cases to include at least these three, which you should weave in wherever they do the most discriminating work: a painless, universal sterility scenario in which everyone alive lives out a full life but the species ends; a last-generation scenario in which the final humans live rich, contented lives; and a scenario in which, centuries after a peaceful extinction, an alien scholar discovers and reads humanity's entire archive. Where two of my required leaves cannot actually be separated by your questions, say so explicitly rather than forcing a distinction. And for any combination of answers in your tree that no philosopher occupies, note the empty node and give me one sentence on whether it looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

The readers I have in mind are educated non-specialists, so keep the node labels free of jargon, and aim for a tree of roughly eight to twelve leaves. I do not need citations or a reading list — just the instrument itself, plus a brief closing note on which leaf, in your judgment, carries the most awkward commitment.
