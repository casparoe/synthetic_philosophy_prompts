I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade, and one thread reappears with metronomic regularity: someone summarizes the moral foundations literature — self-described liberals grounding their judgments mostly in care and fairness, self-described conservatives weighting loyalty, authority, and sanctity roughly as heavily, and moral judgment on all sides being largely intuitive with the reasoning supplied after the fact — and within a few comments the same descriptive picture is being used to argue both that conservative morality is disgust dressed up as principle and the left is roughly right, and that conservatives possess a moral palette liberals are blind to and the left should update toward the right. Same data, opposite morals, and nobody ever produces a map of which inferential steps are actually in dispute.

I'd like that map, as a decision tree suitable for pinning to the top of such threads. The question the tree sorts on: given this empirical picture, what follows, if anything, about which side's moral outlook is closer to correct? Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names — each node a question on which the positions genuinely come apart, phrased as a concrete claim or case that a reader could answer without knowing any labels. For example: imagine it's shown that disgust sensitivity predicts your own position on some issue, controlling for everything else; should that lower your confidence, and would you give the same answer if it predicted your opponent's position instead? Six to eight diagnostic questions is about right for the audience.

Six positions must appear as leaves: (1) asymmetric debunking of the right — the extra foundations, especially sanctity, are unreliable heuristics, while care and fairness form the defensible core; (2) vindication of the right — the broader palette tracks genuine moral goods liberals systematically miss; (3) genetic-fallacy quietism — descriptive moral psychology simply bears no weight on normative questions; (4) symmetric debunking — intuitionism plus post-hoc rationalization undermines everyone's moral confidence equally, favoring global moral skepticism rather than either side; (5) normative pluralism — cross-person diversity in foundations is itself evidence of multiple irreducible moral goods, so both sides are partially right; (6) process-level conclusions only — the findings say nothing about which first-order views are true but a great deal about which reasoning processes to distrust, so the actionable takeaway is epistemic reform rather than political updating in either direction.

After the tree: enumerate the answer combinations none of the six occupies, and for each say briefly whether it looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored. If any two of the six can't be separated by your questions, flag them and propose the additional diagnostic that would split them. Close by identifying which node does the most work — where the forum fights I described are actually located, versus where the combatants are just talking past each other.
