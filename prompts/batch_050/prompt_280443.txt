Last weekend a friend and I had an argument that ran well past midnight, and I want to settle it properly. His position: history is not one damned thing after another; societies show recurrent, quantifiable dynamics (he is enthusiastic about cliodynamics and Turchin-style secular cycles), and a mature science of macrohistory could forecast state instability the way meteorology forecasts storms. Mine: with a few dozen historical 'cycles' at most, uncountable free choices about what counts as an event, and no controlled experiments, any claimed regularity is a curve fitted after the fact. We agreed on almost nothing except this: neither of us could state, in advance, what a theory of historical dynamics would have to accomplish to count as genuinely predictive science rather than elaborate numerology.

That is what I am asking for. Write the desiderata — the requirements an adequate quantitative theory of macrohistorical change would have to meet — with the consideration or case behind each, and mark which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are arguably just the preferences of one camp. Among the candidate requirements I want assessed, though you should not treat this list as exhaustive: that predictions be made and scored before the outcomes are known, rather than fit retrospectively; that the theory specify in advance what counts as an instance of its predicted event (a revolt, a civil war, a state collapse), since the count changes with the definition; that it posit mechanisms connecting its variables rather than merely correlating them; that its cycles be distinguishable from the spurious regularities that smoothing and averaging produce even in random data; that it survive comparison against simple null models; and that it address the reflexivity problem, i.e., that a successful prediction of instability could itself change elite behavior and falsify itself. Flag any pair of desiderata you believe cannot be jointly satisfied in this domain, since if the list contains a genuine tension, that is itself the most interesting output. Then, briefly — no more than a paragraph — say how the secular-cycles, demographic-structural approach fares against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria, not the scorecard.
