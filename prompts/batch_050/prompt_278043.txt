I've been thinking about when it's rational to accept a bet or a trade, and I want to work out what principle, if any, my gut verdicts commit me to. Here are the cases, with the verdicts I actually have:

(1) A friend and I each put $50 on opposite sides of a championship final. We've watched the same games and read the same previews; we just enjoy having something at stake. Fine.

(2) A colleague who is a genuine expert on one of the finalists offers to bet me $50 against my team at even odds. I decline. His willingness to make the offer tells me something.

(3) I sell some shares in a company because I need the cash for a house deposit, not because of any view about the company. Fine.

(4) I sell shares in a company because, after studying it carefully, I judge it overvalued. A stranger buys them from me through the anonymous exchange; I never learn who. Fine in practice -- though I notice this looks structurally identical to case (2), and that bothers me.

(5) A coworker offers to bet me $20, at even odds, that the report I just finished contains a significant error. I decline -- and then I go recheck the report. The offer itself moved me even though no bet occurred.

(6) I pay an insurer a premium to take on my flood risk, and the insurer, who knows far more flood statistics than I do, is happy to take the other side. Fine -- but I can't say why this differs from (2).

(7) A stranger offers to sell me a used car at a suspiciously attractive price. I decline unless I can get an independent inspection.

My tentative principle is that a trade is rational only when it's motivated by a difference in tastes, needs, or risk tolerance between the parties, and suspect when it's motivated purely by a disagreement in beliefs -- at least when each side knows the other is rational and roughly as well informed. But this principle seems to bless (1) while condemning (4) and possibly (6), and I'm not sure I'm willing to pay that price. I've also heard the stronger claim that rational agents who share the same prior and know each other to be rational simply cannot both expect to profit from a trade on a matter of fact, and I'd like that claim tested against my cases rather than assumed.

What I'd like from you: state the best principle (or two or three candidate principles) precisely, clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause; say honestly which of my seven verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate; and tell me what the resulting view predicts for three cases I haven't listed -- buying from a prediction market, accepting a bet from a known contrarian whose judgment is reliably worse than chance, and updating my beliefs upon merely receiving an offer I then refuse.

Please give your reasoning in prose first, and then summarize it in two structured pieces. First, a table with columns: Case (the number); My verdict (as I gave it); Principle's verdict; Match (yes/no); Governing clause (the clause of your stated principle that decides the case); Note (one sentence). Second, a JSON object with fields: "principle" (the final stated principle as a single string); "clauses" (an array of objects with "clause" and "motivating_case"); "overturned_verdicts" (an array of case numbers, possibly empty); "new_cases" (an array of three objects with "case", "predicted_verdict", and "reason"); "unresolved_tension" (a string naming the hardest remaining problem for the principle).
