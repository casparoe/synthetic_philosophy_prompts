I've been a reader of LessWrong and the adjacent blogs for years, and one thing that community taught me is that a debate's standard taxonomy is usually worse than its logical space. I'm drafting a handout on the question of whether psychopaths know that what they do is wrong, and I want the classification built from first principles rather than from the usual list of names.

Please write a decision tree that starts from the observation that diagnosed psychopaths will typically call their own actions wrong when asked, and ends at a named position on what, if anything, they actually know. Each branch node should be a single claim or diagnostic question that genuinely separates the positions: whether moral judgement is intrinsically motivating, whether moral knowledge is exhausted by knowledge-that or also requires understanding or affective capacities, whether the failure to distinguish moral from merely conventional transgressions undermines the apparent knowledge, whether 'wrong' is a fact-stating predicate at all, and whether 'psychopathy' picks out a kind uniform enough for the question to have one answer. Leaves should include the knowledge-deficit view, the motivational-deficit view, the 'propositional knowledge without moral understanding' view, the moral/conventional-distinction view, at least one non-cognitivist option, and the 'ill-posed question' option.

Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment, flag any unoccupied combinations of branch answers with a brief diagnosis of why the position is empty, and note any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate. Phrase every node so that someone could answer it without knowing which position it leads to. Please write your answer in American English.
