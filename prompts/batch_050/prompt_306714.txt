So I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I keep getting assigned pieces about AI. The thing I can't get my head around is this debate about "chain-of-thought faithfulness" -- whether when a language model writes out its reasoning step by step, that write-up actually reflects the process that produced its answer, or whether it's more like a press release written after the decision was already made.

Here's the thing: this is basically my whole job. I spend my life asking powerful actors -- politicians, CEOs, generals, central bankers -- why they did something, and trying to figure out whether the reasons they give me are the real reasons. So I have a pretty well-developed vocabulary for this: sources with genuine access vs. spokespeople who only know the official line, on-the-record vs. background, post-hoc rationalization, access journalism that just launders the institution's narrative, the difference between a source lying and a source sincerely believing their own cover story, what you can and can't learn from documents vs. interviews, etc.

So: translate the chain-of-thought faithfulness debate into that vocabulary. Take the main positions as I understand them -- the CoT is a genuine window into the computation, the CoT is confabulated PR that happens to correlate with the answer, the in-between view where it's faithful sometimes and you can test when -- and restate each one as a claim about sources and reporting. For each position, tell me what translates cleanly, what has to be stretched or distorted to fit, and what just has no expression in journalism-speak at all.

And then the part I actually care about: look at the residue, the stuff that doesn't survive translation, and tell me which side it indicts. Is the untranslatable stuff a sign that my journalism framework is missing concepts it would need for this new kind of source (a source that isn't a person, has no motives, and might not have "access to itself" in any sense my instincts can handle)? Or does the residue expose places where the AI debate is itself confused -- talking as if there's a fact of the matter about "the real reasoning" when my beat would teach me that even for humans the real reason is often a mess of rationalization and self-deception?

One more ask: journalists have actual methods for dealing with untrustworthy sources -- triangulation, checking claims against outcomes, cultivating sources closer to the decision, looking for tells like when the stated reason changes after the decision doesn't. If you know the empirical work on CoT faithfulness, tell me which of those methods have analogues in it and which ones break completely.

No need for citations or anything, I'll chase down the actual papers myself. Casual is fine, I'm just trying to figure out if my instincts are an asset here or if they're going to mislead me.
