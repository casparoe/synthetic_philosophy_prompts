I teach an upper-level undergrad seminar on value theory (14 students, one 80-minute slot) and we're spending a week on tranquilism -- roughly the view that nothing is good in itself except the absence of craving and suffering, so a life with fewer cravings satisfied isn't worse than a life stuffed with satisfied cravings. Students always nod along to the asymmetry in the abstract (failing to satisfy a craving seems bad in a way that never-having-the-craving doesn't) but I don't think they actually feel it.

Design an in-class game that makes them feel it. My rough thought is something like: players run little simulated lives where they can choose to mint new cravings for their charges, and the scoring is built so that created-then-satisfied cravings earn you nothing while unrelieved suffering costs you -- so that the "boring" tranquil strategy dominates and the students who played as if satisfaction were a positive good get visibly burned. But I'm not attached to that exact mechanic if you have a better one.

Give me the full rules, materials (nothing fancier than index cards and tokens), the scoring scheme with actual numbers, and roughly how the rounds should be paced across the 80 minutes. Then a debrief plan that maps specific moments in the game onto the components of the view -- including where students will predictably protest that the game is rigged, and how to use that protest productively rather than just conceding it. Also tell me what will go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which feature of tranquilism the game can't capture (I'm guessing the phenomenology of relief vs. mere non-craving, but you tell me).
