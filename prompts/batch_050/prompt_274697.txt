Next month I am presenting a paper at a small interdisciplinary workshop on deception and epistemic agency, and I want to be prepared for the question period.

The thesis. I argue that LLM hallucinations — confident, fluent falsehoods produced in response to ordinary, non-adversarial prompts — fall into none of the three categories the existing discussion tends to offer. They are not lies, since lying requires an intention to deceive, and the system has no intentions regarding the truth of any particular output. They are not bullshit in Frankfurt's sense either: Frankfurt's bullshitter is defined by an attitude of indifference toward whether what he says is true, and I hold that a statistical text generator has no attitudes at all — one cannot be indifferent to a distinction one does not draw. Nor are they errors, because I argue that error, in the epistemically assessable sense that matters here, attaches only to assertions, and LLM outputs are not assertions: no speaker undertakes commitment to their truth. (I explicitly distinguish error from mere misrepresentation — a faulty map can misrepresent without erring, and I grant that outputs misrepresent.) My positive claim is that hallucinations constitute a fourth category, which I call unowned testimony: utterances that carry the surface marks of assertion — declarative form, confident register, citation-like apparatus — with no asserter behind them. On my view, whatever moral failure is involved belongs not to the system but to the designers and deployers who present such utterances to users as though they were assertions.

The audience. About a dozen people: several philosophers of language who work on the nature of assertion and testimony, including at least one committed defender of the assurance view of testimony; two moral philosophers whose published work builds directly on Frankfurt's account of bullshit; a clinical neuropsychologist who studies confabulation in patients with memory disorders; and three NLP researchers from a lab that constructs truthfulness benchmarks, who I suspect regard the entire taxonomic question as armchair semantics that the measurement literature has rendered obsolete.

What I need. A prep sheet, not a defense. Give me the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise — as distinct from the ones a general philosophy audience would raise — ordered by how much damage each would do to the central claim if it landed, and each phrased the way the person who will raise it would phrase it. For each objection: my reply in the two minutes I would have, a note on where that reply is weakest, and what, if anything, I should concede in giving it. Distinguish the objections that genuinely threaten the thesis from the ones that are terminological disputes I can absorb by stipulation. And include the question I am hoping nobody asks, together with the best answer I can give if it comes.
