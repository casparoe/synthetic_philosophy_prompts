Two friends in my bio reading group got into it over whether species are actually real, and it started with those grizzly–polar bear hybrids that keep showing up in the Arctic. (1) Dan said this is basically fatal for the biological species concept: if two "species" can mate in the wild and produce fertile offspring, then by the standard definition they're one species, which nobody believes, so the definition fails. (2) Maria said the definition never required total isolation, just that gene flow stays limited enough that the two groups remain distinct -- a handful of hybrids in a melting Arctic doesn't erase the boundary. (3) Dan replied that "limited enough" is doing all the work there, since any amount of interbreeding can just be declared insufficient, making the concept unfalsifiable; and anyway the fact that biologists run dozens of rival definitions (phylogenetic, ecological, morphological, whatever) shows "species" is a bookkeeping convenience, not a real kind of thing. (4) Maria said the rival definitions mostly agree on actual cases -- every concept counts robins as one species -- and where they disagree it's because they're tracking genuinely different real things (gene flow vs. ancestry vs. niche), so species pluralism is like measuring length in different units, not like making stuff up. (5) Dan's closer: the units analogy backfires, because meters and feet measure the same underlying quantity and are interconvertible, whereas rival species concepts cross-classify organisms with no common currency -- which is exactly what you'd predict if "species" were like "weed," a label that serves our purposes rather than carving nature at a joint. I stayed out of it, but please adjudicate move by move: say who's right on each point, who's more right overall, and whether there's a distinction both of them are missing (e.g. between species as a rank/category and particular species taxa) that would dissolve part of the fight.
