I serve on the five-member board of a small family foundation that awards an annual early-career research prize of $100,000. This year our selection process deadlocked: after two rounds of review, twenty finalists remain whom the reviewers judge to be effectively indistinguishable in merit. The donor's will anticipated this situation and stipulates that, in the event of a tie, the prize be awarded by what it calls a Platonia procedure, apparently named after a puzzle by Douglas Hofstadter: each finalist is informed of the rules and then independently and secretly decides whether to submit a claim; if exactly one finalist submits, that finalist receives the full prize; if zero or more than one submit, no prize is awarded that year and the money rolls over into next year's award. Communication between finalists about their decisions is prohibited, and we have no practical way to police it anyway. Three board members want to run the procedure as written, arguing that the donor's intent controls; one wants to substitute a simple random draw, arguing that the Platonia procedure is wasteful and capricious; one proposes a modification in which each claim must be accompanied by a non-refundable $500 application fee payable to the foundation, on the theory that this will deter frivolous claims and make the one-claimant outcome more likely.

The junior member who favors the lottery circulated a note arguing as follows: since the finalists are symmetrically situated and forbidden to communicate, rational play means each adopts the same mixed strategy; the probability that exactly one of twenty finalists claims, when each claims with probability p, is 20p(1−p)^19, which is maximized at p = 1/20 and even then equals only about 0.38 — so the procedure burns the prize roughly three times in five, whereas a lottery never does. The board chair countered that this analysis assumes the finalists reason independently, and that the donor, a devotee of Hofstadter's writings on superrationality, believed that sufficiently reflective agents in symmetric situations would each take into account that the others were reasoning identically, and would thereby do better than the mixed-strategy analysis predicts. She also argued the fee modification changes the game in the foundation's favor by screening for finalists who value the prize more. I am unconvinced by parts of both notes but haven't sorted out which parts.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before our vote: roughly 800 to 1,200 words, recommending one of the three options (run the procedure as stipulated, substitute a lottery, adopt the fee modification) as our formal position, with a clear bottom line up front. The memo should work through the decision theory carefully — including whether the chair's appeal to identical reasoning actually improves on the 0.38 figure or merely relabels the problem, and what the fee does to the strategic structure — and it must give the strongest version of the losing side's case rather than a strawman. Where the underlying game theory is settled mathematics, say so plainly; where a claim depends on a contested view about rationality, cooperation, or the weight of donor intent, flag it as contested and do not present it as the received view.
