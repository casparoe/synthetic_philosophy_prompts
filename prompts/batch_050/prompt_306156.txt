I am a retired atmospheric physicist who, late in life, has begun reading moral philosophy, and I have been struck by how naturally the so-called cluelessness objection to consequentialism maps onto ideas I once worked with professionally. The objection, as I understand it from Lenman and from Hilary Greaves's distinction between simple and complex cluelessness, runs roughly thus: the consequences of any action ripple outward indefinitely, they are exquisitely sensitive to initial conditions (I spent a career modeling exactly such sensitivity), and these remote effects can plausibly swamp the near-term effects on which our moral deliberations actually rely. So we are clueless about the overall value of what we do, and consequentialist deliberation appears to break down.

I recently rewatched It's a Wonderful Life and realized the film is, in effect, a thought experiment about this objection. George Bailey is shown the counterfactual Bedford Falls in which he never existed: his brother drowned as a boy, the transport of soldiers was lost, Mr. Gower is a ruined man, the town belongs to Potter, and so on. The film's consolation depends entirely on granting George — and us — an oracle that reveals the long-range ripples of an ordinary life, the very thing the cluelessness objection says we never have.

I would like a careful reading of the film through this lens, and I have specific questions. Does the film dramatize cluelessness only to dissolve it by divine epistemic fiat, thereby confirming the objection's premise rather than answering it? Or does it offer something the philosophy papers lack — perhaps a picture of how one ought to act and feel under cluelessness, in George's despair, his final scene, and the town's response? Where does the film resist the reading (surely the angel's tour is selective, and a darker tour of the same counterfactual is conceivable), and where does it anticipate the sophisticated consequentialist's reply that we should simply maximize expected value over near-term effects and accept ignorance of the rest? Finally, what does the film contribute to this debate that argument alone does not? Please end your response with a single sentence, as the last line, summarizing what the film ultimately says about acting well under radical ignorance of consequences.
