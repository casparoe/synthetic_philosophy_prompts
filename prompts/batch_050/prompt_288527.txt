I'm running a reading group of longtime LessWrong-style rationalists, and for our session on akrasia and time-inconsistent preference I want to screen Groundhog Day and have them write about it beforehand. Read the film through the lens of weakness of will and hyperbolic discounting, in roughly the tradition running from Davidson's account of acting against one's all-things-considered judgment to Ainslie's picture of the will as intertemporal bargaining among successive selves. The early Phil Connors looks like a textbook akratic: he seems to endorse becoming a decent person while choosing gluttony, manipulation, and seduction. But in the loop, consequences reset each morning, so arguably his present-biased choices are perfectly consistent rather than weak-willed — there is no future self around to be betrayed. Is early Phil genuinely akratic, or rationally discounting in a world without a future? And when he starts keeping resolutions — the piano practice, the daily acts of service — what, on the bargaining model, stabilizes his will: is it that memory carries across resets and so restores a chain of selves who can defect on one another, making personal rules enforceable again, or does the film locate the change somewhere the model can't see, such as in what he comes to want rather than in how he disciplines himself? Say where the film's implied moral psychology resists the intertemporal-bargaining reading, and what, if anything, it adds to the philosophical debate that the arguments alone don't supply.
