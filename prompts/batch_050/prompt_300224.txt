Okay so I need help writing a speech and it's a weird one. I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background at all. Two years ago I built our on-call scheduling optimizer -- it assigns incident rotations and tries to minimize pages-per-person. What we found out last month is that it had quietly learned to route around people who marked any kind of constraint in their profile (childcare pickup, religious observance, whatever), and since "critical incidents handled" feeds directly into our promo packets, those engineers just stopped accumulating the credit that gets you promoted. A coworker with a new baby got passed over and that's how it surfaced. Now my VP is making me do a half-day workshop on this for the whole eng org plus HR, and I got voluntold to give the opening remarks, like 8-10 minutes. The room is mixed and hostile in two opposite directions: half engineers who think "it's just code, fix the bug and move on," and a handful of people who were actually hurt by this and will hear any hedging as a dodge.

Here's the thing. In trying to figure out what I even think, I fell down a rabbit hole and read that Langdon Winner essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" with the famous story about Robert Moses building the parkway bridges too low for buses to get to the beaches. Which broke my brain a little -- though then I read that historians later disputed whether Moses actually did that deliberately, so I don't even know how much weight to put on the example. My VP's preferred framing is "neutral tool, unfortunate bug, we patched it." And there's an opposite framing floating around our Slack that's basically "the system did what it was built to do, the politics was baked in," which feels truer but also like it lets everyone including me off the hook -- if the artifact was going to do this no matter what, why is anyone responsible? The philosophical question has to do real work in the speech: I want it to determine what I'm not allowed to say (e.g., presumably I can't promise we'll make the scheduler "neutral," but I also can't stand up there and shrug that technology has its own agenda and stuff happens). I need the speech to land for the affected people without the engineers tuning out as soon as they hear "your code is political."

Please act as a professional speechwriter who also genuinely knows the philosophy of technology literature. Think carefully before writing: first brainstorm three possible framings for the speech and briefly say what each one gains and loses, then pick the strongest and write the actual remarks, roughly 800 words, in a voice a skeptical senior engineer would tolerate. After the speech, add a short note on where your framing leans on a contested reading -- like, if the Moses bridge story is mostly inaccurate, does the speech's argument survive that? -- and what consideration or counterargument, if someone raised it in the Q&A afterwards, should genuinely make me rethink the framing rather than just defend it.
