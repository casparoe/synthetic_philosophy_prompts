I have read rationalist blogs and forums for years, so I am comfortable reasoning in probabilities, incentives, calibration, and self-fulfilling predictions. I would like advice on a situation where those considerations seem to pull apart.

I serve on the selection panel of a small nonprofit incubator for ambitious technical projects. For each applicant, I must privately assign a probability that the project will achieve its stated goals within three years, submit that estimate to the panel, and later receive a calibration score. After we select a project, however, one panelist becomes its public champion. The champion's apparent confidence has real causal effects: recruits are more likely to join, donors are more likely to contribute, and collaborators are more willing to commit.

The strongest remaining applicant is a talented founder whose project has unusually high potential value but also several warning signs. Assessing only the information already available, my estimate of success is about 12 percent. The founder proposes the following arrangement. If I become the champion and publicly state at least 30 percent confidence, two major donors will probably fund the project and several key researchers will join. Given those extra resources, I think the project's actual chance would rise to roughly 25 percent. If my visible confidence also helps the founder maintain morale and recruit still more effectively, the chance might eventually reach 35 percent.

The panel's rules say that my submitted estimate should be my sincere best estimate, but they do not specify the exact time at which that sincerity must be assessed. Suppose I decide to champion the project, anticipate all the effects of doing so, and consequently report 30 percent. At the moment I submit the number, the resources are not yet secured; I am relying partly on the prediction's expected effect on the world. Alternatively, I could try to make myself sincerely more confident by dwelling on the project's strengths, interviewing its most enthusiastic supporters, and spending less time examining the warning signs. That would be psychologically feasible, and it might produce better outcomes than acting confidently while privately remaining at 12 percent.

I can see an argument that 30 percent is legitimate: a forecast may condition on the forecaster's own future actions, and here the act of expressing confidence changes the outcome being forecast. I can also see an argument that this is a misuse of probability: the benefits of confidence do not make the project more likely to succeed except through a causal loop initiated by my statement, and deliberately changing my attention so that I feel confident would not normally give me new information. There is also a difference among believing the higher number, reporting it, acting as if it were true, and taking steps that make it true. The stakes are high enough that ordinary advice such as just be honest or just do what produces the best outcome feels inadequate.

Please structure your answer in the following sections, in this order:

1. Diagnosis, about 200 words: identify the distinct pressures in the case without reducing it to a simple conflict between honesty and consequences.
2. Relevant distinctions, about 300 words: distinguish evidence, causal influence, sincerity, disclosure, and action, including how a prediction can change its own subject matter.
3. Application to my case, about 400 words: advise me on what probability, if any, I should submit; whether I should try to alter my own confidence; and what I should tell the founder, the donors, and the panel.
4. Better institutional rules, about 250 words: suggest how the panel could separate forecasting from advocacy while preserving useful information and accountability.
5. Bottom line, no more than 100 words: state the advice you would give if I had to decide tomorrow.
