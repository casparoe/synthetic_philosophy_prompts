For my metaphysics seminar this semester I'm writing a paper on Leibniz's identity of indiscernibles, and honestly most of what I "know" about the debate comes from one of those great-arguments-in-philosophy books I found on the library sale shelf. Here's how it summarizes things: "Leibniz's identity of indiscernibles says that if two things share all their properties, they're really one and the same thing, and he held this as a necessary truth: God would never create two perfectly alike things, since he'd have no reason to put one here rather than there. Max Black's counterexample settles the matter. Imagine a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres, a mile apart, and nothing else, forever. We can coherently describe such a universe, and whatever is conceivable is possible, so a world with two indiscernible things is possible and Leibniz's principle is simply false. The standard reply -- that the spheres differ in their spatial properties, one being here and the other there -- is circular, since you can only say the spheres occupy different locations if you've already assumed they're two things and not one."

Something about this bugs me, though I can't put my finger on exactly what. My roommate (who took this class last year) swears the God-no-reason stuff is a *different* Leibniz principle than the indiscernibles one, and I also vaguely remember our prof saying in lecture that whether the principle is necessary or just contingently true actually matters a lot for how Black's case lands, which the book doesn't mention at all. But the book is by an actual philosopher, so maybe I'm wrong and my roommate misremembered.

So: how fair is this summary, really? Go through it and separate what a specialist would accept from what they'd wince at -- is the "conceivable therefore possible therefore refuted" move as clean as the book makes it sound, is the circularity charge against the spatial-properties reply the whole story, and is the book right to present the God point as just spelling out the indiscernibles principle? Also, if the summary is patched up where it needs patching, does its bottom line (Black wins, principle dead) still follow, or does the correct version of the debate leave things more open?
