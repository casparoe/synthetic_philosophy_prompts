The exam question was: "Explain the cluelessness objection to consequentialism. Some consequentialists respond by defining right action in terms of expected value rather than actual total consequences. Does this response succeed? Justify your answer."

The student's answer was: "Consequentialism is the view that an act is morally right just in case its consequences are at least as good as those of any alternative open to the agent. The cluelessness objection, first pressed by Bernard Williams as part of his well-known critique of utilitarianism, observes that the consequences of any act ramify indefinitely into the remote future, and that these remote consequences will typically swamp, in aggregate value, the near-term effects we can actually foresee. Whether I drive or cycle to work today slightly alters innumerable events — who misses which train, which conversations happen — and such ripples can determine which people exist, and what befalls them, centuries hence. Because the remote effects dominate the value of my options, and because I know nothing about them, I am clueless about which act is right. Consequentialism thus seems unable to guide deliberation: it instructs me to produce the best outcome whilst leaving me with no usable way of identifying it. The expected-value response avoids this by relativising rightness to the agent's evidence: an act is right if and only if it maximises expected value given what the agent has reason to believe. On this view the objection dissolves. Where the remote consequences of two options are equally likely to be good or bad, their expected contributions cancel, and the choice is settled by the foreseeable near-term differences. Cycling beats driving because its expected near-term effects are better; the distant future is a wash on both sides. The response therefore succeeds. Expected-value consequentialism never asks the agent to know the remote future, only to weigh it even-handedly, so cluelessness about remote outcomes cannot paralyse it. The same cancellation argument extends to rule consequentialism, since the remote effects of a rule's general acceptance wash out in exactly the same way."
