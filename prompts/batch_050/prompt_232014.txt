I am a novelist at work on a book set partly in a seventeenth-century clockmaker's shop and partly in a near-future city run by an algorithmic council, and one of my characters, a disgraced tutor who repairs automata, keeps a battered copy of Hobbes's Leviathan by his workbench. I want his margin notes on the book's Introduction to be philosophically credible, but I am struggling with the passage myself. Hobbes writes: "Nature (the art whereby God hath made and governes the world) is by the art of man, as in many other things, so in this also imitated, that it can make an Artificial Animal." He then asks: "For what is the Heart, but a Spring; and the Nerves, but so many Strings; and the Joynts, but so many Wheeles, giving motion to the whole Body, such as was intended by the Artificer?" I understand the individual words, but not what work the comparison is doing. Is Hobbes saying that living things merely are machines, or only that we may usefully describe them as if they were? And when he goes on to call the commonwealth an artificial creation in the same breath, is he suggesting that a state is a kind of machine too, with the sovereign as its spring?

What I most need is a careful unpacking of the reasoning, aimed at someone intelligent but not trained in philosophy, so that I can give my character a plausible interpretation without flattening Hobbes into a simple materialist cartoon. Specifically: how does this mechanistic picture of bodies relate to the logic of the social contract that follows, where people supposedly reason their way out of a condition of war by agreeing to obey a common power? If human beings are just systems of springs and wheels, does that strengthen the contract argument (because predictable, self-moving creatures can be counted on to seek their own preservation) or threaten it (because genuine agreement seems to require something more than mechanism)? And who is the "Artificer" of the automaton-like state — God, the contracting multitude, or the sovereign they create?

Please write your explanation in American English, in a few paragraphs rather than bullet points, and feel free to note where the passage admits of more than one reasonable reading. I would rather my character's notes show him wrestling with a real tension than confidently settling on the single correct interpretation.
