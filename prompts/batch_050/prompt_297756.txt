I recently joined the program committee of a small international cultural foundation whose draft charter describes its mission as “defending Western values.” At our first meeting, the committee divided into two camps, and I have been asked to help them produce a joint statement rather than another unresolved debate.

The first camp, the continuity defenders, holds that the phrase can coherently name a historically formed repertoire: individual conscience, equal civic standing, public criticism of power, rule-governed institutions, and the capacity for internal reform. They deny that these values are unique to the West, consistently practiced there, or grounded in any ethnic essence, but maintain that the West can still possess recognizable traditions, institutions, and habits of justification.

The second camp, the boundary critics, holds that “Western values” primarily functions as a political boundary-marker. In their view, the phrase gathers heterogeneous ideals into a misleading inheritance, represents exchange and appropriation as ownership, and shifts its meaning depending on whom a speaker wants to include or exclude. They would rather name specific commitments than invoke the West.

Draft the joint statement they could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. It should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. Distinguish at least four possible claims about a value: its content, its genealogy, its present distribution, and the rhetorical work performed by calling it Western. Do not let the camps agree merely that the issue is complicated.

2. A disagreements table. State each remaining disagreement twice, once in words the continuity defenders would accept as a fair formulation of their position and once in words the boundary critics would accept. Identify disagreements that survive clarification, and separately identify any apparent disagreement that turns out to concern different uses of the phrase.

3. A section of cruxes: sub-questions such that settling one would require at least one camp to revise its position. For each crux, specify a case or test both camps accept as relevant and record in advance what each expects it to show. Consider, where useful, replacing “Western values” with an enumerated list of commitments; replacing it with “values historically salient in Western institutions, though neither exclusive to them nor consistently honored by them”; and examining a political speech that invokes Western values to justify exclusion while praising tolerance. Explain what each substitution or case can and cannot establish.

The statement should be suitable for adoption by the foundation, but it must not adjudicate the dispute by compromise, impose a definition both camps privately reject, or recommend readings. Its purpose is to make the agreement genuine and the residual disagreement exact.
