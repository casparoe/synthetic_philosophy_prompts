I sit on the nosology committee of a professional body that publishes a diagnostic manual, and we are in the middle of a revision cycle. Every cycle we receive proposals to add new conditions, and we have no principled way of evaluating them: each proposal is argued ad hoc, and the loudest faction tends to win. I would like you to write a standing decision procedure we can apply to any candidate condition, grounded in what the philosophy of medicine actually has to say about the concept of disorder — the dispute between naturalist accounts (disorder as a failure of statistically normal biological function), normativist accounts (disorder as whatever is disvalued and in need of remedy), and hybrid views that require both a dysfunction and a harm.

The procedure must be runnable by a committee of eight in a single ninety-minute session, by people who have not read the philosophy. I want the tests in order, what evidence or argument counts as passing each test, and an explicit stopping rule: at which step a candidate fails and is excluded, and at which step the procedure must hand the question to a value judgment — naming whose values are doing the work at that point (the profession's, the patients', the wider culture's) rather than letting it pass in disguise as a factual finding. Where the naturalist and normativist traditions would run a case differently, the procedure should make the fork visible rather than silently picking a side; I would rather have a procedure that tells us where we are actually disagreeing than one that papers over it.

The procedure has to cope with the standard hard cases, so build in handling for each: a condition that is clearly a dysfunction but that the people who have it do not experience as a harm and do not want treated (as some deaf and some autistic people argue); a statistically normal response to genuinely bad circumstances (grief after bereavement); a condition that is harmful only because of how the surrounding society is arranged; and a condition whose only evidence of dysfunction is that the behavior is rare. In each case, say what the procedure should output and why.

Then run the finished procedure twice, in full: once on the historical classification of homosexuality as a disorder, and once on a hypothetical proposal for a disorder of persistent, debilitating romantic jealousy. One of these runs should be chosen so that the procedure delivers a verdict I will find unwelcome — either excluding something I would want included or vindicating something I would want excluded — and you should say plainly that this is what has happened, rather than adjusting the procedure to avoid it.

Close with a candid assessment of what the procedure cannot do: which decisions it will get wrong or leave genuinely open, and whether a neutral procedure in this domain is even possible, or whether every operational test smuggles in the very naturalism-or-normativism choice it was meant to postpone.
