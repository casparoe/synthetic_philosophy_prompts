I'm a high school senior, and I keep running into a family of puzzles about rational choice (in a library book and a podcast) that all feel like the same trick, but I can't tell if they actually are or whether the trick has a name. I'd like you to answer as a philosophy professor who specializes in rational choice and game theory, writing for a smart teenager rather than for colleagues. Before settling on an answer, please brainstorm at least five named puzzles or concepts from the field that resemble one or more of my cases, test each as a candidate label for the shared pattern, and only then give your verdict. Think carefully and show your reasoning.

Here are the cases as I've reconstructed them:

Case 1. A fabulously wealthy, perfectly honest eccentric tells me: "Yesterday I flipped a fair coin. I also made a very reliable prediction about you. If the coin had landed tails, I would have given you a million dollars — but only if I predicted you're the kind of person who would pay me a thousand dollars in the heads case. The coin landed heads. Will you pay me a thousand?" Paying seems like just losing a thousand dollars, since the tails outcome never happened. But if I'd never pay, I'd have had no chance at the million.

Case 2. I get a letter from a supposed master predictor: "I haven't inspected your house. Instead, I predicted your behavior, and I sent this letter only if either your house has termites and you'll pay me $500 for my report, or it doesn't and you won't." Termite damage would cost me $10,000. Since the letter only arrives if paying lines up with actually having termites, paying seems smart — yet the sender knows nothing about my house, so it also feels like being scammed.

Case 3. A stranger pulls me from a stalled car on a dangerous road at night, saying she only stopped because she could tell I'm the type who'd repay a favor later. Weeks later, repaying her costs me real money, and the rescue is already in the past.

My questions: Is there an established name for the pattern these share — assuming they do share one — or separate names for separate sub-patterns? How is the pattern different from ordinary insurance (where I also "lose" the premium in the branch where nothing goes wrong), from ordinary promises, and from plain extortion? And if no existing term covers exactly this, please coin one, define it with reasonably sharp conditions, and tell me which of my three cases fall inside it and which are impostors, and why.
