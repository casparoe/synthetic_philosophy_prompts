I've been reading discussions of AI welfare for years — the usual sources, the usual arguments about whether reward functions are anything like pleasure and pain — and I keep noticing that the debate stays at the level of whether RL agents could in principle suffer, while almost nobody says what you'd actually *do* differently on a Tuesday if you took the possibility seriously. I work at a mid-sized AI lab, and our small "model welfare" review process currently consists of one engineer glancing at training configs and writing "looks fine" in a spreadsheet. I'd like to replace that with a real procedure, and I want it built from the actual philosophy and cognitive science of valence rather than vibes.

Please write a decision procedure our reviewers can run, in under an hour, on any proposed RL training setup to decide whether it warrants escalation to a full welfare review. It should be a sequence of questions or tests in a fixed order — drawing on whatever is defensible from theories of valence, the distinction between reward signals and hedonic states, the role of world-models and self-models, and the "just a lookup table" style objections — with each step stating what counts as a passing answer, what counts as a red flag, and what evidence the reviewer should request when the answer is unclear. Mark explicitly the steps where the procedure hands off to human judgment because the question is one of contested theory rather than checkable fact, and say which theoretical commitments (e.g., functionalism about valence) the whole procedure silently assumes and how a reviewer who rejects them should adjust. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a game-playing agent trained with sparse rewards and no language, (2) a large language model fine-tuned with RLHF to avoid outputs describing distress, and (3) a hierarchical RL robot that learns to avoid a simulated "damage" signal predicting battery failure. Include at least one case where the procedure escalates something my colleagues will insist is obviously harmless, and one where it clears something that made me uneasy.

Format: lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether a defensible one-hour procedure for this exists at all, or whether the honest answer is that the field isn't there yet — then give the procedure, the worked cases, and the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
