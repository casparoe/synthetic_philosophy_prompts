I run a small interview series for our department's graduate colloquium, and next month I'm interviewing a philosopher who defends a thesis she calls deliberation crowding out prediction: while you are genuinely deliberating about what to do, you cannot coherently assign credences to the options still on the table, because treating your own future action as a subject of prediction means treating it as something that happens to you rather than something you settle. On her view, as soon as I start asking myself how likely it is that I'll end up ordering the fish, I've stopped deciding whether to order the fish and started forecasting someone who might as well be a stranger. The view has some intuitive pull -- deciding doesn't feel like betting on yourself -- but I want to press her on where it gets uncomfortable.

Draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that do more than get her to restate the thesis. I want the sequence to cover at least these pressure points: cases where self-prediction seems obviously legitimate and even necessary (I know from long experience how likely I am to bail on a morning run, and it would be irrational to plan as if I didn't); Newcomb-style situations, where the accuracy of a prediction of one's own choice is precisely what's at stake; the question of whether her ban applies only to the moment of deliberation or also to earlier planning stages (may I assign credences tonight to what I'll choose tomorrow morning?); and what her view implies for game theory and for forecasting institutions like prediction markets, which seem to put prices on agents' own future behavior all the time. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give -- she tends to respond to hard cases by saying the agent in your example has stopped deliberating and shifted into a purely predictive stance, so at least some follow-ups should test where that line falls and whether it can be drawn non-circularly. End the sequence with a question about what it would take to change her mind.

Alongside the questions themselves, give me a summary table with exactly four columns: Q (the question number), Target (the pressure point or commitment the question is aimed at, in a few words), Likely Reply (the answer you expect her to give, one sentence), and Follow-Up (the follow-up question that presses on that reply). Keep the questions themselves conversational enough to read aloud, but the table entries terse.
