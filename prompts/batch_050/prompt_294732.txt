So I just got out of a nearly three-hour argument with my friend Marcus, who I've been doing a philosopy reading group with (we worked through some Hume and then the grue stuff, where a thing counts as "grue" if it's green and observed before some future time t, or blue and not observed before t, so every emerald we've ever seen is equally evidence that all emeralds are grue). Marcus's whole position basically collapsed into one sentence he kept repeating like a mantra: the future resembles the past. His line was that Hume's "problem" is just that this principle can't be deductively proven, which is fine, it's a reasonable background assumption, and that grue changes nothing because the future will resemble the past in the respects that actually matter, and "grue" obviously isn't one of those respects since it's a gerrymandered predicate nobody would naturally use. When I asked him what "resemble" and "the respects that matter" mean without already knowing which predicates are the good ones, he just said I was playing word games and the slogan is obviously true. The argument ended with him saying that if I reject "the future resembles the past" I have no right to expect the sun to rise tomorrow, which felt like a cheap shot but I couldn't articulate why.

Here's what I want: can you take that slogan apart and lay out, as a menu, the different precise claims it could actually be making? Like, off the top of my head it might mean something like "the laws of nature don't change over time," or "regularities observed so far will continue," or "the future resembles the past in the respects picked out by our best scientific vocabulary," or some weaker statistical version, and probably others I haven't thought of. For each reading, tell me whether it's (a) a trivial platitude that nobody denies, (b) substantive but the kind of thing Hume showed we can't justify non-circularly, (c) outright vacuous in the way Goodman seemed to be getting at — since any two stretches of history "resemble" each other in infinitely many respects and differ in infinitely many others, so resemblance simpliciter constrains nothing — or (d) actually false. Most importantly: which of these readings does Marcus's argument actually need in order to dismiss grue as mere word games? My suspicion is that the only version strong enough to rule out grue is the one that builds the privileged predicates into "resemble," and that that version is either circular or just a restatement of the problem rather than a solution, but I want this laid out cleanly enough that I can bring it to the next reading group session.
