I was at the pub last weekend with two mates and they got into a proper row about philosophy and science, and it's been bugging me since. I've got no background in either subject (I left school at 16 and fix boilers for a living) but I like hearing clever people argue, so I just sat there and took it in. I'm going to try and reconstruct the argument as best I can and I want you to tell me who was right about what, move by move, and who's more right overall.

Background: Callum did a physics degree and now works in data something-or-other, he's very much of the "science solved it" school. Jess reads a lot of popular philosophy. Anyway:

1. Callum's opening shot was basically: philosophers banged on about "what is time" and "what is matter" for two thousand years and got nowhere, and then physics just went and answered it (relativity, atoms, whatever). So philosophy isn't a way of finding things out, it's what you do while you're waiting for science to catch up. He said famous physicists have been saying for ages that philosophy is dead or useless and they've got a point.

2. Jess said that's cheeky because science itself runs on assumptions it never tests. Like: why should the future resemble the past, why prefer the simpler theory, why think experiments tell you anything about the world at all? Those aren't scientific results, they're philosophical commitments scientists take on trust. So scientists are doing philosophy whether they like it or not, just badly and unexamined.

3. Callum said that's not philosophy, that's just "what works". You don't need a philosopher in the room to tell you to trust evidence. Planes fly, phones work, that's the justification, end of.

4. Jess said "what works" is itself a philosophical position, and a contested one. There's a difference between saying our theories are literally true of reality and saying they're just good prediction-machines, and that difference actually matters when scientists argue about stuff like whether electrons are real things or whether the multiverse counts as science. She also said Hawking, one of the very people Callum was channelling, ended up defending a view about models and reality that's basically a philosophy position with a fresh coat of paint.

5. Callum then said fine, name me one actual concrete thing philosophy has contributed to physics. Not vibes, a contribution.

6. Jess said the whole business of working out what quantum mechanics actually means -- locality, hidden variables, whether measurement is special -- started as philosophical worrying, and Bell's theorem (which Callum had heard of, thankfully) came out of exactly that kind of worrying about what the theory was really saying. That's not nothing.

7. Callum said Bell was a physicist though. So that's physicists doing physics, not philosophers doing philosophy. If anything it proves his point: the question only became productive once a scientist got hold of it.

8. Jess said that's moving the goalposts -- if any clear thinking about the foundations of a theory counts as "science" the moment it succeeds, then "philosophy never contributes to science" becomes true by definition and unlosable, which is a bit convenient.

9. Callum said if philosophy just means "thinking carefully about hard questions" then sure it contributes, but then the claim is trivial and doesn't rescue philosophy as an actual subject with departments and journals, which is what he was slagging off in the first place.

At that point the food arrived and it fizzled out. So: go through the nine moves and rate each one -- solid point, half a point, or nonsense -- with a bit of justification for each. Also tell me if either of them is missing something big that would settle it, and who came off better overall. Please answer in British English, and don't just sit on the fence, I want actual verdicts.
