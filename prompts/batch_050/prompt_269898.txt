Last month, over a very long dinner, I got into an argument with a close friend — a psychiatrist — about whether anyone should be permitted to bind their future self against that self's own later protests. Her view is that the whole idea rests on a confusion: a patient's contemporaneous, apparently competent refusal, she insists, always trumps an earlier instruction, and dressing self-binding up in Homeric language as a "Ulysses contract" changes nothing. I disagreed, and the argument pushed me to develop a position I am now presenting next week to my hospital's clinical ethics committee. The audience matters here: several psychiatrists who share my friend's instincts, a palliative care physician, the hospital's attorney, and a social worker. None of them are philosophers, and none will be moved by citations to the literature on personal identity.

My proposal is that patients with severe, episodic bipolar disorder and a documented pattern of discontinuing treatment during manic episodes should be able to execute an advance directive authorizing treatment during a future episode — and, this is the contentious part, that the directive should remain binding even if the patient, mid-episode, passes a standard bedside capacity assessment and refuses. My framing borrows from constitutional theory: just as some constitutions entrench certain provisions against ordinary amendment, demanding supermajorities or declaring them unamendable altogether, the lucid self who drafts the directive occupies something like a founding moment whose decisions deserve entrenchment against later revision by a self whose deliberation is compromised. A manic refusal, I will argue, resembles an amendment rammed through under mob pressure more than it resembles genuine constitutional politics, and a legal order that honors the latter but not the former has things backwards. So the committee is getting not just a policy proposal but an analogy I intend to lean on heavily.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will actually come, phrased the way these particular people would phrase them — clinicians worried about capacity assessments, documentation, and liability, not philosophers worried about person-stages. For each, indicate which objections have real force, what I should concede up front rather than fight over, and where a two-minute reply can hold the line. Tell me where my constitutional analogy is most likely to be turned against me — I suspect a constitution's amendment procedure and my patient's inability to "ratify" her directive anew is a soft spot, but I want that pressure-tested. Above all, identify the weakest point in the position, the one I should be rehearsing rather than hoping nobody notices, and give me the question I am quietly praying nobody asks.
