I'm a PhD student in cog sci and I run our department's journal club. Next month I'm doing the mental imagery debate -- Kosslyn's camp versus Pylyshyn's camp -- and honestly I'm dreading it, because every time this comes up the session goes the same way: someone presents a scanning-time or mental-rotation result, someone else says that result is equally compatible with propositional representations plus tacit knowledge, the first person says that's an unfalsifiable reinterpretation, and we all go home. Fifty years in and the smart people seem no closer than they were in the seventies.

So rather than another rehash of the experiments, I want a diagnosis of why this debate won't die, that I can use to structure the session. Specifically:

What kind of disagreement is this actually? Some candidate answers I want you to seriously weigh, not just list: (a) it's a real empirical dispute that the right experiment could settle and we just haven't run it yet; (b) it's underdetermination in principle -- behavioral data can always be mimicked by either format, so no reaction-time or accuracy result could ever decide it; (c) it's partially verbal -- both sides may agree about the underlying computation and are fighting over what deserves to be called a "picture-like" representation; or (d) the sides are answering different questions under one banner, one about the format of stored representations and one about the format of what's actually processed at a given moment.

A few things I want the diagnosis to engage with directly: Pylyshyn's demand-characteristics and tacit-knowledge objections to the classic scanning studies, the question of whether neuroimaging (retinotopic activation in V1 during imagery, that kind of thing) actually broke the impasse or just relocated it, and the claim you sometimes hear that the debate dissolved once everyone accepted that format and content come apart. My own working hypothesis is (b) with a bit of (c) -- that once you grant both sides enough auxiliary machinery, the theories become behaviorally equivalent, and what's left is a fight over which description is "natural." But I might be wrong and I'd rather be told so.

Finally, given your diagnosis: what would actually count as progress here? A specific kind of experiment, a formal result about representational formats, a conceptual distinction everyone should adopt, or the conclusion that the question as posed is ill-formed and the field should stop asking it? I want to end the session with an answer to that, not a shrug. If your verdict is that the debate is genuinely unresolvable, say why that isn't just pessimism.
