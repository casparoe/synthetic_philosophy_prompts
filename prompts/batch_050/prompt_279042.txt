I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've been working my way through Spinoza's Theologico-Political Treatise. In Chapter 17, while arguing that democracy is the best and most natural form of state, Spinoza claims that we have little to fear from irrational legislation under majority rule, because it is nearly impossible for the majority of a large assembly to converge on an irrational proposal. My engineer's intuition first read this as an aggregation argument: many noisy individual judgments, pooled, cancel out each other's errors, the way independent estimates do in a well-designed distributed system. But the argument seems to hinge on the errors being independent, and everything I know from outside philosophy about how opinions actually form — herding, information cascades, motivated reasoning, groups polarizing toward whatever their members already lean toward — suggests errors in real electorates are heavily correlated, which breaks the aggregation result entirely. Write an essay evaluating whether Spinoza's claim survives this objection. Address: whether he is really making a probabilistic claim about correct decisions or a different claim about stability and consent (and which reading the surrounding chapter supports); what correlated error and group polarization do to the probabilistic reading; and whether the strongest reconstruction of his defense of democracy needs the multitude to be reliable decision-makers at all. Take a position and defend it — the argument stands, stands only in repaired form (say what the repair is), or fails.
