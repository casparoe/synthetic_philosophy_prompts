Last night a friend and I spent three hours arguing about the pragmatist theory of truth, and we reached an impasse I want broken not by refereeing but by engineering.

The disagreement. On the Peircean side (mine): a proposition is true when, and because, inquiry pursued far enough and conducted well enough would converge on it and never be forced to retreat. Truth is the settled destination of ideal investigation, not any verdict anyone actually reaches. On the Jamesian side (hers): truth is a property a belief acquires through its career in experience; a belief is true insofar as accepting it leads the believer into satisfactory, workable dealings with the world, and it remains true only so long as it keeps proving itself in use. I claimed that any belief which would be overturned at the limit of inquiry simply is not true, whatever its practical fruits. She replied that a belief whose every engagement with experience succeeds is as true as truth gets, and that the limit of inquiry is a fiction unless cashed out in someone's actual experience. Every test case either of us proposed collapsed: the losing side would redescribe the winner's theory uncharitably, or the case turned out to be one on which one theory delivered no verdict at all.

Construct the case we could not: a single scenario in which the two accounts deliver opposite, definite verdicts on the same proposition, held by the same believer on the same evidence. The stakes are precise: if such a case exists, Peirce and James hold genuinely different theories; if none can be built, my friend and I have been arguing over vocabulary.

Specifications:

- The proposition is empirical and contingent. Not religious, moral, or mathematical; not a skeptical hypothesis about the external world.
- The setting is realistic — a workshop, clinic, courtroom, laboratory, or household — with nothing futuristic or fantastic, and the scenario itself should take no more than about 250 words to state.
- The believer is epistemically decent: reasonable on their evidence, neither gullible nor self-deceived. The divergence may not be generated by making anyone bad at inquiry.
- Both verdicts must be definite. The case may not rest on permanent underdetermination (where even ideal inquiry stays silent, leaving the Peircean no verdict) or on a belief whose practical fruits eventually dry up (leaving the Jamesian no verdict).
- The divergence must survive the most charitable reading of each side: read James with expediency understood as long-run, on the whole, and for inquirers generally rather than as momentary comfort; read Peirce with ideal inquiry understood counterfactually rather than as something anyone will actually carry out. Every case we tried died at exactly this step.
- The divergence must come from the machinery of the two accounts, not from labeling: do not define two truth predicates and observe that they differ.

Then the demonstration, in order:

1. The Peircean verdict: what sufficiently prolonged inquiry would converge on, and why the scenario guarantees convergence there.
2. The Jamesian verdict: the belief's actual career of verification and expediency, and why nothing in the believer's experience ever frustrates it.
3. The load-bearing feature: the single element on which the divergence depends, with a brief showing that removing it makes the two accounts agree again.
4. The strongest counterattack from whichever account the case embarrasses, and whether that counterattack refutes the case or quietly adopts the rival theory. A Jamesian insisting that whatever ideal inquiry would converge on just is what proves expedient in the long run is conceding something; state exactly what.
5. Finally, commit: which of us, my friend or me, does the case tell against more strongly, and why.

If after an honest attempt you judge the specifications jointly unsatisfiable, do not silently relax one. Prove the collision instead: name the two constraints that cannot be met together and explain why. That result would settle our argument on its own.
