Okay so here's something that's been nagging at me and I want the full philosophical treatment.

Background, roughly as I understand it: in the US legal system (and I think most systems like it), the state can compel your *body* as evidence but not your *mind*. Police can force a blood draw from a DUI suspect, take your fingerprints and DNA, make you provide a handwriting sample or speak words aloud so a witness can hear your voice, make you stand in a lineup. But they cannot compel you to answer questions against yourself. Courts have treated this as a fairly clean line for decades: physical evidence is compellable, testimony is protected.

Two wrinkles that make it less clean. First, a handwriting sample is something you produce through a deliberate act, and it leaks information about your mental states, yet it counts as "physical." Second, the same system that refuses to *compel* your testimony also generally refuses to let you *volunteer* neural evidence on your own behalf — you can't walk in with polygraph results saying "see, I believe I'm innocent." So it's not a simple "your mind is sovereign and you may do with it what you want" picture.

Now the occasion for my question: consumer neurotech. EEG headbands sold for sleep and focus, and whatever the implanted-BCIs-for-paralysis stuff becomes as it goes elective. The data these things collect sits on company servers and is neither obviously "your body" nor obviously "your testimony." Picture a prosecutor subpoenaing someone's headband logs to reconstruct their attention/fatigue state during an accident, and extrapolate from there toward more content-like readouts as decoding improves.

Here's what I actually want:

1. Reconstruct the implicit philosophy of mind underneath the physical/testimonial line. What does testimony have to *be*, and where does the mind have to *be*, for the line to sit where it does? Does it presuppose some container picture where mental content only leaves the head via an act of communication? Does a functionalist or extended-mind view move the line, and if so where?
2. Which rival theories of mental content does the doctrine quietly rule out — and be honest about where it's just philosophically underdetermined, i.e. where several different theories would license the same practice, so the doctrine doesn't actually commit to anything. I suspect there's a lot of that and I'd rather have it flagged than have a tidy theory overfit to a messy doctrine.
3. Is the doctrine even internally consistent? The handwriting exemplar thing seems like a genuine tension, not just an edge case.
4. The payoff: taken seriously, where does BCI-derived data fall under the doctrine's own implicit theory? And what would concretely have to change in legal practice if we swapped that theory for a flat information-theoretic view — evidence about your mental states is evidence about your mental states, whether it exits through your mouth, your hand, or your motor cortex?

Don't give me a reading list, I just want the analysis. If parts of this are less philosophically loaded than they seem at first, say so — I have a habbit of seeing deep structure in what might just be path dependence plus judges not liking lie detectors.
