I'm a 2L and in my spare time I've been reading epistemology for fun, and I think I stumbled on the lottery paradox living a second life inside my evidence textbook, but I'm not sure. Here's the hypothetical our prof gave us. A concert venue has 1000 attendees, and the organizers can prove that only 100 of them bought tickets -- the other 900 snuck in over the fence. They sue one attendee, picked more or less at random, for the ticket price. The civil standard is preponderance of the evidence, i.e. more likely than not, and it's 90% likely this particular guy didn't pay, which is way over 50%. And yet apparently no court would find for the organizers on that evidence alone, and the intuition pump is supposed to be that this outcome is correct. Meanwhile, if the organizers had one shaky eyewitness, or some physical trace with a comparable error rate, a similar 90% could be plenty. Same number, completely different result.

So my question: does this kind of case -- where a bare statistical probability, however high, seems not to be enough on its own, while seemingly weaker evidence with the right pedigree is enough -- have its own name and literature, seperate from the lottery paradox? Or is it literally just the lottery paradox transplanted into a courtroom? I ask because I'd expect the epistemology to carry over wholesale, and it doesn't seem to: in the lottery case the standard moves are about whether you're even justified in believing "this ticket will lose," whereas here everyone grants the 90%, and the fight is about whether 90% of the wrong kind does any work at all. And the thing that really bugs me is that DNA evidence is also "just statistics" -- the match probability is a number derived from population frequencies, not from anything this defendant did -- and courts eat that up. So whatever the line is, it has to let DNA in while keeping the gatecrasher case out.

If there's an established literature on this, does anyone in it actually have a principled criterion for when statistical evidence suffices and when it doesn't, one that explains both halves of the pattern in a single account? The gestures I've seen -- the evidence has to be tied to this defendant in particular, or the belief would have been formed differently if he had in fact paid -- seem to either rule out DNA too or rule the gatecrasher back in, depending on how you cash them out. I'm not just after a label to drop in a seminar paper; I want to know what the best carving of the phenomenon actually is, what it excludes, and where its edges get messy.
