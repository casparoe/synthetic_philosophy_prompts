I am a novelist by trade, not a philosopher, and in six weeks I am to give the keynote at a university colloquium on Levinas and literature. My talk argues outright a claim my latest novel was written to explore: that sustained realist fiction can place a reader under the command of the face of the Other rather than merely describe that command. The novel alternates between a magistrate who adjudicates asylum claims and the voice of one applicant whose account keeps escaping the legal categories meant to contain it; my contention is that a reader who submits to such a narrative is made answerable in something like the way Levinas takes the encounter with the Other to make one answerable, and that fiction can accomplish this precisely where philosophical prose structurally struggles to, since prose must thematize what it touches and thereby convert the saying into the said. So my thesis is not that novels can illustrate Levinas, but that a certain kind of novel can enact ethics as first philosophy for its reader.

The audience will be a philosophy department in the proper sense: a dozen Levinas scholars and phenomenologists, several of whom have written seriously on the saying and the said; two or three aestheticians; a pair of analytic philosophers of literature who are courteous but openly doubtful that fiction performs any philosophical work that argument could not perform better; and a swarm of graduate students. No one in the room will be hostile to Levinas himself. The hostility, or at least the chill, will be reserved for a novelist laying claim to philosophical ground. I should add that they will know their Levinas better than I ever will, including his own early ambivalence about art -- the early postwar essay in which he treats the artwork as a kind of shadow of the real, and his recurring suggestion that aesthetic enjoyment is an evasion of responsibility rather than a form of it.

Prepare me for the question period. I want a prep sheet, not a defense. Give me the eight to ten objections this audience will actually raise, ordered roughly by how likely they are to come and how much damage each could do to the talk, and phrase each one the way the person who would raise it would phrase it -- the phenomenologist who has spent a career on Totality and Infinity will not put the objection the way the analytic philosopher of literature will. For each: what I should concede at the outset, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Distinguish clearly between objections that genuinely threaten my thesis as stated and objections that only threaten a stronger, more romantic thesis I may be tempted to drift into under pressure, so that I know what not to improvise. I expect the distinction between the saying and the said, and the question of whether a fictional character -- constituted, known from within by its author, closed inside a finished form -- can count as an Other at all, to dominate the evening; but I want the full range, including whatever the aestheticians and the skeptics of literature-and-philosophy will bring. And include the one question I am most hoping nobody asks, together with the least bad answer I could give to it.

Finally, I would like the sheet to be honest about its own footing. It is extrapolating from positions and dispositions, not from knowledge of these particular people; please say, for your predictions as a whole and for the individual replies, how confident you are, and single out the one point in the prep sheet -- whether an anticipated objection, a phrasing, or a suggested reply -- that you are least sure of.
