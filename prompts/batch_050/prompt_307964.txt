I'm writing the final exam for my upper-level undergrad seminar on Nietzsche (third/fourth years, they've read all three essays of On the Genealogy of Morals). I want one essay question built around this passage from the start of the Second Essay, §1 (Samuel translation), where he's explaining what it takes for an animal to be able to promise:

"—so that it is by no means a mere passive inability to get rid of a once indented impression, not merely the indigestion occasioned by a once pledged word, which one cannot dispose of, but an active refusal to get rid of it, a continuing and a wish to continue what has once been willed, an actual memory of the will; so that between the original "I will," "I shall do," and the actual discharge of the will, its act, we can easily interpose a world of new strange phenomena, circumstances, veritable volitions, without the snapping of this long chain of the will."

A few lines later he says man first had to become "calculable, disciplined, necessitated even for himself and his own conception of himself, that, like a man entering a promise, he could guarantee himself as a future."

Here's the tension I want the question to force students into. In Essay I (the lightning-flash passage) Nietzsche ridicules the idea of a doer behind the deed, a neutral subject that stands behind its acts and could have done otherwise. But promising as he describes it here seems to need pretty much exactly that: something that persists between the "I will" and the doing, that binds its future self in advance, that can vouch for itself across time. So does the account of promising smuggle back in the metaphysics of the subject that Essay I trashes? And whichever way students go on that, they should have to say what follows for responsibility and guilt: does the genealogy of the "memory of the will" debunk our practice of holding people to their word, or does it explain why that practice is possible, or somehow both at once? I want them to take a position, not survey options.

Please write:

1. The exam question itself, worded so it can't be answered by retelling the master/slave morality story from Essay I, and so that a student has to actually work with the language of the passage (the "active refusal," the "memory of the will," the "long chain of the will") rather than paraphrase it vaguely. They get 90 minutes.
2. A model answer of about 600 words (the passage obviously doesn't count toward that). The model answer should take a definite stand on the consistency question and should draw the consequence for responsibility explicitly.
3. A marking scheme out of 20, broken into specific must-hit moves with marks for each, and for each move a one-line note on how students typically get it wrong. Make clear what seperates an A answer from a B answer -- I want the scheme to name the difference concretely (e.g., a B answer notices the tension with Essay I but just reports it, whereas an A answer does X with it), not just "more depth."
