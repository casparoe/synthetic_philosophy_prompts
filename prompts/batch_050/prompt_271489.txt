Minimum wage law creates a puzzle I keep coming back to, and I want to understand what it assumes without having to take a side on whether the law itself is good policy.

Here is the practice, stated concretely. In most developed countries, it is a punishable offense for an employer and a worker to agree on a wage below the statutory floor -- say, five dollars an hour -- even if the worker is an adult of sound mind, fully informed, and genuinely eager to take the job because the alternative is no job at all. The state treats that voluntary contract as illegitimate and overrides both parties' consent. And yet the very same legal system permits arrangements that look, on their face, strictly worse for the worker by the law's own implicit standard: unpaid internships at profitable companies are lawful provided they satisfy certain conditions, volunteering at a nonprofit for zero dollars an hour is unproblematic, and I may spend a decade writing a novel or training for the Olympics with no wage at all. So the law forbids working for little money but freely permits working for none. It blocks a contract two adults want, in the name of protecting one of them, while standing aside when the "protection" would seem even more needed.

What I want to know is what philosophical commitments this pattern presupposes. Specifically: does the practice presuppose a particular theory of consent -- that below some threshold, agreement is not truly voluntary, or that voluntariness is not sufficient for legitimacy? Does it presuppose a theory of exploitation in which the wrongness of a transaction depends on what the worker receives rather than on what either party chose? Does it quietly assume that the value of labor, or of a person's dignity, is the kind of thing that cannot be alienated by contract, the way we think liberty itself cannot be? And does the carve-out for volunteering and internships presuppose that the wrongness attaches to *underpaid* work but not to *unpaid* work -- and if so, what coherent theory could draw the line there rather than at the worker's overall situation?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can: identify what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits well with (for instance, whether it needs any theory of exploitation at all, or whether a purely consequentialist rationale about labor markets would license the same rules). Tell me whether the permission for unpaid work is a genuine inconsistency in the underlying commitments or whether some defensible distinction reconciles it with the wage floor. And finally, tell me concretely what would have to change in the law if we replaced the presupposed view of consent with a thoroughgoing liberal one on which any informed adult agreement between competent parties is beyond the state's business -- which currently illegal contracts would have to be permitted, and whether any rationale for a wage floor could survive that replacement.
