I'm a political theory PhD student, and my dissertation has a chapter on how states may permissibly organize their defense -- conscription, professional volunteer armies, and the armed-neutrality militia model (Switzerland and Finland, plus Sweden which abolished conscription and then brought it back). The problem is that every paper I've read evaluates these arrangements using one pet value and then declares victory: the republicans say conscription builds civic virtue and ties war-making to the citizenry, the liberals say the draft is forced labor and a volunteer market solves it, the defense economists just want the most capable force per dollar, and the armed-neutrality people have this whole thing about militias making wars of aggression structurally impossible for the state. My advisor told me to stop adjudicating between them and first figure out what a theory in this area is even supposed to do. So that's what I'm asking for.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of legitimate defense institutions has to deliver. Which cases must it get right (does it have to condemn mercenary outsourcing, and if so on what grounds? must it explain what's wrong with a draft whose deferments fall only on the rich? can it permit a professional army that becomes a separate caste with its own politics?). Which distinctions it can't collapse -- I'm thinking of defensive vs expeditionary capacity, the wrongness of being coerced into killing vs into dying, fairness in distributing the burden vs the total size of the burden, but tell me which ones I've missed or which of mine are actually spurious. Which empirical facts it's not allowed to contradict (demographics, what modern war actually requires of personnel, what happens to civil-military relations under each model). And crucially: which of the standard demands are genuine requirements and which are just the prejudices of one camp dressed up as criteria -- I suspect the civic-virtue demand and the efficency demand are both candidates for the prejudice bin, but I want the argument.

Then the part I care most about: flag any subset of the desiderata that looks jointly unsatisfiable. My hunch is there's a trilemma between equality of sacrifice, individual liberty of occupational choice, and military effectiveness, and that every real institutional arrangement is just a choice of which horn to sit on -- if the desiderata list itself can prove something like that, it does more work than any verdict. Tell me which single requirement knocks out the most existing positions. A short scoring of the three institutional models against the final list is fine at the end, but keep it brief; the criteria are the deliverable.
