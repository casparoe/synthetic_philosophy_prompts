I'm a high school senior, and my biology teacher has asked me to give a twenty-minute guest lesson to the freshman honors class on what heritability actually means. The occasion is that our school board is debating funding for an academic enrichment program, and one board member argued against it by citing a study reporting that intelligence is roughly sixty percent heritable — several freshmen have since repeated that number as if it settles the matter. Please act as an expert in behavioral genetics who is also a veteran science communicator, and think carefully, brainstorming before you answer, about how this concept is predictably misunderstood by smart fourteen-year-olds.

Don't write the lesson for me; plan how I should teach it. I need: the order in which the pieces have to be introduced and why — what the freshmen must already grasp about variation within a population before the word heritability can do any work, and what has to be in place before I can explain why a high heritability estimate says nothing about whether a change in environment could make a difference; the single carrying example to build the whole lesson around — I'm torn between height (highly heritable, yet average height has risen dramatically over a few generations of better nutrition) and the thought experiment of genetically varied seeds sown in two soils of different quality — so tell me which carries the idea with fewer loose ends, and what the winning example must contain and must not contain; the misconceptions they will form (I expect at least 'highly heritable means fixed and unchangeable' and 'heritability within one group tells us why groups differ from each other'), and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off; one closing question that reveals whether they've understood the concept rather than memorized a slogan, including what a confident wrong answer would sound like; and what I should openly refuse to explain at this level, with a way of saying so that doesn't imply the genuinely unsettled parts are settled.
