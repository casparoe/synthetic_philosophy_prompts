Here is the exam question the student was answering:

Can an omission — for instance, a gardener's failing to water a plant that consequently dies — count as a genuine cause of an outcome? Present the main obstacle to treating omissions as causes, explain how the counterfactual theory of causation handles such a case, and assess whether the theory's treatment of omissions constitutes a serious objection to it.

And here is the student's answer:

We ordinarily treat omissions as causes without hesitation: we blame the gardener for the plant's death, and the law convicts the parent who fails to feed a child. The counterfactual theory of causation, defended by David Lewis, vindicates this practice. It analyses causation in terms of counterfactual dependence: C causes E when, had C not occurred, E would not have occurred. The gardener's omission counts as a cause of the death because, had the gardener watered the plant, it would have survived.

The main obstacle is that absences are not events. An omission is not anything that happens; there is no occurrence to stand in the causal relation. Lewis himself pressed this objection in his later work and conceded that it is a serious difficulty for his own account, since his analysis of causal dependence between events has no obvious application to things that never happen.

A further problem is that the theory proves too much. If the gardener's failure to water caused the death, then so did the Queen's failure to water the plant, my neighbour's, and everyone else's, since the corresponding counterfactual holds in each case. The theory therefore collapses into the absurd view that everything causes everything else.

Defenders of the theory reply that which omissions count as causes is sensitive to context and to what is normal. The gardener's omission is singled out because watering was his duty; deviations from the expected default, not every absent event, are treated as causes. This is supported by cases of prevention: when a bodyguard tackles a second assassin before he can fire, the assassination is prevented, which is simply another instance of causation by omission.

I conclude that omissions can be genuine causes, that the counterfactual theory explains this well, and that the objection from absences, though Lewis took it seriously, can be defused by appeal to normality and context.
