In my ancient philosophy reading group, discussion of Aristotle’s account of money and exchange has stalled over a single interpretive crux in Nicomachean Ethics V.5. The relevant argument can be paraphrased as follows: exchange requires unlike goods to be made commensurable; money provides a conventional measure that makes such comparison possible; and chreia—variously rendered as need, use, or demand—is what binds exchange partners together.

Three interpretations keep recurring. The psychological reading treats chreia as subjective want or utility: goods become comparable because people desire them to different degrees, while money gives those otherwise incommensurable wants a common practical scale. The social-function reading denies that Aristotle is reducing value to individual psychology. It takes chreia to be the reciprocal dependence produced by a division of labor within a polis: a builder and a shoemaker can exchange because each occupies a role that generates needs the other can satisfy. Money therefore facilitates social reciprocity rather than measuring a private mental magnitude. The anti-economic reading denies that Aristotle is offering a theory of value at all. On this view, the passage concerns the conditions under which reciprocal justice is possible; money is a device for achieving proportional requital, not an explanation of what determines ordinary market prices.

After several meetings, nobody has moved. Each side accuses the others of importing a later economic theory into Aristotle, while the anti-economic readers accuse both of asking the passage to answer a question Aristotle never posed. My working hypothesis is that the dispute is stalled because three different questions are being run together: what makes unlike goods comparable, what if anything money measures, and what makes an exchange just. I also suspect that the translation of chreia is often chosen after, rather than before, the larger interpretation has been adopted.
