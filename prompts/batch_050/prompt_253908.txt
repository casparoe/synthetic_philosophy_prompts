Last week I sat in on a long argument between two coworkers at a formal-methods company — Mara, who works on program verification, and Jonas, who trained as a philosopher — about whether Gödel's incompleteness theorems have real teeth outside mathematics. The occasion was a design meeting about whether we could ever build an AI system that certifies its own reasoning as consistent, but it quickly became a general fight, and neither of them would concede anything. It went roughly like this. (1) Mara opened by saying Gödel's second incompleteness theorem settles the design question: any consistent formal system strong enough to reason about itself cannot prove its own consistency, so a self-certifying AI isn't merely hard to build, it's mathematically impossible, full stop. (2) Jonas answered that the theorem has strict preconditions — the system must be effectively axiomatized, contain enough arithmetic, and actually be consistent — and a deployed piece of software is none of those things in the relevant sense, so invoking Gödel here is just dressing up an engineering worry in mathematical costume. (3) Mara replied that this is dodging: model the AI's reasoning however you like, and you face a dilemma — if its reasoning is too weak for the theorem's preconditions, it's too weak to do the interesting general reasoning we want from it, and if it's strong enough, Gödel bites. (4) Jonas then said that even granting the dilemma, Mara's conclusion overreaches: the theorem only blocks proving consistency from within the same system, while a stronger system can prove the consistency of a weaker one — that's how Gentzen proved the consistency of Peano arithmetic using transfinite induction — so the honest upshot is "no internal certification," not "impossible," and humans are in exactly the same position, since none of us can prove our own reasoning consistent either, yet Jonas presumably goes on trusting his. (5) Mara said the Gentzen move just kicks the problem upstairs, since the stronger system then stands in need of its own justification and the regress never terminates, and the human comparison cuts against Jonas rather than for him: if humans can't certify their own consistency, then human oversight can't rescue machine verification either. (6) Jonas closed by saying fine, but then the theorem shows nothing special about AI or institutions at all — it shows something about reasoning in general, and a mathematical result that applies to everything explains nothing about the specific case, so Mara cited a famous theorem to add zero content to what was really just a general skeptical point.

Neither of them is a fool, and I genuinely couldn't tell who won. Then give an overall verdict on who is more right about the original design question, and say what, if anything, both of them are missing — for instance, whether they conflate consistency with correctness or safety, or overlook a distinction between a system failing to certify itself and nobody being able to certify it.
