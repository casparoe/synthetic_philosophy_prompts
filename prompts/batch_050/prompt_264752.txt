No one from a campaign group, which matters I think, because nobody in the room is coming at me from a "ban all killer robots" position.

My talk defends a claim I've been developing: that the whole debate about "meaningful human control" is misframed. Roughly, I argue that control was never the thing we actually valued in human decision-making about lethal force -- what we valued was that a specific, identifiable person could be held responsible and could have chosen otherwise, and that this accountability structure is what made restraint possible. So instead of asking "is a human meaningfully in control of this system?", we should ask "is there a human who is meaningfully on the hook for what this system does?" -- and I claim that most proposed "human-in-the-loop" setups score terribly on my question while looking fine on the standard one, because a supervisor rubber-stamping machine recommendations in eight seconds has "control" on paper but is in no position to be genuinely accountable. I want to argue this makes my framework stricter than the control-based one, not looser, and that it rules out some systems that defenders of human-in-the-loop setups are comfortable with.
