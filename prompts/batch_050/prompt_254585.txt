A guy in my department's reading group presented this argument last week and it's been bugging me, because the conclusion is obviously nuts but every step looks defensble:

(1) If an activity carries a serious risk of grave, irreversible harm to people who never consented to being put at risk, and the worst risks can be screened out in advance at reasonable cost, then the state is permitted to require a license for that activity. (He said this is just the principle behind driver's licenses and medical licenses.)
(2) Raising a child carries a serious risk of grave, irreversible harm to the child, who never consented to being born or to being raised by these particular people.
(3) So the state is permitted to require a license to become a parent.
(4) We already do exactly this for adoptive parents, and nobody thinks adoption screening is a grave injustice, so a workable licensing scheme clearly exists.
(5) A licensing scheme is only real if it's enforced, and the only remotely workable enforcement tools against unlicensed biological parents are removing the children or imposing long-acting contraception as a condition of something.
(6) Forcibly removing newborns from parents who want them, or coercive contraception, is morally beyond the pale.
(7) So parent licensing is impermissible after all, which means at least one of (1)–(4) is false.
(8) (4) is just an empirical observation, and (2) is hard to deny. So (1) is false: serious risk of harm to unconsenting third parties is not, by itself, enough to justify licensing.
(9) But if (1) is false, driver's licenses and medical licenses lose their justification too. That's absurd. So the argument has gone off the rails somewhere.

He also threw in two extra wrinkles for free. First, he said the licensing test would have to screen for whether prospective parents will respect the child's right to an open future (i.e. not foreclosing the kid's major life options early on), and since no test can measure something like that, this is further support for rejecting licensing. Second, when someone objected that licensing protects future children, he replied that you can't wrong a child who doesn't exist yet by failing to prevent its existence — and anyway, any child born to parents who would have failed the test is a different child than the one those same people would have had if they'd passed, so nobody is made worse off by skipping the scheme. That last move felt like a cheat to me but I couldn't say why on the spot.

If it matters, the two things I most want to keep are (6) and driver's licenses, so ideally show me where the argument breaks while preserving both. And please also assess his two wrinkles separately — are they load-bearing or just decoration?
