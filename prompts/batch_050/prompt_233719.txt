I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm commissioning a long-form essay on a question in the philosophy of biology: what makes something one organism rather than a collection of many? The essay should be written for educated non-specialists and should take the debate about holobionts as its centerpiece. A holobiont is a host plus its symbiotic microbes considered as a single unit, and biologists and philosophers disagree about whether such units deserve the title of organism. On one side are physiological criteria: a community of cells counts as an organism when it functions as an integrated whole, policing its own boundaries, cooperating metabolically, and tolerating rather than attacking its members. On the other side are evolutionary criteria: something is an organism when it reproduces through a bottleneck, so that the interests of its parts are aligned because they sink or swim together in the next generation. The trouble is that these criteria pull apart. Most holobionts fail the evolutionary test, because their microbes are acquired horizontally each generation and remain free to defect, yet they often pass the physiological test with flying colors, since the host's immune system and metabolism depend on them.

To keep the essay from becoming a survey of familiar examples, build your argument around a hypothetical that sharpens the conflict. Imagine that genetic engineers produce a line of humans whose essential gut bacteria have been modified to be strictly vertically inherited, passed from mother to child in the egg itself, and made incapable of surviving outside the host or of competing with one another. By the evolutionary criterion, this engineered community now looks like one organism in a way no natural holobiont ever has. By the physiological criterion, almost nothing has changed. Ask whether this imaginary case reveals that the two criteria are really tracking different concepts that should be given different names, or whether one of them is the true account of organismhood and the other a useful but dispensable heuristic.

The essay should weigh both positions seriously and fairly, but it must not end in a shrug. Reach a defended verdict, whether that means arguing that holobionts genuinely are organisms, that they are not and the physiological criterion has been oversold, or that organism is a cluster concept whose criteria can come apart, with an explanation of which resolution best serves biological practice. Roughly 900 words. The response must be continuous prose only, as in a letter or an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Use American English throughout.
