I spent my career in biochemistry and have taken up philosophy in retirement, beginning with the philosophy of law. At my reading group, a proposal that the state may criminalize certain conduct solely on the ground that it is immoral was met with the familiar rejoinder: you can't legislate morality. The phrase ended the discussion, and I suspect it did so undeservedly, because on reflection I cannot tell what it actually asserts. I gather it circulates widely in and around the dispute between H. L. A. Hart and Patrick Devlin over the legal enforcement of morals, though nobody at the table could say which thesis it was supposed to be.

Please disentangle the slogan for me. Set out, as a numbered list, the distinct precise claims it could plausibly be taken to express. I have in mind at least the following possibilities, though I would like your own taxonomy rather than mine: a conceptual claim that legal enactment cannot alter what is in fact right and wrong; an empirical claim that legislation is causally impotent to make people virtuous; a normative claim that a practice's immorality is never, by itself, a sufficient reason to criminalize it; and a jurisprudential claim about the proper aims or necessary limits of law as an institution. For each reading, state the claim precisely enough that one could argue for or against it, and give a verdict: platitude, substantive but defensible, or false. Please also indicate, where relevant, what kind of evidence or argument would settle the matter (history of vice legislation, for instance, versus conceptual analysis of what law is).

Then do two further things. First, identify which reading the slogan must bear if it is to do the work my interlocutor needed it to do, namely to rule out legal moralism without further argument, and say whether that reading is among the defensible ones. Second, say how the strongest reading relates to the actual Hart--Devlin dispute: whether it captures Hart's position, Devlin's, neither, or simply changes the subject, and whether the slogan's plausibility survives contact with cases like the criminalization of bigamy, cruelty to animals, or tax fraud, where the boundary between enforcing morality and preventing harm is hard to draw.
