I co-host a philosophy podcast and next month we're having on a legal philosopher whose big claim is that the courts are wrong to distrust naked statistical evidence: in her view, if the numbers are strong enough, that's all a verdict needs. So she thinks the plaintiff in the Blue Bus case should win if 80% of the buses on the route were blue, and she bites the bullet that the gatecrasher could be held liable for the ticket price if 90% of the crowd snuck in without paying. She's sharp and very practised at making the view sound obviously correct, so I need questions that actually push back rather than set her up for her greatest hits.

Give me eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I'd like the set to cover at least these pressure points, but feel free to add others I'm missing:

- The ordinary intuition that something is missing in the gatecrasher case even when the percentage is 99% -- she'll probably say the intuition is just misfiring familiarity with eyewitness-style evidence, so I need a follow-up that forces her to say what would *ever* count against her view if not that intuition.
- Whether her position collapses the civil/criminal difference: if stats suffice for preponderance of evidence, why not beyond reasonable doubt, and if the answer is just a higher threshold, what the principled line is.
- The reference class problem -- the gatecrasher is 90% likely to have snuck in qua attendee, but maybe 2% likely qua season-ticket holder. How she picks the class without smuggling in the kind of individualized evidence she says we don't need.
- The point that a conviction rate based on matching base rates guarantees that, across many cases, the court knowingly convicts a fixed proportion of innocents, and whether she's comfortable with a system that could compute its own expected number of wrongful convictions.
- Whether her view implies that cold-hit DNA database matches (where the match is found by trawling a database, not by independent suspicion) are straightforwardly sufficient, since that's the case her opponents usually think is actually fine, unlike the gatecrasher.

Also make sure the questions don't let her just re-explain what naked statistical evidence is -- our audience knows the cases already, I want her on the defensive early.
