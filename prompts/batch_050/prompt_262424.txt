I'm a PhD student in philosophy of language, and I've been wrestling with an argument that a member of my dissertation reading group constructed after we worked through Grice's account of speaker meaning. The argument purports to show that the intention-based analysis of meaning collapses into a kind of Humpty Dumpty voluntarism in which words mean whatever the speaker inwardly resolves that they mean. Each step looks defensible on its own, but the conclusion is one I'm confident we should not accept, and I keep failing to locate where things go wrong. I've reconstructed the argument as faithfully as I can:

(1) What a speaker means by an utterance is fully constituted by her communicative intention: she means that p just in case she utters intending to produce in her audience the belief that p (or the belief that she believes p) by means of their recognition of that very intention. (2) A speaker's intentions are facts about her own mind; they obtain independently of the conventional or literal meanings of the expressions she happens to employ. (3) Therefore, what a speaker means on an occasion depends only on her intentions, and not on what her words conventionally mean. (4) A speaker can, on any occasion, intend to produce any belief in her audience — for instance, she can utter the sentence "Grass is green" while intending her audience to recognize that she is trying to get them to believe that snow is white. (5) So, by (1) through (3), on such an occasion the speaker means, by "Grass is green," that snow is white. (6) Generalizing: any expression can be used to mean anything at all, and conventional meaning places no constraint whatsoever on speaker meaning. (7) But if any expression could be used to mean anything, the distinction between what a speaker means and what her words mean would evaporate, mutual understanding would be an inexplicable miracle, and the very possibility of lying or misspeaking would be threatened. Hence the intention-based analysis of speaker meaning proves too much and should be abandoned.

What I would most like to preserve is the broadly Gricean ambition of reducing semantic facts to psychological ones — I do not want to be pushed into taking convention or literal meaning as primitive unless there's no alternative. So: which step of the argument should be rejected, and on what grounds? If the problem lies in an equivocation or an illicit slide somewhere between (1) and (6), identify it precisely and explain why the step looked innocent. If the argument is valid as it stands, tell me which premise is cheapest to surrender, and price out the main alternatives: for instance, what does a refined Gricean account have to add or restrict in order to block the conclusion, what does the convention-first picture cost the reductionist project, and does the speaker-meaning/sentence-meaning distinction survive on each option? Please write your answer in American English.
