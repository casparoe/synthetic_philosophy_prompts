So I'm a staff software engineer at a mid-sized infrastructure company (~400 engineers, we sell long-term data storage), and I've somehow ended up on our "engineering investment council" — the group that approves big multi-quarter projects. Everything gets scored with a cost-benefit model that discounts future benefits at 9% per year. Standard finance practice, apparently.

Here's the fight we're having. Proposal A is an expensive rebuild of our durability layer: roughly two years of work, and most of the payoff lands in years 8–15 (much lower chance of catastrophic data loss, way cheaper maintainence once the old system is gone). Proposal B is a set of cheaper performance wins whose benefits all arrive in years 1–3. Under the 9% discount rate, A loses badly and B wins, and this pattern keeps repeating — anything whose payoff is far out basically can't ever get funded.

One of our senior engineers is arguing that the 9% lumps together things that should be separated: some of it is legitimately about risk (we might not exist in 12 years, the benefits are uncertain, the money could earn returns elsewhere), but part of it is effectively just "benefits later count for less because they're later," and he says that part is indefensible — the people enjoying the year-12 benefits are us and our customers, the same people who'd pay the costs now. Our VP of eng's response is basically: this is how discounted cash flow works everywhere, we're a business not a philosophy seminar, stop trying to reinvent finance.

I have to write the decision memo for next month's meeting. Act as a philosopher who also understands corporate finance and how engineering orgs actually work. Think carefully about this before writing — first brainstorm the strongest considerations on each side (including ones neither my colleague nor the VP has raised), then write the memo itself, around 1–2 pages. It needs an actionable bottom line: should the council keep a flat 9%, split the rate into explicit components (and if so, roughly which components, and whether a "we just care less about later" component should be zero or not), or do something else entirely like tiered rates for different time horizons. The VP's position has to get a fair, steelmanned treatment — "everyone in finance does this" is doing real work in the argument and I want the memo to say exactly what work it is and whether it survives. This will actually get circulated, so it should be persuasive to skeptics, not just people who already agree.

Also, at the end, outside the memo: tell me what would change your mind. Which argument or fact would flip your recommendation, and where in your own reasoning are you least confident — the part where, if the memo is wrong, it's most likely wrong there?
