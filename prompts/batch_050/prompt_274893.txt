A student in my philosophy of technology seminar emailed me a summary of the "low-impact AI" idea that she found on a blog, and asked whether it's solid enough to build her term paper around. Here's the summary, copied over:

"The hardest part of AI safety is telling the machine what we want, since human values are too messy to write down. So researchers have developed a workaround: instead of specifying what the AI should do, you penalize it for how much it changes the world. An AI running under an impact measure tries to leave its environment as close as possible to the way it already was, so it can't knock over the vase, drain the reservoir, or pave the planet, whatever its goal happens to be. Crucially, impact is objective: you just measure how big the change in the world's state is, which is an engineering question rather than a philosophical one. That's the whole appeal — safety without having to solve value alignment first. And when philosophers object that you can't cleanly separate 'side effects' from the very effects the AI was built to produce, they're attacking a straw man, because the measure doesn't need to tell good changes from bad ones; it only needs to keep changes small."

My gut reaction is that the first paragraph gets the motivation more or less right, but the rest feels too tidy, and I can't put my finger on exactly where it goes wrong. Two things nag at me in particular. First, "leave the environment as close as possible to the way it already was" — surely that's not literally the proposal? The world changes on its own all the time; a maximally conservative agent would seemingly have to act constantly, possibly very intrusively, to freeze everything in place, which is the opposite of low impact. Second, "impact is objective, you just measure the change" — measure it on what scale, exactly? Counting atoms moved gives one answer, counting news headlines another, and the choice between them doesn't feel like a neutral engineering call to me.

So: go through the summary and audit it. Which claims would a researcher working on impact measures accept, which are reasonable popularizations, and which are subtly off in ways that matter for a philosophy paper? In particular: does the punchline — that this approach delivers safety *without* smuggling in any specification of value — survive once the misrepresentations are fixed? And is the philosophers' objection about side effects vs. intended effects really a straw man, or does it in fact point at the soft underbelly of the whole approach? I'm inclined to think the blog author is half-right about the straw man charge and totally wrong about why, but I'd like the case spelled out.
