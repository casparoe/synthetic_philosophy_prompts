I'm a junior in high school and one of three student reviewers who run my school's community service program. Students need forty certified hours to graduate, and part of my job is to review submitted activities -- roughly fifteen files a week, maybe ten minutes each -- and decide whether they count toward the requirement or only get logged as extracurricular. Until now we counted anything that benefited someone else. This year the faculty advisor changed the rule after a dispute: a student who organized a large, well-publicized food drive, apparently mostly for her college applications, received full credit, while a student who spends every Saturday visiting isolated elderly neighbors with no organization behind her was initially denied because there was no supervising adult to sign the form. The new rule says an activity counts only if it was done for the right reasons, not merely because it produced good outcomes or looks generous.

Our advisor pointed us to the tradition behind this -- Kant's distinction between acting in accordance with duty and acting from duty, his example of the honest shopkeeper who treats customers fairly because honesty is good for business, and the long-running worry (Schiller's is the famous version) that this picture seems to penalize people who help others joyfully and out of sympathy rather than from grim principle. I understand the distinction in the abstract, but I have to apply it to real files, and I cannot see into anyone's motives. I would like you to turn this philosophical material into a procedure my fellow reviewers and I can actually run.

Specifically, I would like: a sequence of questions to ask of a file, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points where the procedure stops with a definite no and the points where it must hand the decision to a human because the remaining question is one of judgment rather than evidence; and an explicit account of which distinctions have to survive the translation into procedure (for example, how to handle mixed or overdetermined motives -- does an admissible self-interested motive alongside a dutiful one spoil the case, or not?) and which can safely be dropped at our level of stakes. Please also address the epistemic problem honestly: we cannot interrogate students at length or detect self-deception, so the procedure needs defensible proxies for motive, and it should say where those proxies will misfire and what an appeals process should do about that. Consistency across the three of us matters, since students compare outcomes.

Then run the procedure on three cases: the food-drive organizer whose primary motive appears to be her applications; the student who visits elderly neighbors out of genuine affection and enjoys it; and a student who completed all forty hours tutoring younger kids, did it reliably and well, but told his friends he did it only because his parents required it. I expect at least one of these verdicts to be one I will dislike; please do not soften it. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do -- where right-reasons assessment breaks down when outsourced to checkboxes, and what we lose by operationalizing moral worth at all.
