I'm a second-year student taking a philosophy of science seminar alongside an introductory course in human evolutionary biology, and I keep running into the same conversation-stopper. Last week I proposed, in discussion section, that the near-universal human fear of snakes and spiders might be an evolved predisposition rather than purely learned, since it shows up in infants and is easy to condition but hard to extinguish. A classmate immediately said, "that's a classic just-so story — evolutionary psychology can explain anything after the fact," and the TA nodded along. I've since heard the same phrase deployed against explanations of mate preferences, in-group bias, and even taste preferences for sweet foods. But when I pressed my classmate on what exactly the objection was, he couldn't say much more than "it's unfalsifiable, I guess?"

The thing is, I can see at least a few different complaints that "it's just a just-so story" might be packing into one sneer, and I doubt they're all equally good. Sometimes it seems to mean the hypothesis makes no testable predictions at all; sometimes that it's testable in principle but no one has done the testing; sometimes that the explainer is guilty of assuming every trait is an adaptation rather than a byproduct or accident (my professor connected this to what she called the "spandrels" critique); and sometimes it seems to be a wholesale dismissal of evolutionary reasoning about the mind as pseudoscience, full stop. These strike me as very different charges with very different implications: the first is a logical point, the second is a sociological point about the state of the evidence, the third is a methodological correction, and the fourth is a sweeping verdict on a whole field.

Could you lay out, as a kind of menu, the distinct precise claims that the "just-so story" accusation could be making — from the mildest and most defensible to the most sweeping — and for each one say (a) whether it's trivially true, genuinely substantive, or actually overstated, and (b) whether it would actually justify dismissing my snake-fear hypothesis the way my classmate did, given that there does seem to be cross-cultural and developmental evidence of the sort I mentioned? I'd also like your verdict on what people who reach for the phrase in casual argument most likely mean, and whether that reading survives scrutiny, since that's the version I'm most likely to have to respond to in section.
