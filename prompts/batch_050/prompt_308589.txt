So I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and lately I've been writing a lot about migration and asylum policy, and I keep noticing my gut reactions to cases don't line up with each other. I want to figure out what principle I'm actually running on, if there even is one. Here are my verdicts, as honest as I can make them.

The classic case: a journalist fleeing a government that jails critics crosses a border and asks for asylum. Obviously in, obviously the country she lands in has to take her, at least until they can check her story.

The war case: a family fleeing a civil war where nobody is targeting *them* specifically, their city is just being shelled. My gut says they're still owed shelter, though it feels weaker than case one somehow.

The climate case: a farmer whose land has become unfarmable after a decade of drought, no persecutor anywhere in sight. Gut says not a refugee, though I'd feel like a monster saying it to his face. Something about there being no bad guy changes it for me.

The internal-flight case: a dissident who could be safe if she moved to the far end of her own huge country, but crosses a border instead. I lean toward saying the receiving country can send her back, but I'm not comfortable with it.

The burden-sharing case: a poor country bordering the crisis ends up hosting two million people while rich countries far away take a few thousand and pat themselves on the back. My gut says the rich countries are doing something wrong even if they never turned anyone away at their own border, which is weird because it means you can wrong a refugee without ever meeting one.

The provoked case: a country sells weapons to a regime, the regime uses them on protesters, the protesters flee to... that same weapons-selling country. I feel like they're *especially* obligated to take those people in, more than some uninvolved country would be. Like they made the mess.

The offshore case: a rich country pays a poorer one to hold asylum seekers and process their claims there. Gut says this is cheating even if the processing is scrupulously fair.

And the one I flip-flop on: someone who committed a genuinely serious crime back home, not political, like armed robbery, and who would also face real persecution if returned. Keep him out? Take him anyway?

Is there one principle underneath all this, or am I running two or three different principles that happen to agree on the easy cases? If it takes more than one principle, tell me which cases belong to which. And if some verdict of mine has to go to make the thing coherent, tell me which one and don't be gentle about it. Also tell me what your principle says about a case I didn't list: a country that simply builds a wall and processes nobody at all, so it never technically refuses anyone.

Please answer in American English.
