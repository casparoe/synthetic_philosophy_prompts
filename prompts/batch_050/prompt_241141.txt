Suppose a small interdisciplinary institute can fund a research group of four philosophers for five years to work at the intersection of philosophy and computational complexity theory. Advise them on which questions to prioritize. Among the candidates I have in mind: (1) whether the principle that "ought" implies "can" should be strengthened so that agents, human or artificial, cannot be obligated to perform actions whose identification requires solving computationally intractable problems — and, relatedly, when the optimal action is computationally inaccessible and agents must fall back on approximations, whether the choice of approximation scheme, and the distribution of the resulting losses, is itself a moral decision rather than a merely technical one; (2) whether treating P ≠ NP as a working hypothesis has genuine implications for the nature of mathematical understanding and for the epistemic status of proofs or solutions that exist but no agent could feasibly find; (3) whether computational complexity, rather than mere computability, should ground claims about which physical and social systems admit of useful predictive theory in principle; (4) whether complexity-theoretic hardness assumptions can do substantive philosophical work in epistemology, for example in justifying claims about what an adversary, institution, or observer cannot come to know; or (5) some question I have not listed. I am less interested in a survey of the area than in the defended ordering. For each question, explain what it is upstream of (which other philosophical questions would be settled, reframed, or dissolved depending on its answer), how tractable it is for philosophers without research-level training in complexity theory, and which theoretical or technical developments during the funding period would move it up or down the list. Where the candidates trade off against one another — depth against reach, foundational payoff against applicability — say which trade-offs you would accept and why, and note any candidate that you would deprioritize on the grounds that its resolution would change little downstream even if it were achieved.
