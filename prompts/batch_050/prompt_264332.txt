You are an expert in Bayesian statistics and formal epistemology. Think carefully through each move in the exchange below before giving your verdicts.

Two colleagues of mine, both statisticians, argued at our journal club about whether accumulating data washes out prior opinion. They are estimating the reliability of a new diagnostic assay from a growing stream of correct/incorrect outcomes. Ana began: given enough shared data, any two analysts with strictly positive priors over the reliability parameter will converge to the same posterior, because the likelihood ratio between the true reliability and any rival value grows without bound and eventually swamps any finite prior odds. Ben replied that this slogan needs at least three caveats: (1) a prior that assigns zero probability to some hypothesis can never be overturned by any amount of evidence; (2) convergence results assume the analysts agree on the likelihood, i.e., on the data-generating model — if they disagree about, say, how often the lab contaminates samples, their posteriors can drift apart forever on identical data; (3) if the true data-generating process lies outside the support of one's prior, no amount of data rescues you. Ana conceded the zero-prior point but called it pathological, and answered the other two by saying that disagreement about contamination is just uncertainty about a bigger model: fold the measurement process into the likelihood and the merging-of-opinions results apply again, and a prior with full support over the enlarged model removes the third worry. Ben pushed back on three grounds: first, the enlarged model reintroduces prior choices about nuisance structure, and two analysts can each assign probability zero to the other's model class, so the absolute-continuity condition needed for merging fails in both directions even though they have agreed on every observation so far; second, the standard merging results concern agreement about predictions of future observations, so two analysts can merge predictively while disagreeing forever about the underlying parameter; third, "eventually" does practical no work — with sufficiently lopsided priors, the amount of data needed for agreement can exceed anything obtainable, so for any real analysis the prior never fully washes out.
