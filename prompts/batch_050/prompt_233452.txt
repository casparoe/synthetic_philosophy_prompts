I'm writing a piece on the hinge of history idea — the claim, floating around longtermist circles, that we might be living at a time of unusually high leverage over the long-run future, when most times are not like that. I want to stress-test it against a real episode rather than another abstract argument, and the one I keep coming back to is Asilomar.

The facts I have in mind (do correct me if I've got something badly wrong, but I think this is the standard story): in 1974 a group of molecular biologists around Paul Berg published an open letter asking their own field to hold off on certain recombinant DNA experiments until the risks could be assessed; in February 1975 roughly 140 scientists, plus a few lawyers and journalists, met at Asilomar in California and hammered out voluntary containment guidelines that tiered experiments by riskiness; within a couple of years the NIH had turned this into formal rules, the moratorium was lifted, and the field went ahead at full speed — including, by the end of the decade, the commercial stuff like bacterial insulin production. No catastrophe, and by most reckonings the technology matured about as fast as it would have done anyway.

Analyse the episode through the hinge hypothesis, in both directions.

Theory onto case. First pin down what the hypothesis actually says — I want you to distinguish at least a strong reading (this is *the* pivotal time, where the long-run future gets locked in) from a weaker one (a time that is merely unusually influential compared with most others). Then map it onto Asilomar. Did the participants in effect believe they were at a hinge? Which of their actions make sense only on a hinge-type view — the moratorium itself, the choice of self-governance over waiting for legislators to catch up, the risk-tiering? And what does the hypothesis say they *should* have been doing that they didn't — worrying less about lab accidents, say, and more about who would end up controlling the technology, or about what a world saturated with cheap gene editing looks like?

Case onto theory. Then run it the other way. On the one hand the episode looks like a win for hinge-talk: a field noticed it might be at a consequential fork, paused, deliberated, and governed itself — pretty much the textbook response. On the other hand the aftermath is awkward. The guidelines got absorbed into ordinary regulation, commercialisation happened regardless, and the episode is now remembered as a template for conferences rather than a turning point in history. Does that show the scientists were never at a real hinge, only a felt one — and how is a hinge-theorist supposed to tell the difference in advance, from the inside? Also take seriously the preparedness-paradox wrinkle: if you navigate a hinge well, afterwards it looks as though there was no hinge at all, so "nothing much changed" can't straightforwardly count as disconfirmation. Say where that leaves the hypothesis's testability, because I worry it leaves it unfalsifiable.

Last thing. The hinge hypothesis is normally deployed as a claim about us, now — AI, mostly. Given that Asilomar-style hinge-claims seem to get made fairly often (biotech in the 70s, nuclear weapons before that, arguably the early internet), what should a reasonable person infer from finding *themselves* convinced they're living at the hinge? Base rates, selection effects, the lot. I'd rather an honest "the episode underdetermines this" than a forced verdict.
