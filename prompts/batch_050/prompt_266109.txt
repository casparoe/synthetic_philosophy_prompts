I'm drafting a realist literary novel and need a piece of philosophical machinery for its central dilemma. Construct for me a variant of Kavka's toxin puzzle — the structure in which it can be rational to form an intention to do something while knowing that, when the moment comes, it will be irrational actually to do it — rebuilt to the following specifications. First, no eccentric billionaire, no toxin, no vat, no mind-reading devices: the reward for merely intending must arise from something a working-class character could plausibly encounter (an insurance clause, a plea arrangement, an employer's loyalty test, a spouse's conditions on reconciliation — your choice, but ordinary). Second, the mechanism that detects the intention must be psychologically credible rather than infallible: a perceptive person or an institutional process, not a perfect scanner, and the character must have good reason to believe they cannot fake the intention. Third, the scenario must fit in one paragraph of novelistic prose. After the scenario, do the analytic work: state what rationality requires at the moment of intending and at the moment of acting, explain why these come apart, identify the single structural feature of your case that carries the load, and tell me what happens to the puzzle if that feature is removed. Finally, note how a philosopher who thinks genuine cases of rational-yet-unperformable intention are impossible would try to dissolve your example, and whether their objection would also ruin it as material for fiction.
