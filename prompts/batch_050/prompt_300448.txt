I am a retired experimental physicist trying to make sense of the reference class problem before I trust any single-case probability outside a casino. Fix the purpose as this: a theory good enough for underwriting, triage, and other irreversible one-off decisions, while still honestly connected to repeatable laboratory frequencies. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of reference-class selection must meet: which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must respect, which mathematical and causal facts it must remain consistent with, and what it must say when every individual belongs to indefinitely many nested and cross-cutting classes.

Use this self-contained case as the test bed. A insurer must price a policy for one particular person: 63, former miner, non-smoker for 9 years, one atypical allele, normal imaging so far, and a job history recorded only in three incompatible coding schemes. The database supports many partitions: age band, exposure band, genotype, occupation, region, calendar era, measurement device, and arbitrary intersections thereof. Some subclasses are empty, some are huge but heterogeneous, some are causally irrelevant, and some are merely artifacts of how records were binned. There is a plausible causal diagram, but one important confounder is unmeasured. There are also near-replicas of the same physical setup in which trials are exchangeable for all practical purposes.

For each desideratum, give the motivating case and say whether it is non-negotiable or revisable. At minimum address: recovery of long-run frequency under genuinely repeated exchangeable trials; refusal to privilege the logically finest nonempty class when it is unlearnable; screening off by causes rather than by bare correlations; tolerance of coarse-graining when fine-graining destroys data; preservation of the difference between objective risk, evidential probability, and decision weight; behavior under intervention versus passive observation; and resistance to gerrymandered classes invented after seeing the outcome. Then identify any pairs of desiderata you think cannot be jointly satisfied, especially uniqueness versus informativeness, causal sufficiency versus observational adequacy, and symmetry versus relevance to total evidence. Briefly score long-run frequency, hypothetical propensity, objective-Bayesian symmetry, and causal-graph accounts against the list, but keep the criteria and their failures at center stage. No citations.
