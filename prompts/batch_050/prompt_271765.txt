I'm a journalist working on a long piece about the professional norms of comparative cognition research, and I'm stuck on the logic of one of them. Here's the norm as I've observed it: a lab can publish a study in which scrub-jays re-hide their caches only when a rival bird was watching, elephants comfort distressed herd-mates, or rats free a trapped cage-mate — but reviewers routinely insist the write-up describe the animals as "behaving as if" they understood or felt something, and a manuscript that uses the same mentalistic vocabulary developmental psychologists freely use for toddlers ("she knew," "he wanted to help") gets sent back for revision. The asymmetry is the interesting part: nearly identical behavior in a two-year-old is reported as genuine belief attribution without protest. What does this editorial practice actually presuppose? That faculties come in a natural hierarchy with "higher" explanations always more extravagant than "lower" ones? That the error of over-attributing mind is costlier than the error of under-attributing it, and if so, on what theory of evidence? Or is it really a bet about evolutionary discontinuity — and does that bet survive the field's own commitment to continuity? Please reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me what would have to change in the journals' instructions to reviewers if the field instead adopted a default of continuity.
