So I spent thirty-odd years as an experimental physicist (optics, mostly) and now that I'm retired I've gotten hooked on philosophy of perception. Two ideas keep showing up in my reading that look to me suspiciously like the same thing in different costumes, and before I mouth off about it at my reading group I want a proper comparison.

Idea one: cognitive penetration of perception. The claim, as I understand it, that what you believe, expect, want, or fear can change what you literally see or hear -- not just how you interpret it. Like the anxious person who sees the shadowy figure as threatening, or the classic stuff about prior knowledge shifting how colors or objects look.

Idea two: the top-down machinery in predictive processing / predictive coding accounts of the brain. The brain is supposedly always sending predictions down the hierarchy, and perception is inference, some people call it "controlled hallucination." Priors all the way down, weighted by how confident the brain is in them.

Here's what I want compared, carefully, in prose first:

1. Are these the same phenomenon described at different levels of description (philosophy-of-mind level vs. computational/neural level), or are they genuinely different claims that could come apart? It seems to me that predictive processing says top-down influence is ubiquitous and mandatory, while cognitive penetration is supposed to be a specific, maybe rarer, maybe optional thing -- but I'm not sure that distinction survives scrutiny.
2. If predictive processing is right, does that automatically vindicate cognitive penetration, or can a PP theorist draw a principled line between "perceptual" priors (learned statistics of the environment, expertise, adaptation) and genuinely "cognitive" states like beliefs, desires, and moods leaking in? What would the line even be -- anatomical? functional? about the content of the states involved?
3. The epistemic worry, which is the part that really interests me: if my beliefs shape my perceptions, and then I take my perceptions as evidence for my beliefs, isn't that a vicious circle? Does predictive processing make this worry better (because precision-weighting might keep priors honest) or much worse (because on that view everything I experience is partly prior-driven, so what is evidence even)?
4. Does it matter for any of this whether the top-down signals carry conceptual content vs. just statistical expectations? I have a hunch this is where the real disagreement lives.

One request about how you pitch the answer: please be explicit about what is genuinely settled in this literature versus what is still contested among people who actually work on it. Don't present one camp's position as the consensus if it isn't -- I've been burned by that in pop treatments of my own field and I don't want the philosophy version.

And after the prose, give me a comparison table with these exact columns: | dimension | cognitive penetration (as philosophers use it) | top-down prediction (predictive processing) | same phenomenon, different, or depends on whom you ask |. Rows for at least: level of description, whether top-down influence is optional or mandatory, epistemic circularity risk, role of conceptual content, and empirical testability. If a cell genuinely has no clean answer, write "contested" rather than forcing one.
