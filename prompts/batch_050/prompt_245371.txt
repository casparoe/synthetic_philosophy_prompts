so i'm a phd student in philosophy (working on aesthetics, broadly) and i'm TAing an undergrad seminar on the philosophy of literature this semester, about 16 students, one 75-minute meeting. we're doing the unit on authorial intention and the meaning of a text, and i've been assigned the opening session on tolstoy. instead of lecturing i want to run an in-class activity built around this passage from chapter V of What is Art? (maude translation):

"Art begins when one person, with the object of joining another or others to himself in one and the same feeling, expresses that feeling by certain external indications. To take the simplest example: a boy, having experienced, let us say, fear on encountering a wolf, relates that encounter; and, in order to evoke in others the feeling he has experienced, describes himself, his condition before the encounter, the surroundings, the wood, his own lightheartedness, and then the wolf’s appearance, its movements, the distance between himself and the wolf, etc. All this, if only the boy when telling the story, again experiences the feelings he had lived through and infects the hearers and compels them to feel what the narrator had experienced, is art."

what i want is a game or structured activity that makes students feel the pull in this passage before anyone names the positions. the passage seems to demand three things at once -- the maker has an aim ("with the object of joining"), the maker actually re-lives the feeling ("again experiences"), and the audience actually catches it ("infects the hearers") -- and those can come apart. so the rough skeleton i have in mind: students get secretly assigned a feeling and have to transmit it in a very short text to readers who have to guess it, and then the scoring rules get changed across rounds so that in one round the writer's intended target is what counts, in another round whatever the readers converge on counts no matter what the writer says, and in a third round the writer is allowed to claim afterward that they intended whatever the readers found. but i'm not wedded to this skeleton if you have a better way to stage it -- the non-negotiable is that students run into the divergence between intended feeling, felt feeling, and received feeling themselves, through the incentives, before i put the words "intentionalism" or "anti-intentionalism" on the board.

give me: full rules for each round, materials (index cards, envelopes, whatever), a points scheme with no money involved and a way to keep the competitive energy up, a minute-by-minute timing plan that fits 75 minutes including setup, and the debrief questions that map specific moments of the game onto tolstoy's three conditions and onto the later debate about whether the author's say-so settles what a text means. also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time i run it (students gaming the points, someone writing something actually upsetting, the guessing collapsing into inside jokes, whatever you foresee) and how to handle each. and be honest about what the game can't show -- e.g. whether real literary interpretation is anything like guessing a target feeling -- and what i have to supply in lecture instead.

lead with the bottom line in your first sentence -- the activity in one sentence -- then give the details, no preamble, no restating my question back at me.
