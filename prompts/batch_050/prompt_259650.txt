Here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases about creating people, and I want to know what principle, if any, is doing the work behind them.

1. A couple is told that any child they conceive this year will be born with a condition causing chronic pain -- manageable, but real and lifelong. In twelve months a cheap fix becomes available, and any child conceived after that would be completely healthy. They don't feel like waiting and conceive now. The kid grows up, has a decent life overall, and is glad to exist. My verdict: the parents did something wrong, even though I cant say the kid was wronged -- she wouldn't have existed at all otherwise, and she prefers existing.

2. My friends would be wonderful parents and any kid of theirs would have a great life. They just don't want children. Verdict: totally fine, not even slightly wrong. Nobody has a duty to manufacture happy people.

3. At an IVF clinic a doctor has two embryos: one would become a person with an excellent life, the other a person with a good but noticeably harder life (say, lifelong deafness). On a whim she implants the second one. Verdict: wrong, and wrong in the same way as case 1, I think.

4. A woman knows that any child she conceives will have a life of near-constant suffering that is nonetheless, by the child's own later judgment, barely worth living. She goes ahead because she wants to be a mother. Verdict: wrong. Note this sits oddly next to case 2: creating a barely-worth-it life is wrong, but failing to create a great life is fine.

5. A tyrant pressures his subjects into having children who will be born into slavery. Their lives are hard but worth living, and none of them would have existed otherwise. Verdict: the tyrant acts wrongly, but again no child was wronged.

6. Parents could get their existing toddler a routine operation preventing a future disability and skip it out of laziness. Verdict: obviously wrong, and this one clearly wrongs the child.

So: is there one principle that delivers all six verdicts? The thing that trips me up is that 1 and 3 look like "when you could have created a better-off person instead, you should have," but that principle makes 2 come out wrong -- my friends are passing up excellent possible people every year they wait -- and it doesn't obviously explain why 4 is wrong while 2 is fine. Meanwhile "you can't do wrong unless someone is wronged" kills 1, 3, and 5 in one stroke. I'd rather not stack up three separate principles if one will do, but I also don't want to abandon a verdict that's actually correct.

So give me your best principle (stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause), tell me what it predicts about a case I'm torn on -- suppose everyone on Earth calmly and freely decides not to have kids, even though the people who would have existed would have had wonderful lives; my gut says that's a real loss, maybe even wrong, but I can't square it with verdict 2 -- and tell me which of my six verdicts the simplest defensible principle would force me to drop.
