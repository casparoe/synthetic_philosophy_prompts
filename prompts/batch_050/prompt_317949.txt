Next term I am running a four-session short course for a reading group of final-year undergraduates in politics and economics -- none of whom has studied moral philosophy formally -- on the question of whether it can be wrong to perform an act whose individual contribution to some large collective harm is vanishingly small. Think of the familiar territory: one person's emissions, one person's ballot, one purchase of a product made under bad conditions. I do not want you to rehearse the debate for me; I know the literature. I want you to plan how I should teach the problem across those four sessions.

Concretely, I would like the following. First, the dependency structure: which distinctions and ideas must be in place before the question can even be posed cleanly, and in what order. I suspect, for instance, that the students need a grip on expected value and on imperceptible harms before any of the standard puzzles bite, but I want to know what else sits upstream and why.

Second, the carrying example. I need one concrete case to anchor the whole course. I am tempted to use voting, since they are politics students, but I suspect it is a poor carrier because the efficacy of a vote turns on the probability of a tie, which muddies the moral question with a merely statistical one. Tell me whether that instinct is right, and if so propose a better case -- one where the collective outcome is genuinely produced by the aggregation of individually negligible acts, where the badness of the outcome is uncontroversial, and where the students cannot wriggle out via uncertainty about the facts. State explicitly what properties the example must have, and which tempting features it must lack.

Third, the misconception map. Name the misunderstandings these students will predictably arrive with or form along the way -- I anticipate at least: that the problem is just the sorites paradox in disguise; that expected-value reasoning dissolves it entirely; that it is really about free-riding in the game-theoretic sense; and that an appeal to thresholds or tipping points settles it -- and indicate, for each, at what point in the four-session sequence it will surface and exactly where in the arc I should head it off rather than let it fester.

Fourth, one question or short exercise I can set at the end of the final session that would show me whether they have grasped the structure of the problem -- that is, whether they could recognise it instantiated in an unfamiliar case -- rather than merely absorbed a menu of positions.

Finally, tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so to the group: the nearby machinery that would swallow the course if admitted but whose absence will not corrupt what remains.
