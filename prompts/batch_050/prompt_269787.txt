Exam question: In his 2012 discussion of AI risk, Holden Karnofsky distinguished between "tool AI" and "agent AI." Explain the distinction, give an example of each, and evaluate Karnofsky's central claim about whether tool AI offers a meaningfully safer development path than agent AI.

Student's answer: Karnofsky's distinction is between AI systems that act on their own initiative and ones that merely respond to instructions. An agent AI models the world, forms plans, and pursues goals across many situations, the way a corporation or a person does; the standard example in the essay is a system that "does things" in pursuit of an objective. A tool AI, by contrast, only produces outputs in response to specific user requests and has no persistent goals of its own. Karnofsky gives Google Maps as a canonical example: it calculates routes when asked but doesn't try to steer civilization toward any destination. Tool AI in this sense is more or less what Bostrom later called an "oracle" — a system confined to answering questions — so the two taxonomies line up. Karnofsky's central claim was that tool AI is the safe development path: because a tool has no goals, it cannot engage in instrumental reasoning like resource acquisition or self-preservation, and so the standard arguments for AI risk apply only to agentic systems. He acknowledged that humans might misuse powerful tools, but treated that as an ordinary misuse problem rather than an alignment problem. It follows that existential-risk concerns about AGI are really concerns about a particular design choice — building agents — and could be sidestepped by keeping advanced systems in the tool category. I find this convincing, since the failure modes people worry about, like wireheading or instrumental convergence, all presuppose that the system is pursuing something.
