Yesterday evening a friend and I spent three hours arguing about original versus derived intentionality, and we ended up talking past each other in a way I found genuinely frustrating. He defends the broadly Searlean line: only minds (or at any rate suitably biological systems) have intrinsic aboutness, and everything an artefact's states are "about" is aboutness on loan from its designers and users. I lean Dennett's way: the aboutness of a thing's states is fixed by the design stance and its selection history — natural or artificial — as in his story of the coin-sorting machine built to accept American quarters which, shipped abroad and re-employed, ends up sorting a foreign coin of the same dimensions, leaving no deeper fact about which coin it was "really" meant to detect all along. We agreed on the standard cases and discovered that each of us found the other's standard cases question-begging. What we never managed was a fresh case we both found hard.

So: construct for me a single thought experiment, engineered to the following specifications, in which the Searlean verdict and the Dennettian verdict about the aboutness of a system's internal states come cleanly apart.

Constraints:
1. Realistic, not science-fictional. The system should be buildable with roughly current technology — an industrial sensor system, an autonomous trading algorithm, an animal-training apparatus, a medical device, or similar. No homunculi in vats, no teletransportation, no Swampman-style cosmic coincidences.
2. It must not be a re-skin of the Chinese Room or of the quarter/balboa machine. In particular, do not make the case turn on a symbol whose interpretation is simply ambiguous between two referents, and do not make it turn on whether the system "really understands" — the question is what, if anything, its states are about, and on whose authority.
3. The divergence must be generated, not stipulated. The two verdicts should fall out of applying each position's own criteria to the described facts; do not write "Searle would say X" into the scenario itself.
4. One scenario, describable in a single paragraph, involving facts an engineer could in principle fully document (design history, training data, deployment context), so that the dispute cannot be dissolved by appeal to ignorance.

After the scenario, do the following:
(a) State each side's verdict and walk through the reasoning that produces it, in terms each side would accept as a fair application of its own view.
(b) Identify the load-bearing feature of the case — the single detail such that, if you removed or reversed it, the two verdicts would converge again — and say which side that feature is doing the damage to.
(c) Give the best counterattack available to the side that comes off worse: how would it deny that the case shows what it appears to show, and does that counterattack concede anything it elsewhere wants to keep?
(d) Finally, tell me honestly whether the case would actually move a committed opponent, or whether it merely restates the dispute in fancier dress — and if the latter, say what a case would need to do to be genuinely probative.

Please write your answer in British English throughout.
