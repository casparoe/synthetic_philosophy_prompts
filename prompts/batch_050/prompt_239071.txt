A professor in my history of science course summed up simultaneous invention this way: “Newton and Leibniz, Darwin and Wallace, and Bell and Elisha Gray show that major discoveries become inevitable once the background conditions are in place: if the famous person had never existed, someone else would have produced essentially the same result within a few years. Priority is therefore mostly a reward for being fast, well connected, or institutionally visible, not for making the knowledge possible. Who discovered it matters to historians, but not to science.”

I suspect thsi is partly right but too sweeping. Audit the summary: which claims are well supported, which confuse the inevitability of a problem being solved with the inevitability of a particular solution, and does the conclusion about priority follow? To make it concrete, use what I’ll call a replaceability horizon: for each case, ask how soon an independently developed equivalent result would plausibly have appeared, and be careful about whether “equivalent” means the same problem, core idea, formalism, evidence, or usable technology. Also consider whether focusing only on famous simultaneous cases is selection bias. No bibliography or citations needed.
