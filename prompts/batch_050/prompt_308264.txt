I'm putting together a problem set for my phil of law seminar on "naked" statistical evidence and want to pressure-test my own intuitions before I inflict them on the students. Here are six cases where the entire case against the defendant is probabilistic:

1. The gatecrasher: 1,000 people attended the rodeo, only 499 paid. We sue one attendee picked at random for the ticket price. (p ≈ .501)
2. The blue bus: the plaintiff was sideswiped by a bus, and the defendant company runs 80% of the buses on that route, with no other distinguishing evidence. (p = .8)
3. Same as 2, except the 80% figure comes from a one-day traffic survey of that exact stretch of road at that exact hour, rather than from the companies' overall route shares. (still p = .8)
4. A DNA cold hit: the crime-scene sample matches the defendant, random-match probability 1 in 10 million, but the defendant was only identified by trawling a database of 2 million profiles.
5. The prison yard: 25 prisoners were in the yard when a guard was killed, exactly one of them did it, and there is literally no other evidence. The state prosecutes prisoner #7. (p = .04)
6. An actuarial model trained on past defendants assigns this defendant a 92% risk of violent reoffense, and the state wants to use it as the sole ground for denying bail.

Two tasks. First, rank the six from most to least defensible as the sole basis for an adverse finding, and do it twice: once under the preponderence standard (civil), once under beyond a reasonable doubt (criminal). Give me the rankings as ordered lists with a 0-10 sufficiency score on each case so I can see where the big gaps are. Second, the interesting part: does your ordering just track the posterior probability, or is it driven by something else -- e.g. whether the statistic is in any sense "about" this defendant, whether the reference class is apt, whether the defendant was in a position to rebut the number? And does switching standards actually reshuffle your order, or does it merely move the cutoff? If your two rankings differ, tell me why; if they come out identical, tell me what that implies about what the two standards are really measuring.
