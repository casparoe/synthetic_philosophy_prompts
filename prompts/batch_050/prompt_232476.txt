I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in formal epistemology — 16 students, one 80-minute session — and this year's topic is Bayesian convergence: consistency results in the spirit of Doob and the merging-of-opinions theorem associated with Blackwell and Dubins. Every cohort makes the same mistake: they can recite that priors wash out, but they treat it as a folk claim about open-mindedness rather than a conditional theorem, and they are genuinely blindsided when they learn that the washing-out fails when its conditions are violated. Design an in-class simulation that makes them feel both halves of this in sequence. In the first phase, participants should be assigned — not allowed to choose — sharply different prior distributions over a small, concrete hypothesis space, then shown a common stream of evidence generated by a process they fully understand, updating and publicly announcing their credences round by round, so the room watches initially enormous disagreements collapse at visibly different rates; include a scoring rule (proper, but requiring no real money) that incentivizes honest reporting. In the second phase, quietly violate at least one convergence condition — for instance, give one player an assigned prior that puts probability zero on the truth, or give one faction a subtly misspecified likelihood model — so that someone fails to converge while reasoning impeccably by their own lights, and the rest of the room cannot diagnose the failure from behavior alone. Specify the rules, roles, and materials; the timeline; how the evidence stream is generated and revealed; the payoff scheme; and the debrief, which should map specific moments of play onto (i) the requirement that the truth not be assigned prior probability zero, (ii) the need for agreement on likelihoods, (iii) the difference between merging of opinions and convergence to the truth, and (iv) the difference between an asymptotic guarantee and any claim about how fast priors wash out in practice. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and be frank about which features of the actual theorems — the almost-surely qualifier, the infinite horizon — a finite classroom game structurally cannot reproduce and I must supply by lecture. The phrases "washing out of priors" and "merging of opinions" and the names of the theorems should appear nowhere in the activity itself; students should meet the phenomenon before its name.
