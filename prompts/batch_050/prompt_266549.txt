so i'm putting together a handout for a philosophy of biology reading group and i want a "where do you actually stand on the definition of life?" decision tree. the problem with every version of this i've seen is that people just pick their favorite label (darwinian, autopoiesis, whatever) without noticing what it commits them to, so i want the tree to sort by verdicts on cases, not by which name sounds good.

Here's the setup. Start from a student who accepts the boring stuff -- life is a natural phenomenon, no souls or vital spirits, and "alive" is supposed to be a real predicate of things, not just a vibe. Then build the tree out of questions where the definitional strategies genuinely come apart, like: does something have to reproduce (or be part of a lineage that does) to count? is metabolism necessary, sufficient, neither? does it matter that the thing maintains itself against entropy specifically, as opposed to just persisting? can a single entity be alive on its own, or does life only exist as a population that evolves? is there even one property here to define, or is it a cluster where different cases hit different subsets? phrase each fork so you can answer it from a gut verdict on a concrete case without knowing which definition you're voting for.

the leaves should land on the actual positions: metabolic/physiological accounts, thermodynamic far-from-equilibrium accounts, darwinian accounts (the NASA-style "self-sustaining chemical system capable of evolution" family), autopoiesis / self-producing-bounded-system accounts, cluster or family-resemblance views, and the eliminativist or pluralist leaf where the question dissolves and different sciences just use different criteria. if two leaves can't actually be separated by case verdicts (i suspect thermodynamic and autopoiesis overlap a lot), flag that instead of pretending the tree works. and if some combination of answers is unoccupied -- like reproduction necessary AND metabolism sufficient -- say whether that's because it's incoherent or just because nobody's bothered to defend it.

then the part i actually care about: test the tree on cases. use these: a virus; a prion; a fire (it metabolizes, grows, spreads, even "reproduces" by throwing sparks); a mule or sterile worker ant (alive but reproductively dead); a dormant bacterial spore that's been doing nothing for 10,000 years; a minimal synthetic cell built from scratch; a self-replicating program evolving in a computer; and earth's whole biosphere treated as one self-regulating system. each of these is supposed to be a fork-separator, and if one of them isn't sharp -- if most definitions just shrug at it -- say that too.

give me the tree itself as indented text or an outline, one fork per line with the question stated, and then the case analysis as a table with this schema: columns are Case, then one column per leaf/strategy giving that strategy's verdict (alive / not alive / genuinely indeterminate under it, one or two words each), and a final column "sharpest test of" saying which fork in your tree this case does the most work for. a row per case, eight rows.

last thing: i might be asking the wrong question. if the whole idea of sorting people by definition is confused -- like if the interesting disagreement was never about the extension of "alive" but about something else, or if these cases can't actually separate the positions -- tell me that straight up rather than building me a pretty tree that doesn't work.
