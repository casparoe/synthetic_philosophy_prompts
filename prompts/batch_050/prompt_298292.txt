I'm prepping a unit on peer disagreement for an epistemology seminar and collecting arguments for the steadfast side -- the view that discovering an epistemic peer disagrees with you doesn't rationally require you to lower your confidence much, if at all. The ones I already have and don't need repeated:

1. the self-defeat charge -- conciliationists disagree with steadfasters about conciliationism itself, so by their own rule they should split the difference about their own view, which nobody does;
2. the total-evidence line -- your first-order evidence doesn't vanish when you learn of the disagreement, it's still there alongside the new higher-order evidence, and responding to the total evidence often justifies keeping most of your confidence;
3. the spinelessness worry -- disagreement is everywhere in philosophy, politics and religion, so conciliating across the board is skepticism by another name;
4. the asymmetry point -- I can check my own reasoning from the inside but only have testimonial access to yours, so treating our judgments as two equally reliable readings double-counts yours;
5. the peerhood problem -- judging that someone is my epistemic peer already requires using the very faculties whose reliability the disagreement calls into question.

Can you give me one more argument for steadfastness that isn't on this list and isn't a rewording, special case, or combo of these five? If there's a real one in the neighborhood of the literature, use it; if not, construct one yourself and say so honestly. Either way, tell me whether you actually think it's any good, and which of the five it's closest to if a conciliationist tried to absorb it.
