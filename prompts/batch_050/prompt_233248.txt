Right, so I spent the better part of six years trading on betting exchanges (mostly horses, a bit of football), and my whole working vocabulary is odds, overround, liability, matched volume, steam moves, sharps versus recreational money, the closing line, and so on. Lately I've been reading around the economics of information and I keep bouncing off the formal papers, so I want help with a translation job.

The passage that's nagging at me is this one, from Hayek's "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945):

"This, however, is emphatically not the economic problem which society faces. And the economic calculus which we have developed to solve this logical problem, though an important step toward the solution of the economic problem of society, does not yet provide an answer to it. The reason for this is that the “data” from which the economic calculus starts are never for the whole society “given” to a single mind which could work out the implications and can never be so given."

Here's what I want. Take three things:

1. Hayek's claim in that passage, plus the argument built on top of it (prices as a communication system for knowledge that nobody holds in full).
2. Aumann's agreement theorem ("Agreeing to Disagree", 1976): common priors plus common knowledge of each other's posteriors means the posteriors have to coincide.
3. The no-trade theorem in the style of Milgrom and Stokey (1982): if the current allocation is already efficient and everyone's rational, no new trade can be common-knowledge acceptable to both sides.

Restate each one in the vocabulary I actually think in: backing and laying, posting an offer as a move in the market, matched bets, balancing a book, sharp money forcing the line, the closing price as the market's best estimate. Do it claim by claim, not as loose analogy. For each piece of the formal machinery — the common prior, common knowledge, the state space, risk-neutrality, agents honestly reporting their posteriors — tell me what its exchange-betting counterpart is, or flag it if there isn't one. Don't smuggle in economics-speak and call it translation; if you need a concept my vocabulary genuinely lacks, say so.

Then the bit I care most about: the residue report. What didn't survive? My suspicions, which you should test rather than humour: the gap between "everyone knows X" and "everyone knows that everyone knows X" seems to collapse into "it's priced in", which flattens the iterated-knowledge levels; the common prior assumption has no obvious counterpart since half the point of a market is that people disagree; and I can't tell what in Hayek is descriptive versus normative once you strip it down. For each orphaned claim, give me a verdict: is my trader's vocabulary missing something real, or was the claim doing less work than it looked?

And one question that should turn directly on the passage. Hayek says the data can never be given to a single mind. In my world that's just "nobody sees everyone's reasoning, only the price" — fine, translates cleanly. But the no-trade results seem to imply that if we all shared a prior and were fully rational, nobody would ever bet, so there'd be no matched volume and no price movements for Hayek's communication system to run on. So which is it: is the exchange I know a counterexample to the theorems, a case of their assumptions failing, or evidence that real punters just aren't the agents being discribed? Translate that tension as well.

Answer in British English please, and don't just nod along if I've got one of the theorems wrong.
