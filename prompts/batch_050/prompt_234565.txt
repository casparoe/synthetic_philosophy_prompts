I am a retired physicist, and in recent years I have joined a small reading group of fellow amateurs working through early modern philosophy. We spent several months on Hobbes, and while the political theory went down smoothly enough, I kept getting snagged on something the group wanted to skip past: Hobbes's geometry. He evidently regarded himself as a first-rate geometer, claimed to have squared the circle, and spent years in a bitter printed dispute with John Wallis, who demolished his proofs with what appears to have been considerable relish. Since I am the only member of the group with mathematical training, I have volunteered to lead a ninety-minute session on this episode, and I would like your help planning how to explain it -- not an explanation itself, which I can supply once the structure is right, but a plan.

Here is what puzzles me as a would-be teacher. The obvious temptation is to present the geometry as an embarrassing sideshow: the great political philosopher had a crank hobby, lost every round, and we may move on. But my reading suggests something more interesting, namely that Hobbes's geometry is of a piece with his philosophy -- his insistence that geometrical objects be understood through the motions of bodies that generate them, his suspicion of infinitesimals and of the new symbolic algebra, his conviction that genuine knowledge requires knowing the causes of what one constructs. So plan the session with that dependency in mind: which piece of Hobbes's general philosophy must be in place before his geometric method becomes intelligible rather than merely wrong, and which parts of his geometry, conversely, illuminate the philosophy. Please tell me the order in which to introduce the components, and the one concrete episode or construction the session should be built around -- I am currently torn between the circle-squaring claims themselves and some less famous passage of the dispute, and would welcome a recommendation on what the carrying example must show and what it must not be allowed to become (I worry the squaring of the circle turns the evening into a comedy about a fool).

Finally, anticipate the misconceptions my audience will bring. I can already foresee a few: that Hobbes was simply mathematically incompetent and his contemporaries knew it, whereas I gather his geometry was heterodox and often wrong but connected to live debates about method; that this is a story about ego and nothing else; and, from the other direction, an overcorrection in which I make Hobbes sound vindicated when he was not. Tell me where in the sequence each of these should be headed off, what I should openly decline to cover in ninety minutes (the fine details of seventeenth-century quadrature techniques, presumably), and one question I can pose at the end to test whether the group has grasped why a serious philosopher's failed mathematics is philosophically informative, rather than merely learned a diverting anecdote.
