I cover health policy for a general audience, and I am writing a piece about how public health systems decide which expensive treatments to fund. The pattern I keep encountering is this. An appraisal institute estimates how much health a new drug buys, measured in quality-adjusted life years, and recommends against funding it if the cost per QALY exceeds a fixed threshold — thereby valuing a QALY the same whoever receives it. Yet the same institute pays a premium for treatments aimed at the most severely ill, so that a QALY gained by a patient with very poor health prospects counts for more, even where the weighting means fewer QALYs are purchased overall. What theory of distributive value does this hybrid practice presuppose? Does the severity premium commit the system to prioritarianism, on which a benefit matters more the worse off its recipient, or does it commit the system to valuing equality in the distribution of health for its own sake? The difference seems important, because only the second view is exposed to the leveling-down objection: if equality of health is valuable in itself, that value could in principle count in favor of withholding benefits from the moderately ill when the severely ill cannot be helped, shrinking the gap while helping no one. Please reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say how the appraisal rules would have to change under the reading you reject. Finally, say what would change your mind: which feature of the practice, or which argument, would lead you to a different verdict, and where your reconstruction is most likely to go wrong.
