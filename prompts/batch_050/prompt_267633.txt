A colleague of mine has a standing habit of explaining how moral norms die, and I want to know whether his pattern is predictable enough that I can guess his take on a case we haven't discussed. I have no academic background in history or philosophy -- this is all table talk -- but his verdicts over the years have been consistent in style, and I can reconstruct five of them with confidence.

His explanations so far: (1) The British abolition of the slave trade succeeded, in his telling, because slavery was becoming less profitable relative to wage labor; the abolitionists collected the credit, but the economics did the work. (2) Dueling among European gentlemen died because it came to seem ridiculous: once respectable men started laughing at it, no law or sermon could have saved it, and none was needed. (3) Public executions and bear-baiting ended because audiences lost their taste for the spectacle; the crowd's stomach turned before anyone's principles did. (4) Witch prosecutions ended because educated people stopped believing witchcraft was real; there the argument genuinely did the work, since nobody's purse or honor was at stake. (5) Child labor in industrial Britain ended when industry no longer needed small hands and schooling became compulsory; the moral outrage, again, trailed the economics.

The undiscussed case: whether rich countries will have largely abandoned factory-farmed meat within a century, and through what mechanism. Predict his answer -- which mechanism he'll name and how confident he'll be. But I'm less interested in the prediction than in the machinery behind it. First, tell me which rule he seems to be running: that norms die when the interests sustaining them die; that norms die when the honor attached to them flips; that norms die when the beliefs underneath them collapse; or a pluralist rule that selects the mechanism according to the kind of norm at stake -- and say which of these fits all five verdicts best, noting any verdict that sits awkwardly with it. Second, identify at least two candidate rules that both fit his track record but diverge on the meat case, state what each predicts there, and give your best guess at his actual verdict with your reasons. Third, name one further historical episode -- real, not among my five -- whose known facts would best discriminate between the two strongest candidate rules. I have in mind the disappearance of foot binding in China within roughly a generation; tell me whether that case does the discriminating work or whether a different one does it better.

Please lead with your prediction of his verdict on the meat case in the first sentence, then give the reasoning -- no preamble, and don't restate my question back to me.
