I'm a novelist at work on a book set in a small fictional country whose civic religion is antiquarian scholarship, and I need a prop document that will appear in full in one chapter. The premise: the market town of my novel was founded, three hundred years earlier, by a semi-legendary figure — one Cornelius Vane, about whom four partial accounts survive: a chronicle written by his great-grandson, a hostile memoir by a rival guildmaster, a fragmentary almanac of sayings, and an official town history compiled a century after his death from sources now lost. In my novel, the town's Authenticity Board, a solemn standing committee of municipal scholars, has spent eleven years applying the criteria of authenticity developed in the quest for the historical Jesus — multiple attestation, embarrassment, dissimilarity, coherence — to every deed and saying attributed to Vane. What I need from you is the Board's final report: the official Determination of Authentic Deeds and Sayings of the Founder, written in flawless bureaucratic deadpan, roughly 700 words.

The humor must come from the criteria themselves, deployed with total procedural confidence. Multiple attestation should founder on the question of whether the town history copied the great-grandson's chronicle or preserves an independent tradition, with the Board splitting the difference in a way that satisfies no one. The criterion of embarrassment should hinge on confident guesses about what would have embarrassed chroniclers long dead, so that an episode survives authentication precisely because it makes the Founder look foolish. Dissimilarity should be applied with literal-minded rigor, certifying as authentic only those sayings that resemble nothing the Founder's contemporaries believed and nothing his later admirers wished he had said — thereby producing a Founder with no context whatsoever, a man the Board itself concedes could not have existed, before certifying him anyway. Coherence should be used to rescue a favorite episode that failed every other test, and the report should note, in an unbothered footnote, that the criteria have returned contradictory verdicts on the Founder's most famous saying, both of which are recorded as final. A reader who knows the third-quest literature should recognize real, standard criticisms of criteria-based scholarship being enacted rather than stated; a reader who doesn't should simply enjoy a committee very sure of itself.

One firm requirement: commit. I do not want a report that hedges or an irony so even-handed that it could be read as affection for either side of the scholarly dispute. The Board must issue a definite, itemized verdict — this deed authentic, this saying inauthentic, this one authentic but embarrassing — and the satire must take a clear position on whether criteria of this kind can ever settle who a figure of the past really was. It should land on the skeptical side, though the Board must never notice that it has.
