Last week a friend and I spent an entire evening arguing about nuclear deterrence, and we ground to a halt on a question neither of us could dissolve. I would like your actual credence on it, with the reasoning decomposed rather than gestured at.

The setup is a thought experiment, but one constructed out of standard deterrence logic. Suppose a nuclear-armed state, worried that its threats of retaliation are not credible, adopts the following policy: it builds a second-strike system that is (a) fully automatic, meaning that once armed it retaliates upon detection of a nuclear attack on the homeland, with no possibility of human countermand; (b) publicly announced and credibly demonstrated to adversaries, since a doomsday machine nobody knows about deters nothing; and (c) engineered with the best available safeguards against false alarms. My friend argued that this is the obvious optimum. Deterrence fails, on the standard account, because both sides know that retaliating after deterrence has collapsed serves no purpose -- the homeland is already destroyed -- and so a rational threatener's own rationality hollows out the threat. Removing the human solves the commitment problem at a stroke: the adversary must now reckon with a certainty, not a bluff. I argued that the same logic cuts the other way. Every residual false-positive risk becomes irreversible; there is no longer anyone who can be bluffed into mercy; and a rational adversary in a crisis, knowing the machine cannot be talked down, may be pushed toward preemption precisely because it cannot gamble on restraint at the margin.

The proposition I want your number on: a state that installs such a system thereby lowers the overall probability that a nuclear war involving it ever occurs, relative to retaining human-in-the-loop second-strike forces of equivalent destructive capacity.

Since this is a counterfactual about an unrealized policy, treat resolution as follows: the proposition is true if, given the strategic logic and the empirical record on near-misses and crisis behavior that we actually have, the risk-reduction from enhanced credibility outweighs the risk-increase from automation and adversary reaction. Decompose your credence accordingly. At minimum I want separate rough probabilities for: how much the added credibility actually reduces the chance that an adversary tests the threat (whether by first use, salami tactics, or coercion at the brink), given that human-in-the-loop threats were already partially credible; how much removing human judgment raises the risk of retaliation triggered by accident or misread signals, given the historical rate of false alarms and their past containment; how the automatic system shifts adversary incentives in a crisis, on net toward caution or toward preemption; and whether the commitment problem is even the binding constraint in real deterrence, or whether states already manage credibility well enough that the machine solves a non-problem. Give a number and one line of justification for each, combine them into your final credence, identify which sub-question your total is most sensitive to, and tell me what evidence or argument would move that term most. Finally, say which of the two of us -- my friend or me -- your decomposition vindicates, and where each of us is most likely wrong.
