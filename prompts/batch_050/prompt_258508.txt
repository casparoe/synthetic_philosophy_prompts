I witnessed an argument last week that I was asked to referee and could not settle, and I would like a proper adjudication of it. Two friends of mine, both graduate students in philosophy, disagreed about what Singer's drowning-child case actually commits us to. Here is the exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

Leon's opening claim was that the pond case establishes a fully general duty. If you would wade into a shallow pond to save a drowning child at the cost of ruining a two-hundred-dollar pair of shoes, then declining to spend an equivalent sum on an effective charity, where it can be expected to save a life, is morally on a par with walking past. Physical distance, national boundaries, and the number of other people who could equally well help are, he said, morally irrelevant features that merely feel relevant.

Marta replied that the analogy fails in three places. First, the pond presents an identifiable victim and a uniquely positioned rescuer, and it is that combination which triggers a duty of rescue: a duty to answer an emergency one is actually confronted with. Second, global poverty is a standing condition rather than an emergency, and obligations toward standing conditions are properly discharged through institutions, not through personal case-by-case rescue. Third, where millions are equally able to help, the duty is shared; it does not replicate itself in full for each bystander.

Leon's rejoinder: identifiability is morally arbitrary, since a statistical life is a life, and in any case modern charities will tell you quite specifically what your money does. Calling poverty standing rather than acute is a redescription, not a distinction: the children it kills die acutely, of preventable causes, on particular days. And the failure of others does not reduce what morality asks of you; at most it reduces your culpability relative to theirs. If nine bystanders refuse to wade in, your duty to wade in is not a tenth of what it was. It is unchanged.

Marta then argued that Leon's underlying principle — prevent serious badness whenever you can do so at a cost that is not comparably significant — has no stopping point. It demands giving until the giver approaches the margin of comparability, which amounts to a lifelong schedule of near-maximal sacrifice. The pond case draws its intuitive force precisely from being a bounded, one-off cost; using that intuition to license unlimited demands is, she said, a bait and switch.

Leon answered that demandingness is an objection to morality, not to his argument — perhaps morality simply is demanding. Moreover, the principle contains its own limit in the comparability clause: one's own agency, projects, and continued capacity to give plausibly count as comparably significant, so the duty does not in fact run to destitution. He added that Marta's institutional point is an empirical wager rather than a principle: where institutions demonstrably fail, the duty reverts to whoever can act.

Marta's final move was to draw an asymmetry between the omissions. In the pond case, your inaction uniquely determines that this child dies; you are, by omission, the operative factor at the crucial moment. In the global case your inaction is one omission among billions and settles nothing by itself. Omissions that uniquely determine an outcome, she claimed, carry a different moral weight from omissions that do not.

Please adjudicate the exchange point by point: who has the better of the dispute about identifiability and statistical lives; about the emergency-versus-standing-condition distinction; about the diffusion of responsibility among many bystanders; about whether the comparability clause genuinely supplies a stopping point; and about Marta's final asymmetry between uniquely determining and non-determining omissions. Note anything important that both parties are missing, and then say who is more right overall, with reasons.
