I'm a journalist who covers science and philosophy of mind for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a feature about panpsychism -- the idea that consciousness is fundamental and ubiquitous in nature, present even in basic physical stuff. My editor wants me to go deeper than the usual "are electrons conscious??" treatment, so I want to structure the piece around the combination problem: even if you grant that fundamental particles have tiny experiential states, how do those micro-experiences add up (or fuse, or whatever) into the unified rich consciousness that you and I have? My plan is to give readers a checklist of what any proposed solution has to actually accomplish, so they can judge the proposals in the literature for themselves rather than just taking my word for which one wins.

So: write me the desiderata that a solution to the combination problem must meet to count as adequate. For each one, give the consideration or case that motivates it -- e.g., why a solution has to explain the subject-summing of subjects and not just the aggregation of phenomenal qualities, why it can't simply help itself to brute psychophysical laws connecting micro-experiences to macro-experiences (or can it? is the "no brute laws" demand a genuine constraint or just a prejudice of people who want panpsychism to be more explanatory than rival views?), what it has to say about where my consciousness is located given that my brain is full of overlapping systems of parts, and so on. Distinguish which requirements are genuine non-negotiables that any theory of consciousness combining parts into wholes would face, versus which are internal demands specific to the panpsychist project that a physicalist wouldn't accept. And this is the part I most want for the piece: if some subset of the desiderata looks jointly unsatisfiable -- say, deriving macro-subjects from micro-subjects without remainder while keeping the micro-subjects real and the derivation non-mysterious -- say so explicitly, since that would basically be an argument that the problem is a dead end rather than a puzzle.

Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivations rather than on grading the existing contenders, but if it's quick, a few sentences on how the main families do against the list would help me plan the article's structure -- the view that micro-subjects literally fuse into a bigger subject, the view that only qualities combine while the subject was never composite, the cosmopsychist move that flips the direction and starts from one big cosmic subject, and the phenomenal bonding approach that posits a new fundamental relation. One typo'd caveat: I'm not a philosopher, so if any of the desiderata I've gestured at above are confused or double-counting each other, untangle them rather than just running with my framing.
