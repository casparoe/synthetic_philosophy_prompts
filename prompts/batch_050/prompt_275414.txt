I'm giving a talk at a small alignment workshop in a few weeks and I want to walk in with the Q&A already mapped. The room is mixed: maybe ten people who do empirical ML safety work (interpretability, RLHF, evals), three or four theory people who know the classic corrigibility agenda backwards, one proper decision theorist, and a couple of philosophers of action who mostly publish on intention and diachronic rationality and don't care about AI.

My thesis, roughly: the shutdown problem is usually framed as "how do we build an agent that won't resist being turned off," but that's the wrong target. Shutdown resistance is downstream of the agent's stance toward its own future behavior. Any agent with long-range goals that can model its future selves will find shutting down on request instrumentally bad unless its incentives are arranged just so, and the incentive fixes on the table (indifference over the off-switch event, penalizing interference with the button, making reward conditional on not manipulating the operator) all fail the same way once the agent can modify, copy, or re-derive its own objectives, because the fix is part of the objective, and the objective is exactly what's up for grabs when the agent reflects. So my positive claim: corrigibility isn't a property of a policy or a reward function, it's an ongoing relation between the agent and whoever holds the switch, and it has to be actively maintained the way trust does. Wich means the engineering question shifts from "get the payoff structure right" to "keep the agent genuinely uncertain about whether its current goals are worth keeping," and that's an uglier problem with no clean decision-theoretic statement I can point to.
