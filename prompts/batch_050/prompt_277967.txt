I'm in a phil of mind seminar and we just read William James's 1884 paper "What is an Emotion?" for the emotions unit. The bit that got me is where he writes: "Without the bodily states following on the perception, the latter would be purely cognitive in form, pale, colourless, destitute of emotional warmth. We might then see the bear, and judge it best to run, receive the insult and deem it right to strike, but we could not actually feel afraid or angry."

So his claim is basically: no felt bodily change, no emotion -- the emotion just IS the feeling of the body reacting. My credence that he's right about this (at least for what he calls the "standard emotions" like fear, anger, grief) is only about 0.25, but my roommate in the class is at like 0.85 and we keep talking past each other. Give me your own number, and more importantly decompose it: how much rides on whether felt bodily changes are strictly *necessary* for emotion (what about people with spinal cord injuries or on beta blockers -- do they really feel less?), whether bodily profiles are rich enough to tell emotions *apart* (the old fear-vs-anger-same-physiology objection), whether a cold evaluative judgment really can't count as an emotion on its own, and whether James hedging by limiting himself to "standard emotions" is a fair move or a retreat. Number per sub-question, how they combine, and tell me which one my roommate and I most likely actually disagree about. Also what empirical finding would move your final number the most?
