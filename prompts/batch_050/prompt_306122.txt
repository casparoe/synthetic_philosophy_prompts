I'm in a philosophy of religion seminar and last week we did divine hiddenness -- basically Schellenberg's argument that if a perfectly loving God existed, he'd make sure that anyone capable of a relationship with him at least believed he existed, so the fact that plenty of sincere, open people just... don't believe is itself evidence against God. My friend joked that the whole debate reads like a customer complaint that's been sitting in the support queue for millenia, and now I can't unsee it.

So: write me the official support page. A "Frequently Asked Questions" knowledge-base article from the manufacturer of the universe, in that relentlessly cheerful corporate FAQ voice, responding to the most common ticket: users report they searched for the admin and found nothing. Each answer has to be a real hiddenness response translated into support-speak, and the accuracy of the translation is where the humor lives. So the free will response becomes a note about why revealing the admin directly would void users' ability to meaningfully choose the product; soul-making theodicy becomes a statement that the current configuration is optimized for character development and resilience; skeptical theism becomes something like "some users report issues that our engineers have confirmed are working as intended, though the reasons exceed user-level permissions"; and somewhere in there the claim that the light levels are deliberately calibrated so that users who want to find the admin can, and users who don't aren't forced to (Pascal's point, but don't quote him or name him). Include a "troubleshooting steps" section (step one: sincerely desire a relationship with support) and a status note that this ticket has been open for several thousand years and is currently marked resolved.

Around 500-600 words. Stay deadpan the whole way through -- no winking, no overt editorializing, the FAQ writer genuinely believes they've closed the ticket. A philosopher of religion should be able to read each answer and name the theodicy being paraphrased, and a skeptic should find each one quietly damning, without the text ever saying so. Ideally by the last FAQ entry it should be genuinely ambiguous whether the page has answered the complaint or just confirmed it, because that's roughly where the actual literature ends up anyway.
