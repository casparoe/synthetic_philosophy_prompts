I'm on the allocation committee of a mid-sized foundation (~$40M to move each year across global health, biosecurity, and a couple of smaller portfolios). Our standing rule is basically: fund whatever has the best marginal cost-effectiveness, with a strong thumb on the scale for neglected stuff, on the theory that our dollar goes furthest where fewest other funders are looking.

One of our sharper analysts has blown this up. Her memo argues the neglectedness heuristic rests on treating our decision as if it were the only one being made, when actually there are dozens of similarly-sized funders running the same playbook. If we all converge on "most neglected cause X" the moment it looks best, our choosing X is evidence that they're choosing X too, so the true expected effect of our grant is way different from the naive calculation -- the cause gets flooded, the marginal dollar becomes mediocre, and the "neglected" thing we should've wanted everyone to fund might be the second-best-looking option nobody coordinated on. She wants us to switch to what she calls a coordination-adjusted framework: allocate as if we're picking the portfolio we'd want all similar funders to jointly pick, not the one that maximizes our counterfactual impact alone. Two other committee members think this is galaxy-brained nonsense that will paralyze us, since we can't actually observe or negotiate with these other funders and the whole thing smells like it proves too much (by her logic, why give at all rather than assume others cover it?).

Draft the written opinion I'll circulate before our quarterly meeting. It needs an actual bottom line -- adopt her framework, reject it, or adopt some limited version of it -- plus criteria our program officers could genuinely apply when scoring grants, and a fair statement of the strongest point on whichever side loses. Keep it under three pages; nobody reads more than that.
