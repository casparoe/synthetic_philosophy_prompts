I've been going around in circles on a question that sits underneath a lot of the probability-and-induction discussions on rationalist forums: can a finite sequence be random, or is randomness only ever a property of the process that generated it? I have a tentative principle and a set of case verdicts, and I'd like you to do the reflective-equilibrium work of figuring out what principle, if any, actually captures my intuitions -- and which of my verdicts has to go if none does.

My tentative principle: a finite string is random relative to a given background of knowledge if and only if no description of the string is available, from that background, that is substantially shorter than the string itself. In other words, randomness is incompressibility, indexed to what the evaluator knows or could feasibly compute.

Here are my verdicts:

1. A fair coin is flipped twenty times and comes up heads every time. That particular string is not a random string, even though it was produced by a random process and had the same probability as any other length-20 string.

2. The first 1,000 binary digits of pi, presented to me without attribution, pass every statistical test I'd bother to run. They are nonetheless not random, because a short program generates them.

3. A thousand bits produced by a standard pseudorandom number generator seeded with a 32-bit seed are not random -- even to an observer who doesn't know the seed -- because the effective description length is about 32 bits plus the algorithm. An observer who can't invert the generator is merely ignorant of the compressibility; the compressibility is there regardless.

4. A quantum random number generator produces 1,000 bits, and by sheer coincidence those bits are identical to the first 1,000 binary digits of pi. Here I balk: I want to say this string IS random, because nothing in its causal history exploits the short description. But this verdict contradicts my verdict in case 2, since the strings are literally the same object. I notice I'm confused about whether randomness attaches to the string, the process, or the pair.

5. A 500-bit string for which no program shorter than about 480 bits exists is random, full stop. This is my paradigm case and the one I'm most confident in.

6. Winning lottery numbers of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 feel like a scandal and prompt audits; a jumbled-looking sequence with the same probability does not. My verdict: the audit instinct is correct even granting equal probability, because the patterned outcome is evidence for a hypothesis (a rigged or simple generating process) with non-negligible prior mass, and the jumbled one isn't. Randomness-talk here is really doing model-selection work.

7. I flip a fair coin 1,000 times but secretly discard and reflip any outcome I dislike, keeping only the flips that survive my filter. The resulting record is not random even if it passes statistical tests, because the generation procedure had a selection step. Note the tension with case 4: there I wanted process history to confer randomness; here I want process history to remove it. Maybe those are consistent -- history matters in both -- but then case 2's identical string gets different treatment depending on provenance, which my tentative principle forbids.

What I'd like from you: First, state precisely a principle (or two or three candidates) that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, indicating which case motivates each clause. Second, tell me which verdicts, if any, must be abandoned under each candidate, and be honest if my most confident verdict (case 5) forces me to give up more than I'd like elsewhere. Third, apply your preferred principle to cases I haven't listed: the decimal digits of Champernowne's constant (statistically normal but trivially computable); a string generated by a fair coin whose outcomes I genuinely cannot distinguish from a pattern I have a hunch about but can't articulate; and the complete 10^6-bit record of a roulette wheel at a casino I trust, versus the bit-identical record produced by a simulation. Finally, if the upshot is that "random" is systematically ambiguous between a process sense, a string sense, and an epistemic sense, say which sense deserves to keep the unqualified word and which should be relabeled.
