Bit of a money-pump puzzler I've been chewing on with a mate from my old reading group (we met through LessWrong meetups years back, so she's got all the usual kit — Dutch books, cyclic preferences, resolute choice, the works). We keep a running list of cases and I genuinly can't predict her verdict on the latest one, so I want outside help before I embarrass myself.

Her verdicts so far:

1. Textbook pump. Cyclic strict preferences over three books, pays 50p a swap at a market stall, ends up holding the book she started with, minus £1.50. Verdict: irrational, obviously.
2. Same cycle, same payments, but the woman loves haggling and treats the 50p as the price of an enjoyable afternoon. Verdict: totally fine — she's buying the swapping, not the book.
3. Allais-style choices violating the independence axiom, fully transitive, no pump available. Verdict: fine; the expected utility axioms aren't scripture.
4. Someone who knows her future self's tastes will have reversed (she'll come to love the country life she currently dreads) and plans around that fact now. Verdict: fine; that sort of diachronic mismatch isn't irrationality, it's just being a person spread across time.
5. A spectrum case: strict pairwise preferences along a chain where each step differs by a barely-noticeable amount (shades of paint, say), producing intransitivity across the whole set. Verdict: no irrationality — preferences are grainy, that's life — so long as she isn't actually haemorrhaging anything.

Now the new case. Another friend of ours, Mina: a genuine, coarse-grained cycle over three flats — Hackney over Peckham, Peckham over Bermondsey, Bermondsey over Hackney — and the differences are real and sizeable, nothing barely-noticeable about them. She knows about the cycle, doesn't enjoy flat-swapping in the slightest, her tastes are stable over time, and she's adopted a hard policy of never swapping once she's picked one. Going by her track record, she'll stick to it. So she can't be pumped, and she knows it.

Predict her verdict on Mina, tell me which rule you reckon she's actually been running across all five — 'intransitive preferences are irrational in themselves', 'only what you actually do and lose matters', or something with a negligible-differences clause baked in — and say why the runner-up doesn't fit the record as well. Then describe the one further case whose verdict would settle it between your top two.
