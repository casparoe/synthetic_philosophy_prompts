Some background: I'm a retired field biologist (forty years on freshwater invertebrates, if anyone's curious) who has recently fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole, and I also volunteer on my county's animal welfare advisory panel. We review maybe a dozen cases a year — proposed ordinances, shelter policies, complaints about how people treat animals, occasionally a research facility wanting a permit. We have no agreed framework at all, it's basically whoever argues loudest.

So I picked up Kant's Metaphysics of Morals, specifically the bit near the end of the Doctrine of Virtue where he gets to animals. As I understand the passage (this is my paraphrase, I'm working from a translation), Kant says we have no duties *to* animals at all, strictly speaking, because they aren't persons — our duties regarding animals are really duties to ourselves and to humanity. His reasoning, as I read it: an animal is analogous to a human being in that it can suffer, so when someone is cruel to an animal they weaken and gradually root out in themselves a natural feeling — sympathetic participation in suffering — that is very serviceable to morality in our dealings with other people. Hence the person who is cruel to animals becomes hard in his treatment of human beings. He gives the example of someone who shoots his old dog because the dog can no longer earn its keep, and he also says vivisectionists who carve up living animals are excused when they do it for a purpose that serves humanity, but the cruelty is condemnable when it's done for mere sport or speculation without a real end.

Here's my request. I want you to turn Kant's doctrine in this passage into an actual working procedure my panel could run a case through — a checklist or sequence of questions, in order, that someone with no philosophy background could follow. Specifically:

1. The questions in order, and for each one what would count as a sufficient answer under Kant's framework (e.g., presumably the first question is NOT "does the animal suffer" but something about what the act does to the agent's moral character — tell me if I'm wrong about that).
2. The places where the procedure has to stop and hand things to a human being's judgment because Kant's framework genuinely doesn't settle it.
3. Where Kant's procedure would diverge from the obvious alternative (judging the act by the animal's suffering directly), because my fellow panelists will all be instinctive animal-welfare types and I want to be able to tell them in advance exactly where the Kantian procedure parts company with their gut.
4. Run the procedure on three cases: (a) a resident who had his healthy but aging and arthritic labrador put down because it could no longer hunt; (b) a pest-control company that uses slow poison on rats when faster methods cost slightly more; (c) a university lab doing painful research on live animals with a genuine medical aim but sloppy protocols. I have a feeling the procedure gives at least one answer my panel won't like — possibly on (c) — and I'd rather hear it straight.
5. Finally, be honest about what the procedure *can't* do: where Kant's empirical assumption (that cruelty to animals brutalizes us toward people) is doing all the work, and what happens to the whole framework if that assumption is empirically shaky, since as a scientist that's the first thing I wondered.

One more thing that genuinely puzzles me about the passage and that the procedure will need a ruling on: Kant seems to rest a lot on the *analogy* between animal suffering and human suffering. But if the analogy is what makes the cruel act matter morally, doesn't that smuggle in the idea that the animal's suffering matters in itself after all? If it matters, build me a version of the procedure that keeps Kant's indirect-duty structure but flags every point where it covertly relies on direct concern for the animal. If that's just a misreading on my part, explain why before you build anything.

Keep it practical — two of my fellow panelists are a dog groomer and a retired cop, not scholars, so the procedure itself should be in plain language even if the justifcation around it isn't.
