A colleague of mine in the ancient department keeps reaching for Plato's own image from the end of the Meno when she teaches the value-of-knowledge question: true beliefs, she tells the class, are like the statues of Daedalus -- beautiful, but they wander off unless you tie them down, and knowledge is just true belief with a tether on it. She then uses this to argue that the whole post-Gettier industry is overkill: the only thing knowledge adds to true belief is stability, i.e. not being talked out of it by the next slick speaker, so justification, warrant, reliabilism and the rest are answers to a question nobody needed to ask. I want to push back at our joint seminar and I'd like an audit of the analogy first. Give me the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the statue, the tether, the running away, the person doing the tying -- and then where it breaks. Two places I'm suspicious about: whether "staying put under questioning" is really what we want from knowledge or whether that's a feature of the believer rather than the belief, and whether the analogy can even make sense of Gettier cases, where the belief never runs away but still isn't knowledge. Then tell me whether her deflationary conclusion survives once the disanalogies are priced in, or whether the statue picture was doing all the work. If there's a better analogy for what knowledge adds, offer one.
