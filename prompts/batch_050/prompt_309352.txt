I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm preparing a feature on the puzzle of why anyone should bother with actions that make no causal difference—voting in mass elections, donating to vast relief funds, cutting one's own emissions. I'd like a fictional companion piece to run alongside it. Write a short story, roughly 800–1,000 words, about a protagonist who acts on evidential rather than causal reasoning: she cooperates not because her own choice changes anything, but because she takes it as evidence about the choices of the thousands of psychologically similar people facing the identical decision. Give her a foil—perhaps a friend or spouse—who presses the causal objection: your individual act accomplishes nothing, so the rest is superstition dressed as logic. The story should let both positions breathe. Include at least one scene where evidential reasoning leads somewhere genuinely unsettling or counterintuitive (for instance, cooperating in a one-shot encounter with a stranger purely on the grounds that someone that similar to her is probably making the same call), and end on an outcome that leaves open whether the protagonist was rational or merely lucky. Please keep the story free of jargon: no character should ever use phrases like decision theory or expected value, even though the piece should be intelligible to a reader who knows exactly which debate it dramatizes.
