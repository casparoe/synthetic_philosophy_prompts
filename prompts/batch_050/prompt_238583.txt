After a career in experimental physics I have been reading philosophy of mathematics in retirement, and I keep returning to the axiom of choice. I understand its standard equivalents and some of its unsettling consequences — the Banach–Tarski decomposition, non-measurable sets, the well-ordering of the reals — and I know it is independent of ZF. What I cannot get a grip on is how a thoughtful person should distribute confidence about it. So: what is your credence that the axiom of choice is true — not merely consistent or fruitful, but correct as a claim about sets? If you think the question lacks a determinate answer, say so, but treat that as part of the problem rather than a way of declining it. Decompose the credence into the sub-questions it rests on — for instance, the probability that there is a single intended universe of sets about which the axiom has a fact of the matter, as opposed to a multiverse or formalist picture on which it is true in some structures and false in others; conditional on there being such a universe, the probability that choice holds in it; and any further terms you think belong, such as whether truth is even the right standard here or whether fruitfulness and explanatory role should substitute. Give a number and one line of justification for each term, then the combination, and tell me which term the final figure is most sensitive to and what argument or mathematical result would move it. Finally, mark clearly which parts of your answer reflect settled mathematics and which are genuinely contested among set theorists and philosophers — I do not want a contested position presented as the consensus view.
