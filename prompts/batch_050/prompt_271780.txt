I sit on the three-person integrity panel of a regional charity lottery, and about twice a year a drawing produces something the public calls impossible, at which point we have to decide whether to order a machine inspection, void the draw, or publish an explanation and move on. Over the years I have developed strong gut verdicts, but I cannot articulate the principle behind them, and I would like one, because the newest panel member keeps catching me applying what look like double standards. My verdicts: when the machine drew 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 I voted for a full inspection, even though I know that combination has exactly the same probability as any other. When it drew 3, 11, 19, 27, 36, 44 I voted to let it stand without a second thought. When a vendor proposed replacing the physical draw machine with successive digits of pi starting from a secret offset, I called it unacceptable, even though I could not explain how any bettor could exploit it; and yet when the same vendor demonstrated a quantum random number generator, I accepted it, and then when the quantum device produced forty zeros in a row on a test run I said I would send the unit back, despite genuinely believing that quantum outcomes are undetermined. Finally, in an exercise we ran internally, a colleague showed me two strings of a hundred coin-flip outcomes, one recorded from an actual coin and one invented by an intern told to make it look random; I picked out the intern's correctly, on the grounds that it alternated too much and had no long runs. I called the intern's string non-random and the coin's string random, while admitting that had the coin happened to produce the intern's exact string, I would have said the reverse.

What I want is the principle, or small set of candidate principles, that best systematizes these verdicts. I know the literature offers several notions -- randomness as incompressibility, randomness as typicality with respect to a hypothesis class or a battery of statistical tests, randomness as a property of the generating process rather than the output, and randomness as fundamentally epistemic -- but I do not know which combination delivers my judgments, or which of my judgments no coherent principle can deliver. Please state your best candidate precisely, with the clause of the principle that each of my verdicts motivates; tell me which of my verdicts you think I have to abandon and why (I suspect the pi verdict and the forty-zeros verdict are in tension, but I cannot make the tension precise); and give the principle's verdict on cases I have not listed, including at least: a string that is highly compressible but verifiably produced by a certified quantum device, and a lottery that uses digits of pi whose normality is assumed rather than established. Finally, throughout, please separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on algorithmic randomness from what remains contested -- for instance, whether the randomness of a finite sequence is even a well-defined notion rather than a loose borrowing from the infinite case, how much the incompressibility account depends on an arbitrary choice of reference machine, and which of the empirical claims I have leaned on (about pi, say) are conjecture rather than theorem. I would rather be told that some of my intuitions are irreconcilable than be handed a tidy theory that papers over it.
