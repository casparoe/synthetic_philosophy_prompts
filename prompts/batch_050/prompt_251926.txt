Next semester I am introducing Dennett's intentional stance in my first-year philosophy of mind module, and I want to use the framework to reach the question my students actually care about: when an AI chatbot tells us it believes the meeting is on Thursday, does it believe anything at all? I have one fifty-minute session. I am not asking you to explain the intentional stance to me; I am asking you to plan how I should teach it.

I would like the plan to cover the following, in whatever order your analysis dictates.

First, the dependency structure. The session's components are the three stances (physical, design, intentional), the assumption of rationality that licenses the move to the intentional stance, the idea of a real pattern, and the realism-versus-instrumentalism question about the beliefs thereby attributed. Which of these must come before which, and which is genuinely load-bearing for the AI case versus safely deferred to a later week? I suspect students cannot evaluate the chatbot case until they see that attribution is a predictive strategy constrained by a rationality assumption, not an act of inner detection, but tell me if I have the dependency wrong.

Second, the carrying example. I intend to build the session around a chess engine that sacrifices a piece: a case where the design stance is computationally hopeless and the intentional stance is the only tractable one. Defend or replace this choice, and in particular tell me why the thermostat and the vending machine, the usual openers, would mislead first-years here. The example needs to make the stance feel forced on us rather than optional, while leaving room for doubt about whether anything is really believed.

Third, the misconception map. I expect at least these three: that the intentional stance is a respectable name for anthropomorphism; that Dennett must think beliefs are fictions, since their reality is a matter of predictive usefulness; and that whatever is best predicted intentionally therefore has beliefs, which would licence attributing them to rivers and thermostats. For each, tell me the point in the sequence at which I should head it off, and what the chatbot case does to it — I take it the third misconception is precisely where the AI question bites.

Fourth, the check for understanding: one question I can pose in the final ten minutes whose answer would show the students have grasped that belief attribution, on this view, is constrained but not conventional — rather than merely learned to say so.

Finally, tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, and say so in the session.
