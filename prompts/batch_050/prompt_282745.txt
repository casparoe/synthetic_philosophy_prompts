I am a novelist, and across several books I have sketched imaginary civilizations that my readers have learned to read as verdicts: some of my worlds are plainly utopias, others plainly warnings. The pattern so far, as best I can state it, is this. A world of eight billion people living decent, unremarkable lives I have portrayed as preferable to a world of a few million in ecstasy. A world of ten-to-the-thirtieth people, each with a life barely worth living — enough small comfort to make existence better than nothing, and no more — I have portrayed as a horror by comparison with our own modest scale. And in one novel I introduced, midway through, a new generation whose lives were clearly good though slightly below the standard of the existing population, at no cost to anyone already alive, and the narrative treated their arrival as a gain.

I am now planning a new book, and I would like to remain consistent with the writer my readers take me to be. The new world: several billion people living modest, quietly contented lives — real but shallow pleasures, no deep loves, accomplishments, or griefs, lives clearly worth living yet short of anything one would call good — alongside a few million who live lives of genuine excellence.

Which way does my track record lean: triumph, tragedy, or something in between? Please set out the candidate principles about the value of populations that fit my earlier verdicts, say what each predicts for this new world (I suspect at least one principle condemns it while another commends it), give your best guess as to which principle I am implicitly writing under and why, and describe the one further fictional world whose verdict would settle the matter.
