I am setting the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on free will and philosophy of mind, and I need one essay question built around the Sphex case that cannot be answered by retelling the usual story. Students know the standard material well: the Sphex ichneumon wasp that mechanically re-runs its nest-inspection routine no matter how many times the experimenter moves the paralyzed cricket, Dennett's use of it to raise the worry that humans might be "sphexish" in the same way, and the regress problem that our flexible reasoning about our own reasons could itself be mechanically generated.

To force the crux, the question should include the following original scenario of my own devising, which I want you to quote in full as the prompt's setup: An engineer builds two warehouse robots. ROUTE-1 is hard-coded: if a box it is carrying is displaced from its grip, it returns to a fixed docking point and restarts its entire delivery sequence from the beginning, no matter how often this happens. ROUTE-2 has a learning system: after repeated disruptions it re-plans, invents new paths, and eventually devises strategies its designers never specified, such as negotiating with other robots to clear corridors. However, a systems audit reveals that ROUTE-2's flexibility is produced by a fully deterministic, fully inspectable optimization process: every "novel" strategy was the unique output of its inputs and its training history, just as ROUTE-1's restarts are the unique outputs of its inputs. The question should ask students whether the audit shows ROUTE-2 to be sphexish after all, and to use the case to identify exactly what, beyond mere flexibility or unpredictability, the Sphex case is supposed to be a threat to -- and whether anything in the human case supplies that further ingredient, or whether the Sphex argument actually proves too much or too little.

Please deliver: (1) the exam question itself, roughly 120-180 words including the scenario, stating the mark weighting (40 marks, 75 minutes, closed book) and instructing students to engage with the regress problem; (2) a model answer of about 600 words written at the level of a strong final-year student, which must take a definite position rather than surveying both sides; and (3) a marking scheme broken into bands (First, 2:1, 2:2) that names the specific argumentative moves a First-class answer must contain -- for instance, distinguishing behavioral flexibility from whatever property the Sphex example actually threatens, and addressing whether deterministic generation of novel behavior preserves or destroys that property -- and the specific errors that should cap an answer at a 2:2, such as treating flexibility simpliciter as the criterion or treating predictability as equivalent to sphexishness.
