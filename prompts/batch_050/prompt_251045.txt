I am a retired research chemist who has taken up philosophy in earnest over the past two years, and I am presently working through the debate between intellectualists and anti-intellectualists about knowing how: Ryle's regress argument on one side, and the contemporary view, associated with Stanley and Williamson, that knowing how is a species of knowing that, on the other. I am preparing a short discussion paper for my reading group and would like a thought experiment built to order.

The target: a case in which the intellectualist account delivers the verdict that the subject knows how to do something, while the intuitively correct verdict — the one Ryle's side would give — is that the subject does not know how to do it. In other words, I want a case where intellectualism over-ascribes know-how, not one where it under-ascribes it.

Constraints on the construction:

1. The subject must possess all the propositional knowledge the intellectualist could demand: S knows, of some way w of doing the thing, that w is a way for S to do it, and grasps this under whatever practical mode of presentation the intellectualist requires. The case must not leave the intellectualist room to reply that the propositions were grasped only theoretically or descriptively; that escape hatch has to be closed by the construction itself, not merely asserted closed.
2. The case must not trade on mere physical incapacity. No paralysis, no missing fingers: the distinction between lacking the ability and lacking the knowledge is a separate issue, and I want the pressure to come from the knowledge side alone.
3. The setting should be ordinary — a kitchen, a laboratory bench, a sailing dinghy — with no science fiction, no brain manipulation, and no implanted knowledge.
4. The scenario itself should fit in one paragraph, and the divergent verdicts must be generated by the theories applied to the case, not stipulated in the setup.

After the scenario, please do the following: state the verdict Ryle's anti-intellectualism gives and why; state the verdict the intellectualist view gives and why; identify which single feature of the construction is doing the work of separating the two verdicts, and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed; and sketch the strongest intellectualist counterattack and whether it succeeds.

Finally, I would like the response to be candid about its own fallibility. State which consideration, if it turned out to be correct, would make the case collapse and leave intellectualism unthreatened, and identify the point in the reasoning where the construction is most likely to fail. Conclude with the overall verdict in exactly this form, as the final line: \answer{...} containing a short phrase stating whether the case succeeds against intellectualism — for example, "succeeds", "succeeds with qualifications", or "fails".
