I'm a retired molecular biologist (40 years on proteins) who got into philosophy about two years ago, podcasts first and now a small reading group, and we've been stuck for months on the paradox of fiction. As far as I can tell the literature has three live positions and they've been circling each other for decades. The Radford-style illusion theory says our emotional responses to fiction are genuine but irrational or incoherent, since we don't believe the characters exist. Walton's make-believe account says what we feel are quasi-emotions generated inside a game of pretense, not real fear or pity. And the thought theory says the emotions are real and perfectly rational, directed at thoughts or propositions rather than at flesh-and-blood people. Nobody in this debate, as far as I can tell, has ever conceded anything to anybody.

Two hypotheses, offered as hypotheses. First: the dispute is stalled because it's a disguised argument about what an emotion is. The thought theory only looks good if you already accept a cognitive theory of emotion; Walton's quasi-emotion move only looks necessary if you think emotions essentially involve existence-beliefs; so the fiction debate might be a proxy war for the philosophy of emotion and will move exactly when that does and not before. Second: it might be like our old species problem in biology, where a good chunk of the fighting turned out to be people using one word for several legitimate concepts -- maybe "real emotion" is doing that here, and the sides are talking past each other about which phenomenon needs explaining.

I'll also throw in two homemade data points, since thats what I know how to do. When I reread a novel knowing exactly how it ends, the dread on the bad chapter barely diminishes, while re-hearing a joke twice kills it dead. And when I've watched footage I took to be documentary and then learned it was staged, some of the feeling lingers anyway. Do facts like these constrain any of the three positions, or does each side just absorb them at no cost?

So my actual question: what kind of disagreement is this -- verbal, a hidden empirical question that affective psychology could in principle settle, or bedrock intuitions nobody can argue for? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the positions, plus a statement of what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, an experiment, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected. Please write the answer in American English.
