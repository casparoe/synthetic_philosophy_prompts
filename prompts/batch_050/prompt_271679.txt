I write long-form pieces on science and ideas, and I'm working on an article about why sad music sounds sad. In the course of my interviews and background reading, I keep running into the same move, made by composers, music psychologists, and philosophers alike: music is a language of the emotions. The claim is usually deployed to dissolve the puzzle rather than answer it. Sadness is a feeling, sounds are just pressure waves, so how could the second be sad? Well, the reply goes, the same way a sentence can be sad: music is a symbolic system with something like grammar and something like meaning, and we've simply learned to read it. The phrase does real argumentative work in these conversations — it licenses the conclusion that there's no deep mystery here, just an unfamiliar communicative code — and I want to audit it properly before I let it anywhere near my piece.

Give me the full correspondence table first: what in music is supposed to play the role of the phoneme, the word, the syntactic rule, the proposition, the speaker, and the act of assertion — and be honest about which cells of the table have plausible occupants and which have to be left blank or filled by strained candidates. Then take apart where the mapping breaks. Language is compositional and referential; a sad sentence says something about sadness, whereas a sad symphony doesn't appear to say anything at all, and no listener extracts a proposition from it. Language also has a distinction between expressing an emotion and merely causing one in the hearer — does music's version of sadness fall on the expression side, the arousal side, or does the analogy force us to pick in a way the phenomenon resists? I also want the less obvious imports: things the language frame quietly brings along, such as conventionality and learnability, that might turn out to be the actually explanatory part, and whether the analogy survives better if recast as music being like prosody or intonation rather than like language full stop.

End with a verdict on the intended use: does "music is a language of emotion" actually dissolve the puzzle of how sounds can be sad, or does it merely relabel it — and if the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along, say so plainly and tell me what the honest residual puzzle is. Finally, tell me what would change your assessment: what empirical finding or conceptual argument (about musical syntax, about cross-cultural recognition of emotion in music, about compositionality) would push you toward a different verdict, and where in your own analysis the reasoning is most likely to be doing unearned work.
