It's about AI control -- the whole research agenda of "maybe the model is scheming, deploy it anyway but cage it with monitoring, untrusted monitors, honeypots, limited affordances, etc." His claim is that the agenda is basically incoherent, and his argument is annoyingly clean:

(1) A control measure only earns its keep if it stops or catches bad behavior the model would otherwise get away with.

(2) Reliably catching a model's attempts to subvert oversight requires your monitoring to be at least as capable as the model's evasion ability. A monitor that the model can out-think isn't a monitor, it's a rubber stamp.

(3) But if the model's evasion ability is *weaker* than our monitoring stack, then in the respects that matter the model just isn't that dangerous. An AI that can't scheme its way past a setup it can plainly see wasn't going to pull off a takeover anyway.

(4) So the control regime either fails (model out-schemes the monitors) or was never needed (it can't). The window where control actually does work is empty, or close enough that you're just funding theater.

(5) Therefore the only honest positions are: get real alignment and trust the thing, or don't deploy. Control is security LARPing in between.

I *want* to keep (1), I think -- a measure that can't catch anything really is decoration. And I grudgingly buy (2) if I squint. But the conclusion feels like it proves too much, because by the same shape of argument no security measure against any adaptive adversary ever works, and yet... my bank's fraud detection presumably does something? Prisons mostly hold prisoners despite guards being individually dumber than the smartest inmate? I feel like something slips between (2) and (3), or in what "capable enough" is doing in (3), but I can't pin it down and I might just be coping because I like the control agenda.
