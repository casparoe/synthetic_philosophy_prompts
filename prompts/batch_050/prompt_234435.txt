I've been thinking about how much of ordinary life runs on moral compromise, and I've become puzzled about what the practice takes for granted. Here are the kinds of cases I mean:

1. Two friends who prefer different restaurants alternate: one picks this week, the other next week. Both regard the arrangement as fair even though each "loses" half the time, and neither could say exactly what was traded.
2. A legislator votes for a colleague's bill she mildly opposes in exchange for that colleague's vote on her own priority bill. Both sides describe this as ordinary politics, not corruption.
3. In a negotiation, the parties split the difference on a figure, each conceding something to close the deal.
4. Countries strike agreements in which one side accepts weaker environmental targets in exchange for the other's financial contributions — one thing people value is explicitly traded against another.

Yet the same societies that trade constantly also refuse certain trades outright. You cannot sell your vote, however little you care about the election and however much the buyer cares. Courts won't enforce a deal in which a parent accepts money in exchange for exposing a child to risk. Most people recoil at letting a company pay a poor neighborhood for the right to pollute it, even if the residents would accept at some price.

So the practice seems to presuppose two things at once: that quite different values can be weighed against each other and exchanged at some rate (otherwise splitting the difference and vote-trading would be nonsense), and that some values cannot enter any exchange at all (otherwise selling votes would merely be efficient). My questions:

- What theory of value does the trading side of the practice presuppose? Does it require that everything being traded shares some common measure, or can the practice make sense without one? And do my four examples presuppose the same thing — alternating weeks seems different from splitting a price, but I can't say why.
- Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out — say, the view that some goods differ so deeply in kind that no amount of one can compensate for losing the other? Or is the practice compatible with that view, since we only ever trade inside the permitted zone?
- Is there a principled line between the tradeable and the untradeable that the practice presupposes, or is the boundary an unacknowledged patchwork of different theories? We trade money for time constantly but never votes for money — what implicit theory would explain both?
- If the practice strictly presupposes less than it appears to — if several rival theories of value would license exactly these behaviors — which parts are underdetermined? And what would have to change if we adopted a thoroughgoing version of either extreme: treating all values as exchangeable, or none?

I have no background in philosophy or economics, so please flag it if I've described these questions in a way that misses a standard distinction.
