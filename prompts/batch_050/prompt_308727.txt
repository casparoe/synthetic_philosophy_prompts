I'm preparing a short position statement for a graduate reading group in formal epistemology, and before I circulate it I'd like an audit of whether my commitments are jointly tenable. Here is where I've landed:

1. Rational credence need not be precise. For an agent whose evidence is thin or conflicting, the right representation is a set of probability functions (a representor) rather than a single one, and the width of the resulting intervals measures how much the evidence leaves open.

2. Updating should proceed by strict conditionalisation: on learning E, conditionalise every member of the representor on E and retain all the results.

3. Dilation — cases in which learning something leaves your credal state strictly less precise than before — is sometimes the rational response to evidence, not a pathology to be engineered away.

4. Other things equal, accumulating good evidence should narrow your credal state: imprecision is a measure of evidential poverty, so a well-designed agent who keeps learning should, in general, converge towards sharper intervals.

5. The Ellsberg preferences are rational: strictly preferring a bet on the known-risk urn to the corresponding bet on the ambiguous urn is not a mistake, and a decision theory should not be faulted for permitting it.

6. The master norm on any credal state, precise or imprecise, is immunity to sure loss: a state is defective to the extent that it licenses accepting a package of bets guaranteeing a net loss.

7. For decision: where every member of the representor agrees on the ranking of the available acts, follow that ranking; where they disagree, it is permissible to choose by maximising minimum expected utility (Γ-maximin).

8. It is never rational to refuse free, decision-relevant evidence in advance of making a choice: whatever information might do to your credal state, seeing it before you act cannot make you worse off qua rational agent.

My questions are these. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, and which pairs merely look as though they do? Please rank any genuine tensions by severity, and for each one say exactly what would have to give: the weakest revision that restores consistency, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the list. Where an apparent tension dissolves on inspection, show the dissolution rather than simply asserting it. Finally, if a single revision would discharge more than one tension at once, identify it and say what it sacrifices. What I want is a discriminating audit, not an itemised list in which every pair is declared equally problematic.
