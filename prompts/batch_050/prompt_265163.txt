I am a university student currently taking a seminar on categorization and analogy in cognitive science, and we have spent the last few weeks on the Letter Spirit project: the attempt to pin down what makes every 'a' an 'a' across the endless variety of typefaces and handwriting. As I understand the position, it has several distinct claims. First, letterhood is not a matter of sharing concrete visual features; two lowercase a's can differ in almost every measurable way and still be the same letter, because what they share is an abstract configuration of roles -- roughly, which parts open, which parts close, how strokes relate to one another -- rather than any fixed shape. Second, there is a distinction between the letter (the categorical identity) and the spirit (the style that permeates a whole typeface and lets a designer generate a letter they have never drawn). Third, the project insists that no fixed, finite vocabulary of features or parts can exhaustively capture letterform recognition: forcing letters into a rigid grid always produces something a designer immediately recognizes as wrong, and the failure is principled, not just a matter of more engineering effort. Fourth, recognizing a letter is portrayed as an active act of role-mapping, closer to making an analogy than to matching a template.

Meanwhile, in my machine learning course, I have become fluent in a different vocabulary: embeddings and metric spaces, prototype versus exemplar accounts, class boundaries, invariance and equivariance, learned versus hand-engineered features, style-content disentanglement, generative models, and few-shot classification.

Here is my request. Restate the Letter Spirit position, claim by claim, in that machine learning vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which translate only by distorting them (and how), and which have no expression in the ML vocabulary at all. Finally -- and this is the part where I want commitment, not a survey of options -- give me your verdict on the residue. For each claim that failed to translate, say whether the failure exposes a genuine gap in the ML framework (something representation learning cannot say, e.g. the normativity of a designer's judgment that a glyph is not yet right) or whether it exposes a claim that was overstated to begin with (e.g. the principled impossibility claim, given that learned representations routinely succeed where fixed feature vocabularies fail). End with a single overall judgment: is the ML translation a good home for this position, or does it domesticate it beyond recognition?
