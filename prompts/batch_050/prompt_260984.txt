Last weekend a friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about promises. Her position: a promise you never intend to keep is not a promise at all, just promise-shaped noise, the way a cheque signed by someone with no account is not really a cheque. Mine: the promise happens; it is the keeping that fails, and the wrong consists precisely in having made it. Neither of us budged, and I came away thinking the disagreement is really about whether the success of a speech act turns on the speaker's inner state or on getting the surrounding conventions and circumstances right.

I teach a second-year undergraduate course in philosophy of language, about sixteen students, and I want to build a seventy-five-minute seminar around this dispute without lecturing about it. Design a structured in-class activity that makes students feel the difference between an utterance that performs no act at all and an utterance that performs the act but performs it badly, before anyone names the distinction or hears the words performative, misfire, or abuse. The point should emerge from what happens in the room: the rules should require students to bring about institutional changes through speech alone (promises, appointments, verdicts, gifts, apologies, bets), while I quietly rig the conditions so that some attempts fail entirely (wrong authority, an addressee who never heard, a missing convention, an object that was never the speaker's to give) and others succeed despite a secret defect of intention. Students should discover the asymmetry themselves when the session's record is read back, and only then should the debrief map what happened onto Austin's distinction between misfires and abuses and onto the broader claim that uttering words is doing something whose success depends on conventions rather than intentions.

Concretely, I need the setup and materials; a roles-and-rules scheme built around some mechanism by which one person (me, or a designated student) certifies which acts took, with hidden condition cards distributed to players that sabotage felicity in systematically different ways, some rendering the act void and others merely hollow; a timeline for the seventy-five minutes; stakes or scoring that require no money; and the debrief plan. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (students gaming the scoring, the certification mechanism collapsing into perceived arbitrariness, the insincerity cards failing to register as genuine insincerity since everyone knows it is a game) and how to handle each. Be frank about which feature of the real phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce and which I will have to supply in discussion; I suspect the certification mechanism is the weak link, since in actual life there is no clerk.
