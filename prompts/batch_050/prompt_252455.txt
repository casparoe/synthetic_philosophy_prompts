I teach a seminar on nationalism and collective memory, and every year I assign Ernest Renan's 1882 lecture where he argues that nations are held together as much by shared forgetting as by shared remembering — his examples are things like the French having to forget the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre and the Albigensian crusade so that everyone can feel like one people. And every year a student asks some version of: okay, but what would Renan say about the Lost Cause? I'm embarassed to admit I don't have a great answer, so I'm crowdsourcing.

Here's the tension I want you to work through. On one hand, the post-Civil War American South looks like a vindication of Renan's whole theory: a defeated region built a myth (valiant soldiers, noble cause, war not really about slavery), the rest of the country largely let it stand, and sectional reconciliation between North and South genuinely was achieved partly through this shared forgetting/falsification. Textbook Renan, seemingly. On the other hand, the forgetting had a price, and the price was paid almost entirely by Black Americans, whose memories were the thing being sacrificed — so this looks like a case where the "forgetting" that constitutes the nation functions by expelling some members from the nation being constituted. Renan, as far as I know, never seriously considered that the people doing the forgetting and the people being forgotten might be different groups.

So, two questions. First, your best reconstruction of Renan's actual likely position, from his texts and his general orientation: would he have regarded the Lost Cause as the kind of productive myth his theory blesses, or would he have balked at it, and on what grounds? Please don't just split the difference — commit to an answer and defend it, then tell me the strongest evidence on the other side. Second, the harder one: Renan died in 1892, before the science-of-race collapse, before we had a century of evidence about what national myths built on racial exclusion actually do over time. If you transported him to now and gave him that century, does his theory have the internal resources to distinguish a unifying myth from an oppressive one — say, some criterion about whether the myth can survive being openly examined, or whether the forgetting has to stay unconscious to work? Or is the theory just missing a part it needs, and if so, what's the minimal amendment a modern Renanian would have to make? Feel free to bring in Masada or Kosovo as test cases if they help sharpen the answer.
