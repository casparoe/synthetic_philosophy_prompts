Between the mid-1990s and 2015, public opinion in the United States on same-sex marriage moved from roughly a quarter of Americans in favor to a clear majority, culminating in the Supreme Court's decision in Obergefell v. Hodges. Political scientists who have studied the shift attribute it to several distinct mechanisms: generational cohort replacement, as younger cohorts with permissive views replaced older ones; within-cohort conversion, often credited to increased interpersonal contact with openly gay friends, family members, and colleagues; elite and media cueing; and a long legal campaign that framed the issue in the vocabulary of equal protection and due process rather than in the vocabulary of sexual ethics.

Analyze this episode as an instance of reflective equilibrium operating at the scale of a whole society rather than within a single reasoner. I want a careful mapping first. On the side of considered judgments: which concrete intuitions were actually in play and changed -- intuitions about particular couples one knows, about harm, about what a marriage visibly is. On the side of principles: equality before the law, state neutrality among conceptions of the good, the authority of tradition, the definitional claim that marriage just is a union of a particular kind. On the side of background theories: religious doctrine, accounts of the state's role, empirical claims about child welfare that rose and fell over the period. Trace which elements revised which: did contact-driven judgments about concrete cases force revision of principles, or did the judicial articulation of equality principles retroactively recode people's judgments, and does the actual mechanism of change even admit of this kind of reconstruction?

Then tell me where the model breaks. Reflective equilibrium is a normative account of justification for an individual; here the 'reasoner' is a population, much of the movement is demography rather than deliberation, and the process produced not convergence but a stable, deeply opposed minority. Is a society with a permanent dissenting third in 'equilibrium' in any sense the theory recognizes, or does persistent dissensus show the metaphor has been stretched past its content? And run the analysis in the other direction: does the episode vindicate reflective equilibrium as a method, since the trajectory looks like what the method recommends -- mutual adjustment of case-intuitions and principles until they cohere -- or does it embarrass the method, suggesting that what looks from above like reflection was in fact contact, cohort turnover, and framing effects, with the principles serving as rationalizations laid over the result? If the latter, say what is left of the claim that reflective equilibrium describes how moral views are justified, as opposed to how they are retrospectively narrated. Where the theory is simply silent about some feature of the episode, say so rather than forcing it to speak.
