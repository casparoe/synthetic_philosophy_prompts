I run an interdisciplinary faculty reading group at my college — a dozen of us from philosophy, computer science, and psychology, meeting over indifferent wine once a month — and for the past year we have been stuck on a single argument about large language models and the Turing test. One faction, whom I'll call the operationalists, holds that Turing's whole point in "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (Mind, 1950) was to replace the metaphysical question "Can machines think?" with a behavioral criterion, and that systems which now sustain extended, open-ended conversation have essentially met it; on their view, continuing to ask whether such systems really think is exactly the evasion Turing diagnosed. The other faction replies that these systems were trained, quite literally, to imitate human text, so their conversational success is evidence of skillful mimicry rather than thought — that the imitation game has been gamed from the inside, by making imitation itself the training objective. Both sides quote the same paper at each other, the same exchanges recur nearly verbatim from meeting to meeting, and nobody has budged. The operationalists invoke Turing's famous mid-century prediction about interrogator error rates and say, in effect: the forecast came true, so the question is settled. The skeptics say the forecast coming true shows only that the test was easier to pass than Turing imagined.

In our last meeting I tried to break the deadlock by pointing to a passage from Section 2 of the paper that nobody seemed to have read carefully, where Turing himself considers the possibility that his test is unfair to machines:

"The game may perhaps be criticised on the ground that the odds are weighted too heavily against the machine. If the man were to try and pretend to be the machine he would clearly make a very poor showing. He would be given away at once by slowness and inaccuracy in arithmetic. May not machines carry out something which ought to be described as thinking but which is very different from what a man does? This objection is a very strong one, but at least we can say that if, nevertheless, a machine can be constructed to play the imitation game satisfactorily, we need not be troubled by this objection."

My working hypothesis, which I present only as a hypothesis, is this. Turing here acknowledges that the imitation game can fail a genuinely thinking machine (thinking too unlike ours to pass) but treats passing as sufficient to settle the matter in the other direction. Each faction in my group is running exactly one direction of this asymmetry: the operationalists take passing as sufficient, as Turing's last sentence suggests; the skeptics are insisting on the reverse failure mode Turing never contemplated — a machine that passes without thinking, precisely because passing was its training target. If that is right, the debate is substantive but asymmetric to anything in the paper, and no appeal to Turing's text will resolve it. But perhaps I am wrong: perhaps the disagreement is really verbal, with one side answering "can machines now play the game well?" and the other "does playing well show thought?", two different questions sharing one name; or perhaps it is a disagreement about standards of evidence — whether indistinguishable performance is defeasible evidence of mentality or no evidence at all.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of the impasse, not a verdict on machine thought and not a survey of the Turing test literature, which both factions have already memorized. Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, with reasons grounded in what the two positions actually say; tell me whether Turing's handling of the objection in the quoted passage shows he anticipated this stalemate, or whether the skeptic's mimicry worry genuinely escapes his framing; and tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress — a conceptual distinction the group is missing, an empirical finding that would move one side, a reformulated question — or else argue honestly that no progress should be expected, and why. I would like to open our next meeting with your answer.
