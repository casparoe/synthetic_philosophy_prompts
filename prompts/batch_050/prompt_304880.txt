For a graduate seminar in democratic theory, write a decision tree for the boundary problem: the question of who is entitled to participate in a polity's collective decisions, given that the demos cannot itself democratically decide its own membership without circularity. The tree should take a reader from a starting commitment (roughly: I accept that legitimate collective decisions require some form of authorization by the people they concern) to a named position on the franchise.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Choose the branch questions first, work out which combinations of answers they permit, and only then locate the actual positions in that space. Each branch node should be a single question or concrete case that a reader can answer without knowing which position it leads to — no node may presuppose the vocabulary of the positions it separates. Good candidate nodes to consider include: whether the relevant relation to a decision is being affected by it or being coerced by it; whether the relation must be actual or merely possible; whether all those in the relevant relation count equally, or whether the strength of the stake matters; and whether membership in the demos is a status one has independently of any particular decision or must be reconstituted decision by decision.

The following positions must appear as leaves: the all-affected-interests principle (in its simple, decision-by-decision form); an all-coerced or all-subjected principle; a view that grounds membership in some pre-political bond such as shared nationality, culture, or history; and a graded or stakeholder view on which entitlements track the degree of one's interest rather than being binary. If a leaf corresponds to a view that is discussed in the literature but has no stable name, label it unnamed rather than inventing one.

At least three branch nodes must be framed as concrete cases, not abstract principles. Suitable cases include: a downstream country harmed by an upstream country's river policy; long-term residents without citizenship voting in local elections; a referendum on secession and who beyond the seceding region gets a ballot; non-resident citizens taxed by their country of origin; and the interests of future generations in present constitutional choices. Each case should be one on which the positions genuinely come apart.

Note any combination of branch answers that is logically available but unoccupied, and briefly diagnose why: is the position incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored? Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate — for instance, where two positions agree on every case in the tree and differ only in how they describe what they are doing — rather than papering over the overlap. Aim for seven to ten branch nodes and six to eight leaves.

Write the answer in American English.
