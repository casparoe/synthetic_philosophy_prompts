I'm taking a philosophy of mind seminar this semester, and the central thread has been the dispute between first-order and higher-order theories of consciousness. Our professor has a habit of running the room through intuition cases, and over the term I've been keeping a list of her verdicts, because I suspect they're underdetermined in an interesting way. When a classmate asked about a hypothetical alien with a rich, unified perceptual world but no capacity whatsoever to represent or monitor its own mental states, she refused to answer, saying that case "is where the real fight is." We've now reached the AI unit, and next week she's promised to give her verdict on a current large language model: one that, in addition to generating text, can report graded confidence in its own answers, catch and correct its own errors mid-response, and describe (apparently accurately) features of its own processing when asked.

Here is what I'd like. First, predict her verdict on the language model, treating her track record as the evidence. If you think a third rule fits the data equally well, name it. Third, say which rule best predicts her language model verdict and why — noting where the higher-order rule's answer depends on the contested question of whether the model's self-reports are genuine higher-order representations of the right kind, and where the first-order rule's answer depends on whether text-only processing could ever constitute a unified perceptual world. Finally, tell me which further case would do the most to discriminate among your candidate rules: the withheld alien case, a blindsight patient who discriminates stimuli she sincerely denies seeing, or an animal like the scrub jay whose putative metacognition is experimentally contested — and explain what each possible outcome of that case would show.

State which single argument or consideration would most change your prediction of her verdict, and identify the step in your reconstruction where you are least confident — whether it's the assumption that her verdicts are internally consistent at all, the reading of the infant verdict as compatible with a higher-order rule, or the inference from seven cases to a theory.
