I help run a monthly discussion evening at our local library (about ten adults, mixed backgrounds, no philosophers among us, ninety minutes per session). Next month I want to tackle a question that has been bothering me since I started using chatbots. When I ask one of these systems to "show its reasoning step by step," I realized I have no way of knowing whether what it writes down is what actually produced its answer, or just a convincing story assembled after the answer was already settled. And then it struck me that I am in the same position with people: when someone explains why they did something, the explanation is always fluent and sensible, but I have often suspected the real cause was something else entirely — the room, the mood, what the person next to them said.

So here is what I would like designed for us: an activity, runnable in one evening with pen, paper, and whatever small props are easy to get, that makes each participant feel this gap in their own case before anyone names it. My rough thought is that people should make real choices or judgments about something, then write down their reasons, and only afterwards discover that something planted in the situation demonstrably steered them — something that appears in nobody's written reasons. The discovery has to come from the group's own data, not from me telling them; I don't want to give any lecture up front, and I don't want anyone to suspect during the exercise that the point is about their reasons being unreliable, or they will just perform carefulness. Please include a second phase where participants swap and, acting as judges, try to decide from someone's stated reasons alone whether those reasons were the real drivers — I want them to feel how hard that is, because that is exactly my position when I read a chatbot's chain of thought.

Also include: the full rules and timeline; how to keep the choices from feeling arbitrary (small real stakes if possible — we usually pool a few euros for snacks); a debrief that maps each moment of the evening onto the question about language models, including the strongest version of the worry (maybe the written reasoning never drives anything) and the strongest reply (sometimes the reasoning obviously does the work); what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it; and an honest note about which feature of the machine case the activity cannot reproduce — for instance, my participants at least seem to have an inside view that is merely unreliable, whereas it is unclear the chatbot has any inside view at all, and I don't know how to stage that difference in a living room.
