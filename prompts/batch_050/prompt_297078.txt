So this is going to sound a bit random, but: I run a small guesthouse in Portugal and since last summer I basically live on my pocket translator device, since half my guests are French or German and my French is embarrassing. The thing is scarily good now, and my brother and I got into this whole argument over Christmas about whether the device actually "understands" what the guests are saying or is just a fancy parrot. He said this had all been sorted out by philosophers decades ago and gave me this summary of some argument by Daniel Dennett (the "two-bitser" thing?) that he swears settles it against the device:

"Dennett's example is a vending machine gizmo that spits out a soft drink when you feed it two bits. His point is that the machine doesn't really understand what money is, it just reacts to the shape and weight of the coin, and that this is basically Searle's Chinese room insight: the machine manipulates symbols without knowing what they mean. Dennett concedes that only humans have original intentionality, real aboutness, while machines only ever have derived intentionality borrowed from their designers. But he thinks the difference is one of degree, not kind, since our own intentionality was also 'designed,' by evolution. So the Chinese room still shows that a computer that merely shuffles symbols, like your translator, can never actually understand anything, no matter how convincing its output is."

My brother is smarter than me but he also loves winning arguments, so I want to check this before conceding. Which parts of this would Dennett actually accept? I have a vague memory from a podcast that the whole point of the two-bitser was something weirder, like about what happens when the machine gets moved to another country, but maybe I'm confusing things. Where is my brother bending Dennett toward the conclusion he wants? And does his verdict on my translator actually follow from whatever the true parts are, or is he smuggling in the very thing Dennett was attacking? Lay it out for me so I can fight back at Easter dinner.
