So I'm trying to figure out where I actually land on whether stakes affect knowledge -- the pragmatic encroachment debate, the bank case stuff. Instead of picking a theory first and retrofitting, I went by gut on a bunch of cases. Tell me what principle, if any, captures all of them, or most of them, and which one I have to give up.

1. Sarah drove past the bank two weeks ago on a Saturday and it was open. Nothing important rides on it. She says "I know the bank will be open Saturday." My verdict: yes, she knows.

2. Same Sarah, same evidence, but now she has to deposit a check before Monday or she defaults on her mortgage. Now "I know" rings false to me. She should go check. Verdict: she doesn't know, or at least shouldn't say she does.

3. Same as 1, except her husband secretly took out a loan and the same mortgage disaster is looming -- Sarah has no idea. Nothing about her or her evidence changed. My gut says she still knows. This obviously sits badly with 2, and I want that addressed, not smoothed over.

4. Last week, low stakes, Sarah knew the bank would be open (per case 1). This week the stakes went up. Did she know it last week? My gut says yes, she genuinely knew then, even though acting on it now without checking would be reckless. Some people tell me you can't lose knowledge just by the world changing around you like that, but honestly "I knew last week but I don't know anymore" sounds... sort of okay to me?

5. I'm low stakes, chatting with a friend about a surgeon who read the patient's chart that morning and is about to operate. "She knows the patient's blood type." Feels fine to say, even though she obviously should double-check before cutting. But in case 2 I denied knowledge on what seems like the same structure. Not sure these two fit together.

6. The action side feels easier: same evidence, low stakes it's fine to just act on p, high stakes you need more. That doesn't even seem to need the word "knows" -- everyone cuts corners when nothing's at stake. So maybe cases 2 and 5 are really about action and not knowledge at all?

My tentative principle: you know p only if your evidence is good enough that you'd be justified in acting on p in your actual situation. It handles 1 and 2 nicely, but it forces me to say Sarah doesn't know in 3, and my gut won't let go of 3. So: fix the principle, or tell me which verdict has to die and why that one. Also tell me what the repaired principle predicts for cases I didn't list -- flat-out asserting p to someone else in high stakes, cases where the speaker's stakes and the subject's stakes come apart, and whether knowledge comes back if the stakes drop (say, Sarah buys insurance against the default). And if the better fit for my verdicts is some contextualist story where "knows" tracks the attributor's context rather than the subject's situation, say what that costs me.
