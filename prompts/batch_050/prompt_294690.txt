I'm a PhD student in political theory and I'm stuck on a chunk of my dissertation where I have to say, even roughly, when nations come into existence. My supervisor keeps telling me to just pick a side in the nationalism literature (the imagined-communities people vs. the perennialist-ish people) but when I actually check my gut on cases I don't seem to be cleanly following any of the standard views. So here are my verdicts, and what I want to know is what principle, if any, is generating them.

1. France. Gut: France was a nation by 1500 at the latest, probably earlier. I know the whole story about how most people in France didn't speak French or think of themselves nationally until schooling, conscription and railways did the work in the 1800s. Doesn't move me. France feels old.

2. Greece. The 1821 independence generation claimed to be reviving ancient Hellas. Gut: the modern Greek nation is a 19th century construction and the continuity-with-Pericles stuff is myth -- a productive myth, but myth. And I hold this even though Greek-speaking populations were sitting there continuously the whole time. So France gets to be old and Greece doesn't. I can already tell this pair is where the trouble is, but my gut refuses to give on either side.

3. South Sudan, independent 2011. Gut: it's fully a nation already, basicly from day one. Youth is no bar.

4. Yugoslavia. Seventy-ish years of official identity-building, shared state, shared schools. Gut: it never quite became a nation. So seventy years isn't always enough while fourteen can be plenty. Fine, I've accepted age is neither necessary nor sufficient.

5. Scotland. Apparently a lot of the kit -- clan tartans, the Highland imagery -- was codified or straight-up made up in the 1800s. Gut: doesn't matter at all. Scottishness is real and old, and the invented bits are now genuinely part of the thing rather than marks of fakery.

6. Israel/Hebrew. A liturgical language deliberately turned back into a mother tongue within a couple of generations. Gut: spectacularly manufactured, and also completely authentic now. So top-down manufacture doesn't disqualify.

7. The billionaire island. A tech billionaire buys an island, settles 5,000 employees there, commissions an anthem from a composer, pays a chef to invent a national cuisine, appoints a poet laureate, prints stamps. Gut: not a nation, and I want to say it can't become one just by following this recipe -- though I'm hazy on why, given that 3, 5 and 6 seem to say manufacture works fine.

8. The forgery case, my favorite. Suppose historians prove next year that Nation X's founding charter -- a medieval document everyone sincerely believed in for two centuries, taught in every school, quoted in the declaration of independence -- was actually fabricated in the 1800s. Gut: the nation does not get younger on the day the paper comes out. But I can't tell whether that's because its age never depended on the document being genuine, or because I just don't want to punish sincere belief.

What I want from you:

- A principle (or a small set of them) stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without consulting you, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible.
- An honest verdict on which of my cases has to go. My money is on the France/Greece pair being unsalvageable as stated, but maybe the real outlier is the billionaire island and I should just admit manufactured nations are possible.
- I do have a tentative principle of my own: a nation exists from the moment a sufficient share of its putative members imagine themselves as one, regardless of how old or how genuine the raw materials are. Please test it against all eight cases and repair it rather than replace it if you can. Two problems I already see: it makes France a 19th century nation, and it can't exclude the billionaire island unless I add some clause about the imagining not being purchasable, which feels ad hoc even to me.
- What does the repaired principle predict for cases I haven't listed: a Mars colony in 2140 that has deliberately manufactured all its traditions; a breakaway region whose population embraces a national identity the parent state's population rejects; whether the EU could ever become a nation in a few centuries; a diaspora with no territory at all.

And be straight with me: if these verdicts are just a mess and the right move is to drop two of them, tell me which two and why.
