Aristotle, Politics, book 1 (Jowett translation): "For he who can foresee with his mind is by nature intended to be lord and master, and he who can work with his body is a subject, and by nature a slave; hence master and slave have the same interest."

Write me an agony-aunt style advice exchange from classical Athens, about 600 words: a letter from a worried slave-owner, then the columnist's reply. The owner writes in because his newly aquired slave keeps finishing his arguments for him at dinner parties, runs the household accounts far better than he ever did, and correctly predicted the olive harvest -- meanwhile the owner's own talents are mostly carrying heavy things, rowing decently, and nodding along at symposia. His question: by Aristotle's test, which one of them is actually the natural slave here?

The columnist (give him a name like Phronimos) should answer in perfect deadpan Aristotelian orthodoxy, twisting himself into ever tighter knots to restore the master's dignity without once doubting the test itself. The humor has to come from applying the passage honestly: the slide from "foreseeing with the mind" to being born into the right class, the serene claim that master and slave share the same interest, and the bit where Aristotle himself concedes that if shuttles could weave on their own masters would have no need of slaves (paraphrase that, don't quote it). Phronimos must never break character or notice that his advice is dismantling the doctrine -- no winking at the reader. By the end, a reader who knows the Politics should feel the whole natural-slavery argument quietly collapsing under the weight of one inconveniently competent slave, and a reader who doesn't know it should still get why the letter is funny.
