Okay, here's an assignment I keep meaning to give my grad students and never do, so you're getting it instead. I'm teaching the experimental philosophy module again, and the Knobe effect unit always ends in a mess: half the room thinks the side-effect effect shows that the folk concept of intentional action is morally loaded through and through, the other half thinks it shows something about how people talk under conversational pressure and nothing about the concept at all. They argue past eachother for two weeks and learn nothing.

So: draft the joint statement these two camps would produce if they were locked in a room for a proper adversarial collaboration, in the style of the adversarial-collaboration write-ups you see in psychology, where you first agree on what's established and only then localize the disagreement.

Camp A: the asymmetry is a genuine datum about the folk concept of intentional action. People say the chairman harmed the environment intentionally but didn't help it intentionally because moral evaluation is constitutively built into how the folk deploy the concept.

Camp B: the folk concept is morally neutral, and the asymmetry is a performance effect -- pragmatics, blame-assignment, or norm-violation detection feeding into the answer people give, downstream of the concept itself.

Background you should take as stipulated, so nobody has to look anything up: in the original vignettes a chairman is told a new program will increase profits but harm (or help) the environment, says he doesn't care either way, and goes ahead; most subjects call the harmful side effect intentional, few call the helpful one intentional. In follow-up work, similar asymmetries reportedly show up for non-moral norm violations (cases involving aesthetic rather than moral norms), for "in order to" constructions, and there's a line of cases where producing the outcome requires skill, where the asymmetry seems to weaken or change shape. Also stipulate that neither side is an error theorist about all intentional-action talk, and neither side denies the basic effect replicates.

Format, with these sections in this order:

1. Agreed ground -- numbered claims both camps can sign, including claims about the data, about what would count as a "folk concept" in the first place, and at least two claims about what would NOT settle the debate (that last part matters, its where the real discipline is). ~300 words.

2. Statement of disagreement -- one short paragraph per camp, each written so the *other* camp would accept it as an accurate statement of that camp's position, with the exact point of divergence flagged at the end. ~250 words total.

3. Cruxes and predictions -- three or four cruxes. For each: a concrete experiment or argument both sides agree would bear on it, plus each camp's prediction or, where the finding already exists, each camp's explanation of it. Include at least: the non-moral-norm cases, the skill cases, and what should happen if subjects get an explicit definition of "intentionally" before answering. If you think a crux can't actually be adjudicated even in principle, say so here rather than pretending it can. ~450 words.

4. Verbal residue -- which part of the dispute, if any, turns out to be about words (e.g., what "the concept" even picks out, or whether "downstream of the concept" is a stable distinction), and which part survives once that's cleaned up. ~200 words.

Don't adjudicate. The deliverable is a document both camps could actually sign, so if a formulation would make one side balk, fix the formulation.
