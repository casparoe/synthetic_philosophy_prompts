I am a retired experimental physicist who, after a career spent measuring things, has lately taken up the philosophy of probability and decision theory. I have just read Keynes's 1921 Treatise on Probability alongside Ellsberg's 1961 paper presenting the two-urn experiments, and I am struck that the two men seem to be addressing the same phenomenon from opposite sides of a forty-year gap. Keynes, as I understand him, held that probability is a logical relation between evidence and conclusion, that many degrees of rational belief cannot be captured by a single number, that some cannot even be compared with one another, and that one must separately track what he called the weight of the argument standing behind a belief. Ellsberg showed that experimental subjects, offered one urn known to contain fifty red and fifty black balls and another containing a hundred balls of entirely unknown composition, prefer to bet on red from the known urn and also prefer to bet on black from the known urn — preferences that cannot be generated by any single probability assignment to the unknown urn and that violate Savage's sure-thing principle.

Here is my request. Imagine Keynes had lived to read Ellsberg's paper, Savage's axiomatisation, and a sample of the modern literature on ambiguity aversion. Reconstruct, as rigorously as you can and on the basis of what Keynes actually argued, what his position on all of this would have been. Please address at least the following points:

1. Would he greet the experiments as empirical vindication of his claim that degrees of belief are often non-numerical, or would he resist this interpretation — for instance, on the ground that a logical theory of rational belief is not refuted or confirmed by what undergraduates in a lab happen to prefer?

2. How would he answer the objection, descended from Ramsey's critique (which Keynes himself acknowledged had moved him), that talk of non-numerical degrees of belief is idle unless it constrains behaviour, and that the experiments show people's choices obey neither numerical probabilities nor any coherent extension of them?

3. Keynes was famously sceptical in his later economics of the idea that agents maximise mathematical expectation. Would he therefore regard violations of the sure-thing principle not as irrationality but as a reasonable response to differences in evidential weight — and might he connect ambiguity aversion to his account of long-term expectations, the collapse of investment under uncertainty, and the premium commanded by liquidity?

4. Modern treatments often diagnose ambiguity aversion as dynamically inconsistent and therefore as a bias to be trained out of people. Would Keynes accept the premiss that dynamic consistency is a requirement of rationality here, or is this precisely the kind of formalism he would call a pretence of exactitude?

Where you must go beyond the texts, flag clearly where documented Keynes ends and your extrapolation begins. I am not after a bibliography; I want a reasoned reconstruction that a careful reader of both men would find fair.
