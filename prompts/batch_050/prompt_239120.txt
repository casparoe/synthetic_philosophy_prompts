I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting a long essay on what fiction gets right, and wrong, about psychopathy. My peg is the Coen brothers' 2007 film No Country for Old Men and its antagonist, Anton Chigurh, who seems to me to dramatize, almost didactically, a live controversy in moral psychology: do psychopaths actually know that what they do is wrong, or do they merely know that the rest of us call it wrong? The distinction matters beyond the seminar room. It bears on criminal responsibility, since insanity standards in many jurisdictions turn on whether a defendant knew the nature of his act or knew that it was wrong, and it bears on a long-running empirical dispute about whether psychopathic offenders can draw the moral/conventional distinction the way most of us do, whether they feel the force of the reasons they can recite, and in what sense, if any, a person can understand wrongness without being moved by it. I want the piece's centerpiece to be a serious critical reading of the film rather than a colorful hook, and I'd like you to write that reading for me.

Here's what I need it to do. First, read Chigurh against the strongest version of the thesis that psychopaths lack moral knowledge: he can predict and exploit other people's reactions flawlessly, he knows the rules, but nothing in the film suggests the reasons behind the rules ever grip him — he seems to have knowledge-that without anything like knowledge-why, or knowledge without caring. Test that reading hard against the coin-toss scenes, especially the gas station proprietor who wins his life on a call, and the final confrontation with Carla Jean Moss, who refuses to call the toss at all and tells him, in effect, that the coin has no say in the matter — that it's just him. Her refusal strikes me as the film's own verdict on the philosophical question: she denies him the alibi of fate and addresses him as a fully responsible agent, exactly what the psychopath-lacks-moral-knowledge thesis says he isn't. Does the film ratify her stance by having him kill her anyway, or does it leave the question open?

Second, be honest about where the film resists the reading, because my editor will push back here. Chigurh is not a straightforward clinical portrait: he keeps his word to Llewelyn at terrible cost to himself, he seems genuinely aggrieved by perceived violations of professional fairness, and he treats chance as something owed, which looks less like the impulsivity of the textbook psychopath and more like a perverse private code. Is principled fatalism compatible with lacking moral knowledge, or does it suggest he understands wrongness perfectly well and has simply exempted himself — a very different diagnosis with very different implications for blame? And where does Sheriff Ed Tom Bell's bewilderment fit: the film frames Chigurh as something an old lawman can narrate but not comprehend, which is one way of staging the debate (incomprehensible evil versus accountable agency) but may also be a way of evading it. End by telling me what, if anything, a film can contribute to this debate that case studies and philosophical thought experiments cannot — or argue that the honest answer is nothing, and that Chigurh is a Rorschach test onto which each side of the literature projects its own theory.
