I am a novelist at work on a book whose final chapter discloses that the narrator has been lying since page one -- not about events, but about who he is. My editor's advice is the standard craft wisdom: the twist should make readers feel that the earlier scenes were different all along, that they misread as they went. I have been trying to take this advice literally, and it has led me somewhere strange.

Consider the color phi phenomenon. Two dots of different colors are flashed briefly in different positions; observers report a single dot that moves and changes color midway along its path -- the change seemingly experienced at a point in time before the second dot had even appeared. Philosophers of consciousness use this to pose a question about the timing of experience. On one picture -- sometimes called Stalinesque -- the brain quietly constructs the motion and the color change before the experience reaches consciousness, like evidence doctored in advance of a show trial. On the other -- Orwellian -- the subject really did experience two stationary dots, and memory was immediately rewritten so that she sincerely but falsely recalls motion, like history revised after the fact.

My question is what the conventions of my craft presuppose. When a reader reaches my final chapter and feels the early chapters transformed, which model is the craft wisdom assuming? That the reader's original experience of those chapters is retrospectively revised -- that my twist edits a record of experiences already had? Or that narrative experience is never really "live" in the first place, always assembled downstream of information the reader doesn't yet know she'll receive? Does the distinction between these two pictures even survive contact with fiction, where the "experience" being revised was itself constructed from marks on a page? And if, as some argue, the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction dissolves under scrutiny -- there being no fact of the matter about when experience "really" occurred -- does anything in the practice of writing twists actually change, or was the craft wisdom never committed to either picture? Tell me what the convention presupposes, what it merely fits with, and what follows for how I should write.
