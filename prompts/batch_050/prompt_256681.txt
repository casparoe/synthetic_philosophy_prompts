The question:

Q4. Death in Damascus. (a) Set out the case. (b) Which theory or theories of rational decision is it supposed to cause trouble for, and why exactly? (c) How does the problem differ from the one posed by Newcomb's problem? (d) Does the problem depend on the predictor being infallible, or would high reliability do the same work? Explain. [20 marks]

Her answer:

Death in Damascus is a decision problem adapted from an old story. A man meets Death in the market in Damascus, and Death tells him they have an appointment the following day. Death is an infallible predictor and has already made the appointment for whichever city the man will be in tomorrow. The man can stay in Damascus or flee overnight to Aleppo. Since Death has predicted his movements perfectly, whichever city he goes to, that is where Death will be waiting.

The case is a standard problem for causal decision theory, and the problem is instability. Suppose the man comes to believe Death expects him to stay. CDT then tells him to flee: his action cannot causally influence where Death made the appointment, and fleeing is better on that supposition. But as soon as he decides to flee, he thereby aquires decisive evidence that the appointment is in Aleppo, and CDT now tells him to stay. Whichever option he settles on, his own decision gives him conclusive reason to think the other would have been better, so no option survives rational reflection.

The contrast with Newcomb's problem is instructive. There, CDT and EDT simply disagree — CDT says two-box, EDT says one-box — and each verdict is at least stable on its own terms. Death in Damascus is worse, because it does not merely produce a disputed verdict but shows deliberation failing to terminate at all. Evidential decision theory is in no better position here: since each act is equally strong evidence of death, EDT ranks staying and fleeing as equally good, so it too fails to tell the man what to do. The case is therefore a counterexample to both theories.

Finally, the problem clearly depends on Death being an infallible predictor. If Death were merely very reliable, the man's decision would give him only probabilistic evidence about Death's whereabouts, and CDT could simply recommend whichever city Death is less likely to be in. Only with infallibility does his decision settle the location with certainty, so only then does the instability arise.
