I teach an undergraduate module on historical reasoning and political judgment, and I want to run a session on why lessons of history analogies mislead: the way policymakers reach for Munich 1938 or Vietnam whenever a new crisis arises, pick the features of the past case that suit the conclusion they already favor, and quietly discard the rest. My students can all recite the standard warnings (history doesn't repeat itself, analogies prove nothing), but the recitation does nothing for them, because the moment a fresh case arrives, they reach for an analogy as confidently as anyone else. So I want them to be burned once, in class, in a way they remember.

Design a 75-minute activity for about 18 students that makes them feel the pull and the treachery of historical analogy firsthand. My rough seed, which you should improve on or replace: present the class with a fictional international crisis written so that it superficially resembles a famous analogical case (appeasement of a rising power, say), split the class into groups, and let different groups be primed with different analogies for the same underlying facts — one group nudged toward Munich, one toward Vietnam, one toward the Thucydides trap framing of inevitable great-power conflict — so that they confidently produce incompatible policy recommendations and incompatible predictions. Then reveal additional facts about the fictional crisis that were true all along, and show that each analogy steered its group wrong in a characteristic way: Munich-primed groups over-read intent, Vietnam-primed groups over-read quagmire, and so on. The mechanism of failure should emerge from what the groups actually did, not from my lecture afterwards; ideally participants should be able to point to the exact moment where the analogy chose their evidence for them.

Include the full setup with the fictional scenario and the priming materials (written so no prior detailed knowledge of the historical cases is needed), the rules and timeline, how to run the reveal, and a debrief that maps each group's error onto a general feature of analogical reasoning (selection of resemblances, suppressed differences, the analogy doing the work of evidence). Anticipate what will predictably go wrong when this is run — students who know the trick is coming, groups that reach for the analogy despite instructions, a reveal that feels rigged — and say how to handle each. Be honest about which feature of real analogical failure the exercise can't reproduce: real policymakers believe their analogies, and my students will know they're in a game. After the activity description, provide a table with the columns: Phase, What participants do, Duration, Predictable failure mode, Facilitator response, Feature of analogical reasoning it instantiates.
