I am officiating the wedding of two close friends this autumn. She is a devout Catholic for whom faith was never a calculation; he is a decision theorist and a cheerful atheist. As a gift that is half joke and wholly sincere, they have jointly asked me to build the ceremony's central address around Pascal's wager. The congregation will be split roughly evenly between her pious relatives and his analytic-philosopher colleagues, and I need the full text of the address — about five minutes at a reading pace — written to the following specifications.

First, the wager's structure must organize the address rather than decorate it: a decision that cannot be declined, made under irreducible uncertainty, in which the price of committing is nothing next to what rides on the outcome. If the wager could be deleted from the speech without loss, the speech has failed.

Second, the address must run the three standard objections to the wager against marriage and show, accurately, where each transfers and where it breaks: the many-gods objection (why doesn't the same reasoning license marrying anyone whose devotion might turn out to be boundless?); the objection that belief cannot be adopted at will (can love be?); and the objection that a God worth worshipping would see through mercenary faith (and a spouse worth marrying, through mercenary love?). Each answer must be one a decision theorist would concede is fair, phrased so that a grandmother who has never heard of expected value can follow it.

Third, the address must handle honestly the one place where the analogy simply fails: Pascal's argument turns on an infinite payoff, and no marriage, however happy, is infinite. The speech should say plainly what is left of the wager's logic once the infinity is removed — and it must not, at any point, endorse the mercenary version of the inference: no suggestion, even in jest, that either of them said yes because the expected value was favorable.

Fourth, Pascal may be mentioned by name at most once, and the final paragraph should be something the two of them could say to each other without irony.

Give me the text of the address itself, not an outline or a commentary on it.
