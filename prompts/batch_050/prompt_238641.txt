Right, so I'm a novelist, about three-quarters through a draft, and I've hit a structural problem. The book follows six people sharing a crumbling Georgian house, and somewhere around chapter twelve I realised what I'd actually written: each of them is a walking answer to the question Schopenhauer thought he'd settled. If willing guarantees suffering — if every satisfied desire just spawns the next one — what do you *do*? Problem is, two of my characters currently blur into each other, and I can't tell if that's a flaw in them or a flaw in my map of the possible answers.

So: write me a sorting instrument I can run my characters through. Not an essay — a quiz. Eight to ten questions, each one a concrete situation one of the housemates might plausibly face (a small inheritance; a bereavement; a longed-for success that turns out to feel like nothing; the chance to have a child; a betrayal and the option of revenge; a terminal diagnosis — that kind of thing), each with two to four answer options phrased as what the character *does* or *says* in the moment. Crucially, no question should ask anything like "do you think existence is a net negative?" — I want them sorted by their reactions to cases, not by which label they'd reach for at a dinner party.

Then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions. The leaves I definitely need covered: ascetic resignation in Schopenhauer's own sense (quietening the will-to-live until it gutteres out); aesthetic release, where willing is only ever suspended, briefly, through art; the compassion route, where seeing through the illusion of seperate selves does the work; the Nietzschean refusal of the whole premise (affirm the will, suffering and all); anti-natalism as the practical conclusion; the treadmill option, where someone just keeps chasing the next desire knowing exactly what it is; and suicide — but note somewhere that Schopenhauer thought this one got the logic backwards, since it's an act of the will rather than an escape from it. If you think the space has other occupied corners, add them; if there are answer combinations nobody would plausibly hold, mark them and say in a sentence whether they're incoherent or just unloved.

One extra wrinkle. In my notes I've been using a homemade distinction I want the quiz to track as a second axis: what I call "house-leaving" versus "house-proud" pessimisms. House-leaving responses blame *desiring itself* — the machinery — and try to switch it off. House-proud ones blame the *arrangements of the world* and could in principle be satisfied if things went differently. My instinct is that Schopenhauer's own view only makes sense as house-leaving, but that several of my characters are house-proud pessimists who'd be cured by a lottery win, and I want the instrument to expose that.

Finally: flag any pair of positions your quiz can't reliably tell apart, and tell me which question I could add to split them. That's the bit I need most — I'm fairly sure my two blurring characters are a resignation type and an aesthetic-release type who behave identically until something specific forces them apart, and I don't yet know what the something is.
