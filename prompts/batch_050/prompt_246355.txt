I'm a software engineer, and over the past couple of years I've talked myself into a position that my friends find increasingly alarming, so I'd like to stress-test it properly. The position is this: when choosing under uncertainty, the right thing to do is to maximise expected value, full stop — and this holds even when the probabilities involved are minuscule and the payoffs astronomical. In other words, a one-in-a-trillion chance of affecting a trillion lives is exactly as choice-worthy, other things equal, as a certainty of affecting one life, and if the latter option is available but the former offers even slightly more expected value, you should take the gamble. I take this to commit me to conclusions many people call fanatical: preferring a minuscule chance of preventing an enormous catastrophe over a guaranteed modest improvement, being vulnerable in principle to mugging-style offers (hand over something real for a purported tiny chance of something vast), and ranking career and donation options by multiplying implausible-sounding impact estimates rather than asking how confident I am in them.

I arrived here the way an engineer would: every attempt I know of to block these conclusions — capping payoffs, discounting small probabilities, requiring a minimum probability threshold — seems to either violate an axiom I'd independently endorse (transitivity, dominance, continuity) or to generate intransitive preferences and money pumps. So I treat the fanatical conclusions as the price of consistency, and consistency as non-negotiable.

What I'd like from you is a map of what would count against this position. Specifically: which formal results, thought experiments, or structural consequences would be genuinely decisive defeaters rather than merely uncomfortable implications? Which of the axioms I'd have to give up are the most defensible sacrifice points, and what would the world of decision theory have to look like for abandoning each of them to be the right move? Are there defeaters that the position's own defenders — not its critics — would concede as legitimate threats, such as results showing it collapses into something I wouldn't endorse, or cases it cannot even describe (for instance, where the probabilities or utilities aren't the kind of thing that can be multiplied at all)? I'd also like you to separate two different kinds of objection that I suspect are often run together: the objection that expected value maximisation is false as an account of ideal rationality, and the objection that it is unusable as a decision procedure for agents like me whose probability estimates are themselves products of guesswork. Finally, an honest verdict on one worry I have about my own stance: has it been insulated by the move of treating every counterintuitive consequence as a bullet to bite? If nothing could in principle count against it, is that a problem — and is it a different kind of problem for a normative view than it would be for an empirical one?

Where possible, tell me what you would personally find most persuasive if you were trying to move someone in my position, and rank the candidate defeaters by how much work they'd actually do.
