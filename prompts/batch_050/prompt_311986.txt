Debates about transformative AI lean heavily on three historical analogies — nuclear weapons, the printing press, and the Industrial Revolution — and they are usually deployed to settle an argument rather than to illuminate anything. Before I weigh any of them, I want the standard itself. Write the desiderata that a historical analogy for transformative AI must meet to count as genuinely informative: which features of the AI case it must match (dual-use character, speed of capability improvement, concentration versus diffusion of control, irreversibility, susceptibility to arms-race dynamics), which structural differences it must not paper over (nuclear weapons are legible to states and do not themselves build better weapons; the printing press diffused widely but over centuries; the Industrial Revolution had no agent steering it), and which uses the analogy must be able to support — forecasting, governance design, or merely public communication — with the consideration behind each requirement. Mark which desiderata are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of particular camps (AI safety researchers, economists, historians of technology), and say whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable given that AI appears to be simultaneously a strategic weapon and a general-purpose technology, which is the part I most want to see. Then tell me which single requirement excludes the most analogies currently in circulation. Finally, state what would change your mind: which historical evidence or argument would overturn your verdict on the jointly unsatisfiable subset, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
