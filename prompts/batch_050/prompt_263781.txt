I work at a small policy institute that advises regulators on advanced AI systems, and I keep hitting the same wall in internal debates. Several of my colleagues with economics backgrounds insist that AI alignment is nothing new conceptually: it is just the principal–agent problem, the same one economists have spent decades studying in the form of shareholders and CEOs, citizens and bureaucrats, clients and lawyers. On their telling, the tools are already on the shelf — incentive-compatible contracts, monitoring and audits, disclosure requirements, liability regimes — and talk of alignment as an unsolved technical problem is philosophers and engineers reinventing agency theory without citing it. I suspect the analogy is doing real work in licensing that complacent conclusion, but I can't cleanly articulate where it strains.

Please audit the analogy as an argument. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the principal, the agent, the compensation scheme, the monitoring technology, the contract's gaps, the agent's outside options, and the possibility of litigation or exit when things go wrong. Then identify the disanalogies, and — this is the part I most need — sort them into those that merely make the AI case a harder instance of the familiar problem (which my colleagues can absorb) and those that break the analogy's load-bearing structure: for example, whether the "agent" is a unified agent at all, whether the principal can even state what it wants in the form incentives operate on, the asymmetry in capability and speed, the opacity of the agent's reasoning, and whether anything like equilibrium bargaining or repeated-game discipline applies. For each disanalogy, say whether the standard agency-theoretic remedy still applies, applies in weakened form, or fails outright.

Close with a verdict I can take into the next meeting: does the conclusion "this is an old, largely managed problem" survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying that conclusion from the start? If some pieces of agency theory genuinely do transfer, say which ones rather than rejecting the analogy wholesale; and if you think a better analogy exists for the parts where this one fails, name it and say briefly why it's better. Lead with your bottom line in two or three sentences, then the analysis. Maximum 800 words.
