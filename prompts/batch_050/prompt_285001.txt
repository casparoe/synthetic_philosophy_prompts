I am a welfare economist by training: I think natively in terms of utility functions, revealed and stated preference, interpersonal comparability, social welfare functionals, discounting, QALYs, and the various impossibility results about aggregation. I recently encountered the constellation of views in philosophy of mind according to which experience -- possibly including valenced experience, i.e., states that are in some minimal sense good or bad for whatever has them -- is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of matter, present in some attenuated form at very small scales rather than emerging only in brains. Some versions of this view are explicitly motivated by theories that assign a degree of consciousness to any system with the right causal structure, and a few writers in this orbit have drawn the conclusion that suffering might be pervasively distributed throughout nature, with potentially enormous implications for how much suffering exists in the universe and what ethics should say about it. I want to evaluate that conclusion, but I can only think about it in my own vocabulary. So: translate the argument from panexperientialism about valence to the conclusion of widespread ethically relevant suffering into the vocabulary of welfare economics, as rigorously as the exercise permits. Take it in stages -- what does it mean, in my terms, to attribute a welfare level to an electron or a thermostat; what becomes of interpersonal (here, inter-entity) comparability when the entities cannot report, choose, or prefer; what aggregation rule is even being contemplated when the claim is that micro-experiences might sum to something that dwarfs all animal suffering; and what the welfare-economic notion of a zero point or a life worth living could correspond to for a system with no preferences at all. Then give me the residue report: which components of the argument translate cleanly, which survive only under distortion (and say what the distortion is), and which have no expression in my framework at all. For the residue, I want your judgment about whether the inexpressible parts mark a genuine limitation of the welfarist framework -- something ethics needs that welfare economics cannot represent -- or whether they are parts of the original argument that only seemed meaningful because they were never forced into a vocabulary with any discipline. Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
