I spent three years as lead conservator restoring a seventeenth-century terracotta statue that was gutted by a gallery fire; roughly four-fifths of what visitors will see next month is new material I modeled and fired myself, keyed onto the surviving core. I have to give the dedication speech at the unveiling. The audience is awkwardly mixed: museum donors who were explicitly promised the return of "the original," a handful of art historians and fellow conservators who will notice any evasion, the sculptor's descendants, and the general public. Write the speech, in exactly five movements. The metaphysics of material constitution has to do real work in it, not decoration: I cannot honestly say the statue perished in the fire and this is a replica, nor can I flatly say the very same object survived, since the donors and the conservators deserve a position, not a fudge. I want the speech to commit to some coherent view of what it is we are unveiling -- one the professionals in the room would recognize as defensible -- while remaining intelligible and moving to everyone else, and I want it to say plainly what my own hands contributed. After the speech, add a short note identifying where it leans hardest on a contested position about persistence and constitution, and what consideration, if you accepted it, would have forced a different framing of the unveiling.
