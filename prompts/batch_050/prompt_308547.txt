I am a PhD student in moral psychology, and my dissertation concerns whether criminal offenders with psychopathic traits possess moral knowledge in the sense relevant to blame. Together with my supervisor, I have been coding a set of vignettes for a study we are designing, classifying whether the agent described counts as knowing that what they did was wrong. We have agreed on six cases so far, and I am trying to work out what implicit rule my supervisor is applying, because a seventh case is now in dispute between us and I suspect we are each extrapolating a different principle from the same precedents. Rather than tell you our dispute, I want you to reconstruct the situation yourself.

Her agreed verdicts:

1. A neurotypical accountant who embezzles from his employer, experiences guilt before and after, and freely describes his act as a serious violation that no rule-change could make permissible: knows it is wrong.

2. A high-scoring psychopathic offender, articulate and intelligent, who can state society's moral rules flawlessly and predict exactly how others will judge any act, but who treats moral and conventional transgressions alike in interview, saying that assault is wrong in the way that wearing the wrong clothes to a dinner is wrong, namely that it gets you punished: does not know.

3. An autistic adult with markedly low affective empathy, who reports feeling little distress at others' suffering and shows no measurable guilt response, but who reliably classifies moral violations as serious, generalizable, and independent of what any authority permits, and who organizes his life around fairness commitments he will not trade away: knows.

4. A man raised in an insular subculture that endorses revenge violence, possessed of ordinary empathy and ordinary competence at the moral/conventional distinction in every other domain, who sincerely and reflectively judges that killing to avenge a family insult is not merely excused but positively required: does not know that revenge killing is wrong.

5. A patient with an adult-acquired brain injury who retains perfect declarative command of the moral rules she had before the injury and applies them correctly in conversation, but who now lies, manipulates, and steals without any reported inner conflict and without registering others' objections as anything but obstacles: does not know, despite scoring as well as case 1 on every verbal task.

6. A repeat offender with psychopathic traits who, unlike the man in case 2, performs indistinguishably from healthy controls on the moral/conventional distinction task when tested carefully: he says moral rules bind regardless of authority, rates them as far more serious than conventions, and can articulate victims' interests as well as any philosopher, but reports that none of this moves him, feels nothing when he violates the rules, and reoffends at the first opportunity. This is the case we disagree about.

Please do the following. First, predict my supervisor's verdict on case 6, and commit to a prediction rather than hedging between options. Second, identify at least two rules, drawn from positions philosophers and psychologists have actually defended about moral knowledge, that are each consistent with verdicts 1 through 5 but diverge on case 6; state each rule precisely enough that its verdict on case 6 follows. Third, say which rule best explains her full pattern of verdicts and why, noting any verdict that is awkward for your preferred reading. Fourth, describe one additional vignette, different from all seven above, whose verdict would discriminate between the two strongest candidate rules, and say what each rule would predict for it. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which feature of the verdict pattern, or which consideration about what moral knowledge requires, would most plausibly overturn your prediction, and where in your reasoning you think error is most likely to creep in.
