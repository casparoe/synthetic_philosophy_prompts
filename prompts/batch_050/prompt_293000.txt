Write a dialogue between two philosophers of biology arguing over whether a holobiont — a host together with its symbiotic microbes — is itself an organism.

One character defends the view that organisms are, at bottom, what she calls Darwinian individuals: units that can form parent–offspring lineages with reproduction sufficiently faithful and sufficiently bottlenecked (ideally through a single-cell stage) to purge lower-level competition and sustain adaptation at the level of the whole. On her view, most holobionts fail this test: gut microbes are acquired horizontally, reproduce on their own schedules, and can evolve against the host's interests, so "the holobiont" is a temporary ecological community wearing the costume of an individual — and the mitochondrion is the exception that proves the rule, since it gave up autonomous replication and now counts as a part, not a partner.

The other character defends a physiological or immunological criterion of individuality, in the spirit of Thomas Pradeu's work: an organism is the unit maintained as a cohesive whole by a system of immune and regulatory interactions that tolerate some constituents and police others. On his view, individuality comes in degrees and kinds, germline bottlenecks are one historically contingent trick for achieving cohesion rather than its definition, and his opponent's view has the embarrassing consequence that lichens — which do reproduce as integrated units via soredia — count as organisms while humans, whose immune systems actively cultivate and tolerate specific microbial communities, somehow stop at the skin. He should press hard on the aphid–Buchnera case, where host and endosymbiont are metabolically codependent and the bacteria are transmitted through the egg: if that isn't a lineage, what is?

The dialogue should be a genuine exchange, not two lectures in alternation: each character must answer the other's actual objections, concede points where the cases genuinely cut against them, and at least once identify where their disagreement turns out to be terminological versus where it is a real dispute about what biology shows. Let them disagree, in particular, about whether "organism" should track the units of selection, the units of physiological function, or whether the concept should simply be retired in favor of more precise notions.

A request about how to handle the setup: if you think the dialogue as I've framed it smuggles in a false dichotomy, misstates either position, or rigs the cases so one side can't honestly be defended, say so plainly and explain how the framing should change, rather than writing a polite dialogue around a confused premise. I'd rather be told the setup is broken than receive a performance that pretends it isn't.
