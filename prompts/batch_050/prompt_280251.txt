I sit on the research ethics committee of a university hospital, and a question has recently landed on my desk that I cannot stop thinking about. We are reviewing a trial protocol in which participants are given a sedative known to produce dense anterograde amnesia for the day of administration. By coin flip at enrollment, each participant is assigned to either one sedation session (Monday only) or two (Monday and Tuesday). In the recovery room, no participant can tell, and later none can remember, whether a given waking follows their first session or their second. At our last meeting a prospective participant asked: when I come round, what should I think about whether I'll be brought back? Our consent documents are silent on this, and I have been tasked with drafting something.

The question pushed me into the literature on the Sleeping Beauty problem, and I have written a short essay -- part philosophical argument, part policy recommendation -- that I hope eventually to publish. Before I circulate it, I would like critical comments. The essay follows in full.

---

What to Tell the Patient

The trial's structure reproduces the Sleeping Beauty problem almost exactly, with amnesia in place of the magic potion. On the standard telling, Beauty is woken Monday; a fair coin then determines whether she is woken again Tuesday with her memory of the first waking erased. Upon waking, what credence should she assign to Heads? Thirders, following the argument associated with Adam Elga, say one third: conditional on its being Monday, the coin is still fair, so Heads and Tails merit equal credence; conditional on Tails, the Monday and Tuesday wakings are subjectively indistinguishable and merit equal credence; so the three awakening-events split the probability equally and Heads gets a third. Halfers, following the reply associated with David Lewis, say one half: Beauty learns nothing on waking that she did not already expect -- she knew she would be woken -- so her credence in Heads should stay at the prior of one half. Double halfers hold both that Heads remains at one half and that, on waking, Monday does too.

I want to argue that the dispute rests on a mistake, and that the sedation trial shows why. Distinguish three questions. First: what credence is Beauty rationally required to assign Heads upon waking? Second: what betting quotients should she post if offered wagers on Heads at an awakening? Third: what policy should she settle on in advance? I claim the second and third questions are well-posed and that their answers vary with the payout structure, while the first question has no determinate answer at all -- because degrees of belief earn their keep by guiding reasoning and decision, and at a bare awakening, with no decision on the table whose stakes turn on the coin, credence has no work to do and so no fact of the matter attaches to it.

To see this, consider a variant I call the Charity Collection. At each awakening, Beauty may buy a ten-dollar ticket that pays twenty dollars to her designated charity if the coin landed Heads. Under Rule A, every ticket she buys is honored. Then buying is a losing proposition: if Heads, she buys once and nets ten; if Tails, she buys twice and loses twenty; the operative probability is one third, and she should decline. Under Rule B, exactly one awakening's ticket is honored, chosen by a mechanism announced in advance: Monday's if Heads, and if Tails a second fair coin chooses between the two wakings. Now buying is exactly fair, and the operative probability is one half. But nothing about Beauty's evidence differs between the rules -- her phenomenology, her information, her memory loss are identical. What changed is only the decision structure. So the quantity that looks like her credence is really a shadow cast by the payout rule, and the thirder and halfer are arguing over which shadow is the object.

I anticipate a thirder rejoinder: Rule A simply reveals the credence that was there all along, while Rule B gerrymanders the selection mechanism -- it weights Heads-awakenings twice as heavily, compensating the true credence of one third back up to even odds. My response is that this concedes everything. If the probability that governs action is always the credence adjusted for the decision structure, then the invariant ingredient, the credence itself, never does independent work; we could rewrite every analysis in terms of policies and payouts and never mention it. The patient in our recovery room does not need to know what to believe. She needs to know what to do -- and there is always a policy answer to that.

The upshot for consent practice: our documents should not attempt to tell participants how confident to be that a given waking is their first, because there is no answer. They should instead supply decision rules for the choices participants will actually face in recovery -- for instance, whether to purchase optional per-session services -- where the correct rule depends on whether charges are levied per session or once per participant.

I would be grateful for comments on the following, in whatever order suits you.

First, accuracy: have I misstated the thirder argument, the halfer's no-new-evidence claim, or the double-halfer position in any way that a referee would pounce on? I have deliberately kept the summaries lean.

Second, the central thesis: does the inference from credences guide action to there is no fact of the matter about credence at a bare awakening actually go through, or is it a non sequitur dressed up as functionalism? My deepest worry is that the Charity Collection backfires: that Rule A genuinely does reveal a standing credence of one third, and Rule B merely launders it, exactly as my imagined thirder says. Is my response to that rejoinder -- that the invariant credence does no independent work -- sound, or does it prove too much? (It occurs to me that the same move would dissolve ordinary credences too, since those also show up only through decisions; I cannot tell whether that is a reductio of my argument or a consequence I should embrace.)

Third, the analogy: real amnesia is not the philosopher's clean erasure. Memory returns gradually, patients accumulate bodily evidence across sessions, and the same person persists through both in a way Beauty arguably does not across awakenings. Do any of these disanalogies matter for the policy recommendation, or only for the metaphysics?

Fourth, the writing: what is redundant, what is under-argued, and where would you cut or expand? Do not summarize the essay back to me; I know what it says. I would rather have the thesis sink now than after submission. No citations, please -- I will supply those myself.

Finally, a request about how to frame your judgments. For each major verdict you reach -- on the thesis, on the rejoinder, on the analogy -- state explicitly what would change your mind: which argument, counter-case, or distinction would lead you to the opposite conclusion. And flag the step in your own reasoning where you are least confident, so I know how much weight to put on it.
