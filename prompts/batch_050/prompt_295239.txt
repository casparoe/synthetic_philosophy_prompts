I've never studied philosophy or law, but I watch a lot of films and series based on true events, and last month I got into a long argument with my brother about whether the people who make these dramas owe anything to the real people they portray. He thinks once someone is dead the story belongs to everyone; I found myself disagreeing, but when I tried to state my view in general terms I realized I don't have one. What I have is gut verdicts on cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, lies behind them. Here they are, as honestly as I can report them. First, a drama set in the seventeenth century that invents dialogue, compresses the timeline, and merges three real advisers into one fictional character: completely fine, that's just how storytelling works. Second, a film about a medieval queen that invents a private love affair there is no evidence for, in order to humanize her: basically fine, though with a faint queasiness I can't explain. Third, a recent miniseries about a man who died ten years ago that strongly implies he embezzled from his own charity, which the writers admit they invented, while his widow and children are alive: clearly wrong, and not a close call. Fourth, the very same invented embezzlement, but about someone dead for two hundred years with no traceable descendants: still wrong, I think, though less urgently so. Fifth, a film that flatters rather than defames -- it portrays a real, documented architect of atrocities as a reluctant, inwardly humane man: wrong too, but here the wrong seems aimed at his victims rather than at him. Sixth, a film about a real tragedy that invents a heroic bystander who never existed, defaming no one: permissible as fiction, yet I keep feeling the audience is being cheated because most of them will leave believing it happened. Seventh, a drama that implies something damaging and false about a person who is still alive: wrong, obviously, but its wrongness feels continuous with case three rather than different in kind.

The pair I can't reconcile is two and four. In both, the filmmakers invent a false fact about a dead person, and in neither is any living person harmed that I can see -- yet the love affair sits fine with me and the embezzlement doesn't. Maybe the distinction is between inventing an interior life and inventing a deed, or between what audiences will treat as decoration and what they'll treat as biography, or maybe one of my two verdicts is simply mistaken. Cases five and six also pull against any simple principle like "don't damage the dead," because the wrong there, if there is one, doesn't seem to be done to the person portrayed.

What I'd like from you: a principle, or a small set of them, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself, with a note on which clause each of my cases motivates. If no coherent principle accommodates all seven verdicts, tell me which verdict it forces me to abandon and why that one is the right sacrifice. Then test the result against a case I genuinely can't decide: a biopic of a recently deceased artist that depicts only documented, verifiable events but selects and arranges them so that audiences walk away believing an interpretation of her character -- say, that she betrayed her closest collaborator out of jealousy -- which the evidence does not support. Nothing false is ever stated on its face. Is that case three in disguise, or case two? Lead with your answer in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. Please write in American English.
