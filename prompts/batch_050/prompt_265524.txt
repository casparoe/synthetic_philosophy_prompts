I am a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course in the history and philosophy of science, and I am grading take-home essays. Below is the exam question followed by one student's answer. Please grade the answer out of 10 points and give me the specific comments that justify the grade.

The exam question:

"Simultaneous independent discovery is a recurrent pattern in the history of science. Does it show that scientific discoveries are inevitable? In your answer, discuss at least two historical cases, state precisely what the pattern does and does not establish, and assess what, if anything, it implies about the credit owed to individual discoverers."

The student's answer:

"The phenomenon of simultaneous discovery strongly suggests that scientific discoveries are inevitable products of their time rather than the achievements of unique individuals. The two classic cases are the calculus and natural selection. Newton and Leibniz developed the calculus independently in the late seventeenth century, Newton first but publishing later, and their priority dispute consumed both men for years. Darwin had formulated natural selection by the early 1840s but delayed publishing; when Alfred Russel Wallace sent him a manuscript outlining essentially the same theory in 1858, Darwin was spurred to publish On the Origin of Species within months, establishing their simultaneous discovery.

Sociologists of science have compiled long lists of such 'multiples' — the telephone, for instance, where Bell and Gray filed patent applications on the same day — and the standard conclusion drawn from this pattern is that once the relevant preconditions are in place, the discovery will be made by someone. Had Newton and Darwin never lived, we would still have the calculus and natural selection. The discoveries were, in that sense, inevitable.

From inevitability it follows that priority disputes like the Newton–Leibniz controversy are fundamentally misguided: if a discovery was going to be made regardless, then who made it first is a matter of mere chronology, not intellectual merit, and the credit we assign individual discoverers is largely undeserved. The 'great man' theory of scientific progress is thereby refuted.

That said, simultaneity alone cannot strictly prove inevitability, since two cases of convergence could be coincidence, and some discoveries — general relativity, for example — were made essentially by one person, which suggests that certain breakthroughs depend more on individual genius than others."
