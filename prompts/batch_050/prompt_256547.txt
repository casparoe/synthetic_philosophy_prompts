I'm taking a phil of probability seminar and we've been doing self-locating credences. Two people in my section got into an argument after class about the self-indication assumption and it's still bugging me, so I want a referee.

Background: imagine two ways the universe could be, both consistent with physics as far as we know. In Universe Small there's exactly one intelligent civilization (us). In Universe Big there are a trillion civilizations scattered through spacetime. Suppose your priors from physics alone slightly favor Small, say 60/40. The self-indication assumption (SIA) says you should then weight each hypothesis by the number of observers it contains, which flips you to being basically certain of Big. SIA fans bite this bullet; critics say a theory that lets you overturn your physics priors just by noticing you exist is a "presumptuous philosopher."

Here's the exchange as best I remember it:

1. Lena said the presumptuous philosopher case is close to a reductio of SIA. Nothing about cosmology changed, no telescope data came in, and yet SIA tells you to go from mild uncertainty to near-certainty about the large-scale structure of the universe. A principle that generates that much confidence from an armchair is broken.

2. Marcus said the verdict isn't as crazy as advertised. Of course the mere proposition "I exist" shouldn't shift your credences much -- but SIA isn't updating on "I exist," it's updating on being *this particular observer*, and under Big there are vastly more ways to be a particular observer. That's ordinary Bayesian conditioning on a fine-grained proposition, not armchair cosmology.

3. Lena replied that this is exactly the cheating move: the fine-grained proposition "I am this observer" was guaranteed to be true *by me* under either hypothesis, so it can't carry the evidential weight being put on it. SIA just launders the same update through fancier wording.

4. Marcus said fine, but the alternative is worse. The self-sampling assumption (SSA) -- reason as if you're randomly drawn from your reference class -- is what powers the Doomsday argument, where my birth rank tells me humanity is probably near its end. If armchair reasoning that overturns your priors is the disqualifier, SSA flunks the same test. So Lena's reductio either refutes both views or neither, and she can't just default to SSA.

5. Lena said two things. First, she doesn't have to defend the Doomsday argument; SSA itself is just a sampling rule and you can accept it while rejecting specific applications, whereas the presumptuous philosopher case follows from SIA pretty much directly. Second, even granting Marcus's framing in step 2, SIA seems to double-count: the number of civilizations is already doing work in the physics priors (Big universes might be easier to generate), and then SIA counts the observers again on top.

6. Marcus's last move: then the real fight isn't about SIA vs SSA at all, it's about whether "existence evidence" is ever evidence, and both sides should just argue about that directly instead of trading intuitions about who's more presumptuous.

Who's right about what? I'd love a move-by-move verdict -- which of steps 1-6 are solid and which have holes -- plus whether Marcus's last point is actually a fair truce or a way of escaping a losing position. Also curious if there's anything both of them are missing entirely (my prof hinted something about reference classes but I didn't follow, definately want to understand before the exam).
