So I'm in this political thought reading group and we've spent three sessions on Adam Smith and empire, and honestly we're going in circles. Half the group insists Smith was basically an anti-imperialist before his time -- he rips into the mercantile system, he's scathing about chartered companies, he keeps saying colonies cost Britain more than they're worth. The other half points out that he never actually says Britain should let the colonies go, he floats this scheme of parliamentary union with America where the capital might eventually move across the Atlantic, and he seems fine with empire if it's run as a free-trade commonwealth. Both sides keep citing the same chapter of the Wealth of Nations at each other and nobody budges. Last week someone read out this bit from Book IV, chapter VII, where Smith is contrasting modern colonies with ancient Greek ones: "yet considered it as an emancipated child over whom she pretended to claim no direct authority or jurisdiction. The colony settled its own form of government, enacted its own laws, elected its own magistrates, and made peace or war with its neighbours as an independent state, which had no occasion to wait for the approbation or consent of the mother city." The anti-empire side says: look, that's Smith's ideal, the emancipated child, and everything else is him being tactful with the censors and the politics of 1776. The other side says: he's describing the Greeks, not prescribing, and if he'd really wanted emancipation he'd have said so plainly in 1789 when he revised the book for the last time and left this in.

What I want is not another round of the same fight but a diagnosis of what the fight actually is. Is this a textual disagreement -- people genuinely reading that passage and the ones around it differently -- or is it a disagreement about what would even count as being "anti-imperial" for an 18th century thinker (does opposing conquest and company rule suffice if you're ok with a consensual union of parliaments?), or is it really a methodological standoff about how to read political texts, with one side treating the Wealth of Nations as policy advice addressed to statesmen and the other treating it as a theory of commercial society where the empire stuff is just an application? And connected to that: how much weight should a descriptive passage like the emancipated child one carry as evidence of what Smith wanted, versus evidence of what he thought made some colonies prosper?

I'd also like you to use that passage as a test case: take your diagnosis and show what it predicts each side would have to concede or produce to move -- a distinction about kinds of empire, a fact about Smith's revisions between editions, a decision about what the question "was Smith anti-imperialist" is even asking -- and be honest if the answer is that nothing moves this debate because the two sides want different things out of Smith (a usable ancestor vs. a coherent theory), in which case tell me what the reading group should do with the remaining sessions instead. My own hunch, for what its worth, is that the debate is stalled because "pro" and "anti" imperial are anachronistic poles and Smith is actually sorting empires by whether they're run for merchants' monopoly profit or for mutual trade, so everyone is half right -- but I might be projecting.
