I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of biology, and over the years I have found that students grasp the dispute between etiological and causal-role theories of function much faster when it is presented with some irreverence. With that in mind, I would like you to write a satirical piece I can distribute as a light interlude between our sessions on Millikan-style selected-effects accounts and Cummins-style causal-role accounts. The target should be the positions themselves, not any actual person; invented proponents are welcome.

The piece should be a formal open letter, roughly 600 to 700 words, addressed to the philosophical community by the founding president of an invented body — something along the lines of a Society for the Proper Assignment of Biological Functions — who is a militant etiological theorist. The occasion of the letter is the society's decision to issue a formal censure of the causal-role school for its intolerable permissiveness: on that view, after all, the nose acquires the function of supporting eyeglasses, the heart acquires the function of producing a reassuring thump for stethoscope users, and nothing in nature can ever malfunction, since whatever a part currently happens to contribute is its function. The president should press these charges with deadpan bureaucratic gravity, citing recent verdicts the society has handed down — the appendix's petition for restored functional status, denied pending evidence of past selection; the heart's murmur reclassified as a mere effect with no claim to the title of function; a ruling that a diseased organ can fail at its job, a conceptual luxury the censured school apparently cannot afford.

The humor must come from fidelity to the actual theory, not from invented absurdities. In particular, the president should handle, with visible discomfort and dignified circumlocution, the standing embarrassments of his own view: the lightning-formed Swampman duplicate whose heart, lacking any selection history, the society must regretfully classify as functionless despite flawless performance; the genuinely novel advantageous trait that cannot yet have a function because selection has not had time to file the paperwork; exaptations whose functions must be awkwardly backdated. These evasions are part of the joke, and a reader who knows the literature should recognize that the satirist understands exactly what is being satirized and finds the satire fair. The president must never slip into actually conceding the causal-role framing, even for a sentence — internal consistency is part of the brief. The letter should remain legible to a student encountering the debate for the first time while rewarding the specialist, and it should close with the society's formal resolutions, delivered with complete sincerity. Please write it as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way a real open letter would appear in print.
