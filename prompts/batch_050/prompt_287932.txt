Retired biochemist here, got into philosophy in my sixties and just read James's The Will to Believe right after Clifford's essay on the ethics of belief, and something bugs me. In the lab we "believed" stuff on insufficient evidence all the time — you chase a weird band on a gel for three months because treating it as real is the only way to find out whether it is real, and half of experimental science runs on hunches nobody could justify to a committee. So: please argue that James actually wins this debate, at least for the case of science. Defend the claim that Clifford's rule against believing on insufficient evidence, if scientists actually followed it, would strangle inquiry in the crib, and that James's conditions for permissibly believing beyond the evidence (the option has to be live, forced, momentous, and evidence genuinely undecidable) map surprisingly well onto how researchers decide which hypotheses are worth staking a career on. Also take on the obvious reply — that scientists don't really believe their working hypotheses, they just act as if they're true — because honestly, from the inside, that distinction feels alot shakier than philosophers make it sound.
