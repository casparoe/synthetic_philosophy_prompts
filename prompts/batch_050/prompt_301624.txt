I am preparing a seminar on the philosophy of death for final-year undergraduates, and the set text alongside the philosophy papers is Tolstoy's The Death of Ivan Ilyich. I want a reading of the novella through the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the one who dies, because where we are, death is not, and where death is, we are not.

Please keep three philosophical positions distinct, because my students persistently conflate them: the Epicurean argument proper (death cannot be bad for you since there is no subject left to be harmed and no experience of the harm); Lucretius's symmetry argument (your nonexistence after death should trouble you no more than your nonexistence before birth); and the standard deprivationist reply (death is bad because it deprives you of goods you would otherwise have had), together with the timing problem it inherits (if death is bad for you, when is it bad for you?). The reading should show where the novella engages each of these, and where it slides between them.

Some questions to anchor the reading. Ivan's suffering is almost never about death as deprivation; it is about the impossibility of imagining his own nonexistence, the falseness of the life he has led, and his isolation among people who treat his dying as an inconvenience. Does the novella therefore concede the Epicurean point that death itself is nothing, relocating all the badness to dying and to a wasted life? Or does the sheer phenomenology of the final three days -- the screaming, the struggle against being forced into the black sack -- constitute a dramatised counterexample, a case where the prospect of annihilation is itself experienced as bad in a way the Epicurean argument cannot absorb? And what should we make of the ending, where Ivan falls through the sack, finds light, and asks where death has gone? Does Tolstoy answer Epicurus, or does he evade the question by quietly converting death into a transition to something else -- and if the latter, does that tell us the Epicurean argument is unanswerable on its own terms and can only be refused?

I want a committed reading, not a survey of options. Take a definite position on whether the novella vindicates, refutes, or changes the subject on Epicurus, and defend it; where the text underdetermines the point, say which way you think the evidence leans and why. If the novella resists the philosophical frame at any point, say so plainly rather than forcing the fit, and say what, if anything, fiction of this kind contributes to the debate that the papers do not -- that is the claim I will be defending to the seminar. No citations or references needed; I will add those myself, and please do not quote the text verbatim, since I will be checking page numbers against the translation my students are using.
