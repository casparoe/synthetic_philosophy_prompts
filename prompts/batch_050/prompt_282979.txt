I'm writing a magazine feature on how 18th-century monetary theory holds up against the bubbles it was written in the shadow of, and I'd like a worked case analysis. Take John Law's Mississippi System in France, 1716–1720: a chartered bank issuing paper notes, a monopoly trading company whose shares were bid up in a mania partly fueled by the bank's own note issuance, an attempted merger of bank, company, and state debt, and a collapse once convertibility of notes into coin was questioned. Analyze the episode through Hume's monetary economics -- the quantity theory, the specie-flow mechanism, his claim that an increase in money stimulates industry only in the interval before prices adjust, and his wariness of paper credit. Map the stages: which parts of the run-up does Hume's account of money's short-run non-neutrality illuminate, and does the collapse fit his framework or expose its limits (his is a story about price levels and trade balances, not about asset prices, leverage, or confidence in convertibility)? Finally, run it the other direction: does the Mississippi Bubble confirm Hume's skepticism about paper money, or does it show that his theory, built for specie economies, lacked the concepts -- bank money as a claim, self-reinforcing expectations -- that a bubble like this actually requires?
