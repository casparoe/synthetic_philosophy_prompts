I recently overheard a running dispute between two colleagues, both of whom teach evidence law, about a hypothetical they use in their courses, and I would like it settled. The hypothetical: a pedestrian is struck at night by a taxi whose driver flees. In the town where this occurs there are exactly two taxi companies: Blue Cab operates seventy of the one hundred licensed taxis, Green Cab the remaining thirty, and both fleets cruise the streets at random. There is no witness, no camera footage, no physical trace tying the vehicle to either company. The victim sues Blue Cab. Their exchange went roughly as follows:

(1) Anna: The plaintiff should win. The civil standard is proof on the balance of probabilities, and on the only available evidence there is a seventy percent chance the taxi was Blue's. Courts never demand certainty; every piece of evidence is probabilistic at bottom, so a party who meets the numerical threshold has met the standard, full stop.

(2) Ben: The plaintiff should lose. A judgment of liability has to fasten onto this defendant because of what this defendant did, and a market share does not do that. If the plaintiff wins on this record, Blue Cab loses every such case regardless of the facts, and roughly thirty percent of those verdicts will be false. A court that knowingly distributes liability by lottery has abandoned the enterprise of finding facts.

(3) Anna: That position is inconsistent with ordinary practice. Replace the fleet statistics with a single eyewitness who testifies that the taxi was blue, and suppose field studies put nighttime color identification by witnesses of this kind at exactly seventy percent reliability. Any court would admit that testimony and could properly find liability on it. Yet the epistemic position is identical -- seventy percent confidence either way. Rejecting the base rate while accepting the witness privileges the customary form of evidence over its actual probative worth.

(4) Ben: The positions are not identical. The eyewitness's testimony exists because of something about this particular incident -- this taxi passed before her eyes. When such a witness errs, the trial has made an ordinary factual mistake, which is the kind of error adjudication is built to tolerate. The fleet statistic, by contrast, would be exactly the same in a world where the taxi was Green's; it bears no causal connection to the event at all, so a verdict resting on it does not even purport to track what happened. There is also a further asymmetry: base-rate liability punishes a company for its market share rather than its drivers' conduct, and so provides no incentive to improve anything except perhaps to shrink or rebrand the fleet.

(5) Anna: The causal-connection point proves too much. Plenty of admissible evidence -- fingerprints at a scene the defendant admits visiting regularly, prior convictions used for propensity in some jurisdictions -- derives its force from frequencies rather than from a causal trace of the charged incident. And since civil liability is in part about allocating losses and deterring harmful activity, a rule that systematically tags the statistically likelier wrongdoer may serve those aims better than one that lets every such victim go uncompensated.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible only on a qualification, with a brief justification in each case. Then say what, if anything, both sides are missing.
