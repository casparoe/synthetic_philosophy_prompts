I am the editor of the ideas section of a general-audience magazine, and roughly once a month a submission crosses my desk that invokes Gödel's incompleteness theorems to draw a conclusion outside mathematics: that no machine could equal the human mind, that any legal code must contain irresolvable contradictions, that science can never be certain of anything, that truth transcends all proof, that God must exist because no system can ground itself. I am not a logician, and neither are my fact-checkers, but we are the first line of defense: submissions get about twenty minutes of our scrutiny before I decide whether to reject, send back for revision, or advance to outside review. If a piece reaches expert reviewers and they find the Gödel material is nonsense, we look bad; if I reject a piece whose Gödel material is defensible, I am suppressing a legitimate argument out of my own ignorance.

Act as a mathematical logician who has spent years refereeing popular writing about the incompleteness theorems and who knows exactly which moves in this genre are sound, which are equivocations, and which are open philosophical questions wearing a theorem's clothing. Think carefully, and before settling on a final structure, brainstorm the full range of ways these arguments typically go wrong, then distill.

What I want is a vetting procedure my staff can actually run: a sequence of questions to ask of any passage that invokes Gödel, in order, where each question checks one load-bearing precondition or inferential step of the theorems (for instance: whether the argument has identified a specific formal system at all; whether that system is effectively axiomatized, consistent, and expressive enough to represent arithmetic; whether the conclusion drawn is really about what is provable within that system rather than about truth, certainty, or knowability simpliciter; whether the argument slides from "unprovable in the system" to "unknowable by any means"; and, for arguments about minds or AI, whether an extra philosophical premise — that the mind is, or is not, such a system — has been smuggled in as though the theorem itself supplied it). For each question, state what a passing answer looks like, what a failing answer looks like, and the most common rhetorical disguise in which a failing answer appears. The procedure must be honest about its own limits: mark the points where a submission raises a genuinely contested philosophical question (the Lucas–Penrose style of argument about minds and machines being the obvious case) that a checklist cannot settle, and tell me what to do at those points — presumably to check whether the author presents the contested premise as contested, rather than to adjudicate it myself.

Then run the procedure on three representative submissions, showing the reasoning step by step: (1) an op-ed arguing that because any sufficiently powerful formal system contains truths it cannot prove, no artificial intelligence could ever match human mathematical insight; (2) an essay claiming that the tax code is "Gödel-incomplete" and must therefore contain questions with no answer; (3) a piece arguing that since a consistent system cannot prove its own consistency, all scientific knowledge rests on unprovable assumptions and therefore faith and science stand on equal epistemic footing. I want at least one of these applications to reach a verdict I will not enjoy — either advancing a piece whose conclusion I find implausible because its use of the theorem is nonetheless legitimate or appropriately hedged, or sinking a well-written piece whose conclusion I am sympathetic to.

Throughout, separate clearly what is settled — the theorems themselves, their preconditions, and the moves that flatly misuse them — from what is genuinely contested among philosophers and logicians who know this material, and do not present a contested position as expert consensus in either direction. Conclude with a short statement of what the procedure cannot do: which judgments will still require me or an outside expert, and where a checklist of this kind is most likely to misfire.
