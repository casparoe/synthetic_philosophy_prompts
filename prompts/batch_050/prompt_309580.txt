I am a retired experimental physicist, and over the past year I have been working my way through the literature on decision theory. A colleague of mine — a retired chemical engineer, thoroughly numerate — has been indulging me in a parlor game: I describe pairs of hypothetical lotteries, and she tells me which she would take. The amounts are imaginary, but she deliberates over them seriously, and I have recorded her choices in a notebook along with the expected values, which I compute myself. Unless noted otherwise, the two options in each pair were to be settled by independent random devices, and she understood this.

Her five verdicts so far:

1. $1,000,000 for certain, over an 89% chance of $1,000,000, a 10% chance of $5,000,000, and a 1% chance of nothing. (Expected values: $1.00M versus $1.39M.)
2. A 10% chance of $5,000,000, else nothing, over an 11% chance of $1,000,000, else nothing. (Expected values: $500k versus $110k.)
3. A 50% chance of losing $400,000, else losing nothing, over a certain loss of $300,000. (Expected values: −$200k versus −$300k.)
4. A 0.5% chance of $2,000,000, else nothing, over a certain $5,000. (Expected values: $10k versus $5k.)
5. A 25% chance of $1,200,000, else nothing, over a 50% chance of $600,000, else nothing. (Expected values equal at $300k.)

Next week I intend to offer her two new pairs:

6. A single dial will be spun once, yielding an integer from 0 to 99. Option K pays $1,000,000 if the dial lands on 0–49, nothing otherwise. Option L pays $2,000,000 if it lands on 0–24, nothing otherwise. (Expected values equal at $500k.)
7. Option M: a certain loss of $300,000. Option N: a 99% chance of losing $400,000 and a 1% chance of losing nothing. (Expected values: −$300k versus −$396k.)

Please take on the role of a decision theorist with particular expertise in the Allais paradox and the independence axiom, and help me with the following. Think carefully, begin by brainstorming, and check each candidate principle against all five verdicts before committing to anything.

(a) I believe verdicts 1 and 2 together rule out any expected-utility representation, whatever her utility over final wealth — that is, she violates the independence axiom. Verify or correct my reasoning, showing the decomposition explicitly rather than gesturing at it.

(b) Lay out the candidate principles that might rationalize the five verdicts: expected value maximization, expected utility with diminishing marginal utility, prospect-theory-style probability weighting with reflection at zero, anticipated-regret minimization, a simple certainty premium, or something I have not thought of. For each candidate, state which verdicts it accommodates and which verdicts, if any, kill it outright.

(c) Predict her choice in pairs 6 and 7 under each principle that survives (b). I am especially interested in whether the surviving principles diverge on either new pair, and in whether the fact that pair 6 uses a single dial — so the two options are perfectly correlated rather than independent — changes any principle's prediction.

(d) If the surviving principles converge on both new pairs, design the single further pair that would most cheaply tell them apart, and explain what each possible choice by her would show.

Where her track record genuinely underdetermines the next verdict, say so plainly rather than forcing a prediction.
