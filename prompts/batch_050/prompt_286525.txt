I have no background in classics, history, or political science, but for the past couple of years I have been thinking in my spare time about three famous failures of Athenian decision-making, and I would like help turning that thinking into something practical.

The three episodes, as I understand them from general reading: the Mytilene debate, in which the assembly voted one day to execute the entire male population of a rebellious ally and enslave its women and children, then regretted it and reversed the decision the next day; the Sicilian expedition, in which the assembly committed to an enormous and ultimately catastrophic military campaign on the basis of poor information and great enthusiasm; and the trial of Socrates, in which a large body of ordinary citizens, acting as a jury, condemned a man to death on charges that mixed religion, politics, and personal reputation.

My own rough taxonomy of these failures is this: Mytilene is a tempo failure, where the problem was how fast and how emotionally the decision was made; Sicily is an information failure, where the problem was what the voters did not and could not know about an irreversible undertaking; and the Socrates trial is a jurisdiction failure, where the problem was that some questions should perhaps never be put to a mass vote at all. I realize the categories overlap and may distort more than they clarify.

Here is the practical situation. I belong to a citizens' group advising the government of a mid-sized city that is considering two reforms: binding referendums on major municipal decisions, and standing panels of citizens chosen by lottery to deliberate on contested issues. We can realistically push for perhaps three or four institutional design features over the next few years, and we have to choose where to spend that influence.

So my question: which open interpretive questions about these three episodes matter most for our purposes, and in what order should we try to resolve them? For each question you identify, please explain why its answer would change a specific design choice (for example, cooling-off periods or supermajority requirements for irreversible decisions, required expert testimony and published dissent, or limits on which topics may be put to a vote), whether a non-specialist can reason about it responsibly using only what is well attested, which other questions on your list depend on its answer, and what would move it up or down the ranking. Please also tell me which lessons commonly drawn from these episodes are overrated for modern institutional design, and whether my three-way taxonomy is obscuring something important. I want a defended agenda, not a survey of the debate.
