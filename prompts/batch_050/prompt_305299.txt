I sit on the facilities committee of a mid-sized nonprofit that owns an old building with a courtyard, and at last week's meeting I watched two colleagues, Dana and Marcus, talk past each other for forty minutes about a cast-iron gate between the courtyard and the back alley that everyone wants to take down. The gate is rusted shut anyway, nobody has a key, and it blocks what would be a useful service entrance. Dana's opening move was to invoke Chesterton's fence: if you come across something you don't understand the purpose of, you haven't earned the right to remove it; the rule is not "figure out the reason and then decide," it's "you may only remove it once you understand why it was put up." Marcus answered that this standard is rigged in favor of the status quo, because understanding a thing's purpose is unbounded work -- we've asked the two longest-serving staff members, checked the minutes back to 1987, and found nothing, so on Dana's rule the gate stays forever even though the most likely explanation is that it fenced off a coal delivery that stopped sixty years ago. Dana conceded we probably won't find documentation, but said that was her point in a different register: institutions encode knowledge that never gets written down, so the absence of a recorded reason is weak evidence of the absence of a reason, and the cost of leaving a gate up for another year is trivial compared to the cost of learning, after demolition, that it was doing something we only notice once it's gone -- she gave the example of a client organization that removed an "obsolete" approval step and discovered it had been the only point at which anyone checked for duplicate payments. Marcus replied that this argument proves too much, since it applies with equal force to adding the gate in the first place: somebody once erected it over whatever was there before, presumably in the same ignorance Dana now warns against, so her principle quietly assumes that whoever acted first was wise and whoever acts second is reckless, which is not a principle but a mood. He added that "keep it until we understand it" treats inaction as costless, when the blocked service entrance costs us real money every week, and that a principle which can only ever delay change and never license it will, over decades, fill any organization with rusted gates.

Dana's next move was to accept the asymmetry charge but relocate it: adding and removing really are different, she said, because removal destroys information -- the gate, like the approval step, is the only surviving trace of whatever problem prompted it, and you can always remove it later, but you cannot un-remove it to run the experiment. Marcus said that "later" never comes, that reversibility is a excuse for permanent deferral, and that if the gate's function matters someone should be able to say what bad thing would happen without it -- and in this case we can test cheaply, since the gate is already rusted shut and the alley is already accessible around the side, so the world has been running the experiment for years without incident. Dana answered that this cuts against him: if the gate has been functionally absent for years, then removing it changes nothing, so why incur the cost and the small residual risk at all -- and if it is doing something, the years of rust prove it does that job passively and invisibly, which is exactly the kind of job people forget exists. At that point the meeting ran out and the vote was tabled, and I realized I couldn't say who had the better of it.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the main steps above, say who is right about the specific point at issue and why, rather than just declaring an overall winner. In particular, I want to know whether Chesterton's fence, as Dana deploys it, is a genuine epistemic principle or just status quo bias in a trench coat; whether Marcus's "adding is removing in reverse" argument actually dissolves the asymmetry or merely relocates it; whether Dana's reversibility point survives Marcus's observation that deferral is itself a decision with compounding costs; and what both of them are missing -- for instance, whether there is a principled way to distinguish the gate case from Dana's duplicate-payments case, given that from inside the institution they looked identical before the fact.
