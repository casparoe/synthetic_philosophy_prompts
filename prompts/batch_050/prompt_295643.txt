A colleague of mine defends Robert Brandom's inferentialism with an analogy he treats as close to decisive: words, he says, are like chess pieces. A chess piece is nothing over and above the moves the rules permit it — there is no further question about what the bishop "really is" beyond its role in the game — and likewise, on his telling, an expression's meaning is nothing over and above the inferences its correct use licenses. From this he draws two conclusions. First, that inferentialism dissolves the problem of representation: asking how the word "electron" manages to be about electrons is like asking what the bishop stands for outside the game, a pseudo-question generated by ignoring that roles are all there is. Second, that truth can be at most an intra-linguistic affair on this view — a well-formed stretch of discourse relates to the world the way a legal chess position relates to anything outside the board, which is to say, not at all. I suspect the analogy is doing real argumentative work for him here and that at least the second conclusion rides on it rather than on Brandom's actual position.

Please audit the analogy. Structure your answer in the following sections, in this order:

1. The Mapping (roughly 200 words): give the explicit correspondence table — rules of chess to inferential norms, piece identity to conceptual content, legal move to materially good inference, and so on — and say what plays the role, if anything does, of the players, the board, and winning.

2. What the Analogy Gets Right (roughly 200 words): where the mapping genuinely illuminates inferentialism — meaning-constitution by rules rather than by association with objects, the holism of content, the normativity of correct use.

3. Where It Breaks (roughly 350 words): the disanalogies, with explicit attention to which ones matter for my colleague's two conclusions and which are superficial. Consider at least: that chess moves are correct purely by stipulation while material inferences are answerable to a non-linguistic world through perception and action (entry into and exit from discourse); that chess permits no assertion, no communication between players of different games, and nothing like substitution of co-referring terms or cross-reference between utterances; and that the chess rulebook is fixed and complete while speakers' inferential commitments are partial, contested, and defeasible — a player who moves the rook diagonally is not playing chess, but a speaker who draws a bad inference has not thereby stopped speaking English.

4. The Verdict (roughly 150 words): does either conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table? Address in particular whether Brandom's own apparatus — explaining the representational dimension of discourse through substitution-inferential structure and anaphora rather than dismissing it — amounts to a rejection of the closed-game picture my colleague's analogy suggests, and whether the analogy's real affinity is with a view inferentialists take themselves to oppose.

5. A Better Analogy (roughly 100 words): if the chess analogy misleads more than it reveals, propose a better one for what inferential role semantics actually claims, and say in one or two sentences why it is better.
