I am a retired biochemist who has lately been reading philosophy of mind, and I have been turning over an analogy pressed into argumentative service by the late Daniel Dennett. He contended that AI systems capable of passing themselves off as human interlocutors — his term for them was counterfeit people — stand to genuine persons roughly as counterfeit banknotes stand to genuine currency. Just as forged money, even when indistinguishable in circulation, corrodes the trust on which the entire institution of currency depends and is therefore suppressed with severe penalties, counterfeit people corrode the trust on which human communication and cooperation depend, and so their creation and distribution ought likewise to be criminalized.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of mind, conversant with Dennett's work and with the logic of analogical argument. Think carefully before answering: begin by brainstorming a wide range of correspondences and disanalogies between the two domains, and only then prune your list to the ones that matter. Audit the analogy. What maps onto what? Where does the mapping break? Which features of currency does it quietly import that persons may lack — I am thinking, for instance, of fungibility, of value that is wholly institutional rather than intrinsic, and of a state's monopoly on legitimate issuance? Which of these disanalogies are fatal to the intended use and which are merely decorative? And does Dennett's strong conclusion, legal prohibition, survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or is a weaker conclusion (mandatory disclosure, say) the most the analogy can carry?

Deliver your audit as a table with exactly these columns: Source element (currency and forgery); Purported analogue (AI systems and persons); Mapping verdict (holds / partial / fails); Grounds for the verdict; Bearing on the prohibitionist conclusion. Follow the table with a verdict of no more than 150 words stating, with reasons, whether the analogy can support prohibition, only regulation, or nothing at all — and what alternative analogy, if any, would better serve the concern animating Dennett's argument.
