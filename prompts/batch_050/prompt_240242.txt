Next month I am to deliver the eulogy for my closest friend of thirty years, who was also my most persistent political opponent. I have spent my career as a trade-union organiser on the left; he was a conservative newspaper columnist. We met as young men on rival debating teams, argued in print, on panels and in a correspondence that runs to several hundred letters, and never once voted the same way. He had a phrase he returned to whenever people asked how we stayed friends: he held that echo chambers are built not out of agreement but out of distrust — that people remain inside them less because they only hear their own side than because they have been taught that everyone outside argues in bad faith — and he treated our friendship as a standing refutation of that claim. In his final month he asked me, rather than anyone from his own camp, to give the eulogy, on the grounds that I had never once mistaken him for either a fool or a villain.

The room will be genuinely divided: half his readers, colleagues and political allies; half my own comrades, several of whom regarded him as an apologist for things they find indefensible; and, in the front row, his widow and grandchildren, who have asked only that politics not be fought over his coffin. There are two failure modes I want to avoid: softening the disagreement into a generic tribute of the "we disagreed but respected each other" sort, which would be false to him, since he thought respect without continued argument was mere condescension; and turning the eulogy into one final rebuttal of his positions, which would be false to the occasion.

Write the eulogy. It should be structured around his distrust-not-agreement claim, and the speech should itself demonstrate — in what it is willing to say and what it declines to say — what it means to argue with someone in good faith for three decades and then lose them. It must include at least one position of his that I still consider badly wrong, stated fairly enough that his allies in the room would recognise it as their own view; it must contain no sentence implying that he was, deep down, really on my side; and it must end on the friendship rather than the politics without pretending the politics did not matter. No more than six hundred words, and write only the speech itself: no headings, no stage directions, no prefatory notes.
