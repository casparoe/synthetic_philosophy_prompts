I'm prepping a grad seminar on medieval political thought (and, full disclosure, relearning some of this material myself as I go), and I've gotten stuck on Aquinas's treatment of tyrannicide. In the Summa Theologiae, II-II, q.42, a.2, ad 3 -- the article where he argues sedition is always a mortal sin -- he says:

"A tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler, as the Philosopher states (Polit. iii, 5; Ethic. viii, 10). Consequently there is no sedition in disturbing a government of this kind, unless indeed the tyrant's rule be disturbed so inordinately, that his subjects suffer greater harm from the consequent disturbance than from the tyrant's government. Indeed it is the tyrant rather that is guilty of sedition, since he encourages discord and sedition among his subjects, that he may lord over them more securely; for this is tyranny, being conducive to the private good of the ruler, and to the injury of the multitude."

Here's what I want explained, at the level of the conceptual machinery rather than the history-of-political-thought survey answer. First, the inversion move: Aquinas doesn't just exempt rebels from the charge of sedition, he reassigns the label to the tyrant. What exactly is the logical structure of that move? Is it doing principled work -- sedition is defined relative to a common good that tyranny has already destroyed, so there's no genuine "unity" left to violate -- or is it closer to a rhetorical relabeling that lets him have sedition be always sinful while still permitting revolt? Second, the "unless" clause seems to quietly convert the whole question into a proportionality calculation: revolt is fine unless the chaos costs more than the tyranny does. Does that clause do all the real work here, such that the bold definitional stuff about tyranny "not being just" is actually dispensable? Or can the two parts come apart -- could a revolt satisfy the proportionality test against a merely flawed (not tyrannical) regime, and would Aquinas have any principled way to say no?

And third, the thing I genuinely can't make cohere: who is licensed to act on this? The passage talks about "disturbing" a government, not about private individuals stabbing anyone, and elsewhere Aquinas is famously wary of private presumption. But in his earlier commentary on the Sentences he seems strikingly warmer toward actually killing a tyrant, following Cicero's line on Caesar. I'd like an explanation of how to put these together -- or an argument that they can't be put together and Aquinas changed or hedged his view. Bonus: how does any of this survive the Romans 13 worry he clearly feels the force of? Please don't just tell me what Finnis or the Cambridge History says; I want the conceptual reconstruction, including where it strains. If I'm misreading the passage (my Latin is rusty and I may be leaning too hard on the English) tell me that too.
