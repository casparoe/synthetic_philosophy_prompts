I am a high school senior taking a comparative politics elective, and for my capstone project I have been looking at how differently four Western democracies organize the relationship between religion and the state, even though all four consider themselves committed to religious freedom. England retains an established church whose bishops hold reserved seats in the upper house of Parliament, yet almost nobody experiences this as oppression. Germany has no established church, but the state tax office collects a church tax on behalf of recognized denominations, confessional religious instruction is a standard subject in public schools, and anyone who wants to stop paying must formally deregister from their church, which can carry consequences for access to sacraments and church employment. France treats laïcité as a constitutional identity and bars conspicuous religious symbols from state schools. The United States constitutionally forbids establishment altogether, yet its currency proclaims trust in God and legislative sessions open with prayers. What struck me is that each of these arrangements would be unconstitutional, or simply unthinkable, in at least one of the other three countries, and each country's citizens tend to regard their own system as the obviously neutral one.

For my project I do not want to start by picking a favorite system. I want the specification first: what would a theory of legitimate church-state relations have to get right? Give me the desiderata — the requirements any adequate theory in this area must meet — with the case or consideration behind each one. I would like the list to cover, at minimum: the actual arrangements it must be able to either justify or condemn with a principled reason rather than a shrug of "that is not how we do things here"; the distinctions it is not allowed to collapse (for example, symbolic establishment versus legal privilege versus outright coercion, accommodating a religion versus endorsing it, and individual believers versus religious organizations as the bearers of rights); the hard cases it must take a position on, such as a taxpayer funding clergy salaries, a student told to remove a headscarf at the school gate, an employee fired by a church-run hospital for remarrying, and a small or loosely organized minority faith that cannot qualify for the benefits the large, well-structured denominations receive; and what the theory owes to people who never consented to anything — children raised in a faith, converts, and the religiously indifferent who just want to be left out of the whole machinery.

Two further requests. First, for each requirement, tell me whether it is genuinely non-negotiable or whether it looks more like the prejudice of one national tradition dressed up as a universal principle — I have a suspicion that some of what Americans call neutrality is just separationism and some of what the French call neutrality is just laïcité, and I want that suspicion tested rather than assumed. Second, flag any pair of desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied; for instance, I can imagine "no citizen should be compelled to fund religion" conflicting with "the state may treat religious bodies as public-service providers on equal terms with secular ones," and if a tension like that is real, the list itself becomes a finding I can build the project around. The standard to hold the theory to is this: it should be good enough to advise a constitutional convention for a religiously plural country starting from scratch. If meeting the full list would rule out all four existing systems, say so plainly. You may close with a brief scorecard of the main contenders — strict separation, even-handed accommodation, and moderate establishment — against the list, but keep it short, because the criteria are the part I actually need.
