I chair my university's animal research ethics committee, and I need you to act as a philosopher of mind with expertise in animal sentience who has been co-opted onto the committee as an external advisor. Please think carefully about the case below before writing; I'd suggest beginning your process with a brief brainstorm of the genuinely hard points, then drafting the memo itself.

The case. A behavioural neuroscience group has applied to run a series of experiments on common shore crabs. The stated aim is to test whether crabs merely react to noxious stimuli or actually learn from them in ways that suggest an unpleasant experience: the protocol involves repeated mild electric shocks in one arm of a maze, and measures whether the crabs later avoid that arm even when a preferred dark shelter there would otherwise attract them, whether they will trade off shelter against shock avoidance, and whether the avoidance persists and generalises. Our institution's policy, following national law, treats vertebrates and cephalopods as protected: protocols involving them require anaesthesia where possible, humane endpoints, and formal justification of any suffering. Decapod crustaceans are unprotected, so strictly speaking the committee could wave this through. But we have discretion to impose conditions on any animal work, and the committee is split. One faction argues we should apply a precautionary principle: where the evidence of pain is genuinely open, we should treat the animals as if they can suffer, which in practice means requiring analgesia or anaesthesia wherever compatible with the science, and humane endpoints. The other faction argues that precaution here is not cost-free: it sets a precedent that could engulf all invertebrate work, and in this case it may be self-defeating, since dosing the crabs with analgesics would interfere with the very behavioural readouts the experiment uses to detect pain-like processing.

Please draft the committee's decision memo. It must:

- Reach a clear bottom line: approve, reject, or approve with specified conditions (if conditions, make them precise enough for the researchers to comply with and for us to enforce).
- State what the committee takes to be the relevant distinction between nociception and felt pain, and which kinds of behavioural evidence -- trade-offs, flexible learning, self-protective behaviour, motivational states -- should carry weight, and why; the researchers need to know what showing would or would not have changed our minds.
- Settle the burden-of-proof question explicitly: should unprotected animals be presumed insentient until shown otherwise, presumed sentient where plausible, or handled under some intermediate rule? Give the committee a formulation it could reuse in future cases involving other invertebrates.
- Engage seriously with the strongest version of the losing side's position, including the worry that precaution is self-defeating when the experiment is itself the source of the evidence that precaution depends on.

Write the memo in formal British English, addressed to the principal investigator and copied to the full committee. Around 800 to 1,100 words.
