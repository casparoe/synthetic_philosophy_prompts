I am a retired molecular biologist who spent forty years in academic research and have, rather late in life, developed a serious interest in philosophy of science and applied ethics. Lately I have been following the debate about whether the weights of frontier AI models should be released openly, and I have been trying to write down where I actually stand, since I find that my instincts, formed in a different field, may not all pull in the same direction. Before I embarrass myself at the reading group I recently joined, I would be grateful for an audit of the following commitments. I hold: (1) that openness is the default norm of science, and that any departure from it — secrecy, embargo, redaction — carries the burden of justification; (2) that nonetheless the methodological details of certain gain-of-function experiments on potential pandemic pathogens should not be published, because some information is dangerous in proportion to how usable it is, and this is a justified exception to (1); (3) that whether frontier AI weights ought to be openly released is fundamentally an empirical question about net consequences, to be settled by evidence about misuse, diffusion, and defensive benefits rather than by any a priori principle; (4) that even if the evidence were to show that open release of such weights is net harmful, governments ought not to possess the authority to prohibit the publication of research artifacts, since a state licensing scheme for scientific speech is a greater evil than the harms it would prevent; and (5) that the historical record favors openness even in security-sensitive domains — open cryptography made systems more secure, not less — and I expect the same dynamic to hold for AI capabilities.

What I would like is not a survey of the public debate, which I have already read, but a consistency check of this particular set of commitments. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and how severe is each tension? I ask you to rank rather than to flag everything indiscriminately: I suspect at least one apparent conflict here dissolves on closer inspection, and I would rather have that shown to me than have every pair treated as equally problematic. For each genuine tension, please identify what exactly generates it — whether two principles conflict directly, or whether a principle and a verdict on a case cannot be jointly motivated in the way I seem to want — and state what the least costly revision would be and what it would cost me. I am especially interested in whether the biological analogy I rely on in (2) and (5) can be made to do the work I need it to do, or whether it quietly commits me to a position on AI that I do not in fact hold.

A few requests about form. Do not tell me which of the five commitments you think I should abandon in the end; I want to see the structure of the problem before deciding. Do not recast my position in terms of published authors or named schools unless doing so is genuinely necessary to make a point. And where you think two of my commitments are only apparently in tension, please show the dissolution explicitly rather than merely asserting it, since learning to perform that kind of discrimination myself is half the point of the exercise.
