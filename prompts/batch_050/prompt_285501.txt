At a philosophy department mixer, two grad students I know got into an argument about falsificationism that ended in a stalemate, and I'd like you to referee it. Here's the exchange as I remember it:

(1) Anna: The demarcation between science and non-science is simple: a theory is scientific only if it's falsifiable -- only if it rules out some possible observations. Astrology fails this test because its predictions are so elastic that nothing could count against them, while good physics sticks its neck out. That's the whole criterion and it works.

(2) Ben: No theory is falsifiable by itself, because predictions only follow from a theory plus a stack of auxiliary assumptions, and when a prediction fails you can always blame an auxiliary. That's not a pathology -- it's how science proceeds: when Uranus's orbit didn't match Newtonian predictions, astronomers didn't abandon Newton; they posited an unseen planet, and found Neptune. Since every theory can be protected this way, falsifiability can't demarcate anything.

(3) Anna: Fine, then demarcate practices rather than theories. What makes a field scientific is that its practitioners sometimes accept falsification instead of endlessly deflecting. Astronomers deflected once and then checked their auxiliary; astrologers deflect forever and never pay a price. The criterion survives -- it just applies to communities, not to sentences.

(4) Ben: But then the logic of falsification isn't doing the work; judgments about respectable behavior are, and those judgments cut against you. Astronomers also knew Mercury's perihelion didn't fit Newtonian mechanics and shrugged it off for half a century, and that turned out to be the right call. So sometimes persistent deflection is good science and sometimes quick surrender would be malpractice. Your criterion says "deflect the right amount" -- which just renames the problem it was supposed to solve.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four steps, say whether it's clearly right, clearly mistaken, or right-in-a-way-that-concedes-more-than-the-speaker-realizes, and justify each rating. Then say who, if anyone, is more right overall, and what both of them are missing.
