I retired a few years back after 35-odd years as an experimental physicist, and in an effort not to go stir-crazy I signed up for a philosophy evening course at the local adult education college — and somehow ended up co-running the thing. We've got about 14 regulars, ages roughly 25 to 75, most with no background in philosphy at all, meeting for 90 minutes on Thursday nights. Last month I came across Warren Quinn's self-torturer case and it properly got under my skin. Roughly as I understand it: a man has a device wired into him with a dial of a thousand settings; each notch up pays him $10,000 and increases his pain by an amount too small to consciously register, but at the top of the dial he's in agony. Every single step seems obviously worth taking, so up he goes — and then coming back down has the same trap in reverse, since each notch down relieves only an imperceptible amount of pain while costing him ten grand, so he ends up yo-yoing, miserable most of the time, by a string of choices each of which seemed fine on its own.

What I'd like from you is an activity or game I can actually run with this group that makes them feel the pull of the case rather than just nod along at it. No lecture up front — ideally they don't hear Quinn's name or the story until afterwards. They should be making real, incremental choices with some small but genuine stake (I can supply a few pounds in coins, decent chocolate, whatever works best), and the rules should be arranged so that, if all goes to plan, most of them end up somewhere none of them would have picked at the outset, and only then reconstruct how they got there. Give me concrete rules, materials, a rough timeline across the 90 minutes, and what exactly I should do and say as facilitator. One design question I'd like you to tackle head-on: in Quinn's case the pain increments are literally imperceptible, which is presumably impossible to stage in a classroom — so should the increments in my game be imperceptible too, or merely each individually attractive, and what philosophically hangs on that difference?

Then I'd like a debrief plan that maps what happened in the game onto the case: the climb up, the moment people realise where they've landed, whether your game can produce the coming-back-down oscillation or whether that's simply beyond what a game can do, and the precommitment puzzle — would the rational move have been to announce a stopping point in advance, and if so, how do you pick one when every adjacent pair of settings looks identical? Also be honest with me about what will predictably go wrong when I run it (there's always one clever clogs who spots the trap in the first minute and refuses to play — should I recruit that person as a confederate, or is there a better fix?), and which feature of Quinn's puzzle the activity cannot reproduce so I'll have to supply it in words instead. A bit of real-world resonance in the debrief is welcome — I keep thinking of boiling frogs, pension contributions, and my own blood pressure medication — but I'd rather the game itself stay abstract.
