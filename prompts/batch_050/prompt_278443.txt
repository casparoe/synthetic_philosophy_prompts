The dean of a small translation & interpreting school has, for reasons known only to her, invited me to give this year's commencement address. Reasons known only to her because I left the profession ten years ago and now run a company whose machine translation pipeline has, by my own rough count, wiped out more entry-level translation jobs in our region than any other single thing. I said yes anyway. Now I have to write the thing.

Here's my problem. The speech everyone expects -- the one the faculty row is braced for and the parents are hoping for -- goes: machines convert words, but only you understand meaning; comprehension is the real skill, and the machine only has a hollow copy of it. I think that speech is false and I won't give a false speech. I'm sold on the Darwin-and-Turing point: competence doesn't require comprehension. Darwin showed you can get exquisitely competent organisms out of a process where nobody understands anything; Turing showed you can get flawless performance out of pure mechanism. Comprehension isn't the engine of skill, it's one of skill's occasional byproducts. My own product is, frankly, exhibit A: it comprehends nothing and out-translates most of the juniors I used to hire.

So the speech has to concede, out loud and without weasling, that these 120 graduates spent four years training in a skill that a system with zero understanding now does better than most of them -- and then give them a true reason, not a consolation prize, that those years werent wasted and their careers aren't over. Mixed audience: grads who know exactly how good the models are, parents who half-know, and professors who built the whole curriculum on the comprehension pitch and will hear any attack on it as an attack on them personally. About ten minutes. No heartwarming made-up anecdote from my career, no "human touch" sentimentality, and the Darwin/Turing idea has to be load-bearing -- the speech's actual answer has to fall out of it, not just get name-dropped in minute two. If the only honest conclusion is that I should've declined the invitation, tell me that instead of writing it.
