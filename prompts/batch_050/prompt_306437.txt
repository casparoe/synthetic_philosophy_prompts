Two people I work with have been fighting about how to handle uncertainty in a risk estimate and neither is backing down, its been going on for two weeks now. I want a referee.

The setup: our team has to put a number on the chance that a new sensor design fails within its first year. There's no real track record for this design -- nothing close enough to it to do honest base rates -- and the simulation spits out somewhere between 2% and 40% depending on assumptions nobody can check. The disagreement is about what to do with that.

Mara says you just have to commit to a single number, call it 15%, and get on with life. Her case, roughly: (1) Any actual decision reveals a point estimate anyway. The moment you act -- accept the project, price the warranty, whatever -- you've behaved as if some specific number were true, so the interval is just precision-avoidance dressed up as humility. (2) If you really can't justify any value in the range over any other, indifference says treat them symmetrically; refusing to do that is secretly smuggling in information you don't have. (3) Imprecise credences license crazy behavior: refusing both sides of a bet on the same event, or paying to avoid being shown free information, and any framework that recommends that has gone wrong somewhere.

Deniz pushes back on all three: (1) Acting as if 15% doesn't mean believing 15% -- she's confusing the decision rule with the epistemic state. You can be forced to act without being forced to pretend your evidence is better than it is. (2) A single number can't distinguish "15% based on ten years of failure data on near-identical parts" from "honestly no idea, 2% and 40% are both live," and those are evidentially completely different situations. A representation that can't tell them apart is throwing away information, not being rigorous. (3) The free-information point isn't a bug: if a new measurement could stretch your interval further without giving you anything you'd actually act on, declining it is the correct response to knowing your own evidence is thin.

Then Mara: the interval doesn't even solve the representation problem, because now you owe an account of how confident you are in the bounds -- is it definitely 2% to 40%, or could it be 1% to 60%? -- and you're off on a regress of intervals about intervals. Her single number plus ordinary stated humility gets the same job done with one layer instead of infinitely many. Deniz's response, which I think was meant as a joke but he meant it: "one number plus stated humility" is just an interval wearing a trench coat.

Please go through this move by move -- say who wins each of the specific points (the revelation argument, the indifference argument, the both-sides-of-the-bet objection, the representation argument, the regress) rather than just crowning an overall winner. Then say who's more right on balance, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. And at the end, tell me what would change your verdict: which argument or consideration, if it held up, would flip the overall call, and where in your own reasoning the failure is most likely to be hiding.
