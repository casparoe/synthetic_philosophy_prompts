In a reading group on twentieth-century French philosophy, we spent an evening on Beauvoir's famous dictum that one is not born but rather becomes a woman -- the line that opens the second volume of The Second Sex, and one I had always treated as a single, self-evident claim. The evening disabused me. One member of the group deployed it to argue that since womanhood is acquired rather than given, anyone who undergoes the relevant acquisition counts as a woman in the only sense that matters; a second deployed it in nearly the opposite direction, to argue that femininity is an imposed destiny that a liberated woman should refuse to become; a third used it to wave away a question about whether any psychological sex differences might be partly biological, on the grounds that the dictum had already settled that nothing is. It became clear that the sentence was doing at least three different jobs, and I would like a proper disambiguation. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses this dictum could be expressing, varying along several dimensions at once: what "woman" denotes (a biological class, a social position, an identity, a mythic construction), what kind of "becoming" is at issue (ordinary maturation, socialization, subordination to the status of Other, an existential taking-up of one's embodied situation, or the repeated performance of norms), and how strong the modal claim is (that some, most, or all of what marks women as women is acquired rather than innate).

For each thesis on the menu, give a verdict: is it a platitude (nobody is born an adult, everyone is shaped by upbringing), a substantive but defensible empirical or phenomenological claim, or a claim that is probably false as stated (say, that biology contributes nothing at all to the differences the dictum ranges over)? Then tell me which reading is closest to Beauvoir's own argument in context -- bearing in mind that the second volume follows a long examination of biology, psychoanalysis, and historical materialism as candidate explanations of women's situation, each found insufficient on its own -- and which readings are later reconstructions, friendly or unfriendly, that the text does not obviously license. Finally, and most important to me: for each of the three uses I described from the reading group, identify which precisification the speaker actually needed for the conclusion they drew, and whether the argument survives once the slogan is replaced by that precise thesis. I suspect the first speaker's use requires a far stronger claim than Beauvoir makes, and the third speaker's use requires one that is empirically false, but I want that shown rather than asserted.

Where the scholarly record bears on any of this, please separate carefully what is settled among people who know Beauvoir's work from what is genuinely contested -- for instance, whether she is best read as a forerunner of social constructionism or as a phenomenologist of the lived, already-sexed body, and how her framework relates to later performative accounts of gender. Do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus view; if the translations differ in ways that matter for the precisification, note that too.
