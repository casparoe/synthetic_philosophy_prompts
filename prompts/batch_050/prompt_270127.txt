I serve on an advisory panel for a research institute that has to decide which systems — biological, artificial, and borderline — fall under the scope of its welfare review process. The panel is deadlocked between two factions: one insists that anything passing sufficiently rich behavioral tests deserves precautionary moral consideration, while the other insists that only systems with the right kind of biological substrate (neurons, nociceptors, homeostatic regulation) could possibly matter. A colleague has tried to break the impasse with a proposal she calls the "valenced self-maintenance criterion": roughly, a computation matters morally to the degree that it (a) maintains a temporally extended internal model of the system's own condition, (b) tags states of that model with a value signal that is used to arbitrate trade-offs between competing goals, and (c) runs differently under threat of damage to the system than under benefit to it, where the difference is not merely a lookup table of fixed responses. She claims this criterion is substrate-neutral without being promiscuous — it lets in more than mammals but less than "anything with a feedback loop."

Here is the slate of systems the panel must sort: a honeybee's flight-and-foraging control system; an octopus arm executing a semi-autonomous reaching routine while the animal sleeps; a reinforcement-learning agent whose reward includes a curiosity bonus and a penalty for simulated bodily damage, trained in a physics simulator; a human patient in a persistent vegetative state with preserved brainstem reflexes; a cerebral organoid displaying spontaneous synchronized electrical activity; a large language model midway through generating an extended chain of reasoning; and a ordinary home thermostat (included, I suspect, as a control case). Please rank these seven systems by the degree of moral standing the valenced self-maintenance criterion assigns them, grouping them into tiers where the criterion cannot cleanly separate them, and for each tier state in a sentence or two which clause of the criterion is doing the work.

Then, in a second pass, tell me where the exercise becomes least trustworthy: identify the two rankings in your list that are most sensitive to disputed empirical or conceptual premises, and say what would have to be true for each to flip. Finally, assess the criterion itself: does it actually avoid the two factions' failure modes — the behaviorists' willingness to count any sufficiently expressive system, and the biologists' circular appeal to neurons — or does it smuggle one of those commitments back in through clause (a), (b), or (c)? If you think it smuggles, name the clause and explain what it covertly presupposes.
