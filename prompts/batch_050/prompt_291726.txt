I'm putting together a seminar on promising and want to open with J. K. Rowling's Harry Potter and the Half-Blood Prince. Early in the book, Snape makes an Unbreakable Vow to Narcissa Malfoy — a magically enforced promise whose breach means death — swearing to watch over Draco, shield him from harm, and carry out his mission should Draco fail. Read this device through the question of why promises bind at all. The vow looks like a blunt solution to Hume's old puzzle about how mere words can generate a new obligation: it replaces normative binding with causal necessity. But arguably it thereby misses the point — if Snape complies because noncompliance kills him, is he keeping a promise or merely being prudent? Test the device against the main candidate accounts: Hume's conventionalism, Scanlon-style expectation or assurance views, and rights-transfer theories. Note that Narcissa demands the vow precisely because she does not trust Snape, which suggests magical enforcement substitutes for exactly what promising is meant to supply. Then complicate the reading: Snape fulfills the vow by killing Dumbledore, something Dumbledore himself had privately asked of him, so a single act discharges two commitments that appear to conflict. What does this show about whether bindingness tracks what the promisee actually wants, and about the difference between fidelity to a promise and fidelity to a person? Close by saying where the novel's device resists the philosophical reading — where the magic is doing dramatic rather than conceptual work.
