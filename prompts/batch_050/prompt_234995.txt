so heres a situation my sister and i have been going back and forth on for months, and i want an actual essay about it, not a survey of positions.

Our parents raised us decently. stable home, school fees paid, no abuse, nothing dramatic. but it was always more dutiful than warm — affection mostly expressed through providing — and our dad once said at a family dinner that they did it because it was their job, not to put us in their debt. Now he's got early-stage dementia, our mum can't manage alone, and the unspoken expectation in the family is that my sister, who lives two hours away and is unmarried, should move back and become the main carer. Here's the complication: neither of us is really friends with our parents. phone calls are obligation calls. my sister doesn't confide in them, doesn't especially enjoy their company, and freely admits that if she'd met them as strangers somewhere she'd never have stayed in touch.

Enter Jane English's friendship model of filial obligation: roughly, that grown children don't owe their parents repayment of a debt, because parental sacrifices are more like gifts or favors done out of love and unrequested gifts don't generate debts, but instead owe them something closer to what friends owe friends, where the content of the obligation scales with the actual relationship between the people involved. The question i want the essay to answer: on this model, does my sister owe it to them to move back?

Take the model seriously and apply it properly, including the awkward bits. Whether a relationship can count as a friendship when neither party chose it and one side did years of unrequested sacrifice. Whether friendship-obligations can ever be that demanding in the first place — you wouldn't normally expect even a good friend to relocate their whole life for you, so if the model generates that here it might be generating too much, or maybe friendship obligations are more demanding than people assume. And whether the model has anything to say to parents who did everything right materially but never built the very friendship the obligation is supposed to scale with — does their dutifulness buy them anything, or does it just turn out they get what friends who drift apart get, which is not much?

Also give the pushback its best run rather than strawmanning it: the worry that the model lets adult children off way too lightly, that a duty which evaporates when a relationship goes cold is too contingent a foundation for something as serious as elder care, and that maybe there's a floor of gratitude or need-based duty sitting underneath whatever the friendship supplies. But dont just map the terrain and shrug — take a position on what my sister actually owes, whether that's whatever the friendship model strictly delivers, a modified version of it, or a different account altogether, and defend it.

Two requests about how you write it. First, be honest about the state of the debate: separate what people working on filial obligation broadly agree on from what's genuinely contested, and dont present a contested view as the consensus. Second, no formatting at all — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. continuous prose only, the way you'd write a long email to a friend who thinks in paragraphs.
