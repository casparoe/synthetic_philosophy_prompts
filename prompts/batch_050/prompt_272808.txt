My reading group just finished Kazuo Ishiguro's The Buried Giant, and we fell into an argument we couldn't resolve about whether the novel takes sides in the debate between what I'll call the ethics of memory and the case for forgetting. For context: in the novel, post-Arthurian Britain lies under a mist, breathed by the she-dragon Querig, that has erased both personal and collective memory -- Britons and Saxons live together in uneasy peace largely because neither can remember the massacres Arthur ordered. The Saxon warrior Wistan wants to slay the dragon so that memory, and with it the grounds for vengeance, will return; the aged Sir Gawain guards her, believing the forgetting is what holds back a new round of slaughter; and the elderly couple Axl and Beatrice journey through all of this hoping to recover the buried memories of their own marriage, which the novel hints contain not only tenderness but loss and possibly betrayal.

We tried to read the book against two philosophical positions. On one side, Avishai Margalit's idea that memory can be an ethical obligation -- that "communities of memory" bound by thick relations of shared history owe remembrance to their dead and to one another, and that a people who let their past dissolve have committed a kind of betrayal rather than merely suffered a misfortune. On the other side, the case David Rieff has pressed for forgetting: that collective memory is less often a wellspring of justice than of grievance, that commemorated atrocity licenses the next atrocity, and that peace sometimes requires what remembrance forbids. The novel seems almost engineered to stage this dispute -- but I suspect a careful reading shows it doing something subtler than refereeing.

Here's what I'd like: a reading that takes both philosophical positions seriously and asks where the novel vindicates each and where it resists both. Some specific pressure points. Does the mist count as forgetting in Rieff's sense when it was imposed by the victors rather than chosen by the reconciled -- that is, is Gawain's peace a Rieffian achievement or a Margalitian betrayal dressed up as one? Does the novel complicate Margalit by suggesting that what returns with restored memory is not identity or justice but simply the warrant for the next war? And does the Axl and Beatrice thread -- including the boatman's testing question and the ending's refusal to promise them a shared crossing -- establish that the ethics of personal memory come apart from the ethics of collective memory, so that the marriage and the nation may require opposite answers? Finally, if the novel does add something to the Margalit-Rieff dispute that the arguments alone don't capture, what is it?
