Over coffee last week, two colleagues of mine — Mara, who teaches epistemology, and Leon, who works in cognitive science — got into an argument I couldn't settle, and I'd like you to referee it. The occasion was a mutual acquaintance, Daniel, whose small business is plainly failing. Daniel insists to everyone, apparently sincerely, that things are turning around; yet he has stopped opening his bank statements, cancels meetings with his accountant, and — tellingly — quietly moved his personal savings into an account the business can't touch. Mara's position was that genuine self-deception is impossible, and Daniel is just confused rather than deceived. Her reasoning: deceiving someone requires getting them to believe what you know to be false, so deceiving yourself would require believing both that the business is failing (in order to hide it) and that it isn't (as the resulting state), and no single mind can hold a flat contradiction. What we call self-deception, she said, is really just some combination of optimism, selective attention, and weakness of will — real phenomena, but mislabeled. Leon replied that the paradox is an artifact of insisting that deception must run through belief. Daniel's behavior, he argued, shows the information is registered somewhere — you don't strategically avoid statements you genuinely believe contain good news — while his sincere avowals show it isn't registered in the place that talks. Self-deception is real; it just works by partitioning rather than by contradiction.

Mara pushed back that "registered somewhere" is doing illicit work: acting on information isn't the same as believing it (a thermostat responds to temperature without believing anything), so Leon hasn't shown Daniel both knows and doesn't know — only that Daniel is uncertain, anxious, and irrationally hopeful, which nobody denies. Leon answered that the avoidance is the smoking gun: mere uncertainty makes people *seek* information, and Daniel is fleeing it, and you cannot calibrate your flight from evidence without some grip on what the evidence would show. Mara conceded that was her strongest point but said it proved too much: on that standard, every dieter who avoids the mirror and every smoker who skips the health documentary is self-deceived, and the concept inflates until it just means motivated reasoning, which we already had a perfectly good name for. Leon's final move was to accuse her of inventing the puzzle she's solving: no one ever claimed the contradiction must be *simultaneous*. Self-deception is diachronic — over months, Daniel steers his attention so the damning belief never quite forms — and steering requires exactly the fleeting, unendorsed contact with the truth that Mara calls "mere avoidance." She said that still isn't deception, just gardening of one's own attention, and deception without a deceived belief is a metaphor. They both turned to me, and I mumbled something and changed the subject.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the positions and rejoinders above, say whether it is sound, confused, or subtly shifting the question, and identify any point where either of them concedes more than they realize. Then give an overall verdict: Is Daniel genuinely self-deceived, on the best available understanding of that term? If the phenomenon survives Mara's deflationary redescription, explain what, if anything, is lost by retiring the word "self-deception" in favor of "motivated reasoning" — and if it doesn't survive, say what a convincing case of real self-deception would have to look like.
