I work on a data platform that aggregates crop-yield forecasts, food-price series, and conflict indicators, and our team has fallen into a recurring argument about what, conceptually, a famine even is. Half of us come at it like distributed-systems people: a famine is a supply-side outage — harvests fail (drought, blight, war destroying infrastructure), aggregate calories fall below subsistence, and mortality follows roughly mechanically. The other half keeps citing Amartya Sen's entitlement analysis of the 1943 Bengal famine and similar work on Ireland in the 1840s and China in 1959–61, arguing that most modern famines are allocation failures: food existed and even moved through or out of the affected region, but certain groups lost their economic or legal ability to command it, so the right unit of analysis is who could demand food, not how much food there was. Each camp accuses the other of building the wrong model.

I have no philosophy or political-economy background, so I can't adjudicate this, but I'd like to turn the fight into something productive. Please draft the joint statement the two camps would produce if forced into an adversarial collaboration before our next architecture review. Specifically:

1. A section of agreed claims both camps can sign — e.g., claims about the historical episodes above (Bengal 1943, the Irish famine, the Great Leap Forward famine), about what a complete explanation of famine mortality must cover, and about what kinds of policy response each model implies. Keep these substantive rather than so hedged they're empty.
2. A disagreements section stating each residual dispute in wording both sides would accept as fair to their own position. I suspect these include: whether aggregate food-availability decline is necessary for famine, merely typical, or neither; whether markets routing food away from starving regions count as "the system working as designed" or as a failure mode; and whether Sen's argument that famines essentially do not happen in functioning democracies with a free press is causal or correlational.
3. For each disagreement, the crux or cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one camp to update — and, where possible, a concrete historical case, data pattern, or thought experiment both sides agree bears on the crux, with each side's prediction about it recorded in advance.
4. A verdict on whether any part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive (e.g., the two camps using "cause of famine" to mean different levels of explanation, the way proximate cause and root cause differ in an incident postmortem).

After the document itself, give me a JSON object summarizing it with this schema: "agreed_claims" (array of strings); "disagreements" (array of objects with fields "neutral_statement", "supply_side_position", "entitlement_position"); "cruxes" (array of objects with fields "crux", "agreed_bearing_evidence", "supply_side_prediction", "entitlement_prediction"); "verbal_components" (array of objects with fields "issue", "why_verbal", "what_would_make_it_substantive").
