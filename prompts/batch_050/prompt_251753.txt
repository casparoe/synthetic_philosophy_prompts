I want a pair of thought experiments about Benatar's asymmetry — the claim that coming into existence is always a harm, because the absence of pain is good even when there's nobody around to enjoy that good, while the absence of pleasure isn't bad when nobody exists to be deprived of it.

First case: a small-scale scenario — one ordinary couple deciding whether to have one kid, realistic prospects, no dystopia, no extinction — where Benatar's asymmetry and a simple symmetric welfarism (pain bad, pleasure good, counted the same way for existing and merely possible people) agree on what the couple should do, but reach the verdict by different routes.

Second case: modify exactly one feature of the first so the two views come apart in their verdicts. Keep it small again — no trillions of future people, no sci-fi, no utility monsters.

Constraints: the kid's life has to be described in ordinary terms (health, work, relationships, how much chronic pain, etc.) rather than labeled "net positive" or "net negative" — the verdicts should fall out of applying the theories to the described life, not from your adjectives. And don't smuggle in person-affecting intuitions to do the asymmetry's work for it; if part of the pull in the first case is just the plain thought that you shouldn't create a miserable person, flag that explicitly.

After the two cases: say which feature you changed and why it's the load-bearing one, walk through each theory's reasoning in each case, and then give the best move available to the symmetric welfarist who wants to resist the verdict in the second case — in particular, whether they can reject Benatar's premise that absent pain is impersonally good without ending up committed to something like totalism and its population-ethics headaches.
