Last night I spent four hours arguing with a close friend about personal identity, and we ended exactly where we started, which has me wondering whether there was ever anything to end at. The setup was the classic one: I pressed Reid's brave officer case against the Lockean memory criterion -- the general remembers storming the standard as a boy, the officer who took the standard remembers being flogged at school but no longer remembers the boyhood storming, so by transitivity of identity the general is the boy even though he cannot remember him, and a memory criterion appears to be caught contradicting the logic of identity itself. My friend, who defends a broadly psychological-continuity view, replied that the objection only bites a naive one-step memory criterion and dissolves once you allow overlapping chains of memory connections -- the general is connected to the boy indirectly, through the officer, and that's all the view ever needed. I countered that this fix just relocates the problem, because Reid's case can be rebuilt so that no chain exists (say the officer's memories were wiped between stages), and the real question is whether the friend is willing to deny identity in cases where we'd all say the same person persists -- amnesia, deep dementia, the person I was at two years old.

Here is what I want diagnosed, not adjudicated. We both knew all of these moves going in -- the transitivity objection, the chain fix, the circularity worry about defining memory in terms of identity rather than the other way around, the point that Locke's talk of persons may have been doing forensic work about responsibility and punishment rather than metaphysical work about what persists. Nothing either of us said was news to the other, and nothing moved either of us. So: what kind of disagreement is this, really? My tentative hypothesis is that my friend and I are actually answering different questions under one heading -- he is asking what relation must hold for later-me to inherit the concerns and obligations of earlier-me, which is a practical question memory naturally answers, while I am asking what makes a future being numerically identical with me, which is a metaphysical question that Reid's case shows memory cannot answer -- and that the debate stalls because each side's favorite cases (responsibility for past crimes for him, the logic of identity for me) are simply data for different inquiries. A rival hypothesis I considered is that the disagreement is substantive but rests on irreconcilable starting intuitions about which cases are the fixed points: I treat the transitivity of identity as non-negotiable, he treats it as obvious that the demented patient is the same person as the vigorous author, and neither of us can argue for our fixed points without begging the question. And a third possibility is that the dispute is at bottom verbal, hinging on an equivocation between identity in the strict logical sense and 'identity' as a placeholder for whatever matters in survival.

Tell me which diagnosis best fits, using what each side in this long-running dispute actually says and where each side's arguments gain their grip. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what progress would even look like: a conceptual distinction that would let both sides keep their commitments by partitioning the question, a new kind of case that could serve as a neutral test, or an argument that no such progress is available because the impasse reflects something about the concept of a person itself rather than about our ignorance of the facts.
