I’m giving a short talk to a graduate seminar split between philosophers of mind sympathetic to biological naturalism and formal epistemologists who get twitchy about self-locating probabilities. My thesis is narrower than the usual headline version of Nick Bostrom’s “Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?”: I’m arguing that the simulation argument doesn’t by itself make it probable that we’re simulated, but it does create a consistency constraint—if you expect future civilizations to run many ancestor-simulations, you ought to assign high credence to being in one.

My talk leans on Bostrom’s claim that “It is not an essential property of consciousness that it is implemented on carbon-based biological neural networks inside a cranium: silicon-based processors inside a computer could in principle do the trick as well.” Prepare me for the Q&A: what objections will each half of the audience raise, phrased the way they’d actually phrase them? Which are most damaging? What should I concede, what should I push back on, and what’s the best two-minute reply to each? Please include the objection I’m hoping nobody asks, especially anything turning on whether that passage quietly assumes a stronger computational theory of mind than my narrower thesis needs.
