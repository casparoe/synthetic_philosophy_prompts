You are a philosopher with expertise in both epistemic logic and game theory, and you are known for carefully auditing the analogies that connect the two fields. Please think carefully and methodically; it may help to begin by informally brainstorming the structural features of each puzzle before producing your polished answer.

Here is the situation. A colleague who works on game theory insists that the surprise examination paradox is, in her words, essentially just the backward-induction puzzle of finitely repeated games dressed up in epistemic clothing. Her argument runs as follows. In a finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma or a centipede game, backward induction unravels cooperation from the last round: in the final round there is no future to protect, so defection is dominant; given that, the second-to-last round is effectively the last, and so on until the whole cooperative structure collapses. She claims the surprise exam works the same way: the exam cannot fall on the last day (Friday), since on Thursday evening the students would know it; with Friday eliminated, Thursday becomes the effective last day and is eliminated by the same move; the announcement unravels day by day, exactly as cooperation unravels round by round. She concludes that anyone who finds the surprise exam genuinely puzzling, while accepting backward-induction reasoning in games, is being inconsistent — the two puzzles stand or fall together, so a solution to one is automatically a solution to the other.

I am not convinced the two puzzles are the same in the respects that matter, but I also can't easily dismiss the parallel. Please audit her analogy. Specifically:

1. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine. What plays the role of the final round, of dominance, of common knowledge of rationality, of the unraveling step itself, on the surprise-exam side?
2. Identify the disanalogies, and separate the ones that matter for her intended conclusion from the ones that don't. Some candidates I have in mind: the exam announcement is made by a trusted source whose testimony the students continue to believe even as they use it to derive a contradiction, whereas nothing analogous to a trusted announcement exists in the game; the exam case trades in knowledge and what can be known in advance, while the game case trades in rational choice and what payoffs make eligible; and the exam announcement seems to be self-undermining in a way that mere rationality assumptions are not, since on the final day the students would both know the exam is coming and know (from the teacher) that they wouldn't. Assess whether these differences, and any others you find, actually do any work.
3. Test her conclusion: is it true that the puzzles stand or fall together, such that dissolving one dissolves the other? If a resolution of backward induction (say, denying that common knowledge of rationality holds all the way down) fails to touch the exam paradox, or vice versa, explain exactly where the transfer breaks.
4. If the analogy misleads more than it illuminates, propose a better one — either a closer game-theoretic construction for the exam paradox, or a better epistemic analogue for backward induction — and say briefly what it gets right that hers gets wrong.

Keep the analysis self-contained and precise; I want to be able to send your answer to my colleague without either of us needing to look anything up.
