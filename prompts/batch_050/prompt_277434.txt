Yesterday I spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend about whether a person can assign probabilities to their own not-yet-made choices. She defends the thesis associated with Isaac Levi and Wolfgang Spohn — roughly, that genuine deliberation crowds out prediction: while you are still deciding what to do, your own forthcoming action cannot figure in your reasoning as just one more event with a probability attached, on pain of making the deliberation idle or self-undermining. I think there are perfectly ordinary situations in which a rational agent does exactly this, and does it without anything going wrong. We could not settle it, so I want a case built to order.

Construct a pair of matched thought experiments that isolate what is actually at stake. First, a scenario in which an agent rationally assigns a probability to their own imminent, still-open choice, and the assignment leaves their deliberation fully intact — the prediction neither makes the decision pointless nor rigged its outcome in advance. Second, a twin scenario that differs from the first in exactly one structural feature, where attempting to assign such a probability does become pathological: the agent's self-prediction short-circuits or trivializes the very decision it concerns. Constraints: both scenarios must be realistic and present-day — a workplace, a hospital corridor, a grant committee — with no perfect predictors, no Newcomb-style demons, no neurotechnology reading the agent's intentions off a scanner; the evidence on which the agent bases the self-ascription must be of an ordinary kind (base rates about people in their position, their own track record, testimony from colleagues, a hard deadline). Each scenario should run about one paragraph.

After the two scenarios, do the check. For the first case, state the probability the agent assigns, the evidence it rests on, and why the assignment leaves deliberation untouched. Then identify the single feature that, when flipped, produces the pathology in the twin case, and explain what happens if that feature is removed from the first scenario — does it collapse back into the Levi–Spohn picture, or does something else carry the weight? Finally, say how a defender of the thesis would attack the first scenario (for instance, by insisting that what the agent predicts is a settled disposition rather than a genuinely live choice, so no real counterexample has been produced), and whether that attack succeeds. Please write your answer in American English.
