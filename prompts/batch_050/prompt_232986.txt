Now that I'm retired (35 years as a biochemist, mostly enzyme kinetics) I've been going to a philosophy evening class at the community college, and we just spent three sessions on Bernard Williams and the whole internal vs. external reasons debate. I came in sympathetic to the internalist side because it strikes me as the naturalistic option — a reason has to get some kind of grip on an actual human psyche, not hover in normative space issuing commands to people who couldn't care less. Next week each of us has to bring a written statement of our own position for the group to tear apart, and the tutor read mine over and told me afterwards that it has "at least one real crack in it" but refused point blank to say where. So here's what I've got, and I'd like a proper audit before I embarass myself:

1. A consideration only counts as a reason for me to act if I could come to be motivated by it through sound deliberation starting from what I actually want — correcting my factual errors and ironing out inconsistencies, but not stapling brand-new desires onto me from the outside.
2. It follows that a genuinely cold psychopath, someone who couldn't be argued into caring about others from any of the motivations he really has, has no reason not to kill. He's not being irrational, and we're entitled to lock him up, but reasons-talk just doesn't reach him.
3. Blaming someone is only fair if they had a reason to act otherwise; blaming people for what they had no reason to avoid is like blaming them for the weather.
4. The psychopath in (2) is fully blameworthy for what he does — vicious rather than excused — and I'd blame him exactly as much as anyone else who did the same.
5. Ordinary decent people really do have reasons not to lie, cheat, and freeload, even when they'd profit from it; moral demands apply to them in full force.
6. Ends themselves can't be irrational — you can criticise someone's means or their false beliefs, but not their bare wants, and in that respect I'm with Hume that reason works for the passions rather than the other way round.

What I'm after is an audit, not a lecture on Williams: which of these genuinely collide and which only look like they do from a distance, and for each real collision, what exactly would have to give and what it would cost me. It all reads to me like one tidy package, which is probably exactly why I can't see the crack. Give your verdict as exactly six numbered points, each no more than two sentences, ordered from the most damaging problem to the least (slotting in merely apparent tensions wherever they land in that ordering), with the final point naming the single revision that restores the most consistency for the least damage — and no introduction or wrap-up, just the six points.
