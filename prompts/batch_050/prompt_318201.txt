Here is William James, in the chapter "The Mind-Stuff Theory" of The Principles of Psychology (1890), stating his objection to the idea that minds are built out of smaller minds:

"One of the obscurest of the assumptions of which I speak is the assumption that our mental states are composite in structure, made up of smaller states conjoined. This hypothesis has outward advantages which make it almost irresistibly attractive to the intellect, and yet it is inwardly quite unintelligible. Of its unintelligibility, however, half the writers on psychology seem unaware."

I am designing the final unit of a graduate seminar on panpsychism, and what I want is a set of examinable criteria rather than another tour of the literature. Write the list of requirements a theory of mental combination must meet to count as making the composite-mind hypothesis intelligible in the face of James's charge -- that is, to count as a solution to the combination problem rather than a redescription of it. For each requirement, give the consideration that motivates it, and state whether it binds any adequate theory whatever or is instead a commitment of one particular camp (constitutive panpsychists, cosmopsychists, phenomenal bonding theorists, and so on). Two things I especially want adjudicated: whether the demand that combination be intelligible, rather than an accepted brute fact about experience, is a legitimate desideratum or merely James's prejudice against emergence restated; and whether the finished requirements are jointly satisfiable at all, or whether some pair of them -- the privacy of the component subjects, say, and the derivation of the composite's unity from them alone -- can be shown to conflict, so that the list itself functions as an argument against the view it was meant to test. A short assessment of how the main current strategies fare against the completed list is welcome, but the list is the deliverable.
