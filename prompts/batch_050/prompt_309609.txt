So I just finished jury duty two weeks ago and I can't stop thinking about it, because I ended up being basically the Juror 8 of my trial -- lone not-guilty vote at the start, everyone else ready to convict in twenty minutes and go home. We eventually hung, and I still dont know if I did the right thing or was just being stuborn. Anyway it made me rewatch 12 Angry Men and now I want to write something about it, so help me think this through.

Read the film through the conciliationism vs. steadfastness debate in the epistemology of peer disagreement. The hook I want to use is this bit from Mill's On Liberty (chapter 2, "Of the Liberty of Thought and Discussion"), which I found while doomscrolling about my verdict:

"while every one well knows himself to be fallible, few think it necessary to take any precautions against their own fallibility, or admit the supposition that any opinion, of which they feel very certain, may be one of the examples of the error to which they acknowledge themselves to be liable."

Is Mill's point here basically the conciliationist point -- that finding out eleven equally smart people looked at the same evidence and landed on guilty is itself evidence you have to discount your own view by? And is Juror 8 then doing something epistemically shady, or does the film give him a principled out? I'm especially interested in the fact that he doesn't actually claim to know the kid is innocent at the start, he just refuses to move with the majority -- does that make him a steadfast holdout, or is he secretly the only real conciliationist in the room, taking the disagreement itself seriously as a reason to slow down? Walk me through some of the big set pieces (the initial 11-1 vote, the knife, the old man's testimony, Juror 3's whole thing) and say which position each scene seems to vindicate, and where the film quietly rigs the game -- e.g. it turns out the dissenters were *right* about the evidence, which flatters steadfastness, but real holdouts don't get that vindication.

One thing I want you to be careful about: in the actual disagreement literature, separate what's basically agreed on from what's still live. My impression is conciliationism vs. steadfastness is genuinely contested, so don't write as if one side has won and the film settles it. If there are points where philosophers mostly agree (like, disagreement should do *something* to your confidence in most cases?) flag that as distinct from the open questions. And say where the film dramatizes something the formal literature on disagreement has trouble capturing -- like the social pressure part, the fact that jurors flip partly to end the discomfort, not because anyone's credence moved.
