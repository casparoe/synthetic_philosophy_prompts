I'm TAing for an undergrad philosophy of mind course this term and the professor has me grading the short-answer section of the midterm. Most of them are easy calls, but there's one I keep going back and forth on and I'd like a second read before I commit to a number. The question on the exam was:

Explain what Dennett means by the Cartesian theater and why he thinks this picture survives the rejection of dualism. Then lay out the multiple drafts model of consciousness and show how it applies to at least one empirical case involving apparent motion or perceptual revision.

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

Dennett's Cartesian theater is the idea that there is a single place in the brain where everything comes together, a screen where all the sensory inputs arrive and get presented to an inner observer. He connects this to Descartes, who located mind-body interaction in the pineal gland, but his real point is that giving up dualism doesn't free you from the picture. Materialists still tend to assume there is some finish line in the brain where unconscious processing ends and experience begins, which Dennett calls Cartesian materialism. Any view like that either needs a homunculus watching the show, which just regresses, or leaves it a total mystery why arriving at that one spot should amount to being conscious.

The multiple drafts model is his positive replacement. On this view, perception and thought are carried out by lots of parallel processes of interpretation happening at different places and times, and contents get fixed at many points rather than being funneled into one canonical narrative. Probing the system at different moments yields different drafts, and which draft you get depends partly on when and how you ask. Consciousness ends up being less like a performance in a theater and more like fame in the brain, just a matter of which contents win influence over memory, behavior, and verbal report.

The color phi experiments illustrate this nicely. Subjects are shown a red spot and then, shortly after, a green spot in a different location, and they report seeing one spot that moves and changes color partway across. Dennett considers two hypotheses: an Orwellian one, where the brain fabricates the motion and color change before the content reaches consciousness, like a show trial staging the evidence, and a Stalinesque one, where the experience is veridical at the time but memory gets rewritten afterward. He argues that at short enough timescales there is no fact of the matter about which hypothesis is right, since there is no finish line before which the fabrication would have to occur. He concludes that verbal reports of experience can't be trusted as evidence, which is why introspection-based psychology should be abandoned.
