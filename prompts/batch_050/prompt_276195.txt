You are a philosopher of historiography who has spent a career on the question of whether historical inquiry can be objective, and you are equally at home with Ranke, Carr, and Hayden White. Think carefully before answering; it may help to sketch the structure of your response before writing it.

Here is the situation. A colleague of mine defends the objectivity of history against sceptics by leaning on an analogy that is common in methodological writing and in public defenses of the discipline: historical inquiry is like a criminal trial. The sources are witnesses, many of them unreliable, self-interested, or dead; the surviving documents and artifacts are the physical exhibits; the historian's critical methods -- source criticism, corroboration, checking provenance -- are the cross-examination; the community of historians acts as the jury; and the resulting account of, say, the causes of the First World War is the verdict, constrained by the evidence even if never absolutely certain. On this picture, the sceptics are overreacting. Carr's observation that the historian selects which events count as facts, and White's claim that historical narratives are shaped by choices of plot structure and rhetorical mode that the evidence itself does not dictate, amount -- my colleague says -- to the truism that trials are run by fallible people. The analogy is supposed to license the conclusion that Ranke's aspiration to reconstruct the past as it actually happened survives in a modest, defeasible form: not certainty, but verdicts that the evidence genuinely disciplines rather than merely decorates.

Audit this analogy for me. Specifically:

First, give me the correspondence table: for each element of the trial (witnesses, exhibits, cross-examination, rules of evidence, prosecution and defense, judge, jury, standard of proof, verdict, appeal), state what plays that role in historical practice, or flag explicitly that nothing does.

Second, identify where the mapping breaks, and -- this is the part I care most about -- separate the disanalogies that matter for the objectivity conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Two I want you to engage directly: in a trial, the rules of evidence are fixed in advance and exclude material; in history, by Carr's lights, the historian is the one who constitutes facts as evidence in the first place, so there is no neutral gatekeeper. And nothing in the trial seems to correspond to White's point about emplotment -- the choice of whether a given stretch of the past is told as tragedy, comedy, or romance -- because the trial analogy covers the assessment of evidence but is silent about the literary form of the verdict. Does the analogy's silence on narrative form mean it simply ignores White, or does White's point expose a gap that sinks the analogy's argumentative use?

Third, assess whether the conclusion survives. Once the serious disanalogies are on the table, does the modest Rankean verdict my colleague wants -- evidence-constrained objectivity -- still stand, or was the trial analogy doing the work of smuggling in a neutrality (of procedure, of gatekeeping, of the jury) that historical practice lacks? If it survives, state the strongest version of it that the audited analogy can actually support. If it does not, say what a better governing analogy for historical inquiry would be -- one that accommodates selection and emplotment without collapsing into the view that historians simply invent the past.

Be willing to end on an uncomfortable verdict in either direction: that the analogy is better than its sceptical critics allow, or that it is a piece of professional self-congratulation that dissolves under scrutiny.
