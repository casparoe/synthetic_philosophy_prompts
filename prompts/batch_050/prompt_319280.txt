I am preparing the written statement of commitments for my qualifying exam in aesthetics — my examiners want a page of my own settled views on Hume's essay Of the Standard of Taste, which they will then attack in the oral — and before I submit it I would like a consistency audit. The passage on which my reading of the essay turns is this one, from early on:

"Every voice is united in applauding elegance, propriety, simplicity, spirit in writing; and in blaming fustian, affectation, coldness, and a false brilliant: But when critics come to particulars, this seeming unanimity vanishes; and it is found, that they had affixed a very different meaning to their expressions."

Here is roughly where I have landed:

(1) Hume's empiricism about the rules of art is right: the general rules are founded only on experience of the sentiments people actually have, so there is no a priori standard of taste, and no standard that floats free of how human beings, as a matter of psychological fact, respond to works.

(2) I read the quoted passage as deflating the usual evidence for a shared standard: cross-cultural agreement in general evaluative terms is largely verbal, since it dissolves at the level of particulars; the only genuine evidence for a common standard would be convergence on particular judgments, and even then only among judges whose faculties are not distorted.

(3) Particular aesthetic disagreements are genuine: when one competent judge finds a work powerful and another finds it cold, at most one of them is right. There is a fact of the matter, and talk of faultless disagreement in such cases is an evasion.

(4) Whole traditions can be wrong: the dominant taste of an entire culture or era can be systematically corrupted — by false religion, ideology, or mere fashion — so even a durable majority verdict carries no evidential weight on its own.

(5) The standard is usable, not merely regulative: the sources of distortion Hume names — want of delicacy, lack of practice and comparison, prejudice — can in principle be detected and corrected in contested particular cases, so we can determine, at least sometimes and without circularity, whose judgment is sound.

(6) Aesthetic error costs its bearer nothing: someone whose taste is corrupted enjoys what they enjoy just as fully as anyone; criticism corrects verdicts, but no one is worse off for having bad taste.

My questions are these. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, and how badly? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equal, and for each real tension say precisely what has to give and what the cheapest fix costs. I also want you to discriminate rather than flag everything: I suspect at least one pair here only looks inconsistent, and I would like you to identify it and explain what further premise would have to be added to make it a genuine conflict. If my reading of the quoted passage in (2) is doing more work than the text supports, say so explicitly — that is part of the audit, not a distraction from it. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the set the most. Do not hedge: if the set is tenable as it stands, say that instead of manufacturing problems.

Finally, a request about your own confidence: for the tension you rank most severe, state what argument or consideration would lead you to rank a different one first, and say where your reasoning in the audit — including your interpretation of the passage — is most likely to fail.
