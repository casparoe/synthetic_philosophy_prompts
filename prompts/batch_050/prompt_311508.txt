I was recently appointed to the charter-review commission of a city of about 240,000 after a disputed council election in which one political camp won 41 percent of the vote across the city but only one of nine seats. The commission must now evaluate recurring proposals to retain single-member wards elected by first-past-the-post or replace them with a proportional system. We will consider roughly four proposals each year, must reach a preliminary recommendation within two public meetings, and cannot assume that commission members have training in electoral-system design.

Please turn the main arguments for proportional representation and first-past-the-post into a decision procedure the commission could actually apply. The procedure should be written as an ordered sequence of tests, stating for each test:

- what evidence or calculation is required;
- what counts as passing, failing, or remaining indeterminate;
- when a failure is decisive and when it merely creates a presumption;
- which considerations can reasonably be reduced to a rule and which require explicit judgment;
- when the commission should request more information rather than force a conclusion.

The procedure must address at least proportionality, geographic accountability, representation of dispersed and concentrated minorities, ballot complexity, incentives for strategic voting, party discipline, coalition formation, administrative feasibility, and the fairness of the transition from the existing system. It should also distinguish between partisan elections and formally nonpartisan elections, where proportionality cannot simply be measured by party vote share.

To make the procedure operational, incorporate the following two diagnostics, adapting or rejecting them if they are conceptually unsound:

1. Translation loss: the proportion of voters whose supported candidates or clearly identifiable electoral camp receives substantially less representation than its vote support would predict.
2. Local custody: whether residents in a defined area have a specific officeholder whom they can reward, punish, or petition, rather than being one small part of every representative’s constituency.

Neither diagnostic should become a crude numerical cutoff unless you can defend one. Explain how the commission should handle missing or unreliable measurements.

After presenting the procedure, apply it to these three cases:

1. Seven single-member wards. A cohesive community comprising about 38 percent of the electorate is geographically distributed so that it forms 30–45 percent of the vote in every ward, elects no candidate of its choice, and has not held a seat for twelve years.
2. A five-member citywide council elected by single transferable vote in a nonpartisan race with forty candidates, very low voter information, and historically high rates of exhausted or invalid ballots.
3. A mixed system with six single-member wards and three citywide compensatory seats. The compensatory seats reduce translation loss, but candidates must affiliate with publicly declared electoral lists, even though municipal elections have traditionally been nonpartisan.

At least one application should yield a recommendation that conflicts with the commission’s initial preference for proportional representation. Conclude by identifying the principal ways the procedure could misfire, which electoral-system features it cannot capture, and what records the commission should keep so that later commissions can revise it without starting over.
