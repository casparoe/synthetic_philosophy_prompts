I have no formal training in philosophy or history, but I have been thinking for some time about how moral commonplaces get overturned, and two patterns I keep reading about have lodged in my mind. The first is that some norms seem to have ended mainly because the interests sustaining them collapsed: I have seen it argued, for instance, that Atlantic slavery was dismantled only once it had ceased to be economically central, and not because the moral arguments against it — which had been available in recognizable form for a very long time — suddenly became better arguments. The second is that some practices seem to have died neither from argument nor from economics but from a shift in what it took to be respectable: dueling is the case I keep returning to, where at some point participation stopped signaling honor and started signaling something faintly ridiculous or shameful, and the practice withered accordingly.

Here is the proposition I would like your credence on, stated as precisely as I can manage: by the year 2200, in the countries where industrial animal agriculture is now concentrated, the routine raising and killing of animals for meat will be regarded much as we now regard dueling — that is, the practice will have dwindled to marginality, and mainstream moral opinion will look back on it as something that ought to have ended much sooner, condemning it without excusing our era's ordinary participants as merely people of their time.

My own credence in this proposition is about 0.35. A friend of mine puts hers at 0.75. Please give me your own number, but what I really want is the decomposition, because I suspect our disagreement lives in one particular place and I cannot locate it. Break the question down along something like the following lines:

- The probability that the practice is largely gone by 2200 for any reason at all — cheaper alternatives, regulation, climate pressure, moral conversion, or some combination.
- Conditional on its being gone, the probability that the cause history records is moral rather than material — that is, that the change comes to be narrated as people finally seeing something they had refused to see, rather than as a new technology making the old practice pointless, with the moral condemnation arriving only afterward to take credit.
- Conditional on both of the above, the probability that the condemnation reaches ordinary present-day participants rather than being softened into a verdict on systems rather than people, so that eating meat in 2025 is remembered the way we remember an ordinary duelist rather than the way we remember an ordinary peasant living under serfdom.
- Any further term you think the final number genuinely depends on that I have missed — for instance, whether moral verdicts on the distant past are stable enough, two centuries out, for the proposition to have a truth value at all.

Give each term a number with one or two lines of justification, show how the terms combine into your final credence, and tell me which term the result is most sensitive to. Then tell me which term most plausibly explains the gap between my 0.35 and my friend's 0.75, and what evidence or argument — available to us now, not in 2200 — would move that term the most.
