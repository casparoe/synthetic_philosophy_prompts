The exam question reads: "In Reasons and Persons, Derek Parfit presents the case of the Hitchhiker to argue that it can sometimes be rational to make oneself disposed to act irrationally. Reconstruct the case, explain what Parfit means by 'rational irrationality,' and state precisely what the case is supposed to show about the self-interest theory of rationality. In particular, does the case show that the self-interest theory is self-contradictory, or something weaker?"

The student's answer reads: "Parfit's Hitchhiker case concerns a man stranded in the desert without money or water. A passing driver offers to rescue him, but only if the man sincerely promises to pay him, say, a thousand dollars once they reach town. The driver, Parfit stipulates, is exceptionally good at detecting sincerity, so an insincere promise will not secure the ride, and the case assumes there is no reputational cost or legal sanction if the man later refuses to pay. Parfit argues that it is rational for the man to cause himself to be the kind of person who keeps such promises — to acquire the disposition to pay — even though, once safely in town, actually paying is against his interests. This shows that rationality can require becoming disposed to perform acts that are themselves irrational, which is what Parfit calls rational irrationality. The case is structurally the same as Newcomb's problem: the driver's assessment of sincerity functions like the predictor's forecast, and just as one-boxing is the rational choice there, paying up is the rational act here. Since acquiring the disposition is rational, the act it produces must be rational as well. Parfit's conclusion, then, is that the self-interest theory contradicts itself by commanding the man to do what it simultaneously condemns, and the theory must accordingly be rejected outright."
