A close friend and I spent the better part of an evening last week arguing about whether something she did to her brother counted as manipulation. She talked him out of selling his share of their late parents' house -- and by her own telling she did it entirely above board: no lies, no withheld information, no threats, just a long conversation in which she made her case. I found myself unable to shake the feeling that something in how she did it was off, but every time I tried to articulate the wrong in the vocabulary of manipulation I knew, it came out sounding like a description of ordinary persuasion. The argument ended in a stalemate, and it made me realize I want a sharper test case than her messy real one -- something engineered to locate exactly where persuasion ends and manipulation begins.

So here is what I'd like you to build. Construct a single realistic scenario -- a family, friendship, or workplace setting, one paragraph or so, no science fiction, no hypnosis, no subliminal anything -- in which two of the standard philosophical accounts of manipulation deliver opposite verdicts. Specifically: the case should come out as legitimate persuasion on the bypass account (the view that manipulation works by circumventing or short-circuiting the target's rational deliberation, rather than engaging it), and as manipulation on the pressure account (the view that manipulation works by subjecting the target to influences or incentives that a reasonable person would find difficult to resist or to weigh calmly, even while reasoning about them perfectly lucidly). The hard constraint is that the influence must be fully overt, factually accurate, and consciously registered by the target -- no trickery, no covert emotional conditioning, nothing the target would be surprised to learn afterward. If it helps, the pressure in my friend's case came from something like this: she chose the moment, the setting, and the sequence of the conversation so that her brother's grief and guilt were at their most salient precisely when he had to answer, and she knew it.

After the scenario, do the bookkeeping. State the verdict each account gives and why, identify the single structural feature of the case that makes the two accounts diverge, and tell me what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed. Then steelman the losing side: explain how a defender of the pressure account would argue that my friend's case is manipulative even granting that her brother reasoned flawlessly, and how a defender of the bypass account would resist -- ideally by offering some principled way to distinguish her conduct from the engineered case, or by biting the bullet and explaining why neither is manipulation. Finish with a sentence or two on what the exercise suggests about whether 'manipulation' names one wrong or several.
