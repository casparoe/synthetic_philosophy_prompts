For nearly three decades now, development economists and economic historians have argued about why some nations are rich and others poor, and the argument seems to me to have hardened rather than converged. I want a diagnosis of the impasse, not another survey of the theories, and I want the answer in American English.

Here is the debate as I understand it, with the evidence each side leans on. The institutions camp points to cases where geography and culture are roughly held fixed and institutions diverge: North versus South Korea, the two halves of Nogales on the U.S.-Mexico border, East versus West Germany. They also lean on the reversal of fortune among former colonies: the places that were richest around 1500, like the Aztec and Inca heartlands and Mughal India, are relatively poor today, while thinly populated North America and Australia became rich, which suggests something imported rather than something about land or climate. Their key econometric move is to instrument for present-day institutions with historical settler mortality, on the grounds that Europeans planted growth-friendly institutions where they could settle and extractive ones where they could not. The geography camp answers that the cross-section is unforgiving: the poorest countries are overwhelmingly landlocked or tropical, malaria and other disease burdens track poverty closely, and temperate-zone settler colonies are exactly the ones that prospered, which their side reads as evidence for climate and ecology rather than institutions. The culture camp is smaller and less quantitative, but insists that neither variable explains why some peoples adopted productive norms and institutions in the first place, and points to cases like the divergent fates of culturally similar regions under similar laws, or the long reach of centuries-old religious and civic traditions in predicting modern economic behavior.

What strikes me is that all three camps have replies to every objection already on the table. The institutions side dismisses geography as a proximate cause that institutions can override; the geography side says settler mortality is itself a geographic variable, so the flagship instrument undercuts its own conclusion; both say the culture camp offers no mechanism and no falsifiable claim; the culture camp replies that the other two have confused correlates with causes at a grander scale.

My hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because it is asking for a kind of causal inference that history cannot support. There is only one realization of world history, the candidate causes are all endogenous to each other across centuries, and every proposed instrument or natural experiment is contestable by the opposing camp on grounds that are not crazy, so the econometrics cannot adjudicate and everyone falls back on prior convictions about which causes are deep. I suspect the disagreement underneath is really about what would even count as a satisfactory answer: a single dominant variable, a conditional structure of the form geography-first-institutions-later, or a concession that the question is too coarse.

Is my hypothesis right? Please diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, drawing on what each camp in fact says when pressed: Is it a substantive empirical dispute that better data or cleverer identification could settle, a disguised dispute about standards of evidence in observational macro-history, a dispute about which question is the interesting one, or a case where the underlying concept of a deep cause of national wealth is itself ill-posed? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a particular kind of evidence, a formal result, a conceptual distinction, or a reframing of the question. If your honest view is that no progress should be expected and the three camps will still be here in thirty years, say so and argue for it rather than offering a diplomatic middle position.
