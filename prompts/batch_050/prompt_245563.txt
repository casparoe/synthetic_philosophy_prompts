I keep running into this bug in my own behavior and I want to know if its a documented thing with a name, because it feels too systematic to just be "I'm lazy".

Concrete examples, all from the last few months:

1. Night before: I set my alarm for 6:30 to go running before work. Fully sincere. I genuinely prefer the version of my day where I run. 6:30 AM: snoozing is obviously correct, no contest. Nothing about the world changed overnight. I didn't learn anything new. The only thing that changed is that the run went from "later" to "now".
2. Someone asks me to review a gnarly legacy module. If it needs to happen this week, I say no. If it's in two months, I say yes happily. Two months later I'm cursing past-me, and I'd say no to the exact same task if offered again for that week. I have done this loop maybe five times now and I still fall for it.
3. I can predict the flip. That's the part that really gets me. I KNOW future-me will bail, with near-certainty, and yet the sincere intention at time of commitment is still there, and the flip still happens. It's not akrasia in the sense of just... not knowing better? I know exactly what's coming. (ok maybe that IS the name, I genuinely don't know)

The workaround I've converged on as an engineer is treating future-me like a hostile-ish teammate: deleting the apps I know he'll abuse, making public commitments so backing out costs something, scheduling the run with a friend so 6:30-me can't unilaterally cancel. Which works, but it feels philosophically weird that the optimal strategy for "one rational agent" is to build defenses against itself. That smells more like a multi-agent system with misaligned incentives than like a single decison-maker.

So: does this pattern -- the time-dependent flip, the predictability of the flip, the self-countermeasures -- have a name, and is there an actual literature on it? Also, where's the line between this and plain old impulsiveness or changing your mind, because sometimes future-me is RIGHT to override past-me (past-me overcommits constantly). If the whole thing doesn't map cleanly onto one established concept, tell me what the closest ones are and how what I'm describing differs, and if nothing fits, propose a term and define it so I'd know which cases are in and which are out.
