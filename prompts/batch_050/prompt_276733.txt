Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two scholars who disagree about the concept of democratic backsliding. The first — call her the diagnostic camp — holds that backsliding is a real, identifiable process in which elected incumbents dismantle the institutions that constrain them, and that Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and arguably the United States are genuine instances. The second — call him the skeptic camp — holds that "backsliding" as commonly used functions largely as a partisan label applied by losers of elections to winners they dislike, and that most alleged cases are better described as ordinary, if aggressive, majoritarian politics within constitutional bounds.

Take Venezuela under Chávez and Maduro as a case both sides accept as genuine democratic erosion, so that the collaboration has a shared anchor rather than arguing only about contested cases.

The document should contain the following:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims both scholars could sign. These should include, at minimum: what both count as democracy's minimal institutional core; what both accept happened in the anchor case; that legal, incremental, and popularly supported changes can still constitute backsliding (or not — if they cannot agree on this, it belongs in the disagreements section); and which institutional moves — court packing, electoral rule changes, pressure on independent media, civil service purges — both accept as evidence of anything at all, as opposed to moves whose meaning depends entirely on interpretation.

2. A disagreements section, formatted as a table, stating each residual dispute in wording each side would accept for its own position. Pay particular attention to separating genuine disagreements from verbal ones. Candidates you should explicitly classify as genuine or verbal: whether backsliding requires an identifiable endpoint or threshold, or is a matter of degree; whether intent of the incumbents must be shown, or only institutional effect; whether a change that majorities support at the time can still count as backsliding; and whether the concept is a diagnostic category or a normative verdict.

3. A cruxes section identifying the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a test or case both scholars agree bears on it and record each side's prediction in advance. Two tests must appear: a mirror-image test (would the skeptic's camp apply the same diagnosis if the identical institutional moves were made by a government of the opposite ideology, and would the diagnostic camp withhold it?) and an alternation test (does the incumbent allow elections it can plausibly lose, and what exactly does each side predict about the contested countries on this measure over a stated horizon?).

4. A closing paragraph stating the single claim on which the two scholars came closest to agreement without quite reaching it.

If, in drafting, you find that a portion of the dispute dissolves once terms are fixed — for instance, if the skeptic only rejects backsliding as a verdict while accepting the underlying institutional description — say so explicitly, identify which portion, and explain why the residue remains worth fighting over.
