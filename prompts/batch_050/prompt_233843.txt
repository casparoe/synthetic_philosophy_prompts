Last night a friend and I spent four hours arguing about a short passage in Turing's 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence, and I want the argument adjudicated by construction rather than by further exegesis. The passage is in section 6, in his reply to the argument from extrasensory perception. Turing grants, for the sake of argument, that the statistical evidence for telepathy is overwhelming; he re-runs the imitation game in imagination with a strong telepathic receiver and sender in place of the usual players; and he concludes that, if telepathy is admitted, the test must simply be tightened up -- the competitors housed in a room shielded against telepathy, on the analogy of preventing one competitor from listening at the wall while the interrogator thinks out loud.

My friend's objection: this remedy is circular. A shielded room can only be certified as telepathy-proof by some test, but telepathy as its believers describe it is not attenuated by distance, walls, or any known physical mediation, so the only available test of the shielding is behavioral -- and the behavioral test is the very imitation game whose validity is in question. My reply: this proves too much, since by the same reasoning no controlled experiment on any contested channel could ever be run, and yet we obviously can isolate variables provisionally.

Settle this by constructing a thought experiment to the following specifications. Build a scenario, set in the present day with no new physics beyond one stipulated fact (telepathy between some humans is real and unshieldable by any known material), in which an imitation game is conducted inside a facility that has passed every physical and statistical certification available, and in which two competent observers, agreeing on every physical fact of the case, nonetheless disagree about whether the game's verdict is valid -- where their disagreement must trace to exactly one feature of the setup, which you name explicitly. Constraints: the interrogator must not be stipulated to be careless, gullible, or biased; the machine must be an ordinary digital computer; no demons, perfect predictors, or stipulated verdicts -- the outcome must fall out of the setup; the scenario itself in one paragraph.

Then demonstrate that the specification is met: state what verdict Turing's tightening proposal licenses in your scenario and why, state what my friend's circularity objection predicts and why, identify the single feature doing the work, describe what happens to the disagreement if that feature is removed, and give the strongest counterattack available to whichever side your scenario comes out against. End with one sentence on whether the exercise suggests the dispute between my friend and me is empirical, methodological, or merely verbal.
