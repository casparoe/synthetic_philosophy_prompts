Ok so my aesthetics reading group has a guy who's convinced he's rescued Hume's essay on the standard of taste from the usual circularity objection, and his argument seems to me to prove way too much. Here it is, roughly as he gave it:

(1) A standard of correctness is empty -- it doesn't really rule anything in or out -- only if the people who supposedly embody the standard can be identified *solely* by their agreeing with its verdicts.

(2) The standard objection to Hume says his standard is empty in exactly this way: we identify the true judges (delicate palate, practiced, unprejudiced, all that) by the fact that they love Homer and Milton and the rest of the canon, and then we justify the canon by pointing back at the true judges.

(3) But the identical structure shows up with color. Grass is green because normal observers in normal conditions see it as green, and normal observers are just the ones who see grass as green. Same circle.

(4) And same with thermometers: a reliable thermometer is one whose readings match the actual temperature, and the actual temperature is whatever a reliable thermometer reads.

(5) Since color facts and temperature facts are obviously fine, a standard isn't empty merely because it can only be specified through its own deliverances.

(6) So the circularity objection to Hume fails, and taste has a standard that's just as good as the thermometer standard -- beauty is as objective as temperature.

The reason I say it proves too much: swap in any self-congratulating club -- wine snobs, the electoral college, a high school clique that decides who's cool by clique vote -- and the same argument certifies its verdicts as "just as good as thermometers."

Which step do I push on? I'd rather keep (5) than the conclusion, and I'd rather keep Hume than give up on color, but I can't see where the wine snobs get excluded. And tell me what it costs me if I reject whichever step you pick, because my friend will ask. Also, does the bit in Hume where the true judges get vouched for by the verdict of *centuries* change anything, or is that just a bigger circle?
