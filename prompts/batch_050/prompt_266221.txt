A colleague recently lent me a well-regarded popular introduction to Marx's economic thought, and one passage, summarizing his views on machinery and automation, has been nagging at me. It runs roughly as follows: "Marx's chapter on machinery in Capital is best read as a qualified defense of the Luddites. He recognized that machines destroy livelihoods, deskill craftspeople, and intensify toil, and he treated these harms as intrinsic features of the technology rather than of the social arrangements governing its use. At the same time, Marx argued that machinery is itself a source of new value: because a machine embodies labor and then out-produces any hand worker, capital invested in machinery yields surplus value in much the way labor does, which is why capitalists rush to mechanize. Late in life, however, Marx's unpublished notebooks suggested a reversal. Once automation advances far enough, he thought, direct labor time would lose its role as the measure of wealth, wage labor would become untenable, and capitalism would therefore collapse under the weight of its own technological progress, without much need for political struggle to finish the job."

My sense is that this is mostly competent but goes wrong somewhere -- in particular, I suspect the Luddite framing is misleading, though I can't put my finger on exactly what Marx's actual position was. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what misrepresents Marx's stated views. For each correction, I'd also like to know whether the passage's broader conclusions about Marx on automation -- that he was ambivalent-to-hostile toward machinery as such, and that he expected technological development to end capitalism more or less automatically -- survive once the errors are fixed.
