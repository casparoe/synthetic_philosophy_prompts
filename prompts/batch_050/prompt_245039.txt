Me and my reading group have a running fight we need settled. We've been treating Machiavelli's takes on individual rulers in The Prince and the Discourses like a common-law track record, and now we've invented a hypothetical ruler and can't agree what verdict he'd get.

The verdicts we all accept from the texts:

- Agathocles of Syracuse: rose from nothing, seized power by butchering the senate, then held Syracuse successfully for years and died in his bed. Machiavelli admits the man had serious virtù but flatly refuses to count him among the truly excellent, because of the cruelty and the betrayal of his friends.
- Cesare Borgia: crushed his rivals, brought order to the Romagna, planned for contingencies. Machiavelli basically holds him up as the model new prince, and blames his collapse entirely on outrageous bad luck -- his own illness hitting at the exact same time as his father's death, the one thing no amount of foresight covers.
- Giovampagolo Baglioni: had the pope and the whole college of cardinals in his hands at Perugia and let them walk. Machiavelli slams him not for being too ruthless but for failing to be ruthless at the one decisive moment.
- Fabius Maximus: won by stalling and caution. Machiavelli praises him, but explicitly says it worked because Fabius's cautious temperament happened to match what the times demanded -- same temperament, different era, and he'd have lost.

Here's our hypothetical: a minor duke takes his city in a single night by inviting the ruling council to a banquet under safe conduct and killing them all, including a cousin who had already surrendered on a promise of his life. After that one night, nothing. He rules mildly for twenty-five years, keeps his word obsessively, the city prospers, and he dies beloved and in power.

Two of us say Machiavelli praises him: the violence was one-time, done at the start out of necessity, then dropped -- cruelty used well, textbook Cesare. Two of us say he gets the Agathocles treatment: success and even virtù, but the betrayal of a kinsman under oath bars him from real glory, same as it barred Agathocles.

So: predict his verdict on our duke, say which historical case is the closest analog, and lay out the distinct rules that each fit all four verdicts above but diverge here. If the record genuinely underdetermines it, tell us which further hypothetical case would settle which rule he's actually running. Don't just split the diference -- we want an actual prediction with reasons.
