You're an experienced political philosophy instructor who's run lots of in-class simulations — please help me design one, and think carefully about what actually makes a tradeoff bite for students rather than just gesturing at it.

I'm teaching an upper-level course on federalism (16 students, one 75-minute session, mix of US and European students). We've covered the standard material on subsidiarity — the principle that decisions should be taken at the lowest level of government capable of handling them — and everyone can recite it, but they treat it as bland common sense and can't see why anyone would ever want to centralize anything. The whole debate feels weightless to them.

I want an activity that makes them *feel* the tension rather than have me lecture it. My rough idea: a mini constitutional convention where small groups assign a list of policy areas (education curricula, air quality, marriage law, pandemic rules, speed limits, welfare benefits...) to the local, regional, or national level. But it needs a twist — maybe hidden information about how heterogeneous regional preferences are, or asymmetric info about cross-border spillovers, or a shock halfway through (a pollution incident, an outbreak, a fiscal crisis) that forces them to live with whatever allocation they picked — so that they stumble into the tradeoffs themselves: preference-matching and local knowledge on one side, externalities and economies of scale on the other, plus maybe the laboratories-of-democracy point about experimentation.

Give me: concrete rules, materials, any roles, a rough timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened in the game onto the actual arguments for and against decentralization. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle that, and be honest about which features of real federalism (fiscal transfers? courts adjudicating competences? exit and voice?) the activity can't reproduce.
