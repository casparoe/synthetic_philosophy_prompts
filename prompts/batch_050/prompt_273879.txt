I teach a third-year political philosophy seminar, about 16 students, one 90-minute session, and we're doing luck egalitarianism. Design an in-class game that makes them feel the pull of both halves of the view from the inside, not just nod along to it: first the brute luck / option luck distinction (it seems obviously unfair that someone ends up worse off through no choice of thier own, but basically fine when they knowingly gambled), and then the harshness objection (the moment someone's option luck actually turns out bad, shrugging and saying "well, you chose this" starts to feel monstrous). The catch I want designed in: the game should run long enough, or be structured such, that the same student who earlier defended "their gamble, their problem" ends up on the wrong end of it, or has to watch a friend on the wrong end of it.

The game needs: actual rules and materials, a point scheme that works without real money, and a mechanism that cleanly separates brute luck from option luck at the mechanical level (some disadvantages just get dealt to you by the deck, others you explicitly buy into -- and the students should be able to feel the difference in how those losses land). Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the theory, including the moment where students will predictably start demanding some kind of floor or safety net and thereby reinvent the harshness objection themselves before I've named it. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- I suspect some students will start treating the points as real stakes and get genuinely upset, or someone will figure out how to game whatever tracks their "responsible choices," and I want to know how to handle both. And be honest about which feature of luck egalitarianism a game like this structurally cannot capture and I'll just have to supply in discussion.
