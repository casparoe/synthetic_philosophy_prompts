Last spring I took over as director of a small primary school, and my deputy and I have spent the autumn rewriting the school's character-education charter. I came up through the Mencian wing of classical Chinese thought: I hold that children arrive with moral sprouts — the spontaneous alarm anyone feels at seeing a toddler about to fall into a well, the native capacities for compassion, shame, deference, and a sense of right — and that education extends and protects these rather than installing virtue from scratch. My deputy is a convinced Xunzian: she holds that children arrive with desires that outrun any inborn guidance, that crooked timber needs the steam and the press-frame, and that ritual and conscious artifice do not corrupt human nature but construct morality where nature supplied none. After months of arguing, and because the board wants a single charter we will both sign, we have agreed to try an adversarial collaboration instead of one more debate.

Draft the document we should produce, in four parts:

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of statements we can both sign without wincing — about what children are actually like, what classrooms, rituals, and discipline demonstrably do, and what each of our traditions genuinely says. These must be substantive rather than vacuous, and any formulation a careful reader of Mencius or Xunzi would reject as caricature does not belong here.

2. Verbal disagreements dissolved. A short section identifying at least two points where our year of argument turned out to be about words — for instance, whether we mean the same thing by "nature" (xing) or by "good" — with each contested term disambiguated so that what remains is genuine disagreement rather than cross-talk.

3. Residual disagreements, exactly stated. A table: in one column my position worded as I would word it, in the other hers as she would word it, each row a single precise point of disagreement rather than a cluster.

4. Cruxes and advance predictions. Identify the two or three sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of us. For each crux, name a concrete observation or stipulated finding we both agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side would predict — for example, what a Mencian versus a Xunzian should expect of children raised in warm, attentive homes entirely without ritual or formal discipline, or of a school that abolishes its rules for one year and relies on adults merely modeling decency.

Aim for the tone of a charter preamble: measured, concrete, and usable by readers who are not scholars of the Warring States period. Begin with the document itself — no preface, no commentary, no restating of this brief.
