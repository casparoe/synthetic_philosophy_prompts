I'm trying to work out what I actually think about forgiveness by consulting my reactions to cases, and I'd like to know whether any coherent principle captures them. My verdicts:

1. A friend betrayed my confidence, later apologized sincerely, and did what he could to repair things. Forgiving him seems appropriate, perhaps even called for.
2. The same friend, unrepentant and mocking my hurt: forgiving him now would seem like a failure of self-respect, though I might still legitimately let go of my anger for my own sake.
3. A stranger wronged my late father years ago. I don't think I can forgive him on my father's behalf — only for what the wrong did to me.
4. And yet I do think a mother can forgive her child's murderer, and not merely for her own loss.
5. My grandmother insists, on religious grounds, that I am obliged to forgive my abusive uncle whether or not he ever repents. That strikes me as simply false: forgiveness is not the sort of thing anyone can owe.
6. Still, if a wrongdoer has fully repented and repaired everything repairable, a flat refusal to forgive ever after starts to look like cruelty or score-keeping rather than self-respect.
7. Forgiving a debt, by contrast, is entirely at the creditor's discretion and requires no apology from the debtor at all.
8. Finally, when someone tells me they've forgiven a wrongdoer purely as therapy — for their own peace of mind, with no regard for the wrongdoer — it no longer feels like forgiveness, whatever its benefits.

Is there an account of forgiveness that delivers all or most of these verdicts, stated precisely enough that I can see which cases motivate each clause? If two of my verdicts cannot be jointly saved — I suspect (3) and (4), and possibly (5) and (6), are the trouble spots — tell me which one a principled view should overturn, and why. I'd also like to know what the resulting account says about cases I haven't listed: self-forgiveness, forgiveness extended by institutions rather than persons, and forgiving the dead.
