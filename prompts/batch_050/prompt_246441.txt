I help run a rationality-adjacent reading group that meets weekly at a university (about 14 regulars, mix of grad students and professionals, all comfortable with expected-value reasoning, most steeped in the effective altruism literature). We've been reading about triage, QALYs, and the value of a statistical life, and I've noticed a failure mode in our discussions: everyone can state the standard positions fluently, but the stated positions and the revealed preferences feel badly disconnected. People who defend QALY maximization in the abstract balk at it in concrete cases; people who insist every life is infinitely precious turn out to have very finite budgets. Design a two-hour activity for the group that forces this gap into the open before anyone names it -- something where participants must actually allocate scarce resources across people (not argue about allocating), where the rules or the information structure quietly recreate at least three of the classic fault lines: identified versus statistical lives, life-years versus lives saved (so that saving a young person and an elderly person can't both count the same), and the aggregation problem where many small benefits to many people compete against one big benefit to one person.

A wrinkle I'd specifically like engineered in, because I think it's where our group's intuitions are least examined: run the allocation twice under different information regimes -- once where beneficiaries are faceless units in a spreadsheet, and once where some of them get names, ages, and one-sentence backstories partway through, so participants watch their own prior allocations suddenly look monstrous to them. I want them to feel the pull of the rule of rescue as a force acting on them, not as a fallacy they've read about. If you can also work in a moment where the group must decide as a committee rather than as individuals, so they discover that aggregation of *preferences* about lives is its own problem, even better. Real but small stakes would help if you can think of a clean way to implement them (we have a modest snack budget that could be on the line).

Please include: the full rules and setup, a timeline, what materials I'd need to prepare, what the facilitator should and shouldn't say up front (no lecture, and no one should hear the term QALY until the debrief), and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the theoretical fault lines -- including, for each moment, where a participant could defend what they did and what that defense costs. Predict what will go wrong when we actually run it (e.g., people gaming the numbers, someone refusing to allocate at all, the group converging too fast on utilitarian answers because of selection effects in who attends a rationality group) and tell me how to handle each. End with an honest note on which features of real triage the activity structurally cannot reproduce -- I'm thinking of things like genuine uncertainty about outcomes, the moral weight of being the actual decision-maker rather than a player, and whatever else you think I'm missing -- and how the facilitator should keep participants from over-updating from a game.
