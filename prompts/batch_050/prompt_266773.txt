I do not have academic training in philosophy or statistics, but I serve on a municipal review board that decides whether new technologies may be tested in public spaces. We have adopted the following rule for novel systems with no trustworthy failure record.

First, analysts may not answer unknown or give only a range of probabilities; they must assign a definite probability distribution to a specified list of outcomes. Second, they must write down every constraint supported by evidence, such as physical limits, laboratory results, legal definitions, and the number of people exposed. Third, among all probability distributions satisfying those constraints, they must choose the one with maximum entropy, understood roughly as the distribution that adds the least structure beyond the stated constraints. We call this the neutral prior. Fourth, they must update it by Bayes’s rule as evidence arrives. Finally, analysts are scored with a squared-error rule that rewards assigning high probabilities to outcomes that occur and low probabilities to those that do not.

A dispute has exposed a problem in the rule. For a delivery robot, one analyst initially divided accidents into serious and not serious and therefore assigned a probability of one-half to serious. Another divided serious accidents into injuries and property damage, making the prior probability of a serious accident two-thirds. A third divided injuries according to the part of the body affected, producing yet another result. The board’s written policy says to use the finest practically relevant classification, but nobody can explain what makes one classification practically relevant without already making judgments about which risks matter. Similar disagreements arise when one analyst places a uniform distribution over an unknown failure rate and another places a uniform distribution over its logarithm.

What conception of probability, evidence, and rationality does our institutional practice presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose that ignorance can determine a uniquely rational prior, that an event space can be identified independently of our interests, or merely that a public institution needs a repeatable convention? Which rival approaches—subjective Bayesianism, frequentism, imprecise probabilities, or refusing numerical probabilities under deep uncertainty—would the practice exclude, and which could accommodate it as one policy among others?

Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Then assess whether its parts are jointly coherent: maximum entropy as neutrality, the appeal to the finest relevant classification, compulsory point probabilities, Bayesian updating, and accuracy scoring. If the board abandoned the claim that maximum entropy is uniquely rational but retained the rest of the practice, what would have to change? Please answer in American English.
