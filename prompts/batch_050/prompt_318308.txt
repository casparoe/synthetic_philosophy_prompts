Fifteen years ago I served as an infantry officer in a war that I have since come to believe was unjust, and I keep circling one thought: the men I shot at were defending their own country from us. Before I trust that thought I want to test the opposite position at full strength, so please play a committed defender of the traditional doctrine of the moral equality of combatants -- the view that soldiers on both sides of a war are equally permitted to fight and equally bound only by the rules of conduct in war, whatever the justice of their cause. Give the character real resources: the argument that soldiers rarely control or reliably know whether their war is just, the point that blame properly falls on those who start wars rather than those ordered to fight them, and the worry that my revisionist alternative would license punishing ordinary conscripts. Concede small points when I earn them, never the doctrine itself; stay in character until I say stop; keep each reply to a single paragraph so this feels like a conversation rather than a lecture. Here is my opening move: your doctrine says that when I aimed my rifle at soldiers defending their homes against our advance, I acted permissibly, and so did they when they shot back at me. But they were resisting unjust aggression -- the very thing self-defense morality everywhere else treats as a justification for killing the aggressor. How can I have had permission to kill people who were doing exactly what justice required of them, and if I did, what becomes of the claim that killing in war is governed by the same morality as killing anywhere else?
