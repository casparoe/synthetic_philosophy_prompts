I am grading a first-year undergraduate exam in the history of philosophy. The question was: "Set out Locke's memory criterion of personal identity, and explain Reid's brave officer objection to it. Does a version of the memory theory that appeals to chains of overlapping memories escape the objection?" Here is the student's answer:

"Locke holds that a person at one time is identical with a person at an earlier time just in case the former remembers the experiences of the latter. Reid's objection runs as follows. A boy is flogged for stealing fruit from an orchard. He grows up to be a brave officer who, during a campaign, remembers the flogging. Later, as an old general, he remembers his exploits as an officer but has forgotten the flogging entirely. On Locke's criterion the officer is the boy, since he remembers the flogging, and the general is the officer, since he remembers the officer's deeds; but the general is not the boy, since he has forgotten the flogging. Identity, however, is transitive, so Locke's criterion yields a contradiction.

Reid also presses that Locke's account is circular: genuine remembering already presupposes that the rememberer and the subject of the remembered experience are the same person, so memory cannot constitute personal identity without begging the question.

The objection can be defused, however. The general is connected to the boy through an overlapping chain of memories: he remembers being the officer, who remembered being the boy. So identity holds all the way down, and Reid's case shows only that direct memory is not required at every link, not that the memory theory fails."
