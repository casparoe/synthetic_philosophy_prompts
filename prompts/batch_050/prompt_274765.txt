The exam question was: In his 1977 paper "Should the Numbers Count?", John Taurek argues that when we must choose between saving a larger and a smaller group of people, the fact that one group is larger does not by itself give us a moral reason to save it. Explain his reasoning, including the role he assigns to the coin flip, and critically discuss one objection to his position.

The student wrote: Taurek's target is the common-sense aggregationist intuition that it is worse if five people die than if one dies, other things equal. His central move is to argue that "worse" here is misleading: the death of the five is not worse for anyone than the death of the one, because losses cannot be summed across persons. Suffering and loss are always someone's suffering and loss, and no individual experiences the combined loss of all five. Hence each person can complain only about their own prospective death, and the five complaints cannot be pooled into a single claim that outweighs the one person's claim. Because the numbers as such supply no moral reason, Taurek proposes that, as between strangers, I should give each individual an equal chance of being saved, for instance by running a lottery in which every one of the six people has an equal ticket. On his view it would therefore be positively wrong to save the five rather than the one simply because five is more than one. He concedes that he himself would save a friend rather than five strangers, but he treats such partiality as a separate issue from impartial duty. A natural objection, pressed by Scanlon and others, is that giving each person's claim equal consideration seems precisely to require counting them all, and that Taurek's refusal to aggregate leaves his own tie-breaking procedure looking arbitrary: if I may flip a coin, why may I not instead let the greater number tip the balance? The objection at least shows that Taurek owes an account of when equal chances rather than equal weight is the right way of respecting persons.
