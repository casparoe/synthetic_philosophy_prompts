A friend in our physics-philosophy reading group is convinced he's broken the standard resolution of Maxwell's demon, and I cant find the hole. Take Szilard's one-molecule engine: the demon learns which side of the partition the molecule is on, drops in a piston, lets the gas expand isothermally, and banks kT ln 2 of work per cycle. His argument:

(1) Each full cycle nets kT ln 2 of work from a single heat bath.
(2) The second law says that can't happen in a cycle unless at least kT ln 2's worth of entropy gets dumped somewhere per round.
(3) The measurement itself can be made arbitrarily cheap -- reversible measurement schemes exist in principle, so no cost is forced there.
(4) So the standard answer (Bennett, via Landauer) has to be that the demon pays when it erases its memory to reset: kT ln 2 per bit erased.
(5) But the demon never needs to erase anything. Give it a long ledger of blank bits. Each cycle it reversibly swaps the new record bit with a fresh blank bit off the ledger. A swap is a permutation -- logically reversible, costless. Nothing is ever erased, so no Landauer cost is ever incurred.
(6) So the engine runs indefinitely paying nothing: either the second law is false, or measurement secretly costs kT ln 2 after all -- which cant be right, since I look around the room all day without paying any entropy fee.

He says this shows the whole "information is physical" resolution is a shell game: the cost just gets shuffled into an imaginary notebook. I'd like to keep (2), (3), and ideally (4). Which step do I actually reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? If the answer is something about the ledger, I want it cashed out: is a stack of blank bits really a thermodynamic resource, and does his "it's just shuffling" jab land at all? And if I grant him an infinitely long ledger and drop the demand that the whole apparatus return to its starting state, has he actually built a perpetual motion machine or only rebranded a battery?
