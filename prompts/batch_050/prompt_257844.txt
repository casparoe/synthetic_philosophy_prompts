I'm a graduate student in comparative philosophy who took up Wang Yangming seriously only after a disquieting personal realization: for years I sincerely said I knew I should exercise, stop arguing with my father, and finish my thesis, and did none of it. Next month I'm interviewing, for our department podcast, a philosopher who defends Wang Yangming's doctrine of the unity of knowledge and action against the charge that everyday weakness of will simply refutes it. Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having him restate the doctrine. I want at least one question each on the following: whether his view accomplishes anything more than redefining "know" so that whoever fails to act is said never to have truly known (and if so, whether that redefinition does any explanatory work); how the doctrine handles cases where the failure seems to be one of motivation rather than understanding, as with addiction or procrastination; what it implies about the relationship between moral knowledge and factual or technical knowledge, since no one thinks I must master carpentry to know what a chair is; and how Wang's idea of an innate, originally clear moral mind squares with people who act wrongly with apparent sincerity and no felt inner conflict. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give, so that the conversation presses forward rather than circling back to the doctrine's slogan.
