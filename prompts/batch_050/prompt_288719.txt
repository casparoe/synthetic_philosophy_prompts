Write a deadpan parody of an internal compliance handbook for employees of a multinational corporation that has just obtained a landmark ruling declaring it a full moral agent: capable of intention, character, remorse, praise, and blame. Format it as numbered sections with a short FAQ, roughly 850 words, in the polished, soothing voice of corporate HR. The ruling is invented, but the satire should turn on real problems in the debate over corporate moral agency: how an entity with no body can have mens rea; whether intention can be assembled from incentives, reporting lines, meeting minutes, and plausible deniability; how responsibility can be everywhere in the org chart and nowhere in particular; and why an apology issued by a logo may be metaphysically different from remorse.

Include procedures such as a Culpability Intake Form, a quarterly Conscience Calibration, a Responsibility Diffusion Matrix for allocating blame among teams until it becomes statistically insignificant, a Remorse Desk open from 9 to 5, criteria for promoting employees with high moral throughput, and guidance for whistleblowers on distinguishing the company’s bad character from a regrettable alignment of local incentives. The jokes should arise from taking the metaphysics literally inside bureaucratic prose: the firm can be guilty, but only after Legal determines whether guilt is a material disclosure; it can apologize, provided no individual remembers deciding anything; it has virtues, which are listed under Core Competencies.

Do not name real companies, people, cases, or books, and do not ask for citations. The piece should be legible to a general reader while rewarding someone familiar with disputes between collectivists and individualists about group agents, vicarious liability, responsibility gaps, and the performative ethics of compliance culture. Keep the irony sustained and serious in tone: never explain the joke, never say the ruling is absurd, and let the final section imply that the corporation has achieved perfect innocence by becoming responsible for everything in principle and nothing in practice.
