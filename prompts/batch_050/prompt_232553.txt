Pretend you're a philosopher who works right at the intersection of ethics and philosophy of language, the kind of person who's read alot of the recent lying/misleading literature, and I want you to help me survive a talk. I'm a PhD student giving a departmental colloquium next month defending the thesis that there is no morally significant difference, in itself, between lying and merely misleading someone. My rough master argument: what makes deception wrong is that you intentionally get someone to end up with a false belief you could easily have avoided producing, and whether you do that by asserting something false or by saying something technically true and letting the implicature do the dirty work (the classic "there's a garage around the corner" move, said while knowing it's closed) doesn't change what you're responsible for. On my view, the intuition that misleading is gentler gets explained away by other stuff -- sometimes the misleader makes himself less responsible for the uptake, sometimes the hearer had a fair shot at catching on -- but those are contingent features, not anything baked into the lying/misleading line itself.

The audience is the problem. It's a mixed department colloquium: a bunch of moral philosophers who have a soft spot for the orthodoxy that lying violates a special norm of assertion while misleading merely exploits conversational conventions, a few philosophy-of-language people who will definitely want to fight about whether "what is said" vs. implicature is even a stable enough distinction to hang a moral theory on (they'll bring up cancelability), at least one Kant person who's still annoyed about the murderer at the door, and a couple of empirically-minded folks who'll cite folk-judgment studies showing ordinary people think misleading is less blameworthy.

Please think carefully and brainstorm before drafting, then give me a Q&A prep sheet: the seven or eight objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they'd do me, each phrased the way that person would phrase it out loud in a cold seminar room, with a two-minute spoken reply and a brutally honest note on where the reply is weakest and what I'd just have to concede. Include at least one objection that attacks the linguistic machinery rather than the ethics. And finish with the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks -- I suspect it's something about whether my view collapses misleading into just staying silent and letting someone keep their false belief, since I don't know where I stand on omissions, but you tell me if there's a worse one lurking.
