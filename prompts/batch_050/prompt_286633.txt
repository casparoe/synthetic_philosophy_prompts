So I'm a PhD student working on medieval philosophy of religion, and I've somehow agreed to run a one-off seminar for my department's undergrad society on Boethius' solution to the divine foreknowledge problem in Book V of the Consolation of Philosophy. The session is about an hour, and they'll have read (or skimmed) V.3-6 in translation beforehand.

The bit I want the whole session to pivot on is the argument in prose 6 where Boethius shifts the whole problem: God's knowledge isn't a kind of advance prediction of future events, because for God there is no future in that sense — everything is present to the divine gaze at once, the way everything in my visual field is present to me at once. And just as my seeing you sit down right now doesn't force you to sit, God's eternal "seeing" doesn't force what it sees. The necessity, he says, lies in the relation between the knowing and the thing known, not in the thing itself.

Here's my problem: every time I've tried to explain this informally to undergrads it collapses in one of two directions. Either they hear "God is outside time" as a cheap definitional dodge (God just gets to ignore the rules), or they latch onto the spectator analogy and conclude Boethius is saying God is a passive watcher who learns what happens by observing it, which wrecks providence. So don't explain Boethius to me — plan how I should teach it. Specifically:

- What has to be in place conceptually before prose 6 can even look like a solution rather than wordplay? I'm guessing something about the difference between "necessarily, if God knows p then p" and "if God knows p, then necessarily p" has to come early, but I'm not sure whether the eternity stuff or the necessity stuff should come first, and why.
- What example should carry the session? The text's own image of the high vantage point seeing everything below at once is tempting but I worry it invites exactly the passive-observer misreading. Is there a better carrying case, and what does it need to include and exclude?
- Which misconceptions will they form, in roughly what order, and at what point in the arc do I head each one off? Beyond the two above, I suspect "so God has no idea what time it is" and "if everything is eternally present to God then everything already happened" are coming.
- What's one question I can put to them at the end whose answer would show they've got the distinction between foreknowledge and eternal present-knowledge, rather than just having memorized the analogy?
- And what should I explicitly bracket at this level — I'm thinking the question of whether a timeless God can even know tensed facts, and the Molinist alternative — and how do I flag that as deferred without making the solution look like it only works if you don't push on it?
