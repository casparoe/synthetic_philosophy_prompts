So I work at a small policy institute (we mostly do tech governance stuff) and our director said she'd put internal seed money toward one "conceptual" project this year, 4 months max, if someone writes a decent two-page pitch. Nobody else wants to do it because it's not empirical, so it's mine if I can make it sound good. Here's the backstory that made me care: last year I sat in on a hiring round for the first time, and I kept noticing that when I defended a judgment about a candidate ("she doesn't seem rigorous enough," "his work feels derivative"), I genuinely could not tell whether I was voicing a real evaluative preference or laundering a bias, and worse, neither could the colleagues who disagreed with me. Everyone in the room had some implicit test they were running, and everyone's test conveniently vindicated their own judgments.

My half-formed idea for the pitch: most philosophical work on this asks whether an attitude has a bad causal history and is therefore "debunked," but in real life the causal history is almost never knowable, so what we actually need is an epistemic policy for the undecidable case -- rules for how much weight to give an attitude when you can't settle whether it's a preference or a bias. And my suspicion, which I want the project to test, is that the two tests everyone informally reaches for -- "would I still hold this after ideal reflection?" and "do people different from me share it?" -- both quietly presuppose the very distinction they're supposed to detect, because what counts as ideal reflection and who counts as relevantly different depends on which of your attitudes you've already classified as preferences. If that's right, the interesting question is whether there's any test that doesn't beg the question, or whether the rational policy in undecidable cases has to be built on something else entirely (cost asymmetry? reversibility of the decision? procedural fixes instead of epistemic ones?).

Write me the two-page pitch: the question stated precisely enough that it could actually get an answer in four months; why the obvious existing approaches (debunking arguments, just trusting coherence, just deferring to consensus) leave it open; what I'd concretely do month by month, given that the method has to be philosophical rather than empirical -- I'm picturing working through a small set of cases (hiring judgments, moralized disgust, one more you think is illuminating) to generate and stress-test candidate criteria, but argue for that or something better; what a positive result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and one honest paragraph on why a policy institute should care about a piece of pure philosophy. No bibliography or named citations -- I'll slot those in myself, just refer to positions and arguments by description.
