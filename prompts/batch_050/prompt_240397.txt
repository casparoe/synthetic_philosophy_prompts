I'm preparing a seminar session pairing Habermas's theory of communicative action with Sidney Lumet's 12 Angry Men, a film my students tend to treat as a straightforward vindication of deliberation. Read the film through the distinction between communicative and strategic action and through the ideal speech situation: which features of the jury room genuinely approximate uncoerced discourse governed only by the forceless force of the better argument, and which -- the lone holdout's leverage, the pressure of the unanimity rule, Juror 3's intimidation, the later votes that look more like bandwagon than conviction -- are better described as strategic action or as systematically distorted communication? Where does the film resist the Habermasian reading altogether (the accused never speaks, and no one in the room shares his situation), and where might it actually improve on Habermas by showing what arriving at consensus costs? Please end by flagging which elements of your reading rest on settled points in the scholarship on communicative rationality and which turn on genuinely contested questions -- for instance, whether the ideal speech situation is an achievable standard or merely a regulative ideal, and whether consensus can certify validity -- rather than presenting a contested interpretation as the consensus view.
