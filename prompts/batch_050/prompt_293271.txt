I'm a journalist who covers the effective altruism beat, and a philosopher I interviewed walked me through this argument, which is loosely built on Peter Singer's principle from his 1972 paper Famine, Affluence, and Morality -- roughly, that if you can prevent somthing very bad without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, you ought to do it. Here's the version she gave me:

(1) It would be monstrous to walk past a drowning child just to save your new laptop from getting wet.
(2) The only difference between that child and a child dying of malaria abroad is distance and visibility, and neither of those is morally relevant.
(3) So you're obligated to donate to effective charities whenever the money would prevent somthing very bad and cost you less than comparable moral significance.
(4) There's no principled line where this stops short of giving until your own welfare is barely above the recipients'.
(5) And since it applies to money, it applies just as much to time, career choice, and your organs while you're still alive (you have two kidneys).
(6) But clearly nobody is obligated to live like that, so (1) must be wrong -- and she actually bit that bullet and said walking past the pond is sometimes fine.

That last move seems insane to me, but I couldn't pin down where the argument slips. Where does it go wrong, if it does? I'd rather reject (4) or (5) than (1), but I want to know what each rejection costs. Please be upfront about which moves here are considered settled among philosophers who work on this and which are genuinely contested -- don't dress up a minority position as the consensus.
