I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on the ethics of communication, and last week I listened in on an argument between two fellow grad students that neither would let go of, and I'd like a careful verdict. The case they were fighting over is one we invented for a seminar: a job candidate asks a recruiter, point blank, whether the company has had layoffs recently. The recruiter replies that the team has grown every single year she has been there. This is literally true, but only because the company acquired two smaller firms and absorbed their staff; during that same period, most of the original department was quietly let go. The candidate accepts the offer, believing the workplace is stable. The question under dispute is whether the recruiter lied or merely misled, and whether anything moral turns on the answer.

The first student, call her position (1), argued that the lying-misleading distinction does no moral work at all: the recruiter intended the candidate to form a false belief, succeeded, and the linguistic packaging is irrelevant, so calling it 'misleading rather than lying' is a euphemism that exists only to let speakers feel clean. The second student replied, and this is move (2), that the distinction tracks a real difference in how responsibility is distributed: a liar stakes her own authority on a falsehood and invites the hearer to rely on her word, whereas a misleading speaker says something true and the false conclusion is partly the hearer's own inference, which is why we judge a technically true answer less harshly even when the intent is the same. Move (3): the first student answered that this just launders blame-shifting, since a competent speaker predicts the hearer's inferences exactly and exploits them deliberately, so the hearer's 'contribution' is manufactured by the speaker and the culpability is identical. Move (4): the second student conceded that the culpability in this particular case might be identical, but insisted the distinction still matters structurally: lying violates the norm governing assertion itself, while misleading operates within the ordinary conventions of implicature that all conversation depends on, and if we treated every deliberately engineered implicature as morally equivalent to a lie, ordinary talk would become impossible, since we constantly communicate far more than we strictly say and cannot vouch for every inference a hearer might draw.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible with qualifications, and justify each assessment. Then tell me who has the better of the overall dispute, or whether it dissolves once some distinction (for instance between blameworthiness in a single case and the norms that should govern practice) is made explicit, and identify what, if anything, both parties are missing. I'd like the answer in continuous prose only, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables -- refer to the moves by their numbers inline.
