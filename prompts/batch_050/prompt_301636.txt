My reading group has spent three sessions stuck on the Great Divergence: why industrialization took off in Britain and northwestern Europe around 1800 rather than in, say, the Yangzi Delta or Bengal, which by several measures were comparably commercialized and prosperous circa 1750. Two camps have formed. The first holds that Europe's advantage was deep and longstanding — its institutions, scientific culture, property rights, and labor markets — so industrialization was overdetermined and other regions were never close. The second holds that the divergence was late and largely contingent: accessible coal plus New World land and resources relieved ecological constraints that would otherwise have choked Europe too, and absent those windfalls a Chinese industrialization was a live possibility. My own credence in the contingency thesis sits around 0.65; my most stubborn interlocutor is at 0.15.

Give me your credence in a sharpened version of the contingency thesis — say: had Europe lacked both cheap, accessible coal and New World colonies, no European region would have industrialized before 1900 — and decompose it. Assign a rough probability, with one line of justification each, to: coal being individually necessary; the colonies being individually necessary; European institutions and science being independently sufficient within the century regardless; and the Yangzi Delta being on a genuinely industrial trajectory of its own. Then show how the terms combine into your final number, state which sub-question the estimate is most sensitive to, and name the piece of historical evidence that would move it most. Keep the whole answer under 350 words.
