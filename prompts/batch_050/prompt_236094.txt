I am a third-year philosophy student currently enrolled in a seminar on the philosophy of criminal law. For my final paper I have been asked to state and defend my own considered position on when and why the state may punish, and before I commit it to writing I would like to know whether the position I have arrived at is internally coherent. My commitments are as follows:

(1) The point of maintaining an institution of punishment at all is the reduction of crime. If punishment did nothing to deter, incapacitate, or rehabilitate, there would be no reason to keep the institution, and given its costs in suffering it would be positively unjustifiable.

(2) It is absolutely wrong to knowingly punish an innocent person, whatever the expected benefits. Even if convicting one blameless person would avert a riot and save several lives, it must not be done; this is a constraint on state action, not a presumption that can be outweighed by sufficiently good consequences.

(3) The severity of a punishment should be proportioned to the gravity of the offense and the culpability of the offender. Proportionality is a requirement of justice in sentencing, not merely a useful policy heuristic.

(4) Punishment is essentially communicative: it expresses society's condemnation of the offense, and the harshness of the treatment is part of what the condemnation says. A society that punished murder with a modest fine would thereby be saying that murder is a minor matter.

(5) When legislatures fix penalty ranges, they should attend carefully to deterrent effects, and offenses that are difficult to detect may warrant steeper penalties than equally harmful offenses that are easily detected, since it is the expected penalty rather than the nominal one that deters.

Which pairs of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, and which only appear to? Please rank any genuine tensions by severity, and for each one state precisely what would have to be given up or revised to resolve it and what that revision costs. If an apparent tension dissolves once the commitments are stated carefully enough, show how it dissolves. Finally, tell me whether there is a single revision that would restore the most consistency for the least theoretical cost. I have not attached weights to these commitments and I am not attached to any particular one of them surviving; what I want is the diagnosis.
