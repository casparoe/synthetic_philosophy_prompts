Okay so I'm a novelist, and the book I'm drafting has a problem I keep circling back to. My main character is a mid-level customs clerk in a made-up port city, and traders whose cargo he processes regularly bring him gifts — a crate of mangoes, a school fee for his niece, that sort of thing. He never asks, and he'd process the paperwork anyway (probably). Half my beta readers wrote "he's corrupt" in the margins, the other half wrote "that's just how things work there, you'd do the same." The plot sort of hinges on which of them is right and I can't settle it.

So here's what I want. Give me your credence, as an actual number, that the standard Western bureaucratic line on this — no gifts to officials, arm's-length dealing, impersonality as the ideal — is culturally parochial rather than a piece of genuinely universal moral machinery. But decomposed, not just the number: I want the sub-questions it depends on with a rough probability for each, something like (a) the chance there's any coherent universal core to the concept of corruption at all, (b) given that core exists, the chance the specific gift prohibition belongs to it rather than being a local bolt-on, (c) the chance that gift-heavy systems produce the concrete harms anti-corruption people point to (misallocated contracts, eroded trust) even where everyone locally accepts them — plus whatever terms you think I'm missing, then how they combine into your final number. You can use my little test if it helps: a practice counts as corrupt iff the participants would shrink from it being publicly announced, but tell me if that test is load-bearing or garbage.

Then tell me which term your number is most sensitive to and what ethnographic evidence would move it. Keep the whole answer under 300 words.
