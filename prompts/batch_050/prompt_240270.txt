At the end of term my department is holding a retirement dinner for the professor who taught me logic, and I have been asked to deliver the toast. He has spent forty years working in constructive mathematics, and the commitment is not merely professional: he declines, as a matter of intellectual honesty, to assert anything he cannot back with a construction. He is the man who, when a student proves a solution exists by assuming there is none and deriving a contradiction, hands the paper back with "so find one" in the margin; who will not say "either the meeting is on Tuesday or it is not" until he has actually checked the diary; and who once interrupted a wedding speech to point out, cheerfully, that "no one will ever love her more" was a strong universal claim offered without any method for settling it. Roughly half the guests will be colleagues and former students who know all of this and will be quietly auditing the toast; the other half will be his family, his neighbours and his grandchildren, for whom the word constructivism means nothing at all.

Write the toast, of about five minutes when read aloud. The constraint I care about is structural rather than decorative: the speech should itself be something he could not fault on constructivist grounds. Every general claim about him should be witnessed by something concrete — an anecdote, a habit, a plausible invented memory — rather than established by eliminating alternatives, by universal negations ("nobody who met him failed to…"), by lazy dichotomies ("either you followed his lectures or you didn't"), or by confident promises about the future he would regard as unlicensed. Where the toast reaches for the rhetoric of impossibility or exhaustiveness, it should catch itself, or better, find the constructive thing to say instead. The difficulty is that none of this may be explained or signposted within the speech: the family must hear only a warm, funny, slightly eccentric portrait of a man they love, while the logicians notice, perhaps only on reflection, that the toast has been keeping his rules all along. Aim for affectionate rather than reverent; he dislikes piety almost as much as he dislikes proof by contradiction.

Please write everything in American English, and keep the entire response in continuous prose — the toast itself, followed by a paragraph or two telling me where the constructivist discipline bit hardest and which natural toast-moves you had to forgo — with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text and no tables; it should read like a letter.
