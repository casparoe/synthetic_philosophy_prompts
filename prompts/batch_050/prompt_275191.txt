Last week a friend and I argued well past midnight about something that started small: whether she was right to stay in a doctoral program she no longer believes in, given that she has five years invested in it. I invoked the sunk cost fallacy. She replied that the phrase has become a thought-terminating cliché, and defended a position I could not easily answer. Every long undertaking, she said, passes through valleys in which abandoning it looks locally rational; an agent who always stops at such valleys becomes incapable of sustaining any multi-year endeavor at all, and everyone who deals with that agent learns that its commitments expire whenever the calculus wobbles. On her view, honoring sunk costs is not sentimentality. It is the price of being the kind of agent — person or institution — whose promises about the future mean anything, including promises to itself.

I raise this because the argument followed me into work. I am president of the board of a community foundation, and on Thursday we vote on the fate of our largest project. I would like you to draft the decision memo I will circulate beforehand. The facts, as the board has them:

- Seven years ago we approved restoration of a derelict historic theater, then estimated at $3.0 million over three years.
- We have spent $4.3 million to date. The structure is sound and weather-sealed; the interior is unfinished.
- Our construction firm now estimates $2.9 million and eighteen months to completion, but concedes that projects at this stage historically overrun by roughly a third, putting a realistic figure nearer $3.8–4.0 million.
- A completed theater is projected to run an annual operating deficit of about $160,000, which the community would expect us to cover for at least the first five years.
- A $1.1 million restricted gift, our single largest, must by the terms of the gift agreement be returned if we abandon the project before completion.
- Staff analysis shows the completion funds could instead fully endow three programs with strong evidence of impact and no ongoing obligations.
- The neighborhood association has raised $90,000 in small donations; our development director warns that walking away would damage donor trust ahead of next year's capital campaign, though she cannot quantify this. Our foundation's informal identity, much repeated at fundraising dinners, is that we finish what we start.

The board is split, and both factions are using the word "sunk" loosely. The memo should do the following:

1. Give a clear recommendation among three options — complete as planned, complete with reduced scope, or wind down and return the gift — with no hedging, plus any conditions the board could actually attach to the motion (caps, independent cost verification, fundraising thresholds, a re-vote trigger).
2. Sort the arguments on both sides into three piles: reasoning that commits the sunk cost fallacy in the textbook sense; reasoning that invokes the past but is in fact forward-looking once examined (the clawback, reputation, donor behavior, precedent for future capital projects); and reasoning that rests on my friend's commitment thesis. On that third pile, the memo should say whether her argument survives translation from an individual's life to an institution's portfolio, rather than simply assuming it does or doesn't.
3. State the strongest argument against the recommended option honestly and at some length, and identify what single new fact, if it came to light, should reverse the recommendation.
4. Run 800 to 1,000 words, addressed to trustees rather than philosophers. The board includes two people who say "we cannot waste four million dollars" and one who says "sunk costs are irrelevant, full stop"; all three should come away feeling their position was understood, and at least two of them should come away thinking harder.
