For several years I have co-taught a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of dispositions with a colleague whose classifications of hypothetical cases are unfailingly confident and, I suspect, governed by a theory she has never stated explicitly. Her verdicts to date are these.

1. The electro-fink: a live wire connected to a device that would instantly render it non-conductive were it ever touched by a conductor. She rules that the wire is, even so, conductive.
2. A fragile vase encased in packing material such that no ordinary impact could break it. Still fragile.
3. A poison whose intended victim has already received an antidote that will neutralize it in the bloodstream. The poison remains toxic.
4. A glass that has been annealed, so that its internal structure no longer permits shattering. No longer fragile.
5. A wire watched over by a guardian spirit that would divert any current the moment it began to flow, without touching or altering the wire itself. Still conductive.
6. A mimic: an inert, non-conductive wire attached to an external device that would render it conductive the moment it was touched. Not conductive as things stand.

Two new cases are on the syllabus for next week.

(A) The dummy wire. A wire with no conductive core whatever is built into a circuit rigged so that touching it triggers an external generator, which delivers current through the contact point independently of the wire. Conductive or not?
(B) The latent pellet. An inert polymer pellet contains dormant nanostructures that, upon immersion in acid, would assemble a resistant coating out of the pellet's own material before the acid could act. Does the pellet possess, now, the disposition to resist acid?

I should like you to reconstruct her position. First, state at least two precise rules, each consistent with all six verdicts, that diverge on at least one of the new cases; and make clear why the crude candidates — a simple counterfactual analysis of dispositions, and a pure reliability view on which anything that would manifest the response under the stimulus counts as disposed — are each excluded by her record. Second, give each surviving rule's verdict on (A) and on (B), with its reasons. Third, say which rule she is most plausibly running, predict her actual verdicts on the two cases, and identify exactly where her record underdetermines the prediction rather than merely pretending it does not. Fourth, describe one further hypothetical case that would discriminate between the two strongest candidate rules, and say what each of her possible verdicts on it would reveal.
