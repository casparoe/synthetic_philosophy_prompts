So I've been going back and forth with a friend about what you're supposed to do, rationally, when someone just as smart as you disagrees with you, and I realized my own gut verdicts don't line up in any obvious way. I want to know what principle (if any) is behind them. Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. Me and a coworker both do the same messy back-of-envelope cost estimate independently. I get 14k, she gets 20k. I think I should land somewhere in the middle, maybe 17k, even if I feel pretty sure I did the math right. We're about equally good at this stuff.

2. Same coworker and I disagree about whether a political policy works. She's as smart as me and has read the same studies, but I also know her whole social circle is invested in one side of this. Honestly? I barely move my view at all.

3. Two weather apps that I know use equally good models: one says 70% rain, one says 40%. I go with roughly 55%. Feels automatic.

4. I spend an evening arguing for some ethical view, feel totally confident, go to bed. Next morning I can't reconstruct my own reasoning at all, I just remember being convinced. I think I should still mostly trust yesterday-me, who did the careful thinking, over today-me who can't remember it.

5. A stranger at a conference who seems really sharp disagrees with me about something in my own field. I know nothing about his track record. I don't think I should move much, even though for all I know he's better at this than me.

6. Here's where it gets weird: in case 2 I discount my coworker because her judgment might be distorted by her social environment. But when someone points out that *my* judgment on some topic might be distorted the same way, my gut says that's no reason for me to move. I realize this looks bad. Tell me if it's actually inconsistent or whether there's a legit asymmetry.

7. Last one: say a peer and I disagree, and we also disagree about whether we're epistemic peers -- she thinks she's clearly more reliable on this topic, I think we're equals. My gut says the first disagreement might call for splitting the difference but this second-level one definately doesn't, since "split the difference about who's more reliable" seems to just collapse into deferring to her. But I'm not sure that instinct is defensible.

What I want: a principle (or two competing candidates) that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case myself. If saving all seven requires ugly ad hoc clauses, I'd rather hear which verdict to drop and why. I suspect 6 is the first to go but I'd love to be told otherwise.

Format-wise, please structure it as: "The principle" (~a short paragraph), "Working through the cases" (the bulk, going case by case, a paragraph each is fine), "The verdict you should drop" (~a paragraph), "A case you didn't ask about" (one new case with the principle's verdict, short), and "How confident I am" (a few sentences, including the single point you're least sure of). If you end up giving two rival principles instead of one, just do the same structure for the better one and note the alternative briefly. And please don't just gesture at the standard literature positions by name -- I'll fill in citations later, what I need is the actual principle and where it bites.
