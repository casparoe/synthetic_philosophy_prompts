Two friends of mine, Dan and Meera, got into a real argument last week and I want an outside verdict because both of them are sure they've won. Some context: they work at a small software company and the founders asked them to propose a fair split of a $10,000 retention bonus pool between two engineers, Alex and Priya. Both engineers have to stay through the next release or the pool evaporates entirely, in which case each just takes a standing outside offer they already have: Alex's is worth $4,000 and Priya's is worth $1,000. There's also a company policy that no single retention bonus can exceed $7,000. So the question on the table is: what split is fair?

Dan's answer was $6,500 for Alex and $3,500 for Priya. His reasoning: what matters is the surplus over what each person gets if talks collapse, which is $10,000 minus $5,000, so $5,000, and a fair deal splits that surplus evenly, meaning each gets their outside offer plus $2,500. He said the $7,000 cap is a red herring since it doesn't bind at his split, and more generally a rule for fair division shouldn't change its answer when you add or remove options nobody was going to pick anyway.

Meera said that's the wrong way to think about it. Fairness, she said, is about how far each person gets toward the best outcome available to them. Alex's best case is capped at $7,000, which is only $3,000 above his fallback, while Priya's best case is $6,000 (since Alex has to get his $4,000), which is $5,000 above hers. A fair split gives each person the same fraction of their possible gain, and she did the math and got $5,875 for Alex and $4,125 for Priya. The cap matters, she said, because it crushes Alex's best case, and someone whose best case is cramped has a weaker claim on the pool.

Dan came back with two points. First, her rule makes real outcomes depend on hypotheticals: two situations with exactly the same realistic choices get different answers depending on a cap that never binds, which seems arbitrary. Second, it's manipulable, because whoever sets the cap steers the split without changing anyone's actual options, and Alex ends up punished for a policy he didn't write.

Meera replied that his rule has the mirror-image flaw. If the company raised the cap to $9,000, Dan's answer wouldn't move a cent: Alex's possibilities would grow enormously and he would get literally nothing from it. A division rule that never rewards someone when the feasible options expand in exactly their direction, she said, is broken. She also said Dan's even-split-of-surplus just quietly assumes a dollar to Alex is worth the same as a dollar to Priya, which is doing a lot of work.

Dan's last move was that the outside offers reflect actual leverage, since Alex really can walk away with $4,000, and her rule throws that information away in favor of proportional-to-fantasy. She said leverage is just power, and they were asked for a fair split, not a prediction of how a negotiation would go.
