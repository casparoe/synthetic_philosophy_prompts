You are a political philosopher whose research concerns the family, caregiving, and the welfare state, and you have occasionally advised policymakers on the design of parental leave. Think carefully before you answer; I'd like you to begin with a short brainstorming section in which you generate several candidate views the policy might rest on, including ones you will later reject, and only then write the main analysis.

Here is the practice I want you to analyze. In many wealthy democracies, paid parental leave has been redesigned over the past few decades along roughly the following lines. First, what used to be maternity leave has become a shared entitlement divided between the two parents. Second, a substantial portion of that entitlement is reserved for each parent individually on a non-transferable, use-it-or-lose-it basis: if the father (in the typical case) does not take his reserved months, the family loses them; he cannot hand them to the mother. Third, the reserved portion has repeatedly been lengthened precisely in response to low take-up by fathers. Fourth, alongside the leave, the same states typically subsidize full-time childcare from a very early age -- and some of them simultaneously pay a cash benefit to parents who decline the subsidized childcare and look after their children at home themselves.

My question: what does this package of policies presuppose about the family, about caregiving, and about the legitimate aims of the state? Some sub-questions to guide you. The non-transferability is the puzzle at the center: it cannot be explained merely by the aim of enabling mothers to keep their jobs, since fully transferable leave would achieve that whenever the couple wanted it, so the design seems to assume that *who* does the caring matters independently of how much caring gets done and of what the parents themselves prefer. What view of the parent-child relationship, or of gender equality, would make that assumption true? Is the state thereby committed to treating the couple as two individuals rather than a cooperative unit with an internal division of labor that is its own business -- and if so, is that commitment coherent with the marriage law and tax treatment the same states maintain? And is there a tension between the non-transferable quota, which seems to say that the division of care is a public matter the state may steer against parental preference, and the cash-for-care benefit, which seems to say that the choice between paid work and home care is a private preference entitled to neutral support?

In your main analysis, please (a) reconstruct the most plausible implicit theory behind each element of the package, (b) distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes -- what it would be irrational to do without believing -- and what it merely fits well with, and (c) identify where the practice is underdetermined, so that several rival views would license the same design. Finally, say what would concretely have to change in the policy if we replaced the implicit view with either of two alternatives: the household as a single cooperative enterprise whose internal arrangements are no concern of the state, or caregiving as one source of personal meaning among others that public policy should neither favor nor discourage.
