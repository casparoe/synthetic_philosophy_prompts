At a meeting of our policy reading group, two members, Marta and Dev, got into an argument about defunding the police and the state's monopoly on violence, and they have asked me to adjudicate.

Marta opened with a dilemma. Since Weber, we understand the state as the body that claims a monopoly on legitimate violence within its territory, and the police are simply the institution through which that monopoly is exercised domestically. So any serious defund proposal faces a fork. Either the coercive functions — armed response, warrants, detention — are reassigned to some other state agency, in which case the police have merely been renamed, or they are reassigned to no one, in which case the proposal is not police reform at all but the dismantling of the state.

Dev replied that this confuses authority with staffing. The monopoly is a claim about who may authorize and forbid violence, not about which personnel perform particular tasks. An unarmed traffic unit or a civilian crisis team acts under state authorization just as fully as an armed officer does, and the state already delegates adjacent functions — bailiffs, prison contractors, licensed security — without anyone saying the monopoly has lapsed.

Marta answered that the delegation cases prove her point. Licensed guards have no powers beyond those of ordinary citizens. The moment a body may lawfully do what would otherwise count as assault or kidnapping — detain, search, compel — it is exercising the monopoly, whatever its badge says. So either the new responders receive such powers and become police in substance, or they do not, and armed officers must wait in the background as backup, meaning coercion has not been reduced, only moved offstage.

Dev's final answer was that nobody serious promised zero coercion; the aim is less coercion, applied later, by differently trained and differently accountable bodies, and a standing armed backup does not erase the difference an unarmed first response makes. Weber's claim defines what a state is; it says nothing about how coercive tasks must be distributed among agencies, so it cannot bear the weight Marta puts on it.

I could not settle it. Please adjudicate: give exactly six numbered points, each no more than two sentences, stating who is right about which specific move — covering at least the dilemma, the authority-versus-staffing reply, and the police-in-substance rejoinder — identifying what, if anything, both parties are missing, and saying who is more right overall. No introductory or concluding remarks.
