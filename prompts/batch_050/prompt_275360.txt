She was genuinely devastated. The coworker (call him Dan) said her sadness was real but the loss wasn't: nothing was destroyed, some bits changed state on a hard drive, and whatever harm happened cashes out entirely in real-world terms — money spent, hours wasted, feelings hurt — so talking about "losing a garden" is a category error, like mourning a dream you didn't finish.

She pushed back that by that logic a novel is just ink or bytes too, but the garden was a persistent, shared object other people could visit, walk around, and remember, governed by stable rules the same way a coffee table is governed by physics, and if something can be visited, maintained, and damaged, it exists in the only sense that matters. Dan gave ground on the money but not the metaphysics: he said a novel survives the destruction of any single copy, whereas her garden existed entirely at the company's mercy, and anything that can cease to exist because a firm flips a switch was never really *hers* or even really a *thing* — its reality was borrowed, so the actual wrong was breach of consumer trust, not destruction of property. She replied that dependence on other people doesn't make stuff unreal — a language, a friendship, and a debt all exist only because people keep participating, they can all be destroyed by decisions, and losing them is a real loss, not a billing dispute. Then Dan said fine, maybe, but the "virtual object" framing still does no work: everything normatively important is carried by the mundane facts (money, reliance, distress), so calling it a garden adds nothing. Her last move was that it does add something: if the company restored a backup tomorrow, she'd be made whole in a way no refund could manage, and if Dan's right she should be indifferent between a restored backup and a check for the same amount — which she isn't.
