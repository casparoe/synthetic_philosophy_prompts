In my reading group we've been stuck on the particularism–generalism debate in moral philosophy for weeks, and I can no longer tell what the disagreement is even about. The particularists (in Dancy's vein) keep producing cases where the same consideration counts for an action in one context and against it in another: that something would be pleasant is usually a reason to do it, but not when the pleasure is in another's pain; that you promised is usually a reason, but perhaps not if the promise was extracted under duress. The generalists reply that this only shows we need more fine-grained principles: once you specify the consideration fully — that one would take pleasure in someone's suffering — its valence is stable, and principles survive. The particularists counter that the fully specified consideration is no longer anything anyone actually acts on, and that once principles get so specific they cover a single case, generalism has collapsed into particularism. The generalists answer that if the particularist's verdicts across cases are even coherent, there must be some pattern underwriting them, and any such pattern is a principle, so particularism collapses into generalism. Each side now accuses the other of holding a position that is either false or trivial. Please diagnose the stall rather than take a side: is this a verbal dispute about what counts as a 'principle', a substantive dispute about the metaphysics of reasons, or two parties answering different questions — one about deliberation, one about the structure of justification — under a single heading? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, an agreed test case, a clearer account of what reasons are for? Or is the standoff exactly what we should expect, and why?
