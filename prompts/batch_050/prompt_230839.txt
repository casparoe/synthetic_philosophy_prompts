I've been reading the second essay of Nietzsche's Genealogy of Morals and got stuck on this bit from §2 (Horace Samuel's translation):

"The immense work of what I have called, "morality of custom" (cp. Dawn of Day, Aphs. 9, 14, and 16), the actual work of man on himself during the longest period of the human race, his whole prehistoric work, finds its meaning, its great justification (in spite of all its innate hardness, despotism, stupidity, and idiocy) in this fact: man, with the help of the morality of customs and of social strait-waistcoats, was made genuinely calculable."

Write me a satirical government white paper (roughly 600-700 words, deadpan Whitehall-consultation voice, numbered clauses and all) in which the Home Office cites this passage as the intellectual warrant for a flagship policy: transitioning Britain from a guilt culture to a shame culture, on the grounds that shame is the cheaper and more scalable strait-waistcoat. The jokes have to come from the actual psychology of the two emotions -- shame needing an audience (hence the budget line for public noticeboards, CCTV and a national rota of disapproving onlookers), guilt working fine when no one is watching but being ruinously expensive to install and impossible to quality-assure, shame collapsing once the observers leave the room, guilt's awkward habit of attaching to the self rather than the act, and so on -- so a philosopher of emotion laughs rather than winces. The deep irony should stay deadpan but legible: the document reads Nietzsche's genealogy of how we were made calculable as a how-to manual, and never notices. By the end a reader should be genuinely unsure whether guilt or shame comes off worse, and whether a culture could pick even if it wanted to. Answer in British English please.
