So I've been reading the usual blogs forever and the CEV debate keeps coming up in the same shape: one side says coherent extrapolated volition is the only workable answer to "whose values does the AI optimize", other side says the whole idea papers over deep problems with preference aggregation and idealization. Both sides talk past each other a lot imo.

Draft the joint statement that a serious CEV proponent and a serious critic could both sign after an adversarial collaboration. The critic's position is something like: extrapolation smuggles in the extrapolator's values under the guise of "what you would want if you knew more", humans don't have coherent enough underlying preferences for there to be a determinate extrapolation to find, and even if they did, aggregating across billions of people either collapses to a dictatorship of whoever extrapolates well or averages into mush nobody endorses. The proponent's position is the standard optimistic one: the process is supposed to be robust to exactly these worries, idealization doesn't have to be value-laden if you operationalize it carefully, and every alternative (direct instruction, approval-directed, constitutional approaches) has worse versions of the same problems.

I want:
1. A numbered list of claims both can sign -- about what the actual problem of AI value alignment is, about what "extrapolation" would even have to do to count as working, about which failure modes are real.
2. The remaining disagreements, each stated in wording that side would actually accept for itself (no strawmen, I'd notice).
3. The cruxes -- my guess is one is whether "idealized preference" picks out anything determinate or whether underdetermination kills it, and another is whether the aggregation problem for CEV is worse than the aggregation problem every alternative also faces. But tell me if you think the real cruxes are elsewhere.
4. For each crux, something that would actually bear on it: an empirical result, a formal result, or a thought experiment both sides accept as relevant, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

Format: numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table.

Also: at the end, say for the collaboration document itself what would change your mind about it -- i.e., which of the agreed claims you think is most likely to actually be contested by one of the two camps in practice, or where the truce you've drawn is probably unstable, so a real proponent or critic would refuse to sign.
