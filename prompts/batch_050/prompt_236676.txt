I am a novelist at work on a book whose climactic chapter is a long dinner-table argument between a historian of philosophy and a physicist about what David Hume actually believed regarding causation. The chapter's epigraph is this passage from Section VII, Part I, of Hume's An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding:

"When we look about us towards external objects, and consider the operation of causes, we are never able, in a single instance, to discover any power or necessary connexion; any quality, which binds the effect to the cause, and renders the one an infallible consequence of the other. We only find, that the one does actually, in fact, follow the other."

My historian character endorses the so-called New Hume reading: Hume was himself a causal realist who believed there are objective powers or necessary connections in nature, and denied only that we can ever perceive or conceive them -- so the passage above reports our epistemic limits, not the non-existence of the connection. My physicist character holds the traditional reading: Hume thought there is nothing more to causation in the objects than constant conjunction, and that the idea of necessary connection is nothing but the felt determination of the mind produced by habit. Before I can decide which character gets the stronger final speech, I want your committed verdict. Give me your actual credence -- a single number -- that the New Hume reading is correct as an account of Hume's own considered view, and decompose it: your probability that this passage is best read as epistemic modesty about a real connection rather than as a denial of anything beyond regularity; your probability that Hume's broader language elsewhere (his talk of powers concealed from us, his two definitions of cause) tips the balance toward realism; your probability that the question of what Hume believed even has a determinate answer, given that a writer can hold a view in one mood and its denial in another. Assign each term a number with a line of justification, show how they combine, and tell me which sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to and what kind of textual evidence would move it most. Do not leave me with both readings on the table -- I need you to rule, with reasons, for one side of the dinner table.
