I chair the sustainability committee of a residential college, about 600 students, and our dining hall buys everything through one regional food distributor. Earlier this term we voted a proposal out of subcommittee to cut beef from the menu entirely — chicken and pork stay, this is just about beef on emissions and animal-welfare grounds.

The problem is that one committee member, an econ PhD student who I should say is in good faith, circulated a dissent making the inefficacy objection in an annoyingly precise form. Roughly his argument: the distributor places monthly orders to its packer, and by its own ordering policy the order only moves when trailing demand shifts more than a few percent over a quarter. Our college is well under one percent of the packer's total volume and we'd never move trailing demand by more than a fraction of the trigger band. So our dropping beef literally never changes a single order. The cattle that would've fed our students still get slaughtered, the meat gets sold elsewhere a hair cheaper, someone else eats slightly more of it. Net effect on cows killed and emissions: zero, forever. He also preempts the expected-value reply — fine, there's some tiny chance we're sitting exactly at a threshold in a given quarter, but he crunches it and says the expected number of cattle spared per year of our policy is well under one animal, and he calls building policy on that "statistical ethics." His proposal instead: keep the beef, and if the college wants to feel moral, write a yearly check to a climate charity where the causation is real.

I need to draft the committee's decision memo — adopt the beef cut, reject it, or adopt a modified version — and I want it to actually engage him rather than pat him on the head. Specifically it should:

1. Take his arithmetic seriously as stated (don't hand-wave the threshold structure), and say whether and why the expected-impact line still does work even if his numbers are right, or concede it doesn't.
2. Work out whether an institution choosing a standing policy for 600 people, indefinitely, is in the same moral position as one shopper at a supermarket — or whether that's just the individual case with extra steps.
3. Seriously consider at least two grounds for the policy that don't run through marginal causation at all (e.g. what the college participates in or expresses, or what rule we'd want similar institutions to adopt), and be honest about whether those grounds are doing real work or just laundering the same intuitions.
4. Say something fair about his charity-donation alternative, including whether "we could do both" is a dodge.
5. End with a concrete bottom line the committee can vote on, framed so he could write a reply to it rather than just lose.

Casual tone is fine, but it has to read like something that settles a decision.
