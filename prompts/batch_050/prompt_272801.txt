I'm taking a philosophy of math seminar this term and we just spent two weeks on computer-assisted proofs -- the four-colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture as the standard case studies. What struck me is that this debate has been running for almost fifty years now (since Appel and Haken in 1976) and it doesn't feel like it's converged at all. One side says a proof you can't survey, where the crucial step is a computer checking thousands of cases no human could ever check, just isn't a proof in the full sense -- it gives you confidence but not understanding, and understanding is what proofs are for. The other side says this is romantic nonsense: a proof is a proof, mathematicians make errors all the time, human referees are arguably less reliable than verified code, and anyway Flyspeck-style formal verification closes the gap completely. Yet the first side still isn't satisfied even by formal verification, and the second side thinks the first side keeps moving the goalposts.

Here's my working theory, which I came up with after arguing about this with my roommate (who's a CS major and thinks the philosophers are just being precious): I think the disagreement isn't really about computers at all. I think there are two different things people mean by "proof" -- something like a warrant for believing a theorem vs. an explanation of why the theorem is true -- and the unsurveyability debate is just where those two meanings come apart most dramatically, so both sides are right about their own concept and talking past each other. If that's right then the debate can't be settled by better verification technology, only by people admitting which thing they care about.

But I'm not confident in this. Is the impasse really just two concepts sharing one word, or is there a genuine substantive disagreement underneath -- say, about whether mathematical knowledge is essentially something a human mind can possess, or about what sorts of evidence legitimately confer mathematical certainty? I'd like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, backed up by what each side's strongest arguments actually commit them to, rather than a verdict on who's right. And if it is merely verbal, I want to know why it feels so non-verbal to the participants -- is there some deeper stakes doing hidden work? Also: is there anything that could actually end it, like a precise account of what "understanding" adds beyond warrant, or should I expect people to still be litigating this in another fifty years?
