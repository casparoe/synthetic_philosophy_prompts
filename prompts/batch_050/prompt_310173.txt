I'm running a 75-minute discussion section for 16 undergrads who have just skimmed Peirce on the pragmatic maxim, the fixation of belief, and fallibilism. Design an activity called Claims as Action Menus that makes them experience the difference between merely assenting to a proposition and being prepared to act on it.

Please give it an actual structure rather than a classroom discussion: small teams, private information, changing evidence, and a few consequential decisions. The basic mechanic should be that each team receives a claim whose abstract wording stays fixed while the costs of acting on it change. Teams can buy tests, delay, or commit to a practical course of action, but they cannot earn points just by announcing that they believe the claim. Ideally, the rules should push them to ask what observable and practical difference the claim could make, without collapsing Peirce into crude verificationism or the silly idea that truth means whatever is useful.

Include:

- setup and materials, all doable with index cards, tokens, and a projected timer
- detailed rules and a point scheme
- a minute-by-minute timeline
- at least one role that creates uneven access to evidence
- a moment where settling on a belief is genuinely relieving, even though the belief remains revisable
- a way to make overconfidence costly without making endless hesitation the winning strategy
- a debrief mapping specific moments in the game onto the pragmatic maxim, doubt-belief-inquiry, and fallibilism
- what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, especially students treating belief as a bet, confusing practical consequences with truth conditions, or gaming the scoring system
- an honest faciliator note about which parts of Peirce's view the activity distorts or cannot reproduce

No readings, bibliography, or historical trivia questions; the point is for the students to run into the philosophical problem before we name it.
