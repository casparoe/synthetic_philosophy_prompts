So I was rereading Einstein's essay "Why Socialism?" (the one he published in Monthly Review in 1949) and there's a move near the end that I can't quite get my head around. He spends most of the essay arguing that capitalism's problems are structural -- production for profit rather than use, competition forcing wages down and concentrating capital, technology destroying jobs instead of freeing anyone -- and concludes that a planned socialist economy is the answer. But then he immediately warns that a planned economy by itself isn't socialism at all, and that it could just as easily come with the complete enslavement of the individual, and he ends by asking how the bureaucracy of a planned economy can be kept from becoming all-powerful and how the rights of the individual can be protected under it. He basically admits he doesn't have an answer.

Please explain what's actually going on conceptually in this two-step. A few things I'd like untangled: (1) What work is the distinction between "planned economy" and "socialism" doing for him -- is it a definitional point, a political-institutional point, or something about what socialism is *for* given how he sets up the essay around the individual's dependence on society? (2) Does the structural diagnosis he gives for capitalism (that the problem is the incentive system, not greedy people) actually support his worry about bureaucracy, or is there a tension there -- if bad outcomes come from incentive structures rather than bad character, why wouldn't socialist bureaucrats be just as trapped by *their* incentive structure? (3) Is his unanswered closing question a genuine hole in the argument, or does it just show he's treating socialism as a value commitment rather than a worked-out institutional design?

I'd like an actual explanation of the conceptual structure, not a summary of the essay or a rundown of what later socialists thought about planning. Also feel free to push back if you think I'm misreading the passage.
