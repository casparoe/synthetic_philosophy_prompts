I teach nineteenth-century German philosophy and serve as the faculty editor of our department's annual satirical supplement, and I would like to commission this year's centerpiece. Please take on the role of a professional satirist with a genuine grounding in post-Kantian philosophy: the piece must be funny, but the humor has to be earned by the philosophy itself, not merely decorated with terminology. Think carefully before drafting, and begin your work with a short private brainstorming phase in which you list at least six distinct structural features of the dialectic you could smuggle into the piece, then select the strongest four or five.

The assignment: write an internal corporate document — specifically, the facilitator's guide for a newly launched employee-recognition program — authored by a management consultancy whose senior partner read the lordship-and-bondage dialectic in Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit and took it as a manual of best practices rather than as the diagnosis of a pathology. The guide should be presented as numbered sections with facilitator notes, troubleshooting FAQs, and at least one appendix.

The satire must reproduce, in deadpan HR prose, the actual architecture of the dialectic rather than a caricature of it. The recognition the program promises managers must come from employees whose dependence the program itself engineers, and the facilitator notes must register — without ever understanding — why such recognition keeps failing to satisfy: why coerced affirmation is worthless as confirmation of an independent self. Meanwhile the employees, consigned to the actual formative labor of the firm, should be quietly developing the competence, self-certainty, and independence that the program was designed to confer from above on their superiors, whose relationship to the company's products remains one of pure consumption and enjoyment. The FAQ should include at least one entry troubleshooting why mandatory recognition quotas produce diminishing returns, and the appendix should address, with mounting unease, the observation that the people doing the work no longer seem to need the program at all.

Constraints: approximately 800–900 words; sustained deadpan throughout, with no winking at the reader; no named living persons, and Hegel himself should appear nowhere in the document — the consultant's theoretical source should be legible only to readers who already know the Phenomenology. The piece must be fully intelligible and amusing to a reader who has never read Hegel, while containing a second layer of jokes for someone who has. The document should end at the moment the program's internal contradiction becomes unmanageable, mirroring the instability of the dialectic itself, and it should do so in the consultant's own baffled voice rather than through authorial commentary. A specialist should finish the piece feeling that the satire was fair — that the corporate absurdities exaggerate real features of the dialectic rather than invented ones.
