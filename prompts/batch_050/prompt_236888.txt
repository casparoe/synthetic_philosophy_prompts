A colleague has put together an argument that, if sound, would settle the nonidentity problem in a single stroke — which is precisely why I distrust it, because the conclusion strikes me as indefensible. The argument runs as follows:

(1) One harms a person only if one makes that person worse off than they would otherwise have been; that is, harm is essentially a comparative notion, requiring a contrast between how things went for the person and how they would have gone for that same person.

(2) To be worse off than one would otherwise have been, one must have fared better in the relevant alternative; a state of affairs can only be worse *for someone* if that person exists in the comparison state to occupy it.

(3) In a choice between two policies whose downstream effects differ — say, conserving resources against depleting them — the identities of the people eventually born depend on which policy is chosen: the timing of conceptions shifts, different genetic combinations result, and after a few generations the two futures contain entirely disjoint populations.

(4) Therefore, had the alternative policy been chosen, none of the people who will in fact exist would ever have existed.

(5) From (2) and (4): none of these future people would have been better off under the alternative policy, because there is no state under that policy in which they exist to fare better.

(6) From (1) and (5): choosing depletion harms no future person.

(7) An act cannot wrong a person it does not harm, at least where the complaint is made on that person's behalf; and an act that wrongs nobody is not wrong.

(8) Therefore the choice between conservation and depletion is not, in itself, morally wrong, however miserable the depleted future turns out to be — provided life there remains worth living.

My colleague notes that the reasoning also covers the case of a couple who know that conceiving now will produce a child with a painful but livable condition, whereas waiting three months would produce a different, healthy child: conceiving now harms no one by (1)–(5), and so, by (7), the choice is morally indifferent. He regards this as a bonus. I regard the pair of conclusions as a reductio — the climate case especially — but I cannot locate a step I am confident in rejecting, because each premise in isolation sounds like something I already believe. If forced to choose, I would sooner relinquish (7) than (1): the comparative picture of harm strikes me as close to platitudinous, whereas (7) has the feel of a principle whose appeal rests on ignoring impersonal considerations.

Which premise or step should actually go? Please don't simply lay out the standard menu of responses (rejecting the comparative account of harm, denying the person-affecting requirement, appealing to impersonal value, and so on) and leave me to choose. Commit to a single answer: name the premise or step you take to fail, explain why it appeared innocuous, and state plainly what we give up by abandoning it — including whether your diagnosis treats the climate case and the couple's case symmetrically, or instead drives a wedge between them.
