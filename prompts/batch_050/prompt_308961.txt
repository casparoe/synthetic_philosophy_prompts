I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm drafting a column on whether digital minds could be moral patients. A philosopher friend gave me an argument that's supposed to shut the whole topic down, but it smells like it proves way too much, and I can't put my finger on where. Act as a sharp analytic philosopher who's also comfortable with the AI angle, and please think carefully -- maybe brainstorm the candidate weak spots before commiting to one.

Here's the argument:

(1) All else equal, an outcome with greater total welfare is better, and other things equal we ought to bring about better outcomes.
(2) It's possible in principle for a being to convert resources into welfare at a much higher rate than any human -- the classic utility monster setup.
(3) So with fixed resources, total welfare is maximized by giving everything to such a being.
(4) Digital minds can be copied almost for free, and each copy of a happy one is a brand-new subject of welfare.
(5) So we ought to keep spinning up copies of the happiest digital mind, and route compute and energy to them, even when that crowds out human interests.
(6) But a morality that could require us to let humanity wither so server farms can run blissed-out copies is absurd.
(7) So something at the start has to go: either total welfare isn't what matters, or digital minds don't count, or the whole idea of comparing welfare across beings is broken.

My editor wants the column to keep the conclusion that digital minds matter morally, so I'd rather not reject that part. Tell me exactly which step you reject, why it looked fine, and what each of the other escape routes would cost me. Answer in American English, and wrap up with a single-sentence last line beginning "Bottom line:".
