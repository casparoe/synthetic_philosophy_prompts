People keep reaching for the contract analogy whenever consent comes up: informed consent in medicine or research is "like signing a contract" -- there's disclosure (the fine print), a signature (the form), capacity requirements, and doctrines about duress and fraud that void the whole thing. And it's doing real argumentative work: hospitals treat the signed form as settling whether consent was valid, and tech companies lean on the same picture when they say you "agreed" to the terms of service by clicking through.

Audit the analogy for me. Give the correspondence mapping explicitly -- what plays the role of offer, consideration, signature, fraud in the inducement, duress, incapacity -- and then say where it breaks. Some candidates I have in mind: contracts create obligations between parties while consent seems to waive a right or grant a permission; you can often revoke consent mid-procedure but not un-sign a contract; contract law has remedies for breach so a "bad" contract isn't nothing, whereas invalid consent to surgery is just battery. Also say what the analogy quietly imports -- e.g. that the paperwork is the consent rather than evidence of it, or that not reading was your own fault. Finally: does the conclusion "they signed / they clicked, so they consented" survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the contract picture is the wrong source domain, is there a better one (voting? waiving a right? giving someone a key?) or does consent just not reduce to any familiar transaction?
