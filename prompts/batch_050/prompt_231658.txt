I keep circling the same question about Francis Fukuyama's end-of-history thesis, and it isn't the usual one about whether he was right. It's a demarcation question: given how the thesis is constructed, what would even count as evidence against it? Here is the passage that forces the question, from the Introduction to The End of History and the Last Man (1992), where Fukuyama restates the argument of his 1989 essay "The End of History?":

"But these problems were ones of incomplete implementation of the twin principles of liberty and equality on which modern democracy is founded, rather than of flaws in the principles themselves. While some present-day countries might fail to achieve stable liberal democracy, and others might lapse back into other, more primitive forms of rule like theocracy or military dictatorship, the ideal of liberal democracy could not be improved on."

My problem is that I have strong gut verdicts about which real-world phenomena should count against the thesis, and I can't tell what principle they're adding up to. Here they are:

1. China's four decades of sustained growth under one-party rule, with no democratic transition in sight: this counts against the thesis. Not decisively, but genuinely.
2. Iran's theocratic republic: this does not count, even though it has survived over forty years and its founders clearly intended it as an alternative model.
3. The failure of the Arab Spring: does not count. A failed attempt to arrive at liberal democracy is not a rival destination.
4. Democratic backsliding in Hungary, where the institutions hollow out but the language of democracy is retained: does not count.
5. Survey evidence of declining expressed support for democracy among young people in long-established democracies, even while their institutions remain intact: this counts.
6. A hypothetical: in 2150, a single small, prosperous, stable, non-expansionist state organized coherently on non-liberal principles — say, a functioning Confucian meritocracy: does not count.
7. Russia presenting itself as an illiberal model and attracting imitators among elected politicians elsewhere: does not count.

I can see that the passage blesses some of these verdicts — Fukuyama explicitly pre-classifies theocracy and dictatorship as lapses, not rivals, which covers 2 and arguably 4 and 7. But notice the tension between 1 and 6: if a coherent, successful non-liberal alternative counts when it's large and powerful but not when it's small, then the thesis is covertly about power and scale rather than about the ideal — which seems to betray the passage. And notice the tension between 4 and 5: both are erosions inside functioning democracies, yet I want to count one and not the other, and the only difference I can articulate is institutional wobble versus erosion of the ideal's legitimacy itself — a distinction I'm not sure survives scrutiny.

What I want from you:

First, state the most precise principle you can that captures my seven verdicts — something of the form "a phenomenon counts against the end-of-history thesis if and only if…" — and say which case motivates each clause. If no single principle works, give me the two best candidates and what each one sacrifices.

Second, tell me honestly which verdict your best principle cannot accommodate, and argue for either overturning that verdict or complicating the principle, rather than quietly splitting the difference.

Third, interpret the passage. Does Fukuyama's distinction between incomplete implementation and principled flaws, and between lapses and rivals, supply the demarcation my principle needs — or does it render the thesis unfalsifiable, since any apparent counterexample can be reclassified as a lapse? On his terms, is Iran a lapse into theocracy or a competing ideal, and does the passage actually settle that?

Finally, apply your principle to three cases I haven't listed: (a) China stagnates economically but retains one-party rule and grows more repressive; (b) the established democracies converge on depoliticized technocratic administration, with high satisfaction and no ideological contest anywhere; (c) a coherent new ideology — imagine some future ecological authoritarianism — wins serious adherents across many countries but holds state power nowhere. No citations or bibliography needed; reason from the passage and the cases.
