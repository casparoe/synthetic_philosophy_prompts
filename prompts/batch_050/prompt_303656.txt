I work as a backend engineer, and a metaphor I keep reaching for -- both at work and in everyday life -- is that testimony, meaning believing things because other people tell you they're true, is basically a data transfer. When a colleague tells me the staging server was down at 3 a.m., I picture it like receiving a packet: the payload is the claim, and my justification for accepting it comes from metadata I verify independently -- the sender's track record, whether the claim coheres with logs I've seen, whether they had any incentive to misreport. On this picture, testimony is never self-certifying; like an unsigned binary, it only earns trust once you've checked it against things you trust for other reasons.

Lately, though, I've been pressed on this by a friend who studied philosophy. She says the data-transfer picture gets testimony wrong at a deep level: when someone tells you something, you aren't (or at least aren't always) merely inferring the truth of the claim from independent evidence about the speaker's reliability, the way you'd validate a checksum. Rather, the speaker's act of telling itself puts you in a position to believe -- their assurance is doing normative work that no amount of third-person evidence about their reliability could fully replace, the same way a promise creates an obligation rather than merely predicting future behavior. I find this genuinely confusing. It seems to me that any warranted reliance on a teller has to bottom out in evidence somewhere, the way trust in a certificate authority ultimately bottoms out in something you verified yourself. But I also notice that in practice I believe my friends constantly without running anything like a background check on each claim, and I don't feel I'm being irrational.

So: how far does the data-transfer analogy for testimony actually hold? Please lay out the correspondences explicitly (what plays the role of the payload, the checksum, the trusted sender, the man-in-the-middle attack), then identify where the mapping breaks down. In particular, does the analogy smuggle in the assumption that testimonial justification must always reduce to non-testimonial evidence, thereby begging the question against my friend's view? And once the disanalogies are on the table, can either side -- testimony as mere evidence-transmission versus testimony as a sui generis source of warrant -- actually claim support from how communication works, or is the metaphor doing illicit argumentative work for whichever side wields it? If there's a better engineering metaphor for the competing picture, I'd be glad to hear it.
