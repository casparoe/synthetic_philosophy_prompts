So I grew up in a pretty tight-knit Korean immigrant community in Vancouver -- church on Sundays, everybody's parents knowing everybody's business, the whole thing -- and now I'm in my third year of a philosophy degree and we've been reading about individualism vs communitarianism, roughly the West vs East Asia framing, and it's obviously personal for me. Anyway, for a term paper I put together what I thought was a killer argument against the liberal-individualist picture, and the more I sit with it the more I think it proves way too much, but I can't find the bad step. Here it is, roughly: (1) Nobody forms their values, language, or even their sense of what counts as a reason in a vacuum -- all of that comes from some community that raised you. (2) If your very capacity to evaluate and criticize things is itself a product of your community, then any criticism you make is only possible because of the community's own resources. (3) So genuine criticism is always internal criticism -- you're always just using one part of your tradition against another part, the way a reformer invokes values the community already holds. (4) But then criticism from *outside* a community -- say, a Westerner condemning Confucian filial hierarchy, or for that matter a Confucian condemning Western individualism as atomized and lonely -- has no standing, because the critic is importing standards that simply don't apply to a form of life that didn't generate them. (5) And if external criticism never has standing, then no community's practices can ever be wrong full stop, only inconsistent with their own commitments. (6) But that conclusion is absurd -- it would mean nobody anywhere gets to say that, e.g., a society practicing something genuinely cruel is doing anything worse than being untrue to itself, and I don't believe that. So somewhere in (1)-(5) I've gone wrong.

My TA glanced at it and said something like "looks fine to me, maybe just soften (5)," which was not helpful. The thing is, I really want to keep (1) and (2) -- they seem almost undeniable to me, and they're what make the communitarian picture attractive in the first place, plus they fit my actual experience of realizing mid-argument with my dad that I was using Confucian-sounding premises even when I thought I was being a good little liberal. And (3) seems to follow pretty directly from (2)? Like, if the tools are borrowed, the criticism is internal, what else could it be? But I notice the jump from (3) to (4) and (5) is where the conclusion goes from "plausible thesis about how reform works" to "no one can say anything about anyone, ever," and I can't tell whether the problem is a sneaky shift in what "internal" means between the steps, or whether (2) is doing more work than it's entitled to, or whether I should just accept (5) and learn to love it.

Can you tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs? Specifically I want to know: why does the bad step (if there is one) look so natural given the premises before it, and what do the standard moves here -- the person who says all critique is immanent, the person who says reason gives us a standpoint outside any community, the person who tries to split the difference -- each have to give up? If it turns out the argument is actually valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to drop, because I have a strong attachment to (1) and (2) but I'm willing to be talked out of almost anything else.
