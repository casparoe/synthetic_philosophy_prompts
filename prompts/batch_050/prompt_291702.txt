You are a philosopher of history with a strong background in the metaphysics of causation and counterfactual reasoning. Think carefully here: before giving any verdict, reconstruct the strongest version of each side's position, then assess the exchange move by move. Lead with your bottom-line verdict in the very first sentence, then give your reasoning -- no preamble, and do not restate the question back to me.

At a conference dinner last week I listened to two colleagues -- one a historian of early twentieth-century Europe, the other a political scientist who works on great-power conflict -- argue about whether the First World War was contingent. Neither convinced the other, and I couldn't settle it myself. Here is the exchange as accurately as I can reconstruct it.

The historian opened: the war was radically contingent. On the standard telling, the assassins in Sarajevo had already failed that morning, and only a wrong turn by the Archduke's driver put the car in front of Princip. Remove the wrong turn and there is no assassination, no July Crisis, and no war -- at least not that war. Singular causal claims in history just are counterfactuals, and this counterfactual looks about as solid as such claims ever get.

The political scientist replied that Europe in 1914 was primed: rigid alliance commitments, mobilization timetables that could not be paused once started, an ongoing arms race, and planners in several capitals who believed war sooner was better than war later. The Moroccan crises and the Balkan wars had already brought the powers close several times. If not Sarajevo, some other incident within a few years would have lit it. The assassination was a trigger, and triggers are interchangeable; what explains the war is whatever made triggers interchangeable.

The historian answered that interchangeable-trigger talk is unfalsifiable. Several crises had already passed without war, and no matter how many more had passed, the structuralist could always say the underlying conditions were still there, waiting. A claim consistent with every outcome explains nothing. Besides, the timing matters: given a few more years, Russia's military expansion program would have been complete, Germany's generals would no longer have believed time was working against them, and the window would have closed. Structural accounts build timing out of the explanation precisely where timing is doing the work.

The political scientist countered that the historian's counterfactuals are unearned. The very sensitivity the historian celebrates -- a wrong turn changes the world -- means no one can say what the assassination-free world actually looks like, since near-identical worlds diverge wildly. So "no assassination, no war" is not an evaluable claim but a preferred narrative. Far from establishing contingency, sensitive dependence is an argument against counterfactual history as a method.

The historian said this proves too much: by that standard no singular causal judgment about the past -- that an embargo caused a recession, that a storm rather than bad engineering broke a levee -- is ever assessable, and the political scientist makes such judgments constantly. Moreover, one cannot wield this argument against counterfactuals and then explain the war structurally, because structural claims are counterfactuals too: saying the alliance system made war likely just means that most nearby worlds containing that system contain a war.

The political scientist conceded that both sides traffic in counterfactuals but insisted the question is which ones are well supported: claims about what tends to happen across a range of broadly similar cases are tractable, whereas claims about single delicate chains of events are not. The historian's last word was that there are no broadly similar cases to 1914 -- it is a sample of one.

Please adjudicate. Take the exchange point by point: Who is right about whether the wrong turn establishes contingency? Who is right about whether structural explanations are unfalsifiable? Does sensitive dependence undermine counterfactual history, underwrite it, or somehow do both at once? And who has the better of the sample-of-one exchange? Finally, say what you think both parties are missing -- in particular, whether "was the First World War contingent?" might be several different questions being traded under one heading, and whether the trigger-versus-structure framing is a real competition at all.
