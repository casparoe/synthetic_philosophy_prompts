I am a PhD student in philosophy of science, and for the past year my department's metaphysics of science reading group has been stuck in a running dispute between two factions. One defends a broadly Humean picture in the tradition of the best-system analysis: the world is fundamentally a mosaic of local, particular facts, and the laws of nature are nothing over and above whichever generalizations belong to the systematization of that mosaic that best balances simplicity against informativeness. The other defends an anti-Humean picture on which laws are genuine, irreducible features of reality that govern or constrain what happens, whether as necessary connections among universals or as metaphysically primitive facts. Our meetings keep dissolving into the same unproductive exchanges: the Humeans insist that governing is an idle wheel that explains nothing, the anti-Humeans insist that a mere summary of the mosaic cannot explain why the mosaic is the way it is, and neither side will state the other's position in terms the other accepts. I have volunteered to draft, as if for an adversarial collaboration, the joint statement the two camps would sign if they ever sat down to write one, and I want you to produce that draft.

The document should have three parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims: theses both camps can endorse without reservation, which should be substantive enough to be informative rather than vacuous (for instance, commitments about what role laws play in actual scientific practice, what any adequate account must recover about the relation between laws and counterfactuals, and what neither side is claiming). Second, a statement of the residual disagreements, each formulated in neutral wording that both sides would accept as a fair description of their own view and of what divides them; where a disagreement turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, say so explicitly and reframe or discard it. Third, a list of cruxes: sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. I suspect the cruxes include whether laws must explain their instances (and whether the demand for such explanation is even legitimate), whether the best-system account's dependence on standards of simplicity and strength can be made objective without making laws partly mind-dependent, whether the notion of governing can be spelled out in an intelligible way rather than merely gestured at, and whether a Humean mosaic requires an implausible conspiracy-like coincidence among unrelated particulars. But treat these as my conjectures: keep, refine, or replace them as the drafting requires.

For each crux, the statement should specify at least one thought experiment, argument, or case that both camps agree bears on it, with each side's verdict recorded in advance, so that the document identifies what would count as movement. Candidate cases you might use, described so that neither side can quibble about the setup: a possible world containing exactly one particle moving at constant velocity forever (does it have laws?); two worlds with identical mosaics that an anti-Humean wants to describe as differing in their laws (is that description coherent?); a deterministic world whose history happens to fall into an exceptionally simple pattern (do the simple generalizations thereby become laws, or must something further be true?); and the observation that a Humean probability distribution assigns positive chance to future courses of events that would, if realized, falsify that very distribution (is this a defect of the view or a feature any account of chance must accommodate?). Where a case splits into variants on which the camps' verdicts diverge, present the variants rather than papering over the split. The goal is a document a fair-minded member of each faction could put their name to, so please avoid formulations that quietly presuppose one side's framework.
