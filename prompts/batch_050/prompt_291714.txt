I've been reading LessWrong and alignment-forum arguments for years, and the tool AI vs. agent AI dispute still seems to go in cricles. Draft the joint statement two reasonable camps could produce after a serious adversarial collaboration.

Camp one, the tool camp: the safest useful systems should be designed as bounded problem-solvers rather than continuing actors. They answer questions, propose plans, or evaluate options, but have no standing objectives, persistent memory unless explicitly supplied, independent access to resources, or authority to act. The human and the surrounding institution remain the agent.

Camp two, the agent camp: this separation mostly hides the real agency rather than removing it. Once a system is good enough, users will connect it to calendars, code execution, markets, robots, and other models; even if the weights only answer prompts, the whole deployed process becomes a long-horizon agent. So safety work should face the resulting goals, incentives, and control problems directly.

Use one recurring case to keep the discussion concrete: an AI "chief of staff" that only drafts recommendations, but whose outputs are automatically emailed to six human deputies, whose partially automated decisions feed back into the next day's prompt, and whose operator rewards the setup when quarterly profits rise. Nobody gave the model a utility function, and no single human understands the whole loop. Please don't turn the answer into a bibliography; I just want the conceptual work.

The statement should have:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps can sign. These should separate claims about the model itself, the socio-technical loop around it, and the incentives to expand its authority. Avoid empty statements like agency is complicated.

2. A disagreements table. Each disagreement should get one neutral formulation and then the wording each side would prefer for its own position. Explicitly mark any disagreement that appears to be verbal rather than substantive.

3. The main cruxes: questions such that resolving one would make at least one camp revise its position. Possible cruxes include whether agency can be assigned to a component rather than the whole loop, whether repeatedly following a tool's advice differs in principle from delegation, and whether keeping an approval click preserves meaningful human control.

4. For each crux, a concrete experiment, historical pattern, or future observation that both sides agree bears on it. Record each side's prediction in advance, and note any result that would embarrass both sides rather than vindicating one.

5. A short section on the chief-of-staff case explaining which parts of the description each camp thinks are doing the real work. In particular, don't let either side answer merely by saying that the system is obviously a tool because the model has no explicit goal, or obviously an agent because the institution profits.

Keep it constructive rather than declaring a winner. If the honest conclusion is that tool and agent name different points on several dimensions, say which dimensions matter and which parts of the usual debate should be retired. At the end, add a candid note on where the joint statement is most likely to be confused or unfair, and what argument or empirical finding would change your mind about how you drew the cruxes.
