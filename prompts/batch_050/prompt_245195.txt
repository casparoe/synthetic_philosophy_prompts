Last weekend I got into a long and unexpectedly heated argument with an old friend from graduate school about Kant's treatment of animals, and I want an outside referee. The flashpoint was §17 of the Doctrine of Virtue in The Metaphysics of Morals, where Kant (paraphrasing, not quoting) holds that we may kill animals quickly and without pain and work them within their capacities, that cruel treatment of animals is condemned because it dulls the natural fellow-feeling that is serviceable to morality in our dealings with human beings, that agonizing experiments undertaken for mere speculation are to be abhorred when their ends could be reached otherwise, and that even gratitude for the long service of an old horse or dog is only indirectly a duty regarding the animal -- strictly speaking, always a duty of the human being to himself. My friend insists this framework is underappreciated and, applied consistently, delivers all the right verdicts; I insisted it delivers some monstrous ones. Here are the verdicts she stood behind, one by one: beating a dog for amusement, wrong; painlessly euthanizing a healthy dog that has become expensive and inconvenient, permissible; a painful experiment on animals that genuinely cannot be replaced and serves a serious medical end, permissible; the same experiment conducted out of idle curiosity when an alternative exists, wrong; and turning a faithful old carriage horse out to starve rather than paying for its retirement, wrong.

Here is the case we could not resolve: a reclusive lighthouse keeper, the last human on an island, knows with certainty that he will die within days and will never interact with another person. Stipulate, as the case requires, that nothing he now does to his cat can coarsen his dealings with human beings, corrupt his moral dispositions in any way that will ever touch a human, or be discovered by anyone. On his final evening he torments the cat, painfully but not fatally, purely because he finds it diverting. Predict my friend's verdict on this case, and tell me which rule she is implicitly running across her five earlier verdicts: something like Kant's own indirect-duty rule (acts regarding animals are wrong insofar as they erode dispositions needed for duty to persons), a disguised animal-welfare rule (no unnecessary suffering), a virtue rule (wrong insofar as the act itself manifests cruelty or ingratitude, whatever its downstream effects), or some hybrid. The hermit case, I take it, forces the indirect-duty rule toward a verdict she will be loath to give, while the welfare and virtue rules condemn the keeper without strain -- but I want to know whether the five earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine which rule is hers, since all five seem compatible with at least the first three candidates.

So: give me your prediction of her verdict on the lighthouse keeper, with your reasons; say which of her five earlier cases is the closest analog to the new one and why; identify which candidate rules her track record genuinely cannot distinguish; and finally, name the further hypothetical case -- different from the hermit case -- that would best discriminate between whichever two rules remain live after your analysis (for instance, a case designed to separate the no-unnecessary-suffering rule from the virtue rule, which agree about the keeper). If your conclusion is that her five verdicts are simply inconsistent with one another rather than jointly explicable by any rule, say so and identify the tension.
