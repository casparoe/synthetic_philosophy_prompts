I am a retired entomologist who spent some thirty years studying social Hymenoptera, and I have lately been reading the philosophical literature on biological individuality. There I keep meeting an analogy I know well from my own field: the ant colony or beehive as a single organism. The correspondences are familiar — the queen as germ line, the sterile workers as soma, the nest boundary as skin, colony thermoregulation and foraging rhythms as organismal physiology. In my experience the analogy is rarely offered as a mere flourish. It is made to do argumentative work: to license the claims that natural selection has shaped colonies just as it shaped bodies, that workers are organs rather than individuals, that conflict within a colony (laying workers, for instance) is a pathology on a par with cancer, and that the colony is therefore an organism in whatever sense philosophers ought to care about.

I would like an audit of how far this analogy holds. Please set out the mapping explicitly — a table of correspondences is welcome — and then examine where it breaks. Some candidate disanalogies from my own notebooks: workers are not genetically identical to one another, and in multiply-mated species a colony is a mosaic of patrilines; there is no sequestered germ line, since workers retain ovaries and occasionally use them; the parts are motile and can defect, and whole colonies sometimes merge; and there is nothing in a colony comparable to the single-cell bottleneck from which every cell of a body descends. Which of these disanalogies matter for the conclusions the analogy is asked to carry, and which are merely cosmetic? I would also like the test extended to a newer case: the same style of analogy is now applied to hosts together with their microbiomes, where the parts do not even share a line of descent. Does the mapping fare better or worse there?

My own hunch is that everything turns on whether the evolutionary fates of the parts are bound together — whether all fitness must pass through a common bottleneck — but I may well be wrong about that. Test my criterion against your audit, state plainly which of the advertised conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the organism analogy cannot bear the weight put on it, propose a better one and explain what it captures that the old picture misses.
