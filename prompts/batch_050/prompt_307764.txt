I’m a novelist, not a set theorist, so forgive me if I garble the technical bits alot. For a story I’m writing, I’ve been assuming a fairly old-fashioned position: the continuum hypothesis is either true or false of the actual universe of sets. The fact that the usual ZFC axioms can prove neither it nor its negation just shows that those axioms are incomplete, not that there is no fact of the matter. If mathematicians eventually adopted stronger axioms that settled the size of the continuum, they would be discovering the answer, not merely choosing a convention.

What would count against that position? Please don’t stop at independence from ZFC; I already know that part, and defenders of the view think it only exposes the weakness of one axiom system. I want a map of more serious threats:

- Are there conceivable formal results that would badly damage it, such as contradictions high enough in the large-cardinal hierarchy, results showing that every attractive extension settles the continuum arbitrarily, or a collapse among the structures set theorists currently distinguish? What exactly would each result undermine?
- Suppose my fictional future has two equally successful mathematical cultures. One adopts axioms making the continuum hypothesis true, the other adopts equally fruitful axioms making it false. Both agree on ordinary finite arithmetic, both can study the other’s set theory as a coherent structure, and neither can expose the other as confused. Would that count against objective truth here, or only against our ability to know it? Would defenders themselves concede that it is evidence, or dismiss it as sociology?
- Is there a principled difference between saying there are many equally legitimate set-theoretic universes and saying there is one universe but many incomplete descriptions of it? What argument could force a realist to accept the first description?
- Which objections are fatal, which would force major revisions, and which are merely awkward?
- Finally, has the position been made so flexible that no mathematical development could falsify it? If so, is that actually a defect in mathematics, where immunity to empirical refutation is normal?
