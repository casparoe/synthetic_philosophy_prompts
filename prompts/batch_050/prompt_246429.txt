I'm on the organizing committee of a workshop on collective responsibility, and instead of another panel where two speakers give sequential talks, I want to try an adversarial collaboration: two people with opposed views on corporate moral agency produce a single joint statement before the event. Help me draft the document they should aim to produce. One party is a defender of robust corporate moral agency, holding that companies with the right internal decision structures can be genuinely blameworthy agents in their own right, over and above their members. The other is a skeptic, holding that moral responsibility requires capacities (they usually mention phenomenal consciousness and the ability to feel guilt) that no organization has, so that corporate blame is at best a useful fiction or shorthand for blaming individuals. To keep them honest, the workshop has fixed one test case they must both address: a logistics firm whose routing software, after hundreds of individually reviewed and individually defensible tweaks, ends up concentrating delivery-truck emissions in a poor neighborhood; no employee intended this, no single decision caused it, and the company learned of the pattern only when residents organized. Activists say the company itself is to blame; the firm's lawyers say there were errors but no wrongdoer.

Write the joint statement as it should look after a good-faith collaboration. It should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both sides can sign, covering what happened in the test case, what blame practices are for, and what capacities any theory of responsibility must attribute to a blamed party; (2) a table of residual disagreements, each phrased in words the side it describes would accept as accurate, not a strawman; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move one party, where I suspect one is whether the firm's aggregate pattern of decisions constitutes something with belief- and intention-like states of its own, and another is whether blame can do its social work if the blamed entity cannot feel anything; and (4) for each crux, a further case, consideration, or finding that both parties agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction about it recorded in advance so the collaboration cannot be retrofitted later. If some part of their dispute turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, identify which part and say why.

Finally, since a document like this can smuggle in slant while pretending to be neutral, end with a short drafter's note: say where in your statement the wording most plausibly tilts toward one side despite your efforts, and what objection from the disfavored party would force you to rewrite that passage. Also say what evidence or argument, if it emerged, would make the whole adversarial framing collapse — that is, what would show that one side is simply not a defensible position and the workshop should have been a lecture instead.
