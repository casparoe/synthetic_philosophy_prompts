I'm in a second-year philosophy seminar on minds, animals, and machines, with 24 students and one 75-minute session. Our professor keeps saying that sentience and moral status may come in degrees, but our discussions keep collapsing into a binary: either something is conscious and matters, or it is basically an object. I want to design an in-class game that makes students feel the awkward middle ground rather than just talk about it. It should involve familiar-ish cases such as a thermostat, a houseplant, an ant, a fish, an octopus, a chatbot, and a human being, but avoid turning into a debate about which single theory of consciousness is correct.

One idea I had, though you can improve or replace it, is a game where teams first receive entities described only by functional profiles: sensitivity to damage, flexibility of behavior, memory, self-maintenance, learning, social responsiveness, and so forth. Each team has a limited pool of moral protection points to distribute, knowing that some entities will later suffer preventable harms. Only after they commit are the familiar identities revealed, along with a few previously hidden capacities. Teams can revise their allocations, but revisions cost points. The aim would not be to reward one objectively right ranking, but to expose shifts caused by labels, uncertainty, thresholds, and the feeling that some differences are gradual while others seem morally enormous. Could you turn this into a runnable classroom activity with exact setup, materials, round-by-round rules, and a points system that does not trivialize the moral choices?

Please include a debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto the relevant distinctions: degree versus threshold, behavior versus inner experience, species membership versus capacities, and caution under uncertainty. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it, especially students gaming the point system, arguing endlessly over the profile descriptions, or treating the human case as automatically beyond comparison, and explain how the facilitator should handle each problem. Be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce and will therefore need to be supplied through discussion. End your answer with a last line beginning Summary: followed by one sentence stating the central lesson the activity is meant to teach.
