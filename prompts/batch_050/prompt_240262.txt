I run a small family foundation that gives grants to grassroots environmental groups, and twice a year I sit with a five-person committee reviewing about forty applications. Over time we've become uneasy about something we have no good language for: some applicants do everything right on paper but seem motivated mainly by career-building, networking, or the prestige of being associated with our foundation, while others are clearly in it because they care about the river or the wetland itself, sometimes to their own material detriment. We suspect the second kind of group is a better bet, or at least the kind we want to fund, but whenever we try to make this judgment we end up relying on vibes -- someone seemed "genuine" in the interview -- and I worry this is just a way of laundering our personal likes and dislikes. I've thought a bit about the underlying puzzle on my own: whether there's a principled difference between doing the right thing and doing the right thing for the right reason, and whether that difference is ever knowable from the outside.

Please turn this into a procedure my committee could actually run during our review cycle. I'm not asking for an essay on moral worth; I'm asking for an ordered set of tests or questions -- what to ask first, what evidence counts for and against at each step, where the procedure should stop and declare the question unanswerable rather than forcing a verdict, and which judgments can never be removed from it no matter how well it's designed. The procedure has to respect constraints we actually face: each applicant gets about ninety minutes of committee attention total, we can request documents and one site visit but nothing invasive, and whatever we decide must be explainable to the board in writing. It also has to handle some hard cases honestly: the applicant whose motives are mixed (care and ambition in the same person, which is surely most of us); the applicant who started for the wrong reasons and grew into the right ones; and the applicant whose stated reasons are noble but whose choices consistently track publicity. I'd like it to be explicit about where the procedure risks punishing the awkward, the inarticulate, or the private person who simply doesn't perform sincerity well, since some of the best groups we fund are run by people who are terrible in interviews.

To show me the procedure works, run it on three hypothetical applicants: a photogenic start-up nonprofit whose founder left a consulting career, speaks fluently about mission, and has attracted press but whose staffing churn is high; a scruffy volunteer group with no online presence, patchy bookkeeping, and a twenty-year record of unglamorous restoration work; and a group where the evidence points both ways and the procedure itself should tell us to stop and treat motive as undecidable. Include at least one worked case where the procedure delivers a verdict I'll find uncomfortable -- funding someone I personally warm to despite the signs, or declining someone I like -- and tell me at the end what this whole exercise cannot do, even in principle, because other people's reasons for acting may be the kind of thing no checklist can reach.
