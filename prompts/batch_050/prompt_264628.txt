I help organize the discussion track at our local rationalist meetup — a group of about twelve regulars, most of whom have been reading LessWrong and adjacent blogs for years — and we have a 75-minute slot coming up. Nearly everyone in the room can state the Condorcet jury theorem and has absorbed its standard lesson: when individuals are better than chance and vote independently, majority aggregation tracks the truth. Almost nobody has sat with the discursive dilemma, which I'd describe as the jury theorem's evil twin: aggregation that behaves impeccably premise-by-premise can commit the group to a conclusion no majority endorses, or even one every member individually rejects.

Design an activity in which the group produces the paradox itself before anyone names it. Roughly what I have in mind: the room acts as some kind of review board — a grant panel, a certification body, a hiring committee — that must reach a collective yes/no verdict, where the verdict is understood to follow from a small set of conjunctive conditions; each member receives private dossier material so their judgments about the individual conditions are heterogeneous; and the group votes twice, once on each condition separately and once on the verdict itself. If it works, the two aggregation methods return different answers, and the group discovers it has, as a collective, contradicted itself while every member voted sincerely and consistently.

Concretely, I'd like:

1. The scenario, rules, and materials — including how to construct the dossiers so the split emerges from members' actual judgments rather than from rigging. This crowd will smell a setup, and the entire point is that their own sincere votes generate the contradiction. Tell me how to engineer the evidence packets so the outcome is robust rather than dependent on a knife-edge vote split.
2. A timeline for the 75 minutes.
3. A second phase, if one fits: the board, having discovered the two procedures disagree, must itself decide which aggregation rule to adopt going forward — and I'd like that choice to surface the genuine normative stakes, i.e., the premise route is transparent and reason-responsive while the conclusion route yields a stable, actionable collective verdict.
4. A debrief mapping what happened onto the formal structure: premise-based versus conclusion-based aggregation, how the group's collective judgment set ends up inconsistent though each individual's was consistent, and where the jury-theorem intuition they walked in with actually survives this.
5. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — members communicating before voting, someone voting strategically to force consistency, the splits simply failing to materialize — and how to handle each.
6. An honest note on which feature of the actual philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce — for instance, that real aggregation problems involve logically interconnected propositions rather than a clean conjunction, or that the dilemma bites hardest for groups that must act as continuing agents answerable for past commitments — so I know what I have to supply in discussion myself.

One constraint: no lecture up front. The room should hit the wall of the contradiction before hearing the terms "discursive dilemma" or "doctrinal paradox."
