I run the fellowship program at a small foundation. Each cycle we read about 300 applications, and reviewers keep getting swayed by costly displays: applicants who quit their jobs to prepare, who spent years in the field unpaid, who turned down lucrative offers to stay in this work. Half my committee treats these as strong evidence of commitment and likely future performance; the other half calls it rewarding waste and selecting for privilege. I want a decision procedure my reviewers can apply in about twenty minutes per file, grounded in the logic of costly signaling and the handicap principle, that says when a costly display is genuinely informative about what we care about (the applicant will do good work and stick with it) and when it is not. It should specify: the questions to ask in order about the signal's cost structure (who can afford it, what it costs the honest type versus the faking type, whether it is observable and irreversible), what would have to be true for the signal to be informative, and the tests for the known failure modes -- signals that are cheap for the wealthy but expensive for others, signals that predict willingness to sacrifice rather than competence, and signals applicants produce only because we reward them. Then apply the procedure to three cases: (1) an applicant who did two unpaid years of fieldwork, (2) an applicant who declined a high-paying industry job, and (3) an applicant who published extensively while holding a full-time job. At least one case should come out in a way a commitment-valuing reviewer will dislike. Please mark clearly which parts of the procedure rest on settled theory and which depend on empirical claims about human signaling that remain contested, and don't present the latter as established. Close with a table summarizing the three worked cases with columns: Case, Key question that decides it, Verdict (informative / not informative / insufficient information), and Main uncertainty.
