I retired two years ago after four decades in experimental particle physics, and I have spent the time since reading philosophy of science. Next month I have been invited back to my old department to give the colloquium, and I intend to defend a thesis I know will not be popular there. My claim is not the generic one that philosophy is useful to science. It is narrower, and I hope more defensible: that philosophy functions for the sciences as a kind of seed bank, preserving questions, distinctions, and research programs during the long periods when active science has no use for them, so that they remain available when a scientific crisis makes them suddenly relevant again. My exhibits are the familiar ones, though the framing may not be: the atomist tradition carried for centuries outside anything testable before physics finally caught up with it; Bohm's pilot-wave work growing out of what looked to most of his contemporaries like purely interpretive worrying; Bell's theorem emerging from questions the pragmatic generation regarded as settled or meaningless; and Einstein's own testimony about what he owed to his reading of Hume and Mach.

The audience will be mostly my former colleagues: hard-headed experimentalists and a handful of theorists, the sort who read Weinberg's Dreams of a Final Theory and found the chapter arguing against philosophy bracing, who cite with approval the announcement by Hawking and Mlodinow at the start of The Grand Design that philosophy has failed to keep pace with modern physics, and who repeat the remark attributed to Feynman comparing the philosophy of science, from the working scientist's point of view, to ornithology from the birds'. There will also be two or three philosophers of physics in the room, which means I cannot get away with caricature in either direction.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased in the vocabulary these physicists would actually use -- talk of testability, of what gets funded, of where the theorems are -- and ordered by how much damage each would do to the seed-bank thesis specifically, rather than to the generic claim that philosophy is useful. For each objection, tell me what I should concede at once, what I should hold, and a reply I can deliver in two minutes. Identify the weakest point in my own position, the question I am hoping nobody asks, and where each of my replies is soft. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
