hey, i teach an upper-level undergrad seminar on collective action and i'm writing this semester's final exam. i want a question on the tragedy of the commons that students can't answer by regurgitating the textbook, so i came up with a scenario and need you to turn it into a proper exam question, model answer, and marking scheme.

the scenario: a community-run open-source software project where the shared resource behaves weirdly — it dies from neglect if too few people contribute (bugs don't get patched, the project stagnates, users drift away), but it ALSO collapses if too many people pile on (review bottlenecks, a flood of low-quality pull requests the maintainers have to clean up after, maintainer burnout). so the "tragedy" can come from either direction and the healthy zone is somewhere in the middle. assume contributions are observable, anyone can fork the project at any time, and maintainers can reject contributions but reviewing them costs real time either way.

what i want from you:

1. the exam question itself, phrased the way you'd actually print it on the paper. students get 75 minutes for it. they should be told they can use formal tools where helpful (game forms, a sketch of the payoff structure as a function of number of contributors, etc.) but a full mathematical model isn't required.
2. the question has to push past the standard material — reciting hardin's parable or listing ostrom's design principles should get them almost nowhere. the crux i want tested is whether they realize the classic fixes (exclusion, privatization, centralizing authority in a single owner) were all designed for the overuse margin, and that applying them here can actively worsen the underuse margin. so the real question is which margin a given intervention pushes on, and what empirical fact about the project you'd need to know before prescribing anything.
3. a model answer, roughly what a strong student could produce in the 75 minutes.
4. a marking scheme out of 30 marks. for each component, name the specific move that earns the marks, plus a note on the identifiable mistake that should cost them — e.g., modeling the whole thing as a straight prisoner's dilemma when the contribution side is really an assurance problem, or assuming forkability is a free safety valve without asking what happens to the user base and contributor pool when the community splits.

one more thing: sanity-check my scenario first. if the non-monotonic thing needs extra assumptions to actually work (say, congestion costs only binding above some threshold of simultaneous contributions, or maintainers being non-excludable from review duties), then bake those assumptions explicitly into the question instead of leaving it vague — i'd rather the question be precise than clever.
