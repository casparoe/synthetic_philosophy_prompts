So last night my friend and I got into a genuinely hours-long argument about Dennett's "Where Am I?" -- the brain-in-a-vat story from Brainstorms (1978), where Dennett's brain is removed and kept in a vat in Houston, radio-linked to his body, and he goes off to retrieve the warhead. My friend says the whole story has an obvious moral: Dennett-the-person is wherever the brain is, because that's where the thinking physically happens, full stop, and Dennett's confusion in the story is just him being squeamish. I said that can't be right, because the story's entire drama comes from the fact that Dennett *can't* feel himself into the vat no matter how hard he tries, and that this isn't squeamishness, it's a datum. The passage we kept circling was this one, from just after he flips the output transmitter switch off and on:

"Being a philosopher of firm physicalist conviction, I believed unswervingly that the tokening of my thoughts was occurring somewhere in my brain: yet, when I thought 'Here I am,' where the thought occurred to me was here, outside the vat, where I, Dennett, was standing staring at my brain."

My friend reads this as Dennett confessing an illusion he should've been able to shake. I read it as showing that "where the thought happens" and "where the thinker is" come apart, and that any theory which just identifies the two is done for.

Here's what I actually want, since the argument went in circles because we never agreed on what would even settle it: write the desiderata. If someone proposed a theory of personal location -- of where a person is, when their parts get spread across space like in this story -- what would it have to get right to count as adequate? Give me the requirements list, with the case or consideration behind each one. I want things like: which of Dennett's own episodes it has to handle (the failed attempt to think himself into the vat, the switch flipping, the later body-swap stuff with Yorick and Hubert if you know it), which ordinary cases it can't mangle (is it a problem for a theory if it says I'm wrong when I say "I'm in the kitchen"? when a remote drone pilot says "I was over the target"?), which distinctions it has to keep separate (where my thoughts are tokened vs. where my body is vs. where my point of view is vs. where *I* am -- or should some of those get collapsed, and if so which), and whether "preserve what normal people say" is a real constraint or just conservativism. Flag any desiderata you suspect can't all be satisfied at once -- I have a hunch my friend's criterion is cheap precisely because it abandons some of the others, and I'd like to know if the cheapness is avoidable or if every theory pays somewhere. Tell me which single requirement excludes the most live candidates. Then, briefly, score the obvious contenders against the list: brain-location (my friend), body-location, point-of-view location, and the "there's no fact of the matter, the question dissolves" option Dennett himself flirts with. A table is fine for that part. Oh and please answer in American English.
