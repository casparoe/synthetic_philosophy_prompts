I am a retired physicist who has spent the past year or so reading about strategy, rational choice, and their philosophical underpinnings, and I keep running into a pattern of reasoning that I cannot find a precise name for, though it feels like the sort of thing that must have been analysed thoroughly somewhere. The pattern is this: an agent deliberately arranges matters so that some option is removed from their own future choice set -- or so that exercising restraint later would be ruinous for them -- and they do this precisely in order to influence what another party does now. I have noticed it in several guises. In the game where two drivers speed toward each other, the driver who visibly disables or discards their own steering mechanism somehow gains the advantage, which strikes me as perverse. A negotiator who publicly and credibly stakes their entire reputation on refusing to budge seems to do better than one who retains flexibility. And at the level of states, there is the idea of a retaliatory system engineered to respond automatically, beyond the possibility of a last-minute change of heart -- a well-known Cold War film dramatised the point that such a device accomplishes nothing if the adversary does not know it exists, which I take to be a genuine insight and not merely a joke.

What puzzles me is the logic underneath. The arrangement only does its work if, at the decisive moment, the agent truly cannot or will not back down; but if that moment ever arrives, what then happens is precisely the thing nobody wanted -- the collision, the retaliation, the mutual ruin. So the whole scheme seems to rest on a kind of guaranteed future unreasonableness: it is apparently rational to install the device and irrational ever to let it operate, and the adversary's belief that it will operate is exactly what ensures it never has to. I am aware that the word "commitment" floats around this territory, but I am after the narrower configuration: commitment achieved specifically by impairing one's own future agency, one that functions only if the adversary can verify it in advance, and whose payoff depends on its never being carried out. Does the literature have an established name for this exact configuration, and does it treat the apparent paradox -- wise to build, mad to use -- as a genuine philosophical problem, a dissolved confusion, or merely an accepted feature of strategic life? I would also like to know how it is distinguished from neighbouring things: from ordinary promises and contracts, which are enforced by third parties rather than by self-imposed inability; from bluffing, where nothing is actually disabled; and from simply being the sort of person with a reputation for stubbornness, where no device is involved at all.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- whether there is an established term and what it is, or that there is none -- and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. If the configuration as I have described it fits no established concept exactly, I would rather you say so and propose a name with a definition and some boundary cases than stretch an existing label to cover it; I am less interested in the word than in getting the carving right.
