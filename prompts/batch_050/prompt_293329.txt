Working on a take-home for an epistemology seminar and I need to know if my position on peer disagreement is actually coherent before I commit to it. Here's roughly what I believe:

(1) In ordinary cases where I find out an epistemic peer disagrees with me, I should split the difference -- move my credence halfway toward theirs. Equal weight, no thumb on the scale for me.

(2) But when a peer of mine defends steadfastness -- the view that you can rationally hold your ground -- I don't split the difference about *that*. I stay conciliationist. I think the self-defeat worry (that conciliationism tells you to abandon conciliationism when peers reject it) can be answered by letting the view exempt first-order beliefs about disagreement itself.

(3) I figure out who counts as my peer using their track record and general competence, not by looking at whether they agree with me on the disputed issue. Discounting someone's peerhood because of the very disagreement at hand seems obviously circular to me.

(4) Higher-order evidence is just evidence, full stop. Learning that a smart, well-informed person believes p is evidence for p the same way muddy footprints are evidence someone walked through. There's no special sui generis "bracket your reasoning" mechanism; you just conditionalize on the total evidence.

(5) If a peer looks at a simple sum I've done and tells me I've added it wrong, and I re-check it twice and it still comes out the same, I barely budge. I might double-check a third time but I'm not going to 50/50 on arithmetic.
