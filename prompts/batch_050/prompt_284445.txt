So I work in behavior change research — habit formation, self-monitoring, implementation intentions, streak apps, adherence curves, the whole quantified-self world. Recently I reread the section of Franklin's Autobiography where he takes on what he calls the project of arriving at moral perfection, and I couldnt stop reading it as an n-of-1 intervention: thirteen target virtues each with a one-line operational definition, a little book with a grid (virtues down the side, days across the top), evening self-examination with a black mark per fault, strict attention to one virtue per week while the others just get logged, a full course taking thirteen weeks so you can run four courses a year, and later the marks moved to ivory pages so they could be wiped off and reused. Then the project fizzles: Order defeats him, the courses get spaced out to once a year, then once every several years, and he ends with the anecdote about the man who wants his new ax ground bright all over, gets tired of turning the smith's wheel, and decides he likes a speckled ax best. Plus the famous wrinkle that he added Humility last, at a friend's suggestion, and admits that even if he had fully conquered his pride he would probly have been proud of his humility.

Here's what I want. Restate Franklin's project in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can: the tracking instrument and its reliability, the rotation as a blocked design, the operational definitions as target behaviors, the Order failure as a compliance problem, the speckled ax as settling at a local optimum, the wipeable ivory pages as, what, data hygiene? Then give me the residue report — the parts that dont survive translation. My suspicions, which you should push back on where wrong: (1) his targets are dispositions of character, not behaviors, and my vocabulary basically only has behaviors plus adherence measures; (2) the speckled ax passage reads like him claiming the attempt was worth it despite failing its endpoint, which is not a move an intervention study gets to make; (3) the humility thing looks like a target that is structurally hostile to self-monitoring — succeeding at it seems to undermine the success — and I can't even state that cleanly in intervention terms.

For each piece of residue, tell me whether it shows my framework cant express something real, or whether it shows Franklin's project was confused in ways behavior science would have caught upfront (no pre-registered success criterion, no operationalization of character, a target that's reflexively self-defeating). And then the meta-question: was Franklin running a behavior-change program that failed, or was he doing something else entirely that only looks like one from where I sit — and does answering that require stepping outside my vocabulary?
