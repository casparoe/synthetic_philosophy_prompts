I'm presenting a chapter of my dissertation next month to the interdisciplinary theory workshop at my university, and I want to walk in ready for the question period. The chapter argues that the standard reading of Hannah Arendt's "banality of evil" -- as a thesis about bureaucratic thoughtlessness, the unthinking functionary who merely processed paperwork -- misreads her, and that the concept only makes sense when Eichmann in Jerusalem is read together with her later account of thinking as the internal two-in-one dialogue with oneself. My claim is twofold: first, that banality of evil is fundamentally about the failure of this self-interrogating activity, which is why Eichmann's cliches and inability to speak in anything but stock phrases matter to her more than his job title; and second, that the much-cited historical critiques of her Eichmann portrait -- the evidence from the Argentine Sassen interviews and subsequent archival work that Eichmann was a convinced, ideologically motivated antisemite rather than a dutiful clerk -- damage her judgment of the man but leave the concept itself intact, since she always said she was describing a phenomenon, not acquitting a person. I know the severability move is the most contestable part of the chapter.

The audience is unusually well-constructed to attack me from three directions. Two members are Arendt specialists working on The Life of the Mind, who will know the secondary literature on the thinking/willing split and will press me on whether I'm smuggling a moral reading into texts where Arendt insisted she was being descriptive, and on whether the two-in-one dialogue was even worked out when she wrote the Eichmann book. One is a Holocaust historian who treats the Sassen material as decisive and regards any defense of Arendt on Eichmann as a disciplinary embarrassment -- she will not be satisfied with "the concept survives even if the portrait fails." And one is a moral psychologist who thinks the whole debate is badly framed, because decades of work on obedience, conformity, and motivated reasoning shows that ordinary compliance doesn't require either ideological fanaticism or thoughtlessness, and that Arendt's category dissolves under scrutiny from either direction.

Prepare me. Give me the objections in roughly the order I'll receive them, phrased the way each of these people would actually phrase them in a seminar, with a note on how damaging each one is to my two claims separately. For each objection, tell me what I should concede openly and early, what I should hold, and a reply I can deliver in under two minutes. I especially want the question I'm hoping nobody asks -- I suspect it concerns whether my severability move turns the banality thesis into something unfalsifiable, a concept that survives any historical evidence whatsoever -- and an honest account of the weakest point in my own position, the place where I should be ready to give ground gracefully rather than defend to the last.
