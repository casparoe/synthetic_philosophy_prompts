I'm drafting a short paper for a graduate seminar in philosophy of language, and before I commit to it I want an audit of my own position rather than another summary of the literature. Please take on the role of a philosopher of logic and language who knows Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" and the surrounding debates intimately — the Grice and Strawson reply, Carnap's response, the disputes over deviant logics and meaning change — and who is willing to tell me bluntly where my position breaks down. Think through the commitments carefully and step by step before reaching a verdict.

Here is roughly where I've landed. (1) Quine is basically right: there is no principled line between the analytic and the synthetic, because every candidate criterion either presupposes the notion it was meant to explicate or leans on an unclarified notion of meaning. (2) All the same, stipulation is special: when I coin a term by explicit definition, the defining sentence is true by convention, and this conventional status does not evaporate merely because the word later gets absorbed into the language and its origin is forgotten. (3) I accept full confirmational holism: no sentence whatsoever is immune to revision in the face of recalcitrant experience. (4) At the same time, I hold that someone who rejects classical logic — who denies, say, excluded middle — is not genuinely disagreeing with me but merely changing the subject; their connectives mean something different, so there is no real revision of logic, only a new language. (5) Mathematics is continuous with empirical science and owes its confirmation entirely to its place in the overall web of belief, and I take this to follow directly from (3).

Do these five commitments hang together as a package? Please proceed as follows: identify each genuine tension, order the tensions by severity, and for each one state exactly which commitment has to go and what the cheapest repair would cost me dialectically. Distinguish real problems from merely apparent ones — if a pair of commitments looks tense but dissolves on inspection, say so and show the dissolution rather than flagging everything. Where one of my commitments can only be saved by distinguishing two senses of a term I've been using sloppily, perform that surgery explicitly. Note also, where relevant, whether Quine himself could consistently have held my package, since I suspect I'm combining things he kept apart — but don't let the answer collapse into an exposition of his view; the object of the exercise is my five theses, not his.
