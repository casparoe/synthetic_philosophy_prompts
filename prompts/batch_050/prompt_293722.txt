Exam question:

Suppose a public-health agency can choose one of two policies for a population that will otherwise exist under either policy.

Policy A reduces total suffering among that population by 10,000 units. It also causes one additional person to exist. That person will have a mostly very happy life, but will experience 200 units of serious and unavoidable suffering.

Policy B reduces total suffering among the original population by 9,500 units and causes no additional people to exist.

Ignore costs, rights violations, side effects, and uncertainty. How would classical negative utilitarianism, suffering prioritarianism, and a person-affecting anti-harm view compare A and B? Does the happiness of the extra person matter? Does A’s lower total suffering settle the issue? Can the extra person’s suffering count against A even if their life is worth living? Finally, do any of these views imply painless universal extinction if that option were available?

Student’s answer:

Classical negative utilitarianism assigns no intrinsic value to happiness and says we should minimize suffering. Since A leaves the world with 300 fewer units of suffering overall than B, A is required. The extra person’s happiness does not count, except perhaps indirectly if it prevents someone else’s suffering. Their 200 units of suffering do count, but they have already been included in the total.

Suffering prioritarianism is basically the same view, except that suffering matters more when it belongs to someone who is worse off. So it might choose B if the single additional sufferer is sufficiently badly off, even though B permits 300 more units of suffering overall. Thus A is not automatically required under this view.

A person-affecting anti-harm view cannot regard the extra person as harmed. Before they exist there is nobody who can be harmed, and once they exist their life is worth living. Consequently their suffering provides no person-affecting objection to A, so this view should also choose A because it removes more suffering from people who would exist anyway.

On extinction, all three views imply choosing painless universal extinction whenever it is available, because extinction would eliminate all future suffering. Since happiness has no independent weight on suffering-focused views, no possible future happiness can count against that conclusion. This is the main objection to negative utilitarianism, although supporters may say it is a bullet they should bite.
