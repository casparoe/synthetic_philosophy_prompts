You are a professional philosopher with particular expertise in American pragmatism, twentieth-century philosophy of language, and the secondary literature on Richard Rorty. I would like you to think carefully and, before writing anything final, spend a moment brainstorming the pressure points in Rorty's position where a scientifically minded interviewer could push hardest.

Here is my situation. I am a retired experimental physicist who has, in retirement, developed a serious interest in philosophy. I host an occasional seminar-style interview series at my former university's continuing education program, and my next guest is a professor of philosophy who has recently published a book defending a Rortyan line: that the notion of beliefs or theories "representing" or "corresponding to" mind-independent reality should be retired, that vocabularies are tools for coping rather than mirrors of nature, and that there is nothing deep to say about why one vocabulary is better than another beyond its usefulness for some purpose. My guest is a careful scholar who has anticipated the standard objections, so questions of the "but isn't that just relativism?" variety will earn me a well-rehearsed five-minute lecture, not a real answer.

My own intuitions, as someone who spent forty years doing physics, are that the success of theories like quantum electrodynamics demands an explanation in terms of getting something right about the world, and that the convergence of independent measurement methods on the same constants of nature is hard to square with a purely coping-based picture. But I do not want the interview to be me merely voicing those intuitions; I want questions that force my guest to do philosophical work.

Please draft nine to eleven interview questions, sequenced so that each builds on the likely answer to the previous one. The sequence should cover at least the following, though you need not use this order:

1. The status of the claim that talk of representation and correspondence is "unhelpful" -- whether that is itself a theoretical claim about how language relates to the world, and if so, whether the position undermines itself.
2. How the view handles predictive success and the instrumental reliability of mature science, and whether "coping" is doing any explanatory work there or merely renaming the phenomenon.
3. The distinction between justification and truth, and whether Rorty can consistently say both that truth is not a goal of inquiry and that inquiry has goals at all.
4. What the position implies about genuine convergence -- in physics, say, where theories developed for entirely different purposes turn out to constrain each other -- versus mere social agreement within a community of inquirers.
5. Whether abandoning the notion of representing reality leaves us any principled way to criticize vocabularies from within, or only to redescribe them.

For each question, please include (a) the reply you expect a well-prepared Rortyan to give, and (b) a follow-up question keyed to that reply that pushes one level deeper. Where a follow-up trades on a distinction from the philosophy of language or the realism debate, please name the distinction so that I can look it up beforehand. Conclude the sequence with one question that asks my guest what, if anything, could count as evidence that the antirepresentationalist stance is the wrong way to think about science. Aim for a tone that is rigorous but cordial -- I want my guest to feel genuinely pressed, not ambushed.
