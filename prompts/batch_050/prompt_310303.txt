I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy, and a chapter of my dissertation concerns the standing to blame: the idea that an instance of blame can be objectionable not because its content is false, but because of who is doing the blaming. After a recent seminar meeting I realized that my own case-intuitions are considerably less unified than I had assumed, so I would like to work backward from cases to a principle rather than the other way around. Here are my verdicts, stated as honestly as I can manage. First: a colleague of mine who has been unfaithful to his spouse for years, and whose infidelity is an open secret, recently delivered a stern public condemnation of another colleague for essentially the same conduct; the condemnation struck me as objectionable, even though everything he said was true. Second: my uncle, a heavy smoker for two decades who quit after a health scare and has not touched a cigarette since, now berates his brother harshly for smoking; this strikes me as entirely unobjectionable, perhaps even especially fitting, despite the obvious structural similarity to the first case. Third: a neighbor of ours, an excellent parent by all accounts, repeatedly criticizes how we raise our children, in matters that do not affect her at all; her observations are often accurate, yet her blame feels out of place to me in a way that identical criticism from our pediatrician does not. Fourth: two friends of mine jointly planned a reckless prank that went wrong, and afterward one of them has taken to blaming the other, bitterly and repeatedly, for the specific mistake that caused the harm; this too feels objectionable, though the blamed friend did in fact make the mistake. Fifth: a politician who voted for a disastrous military intervention, publicly recanted, and described her earlier vote as the worst mistake of her career, now sharply condemns colleagues who still defend the war; I find her blame unproblematic, though I notice that some of my peers do not.

The case I keep turning over without a settled verdict is this: a parent who was a cruel bully in secondary school, who is now genuinely ashamed of that period and has made some amends, discovers that his teenage son has been bullying a classmate. I believe the father should intervene and blame his son, and yet I feel a residual discomfort that resembles what I feel in the first case, which makes me suspect my intuitions are doing something I cannot yet articulate. What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of precisely stated candidate principles, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible. Please indicate which clause of the principle each case motivates; pay particular attention to what distinguishes the first case from the second and fifth, since all involve blamers who share or shared the fault they condemn, and to what the third case implies, since the neighbor seems to have clean hands and yet still strikes me as out of bounds. Then tell me what the principle says about the bullying father, and about two cases I have not considered: the reformed smoker who, unbeknownst to everyone, still occasionally smokes in secret, and a morally impeccable stranger with no connection to the wrongdoing who nonetheless condemns it. Finally, be honest about the cost: if the most elegant available principle forces me to abandon one of my stated verdicts, tell me which one it would be and whether you think the principle or the verdict should give way.
