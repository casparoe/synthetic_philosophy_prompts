**Why I stopped trusting my gut estimates and started using the pessimist's rule**

Every year or two, my team has to make a call like this: do we migrate to the new database that promises 3x throughput, or stay on the boring old one? Do we build on the startup's API or the established vendor's? Do we roll out the rewrite or keep patching the legacy service?

Here's the thing nobody says out loud: we have no idea what the probabilities are. When the PM asks "what's the chance the new vendor goes under in two years," any number anyone gives is theater. Not a rough estimate -- theater. We have maybe two or three comparable past experiences, no real data, and the people giving numbers are mostly anchoring on how the last incident felt.

So I've started pushing a different rule for these decisions. Instead of asking "which option has the best expected outcome," ask: "for each option, what's the worst realistic outcome? Now pick the option whose worst outcome is least bad." Apparently philosophers call this maximin. I call it the pessimist's rule.

The standard objection is that this makes you paranoid. If you always guard against the worst case, you'd never deploy anything, never switch vendors, never fly on a plane. But I think that objection conflates two different situations. When you have decent probabilities -- say you're deciding whether to add a cache, and you have latency histograms from production -- then sure, do the expected value math. Maximin is for a specific situation: genuine ignorance, plus asymmetric stakes, plus a decision that's hard to reverse. A database migration checks all three boxes. You don't know the failure rates, the downside (data corruption, multi-week outage) massively outweighs the upside (nicer dashboards), and rollback after six months of writes is a fantasy.

There's a second objection I find more interesting: maximin ignores how *good* the good outcomes can be. If staying on legacy means a guaranteed slow decline while migrating means either glory or disaster, the pessimist's rule picks slow decline every time, and over a whole career that's a losing strategy. My answer: that's a feature for infrastructure and a bug for product bets. Infra decisions are about not dying; product decisions are about winning. Different rules for different games.

One last thing. I poked around and found out this argument has a famous pedigree: Rawls apparently argued that people designing a fair society from behind a "veil of ignorance" would use something like maximin -- protect the worst-off position first, because you might end up in it. And this economist Harsanyi apparently fired back that rational people would just average over all possible positions, which gives you utilitarianism instead. I won't pretend I've read either of them, but it made me wonder: if the maximin vs. averaging debate is unsettled even for *society*, where the stakes are highest, maybe the honest move in engineering is to say out loud which rule we're using for each decision instead of hiding behind fake probabilities.
