Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend about the statue and the clay. I held that when a sculptor shapes a lump into a statue, nothing new comes into existence -- there is one object, variously describable. She held that the statue and the lump are two distinct objects sharing their matter, since the lump survives squashing and the statue does not. Neither of us budged, so I have concluded the debate is best advanced by ridicule.

Please act as a satirist with formal training in metaphysics. Before writing, think carefully about which features of this debate are genuinely comic because they are real: the ready proliferation of coincident objects, the quiet assumption that "statue" carves reality better than "lump" or any gerrymandered sortal, and the serene confidence with which each side claims common sense for itself. Let those features generate the humor rather than inventing exaggerations.

Then write the piece: an internal claims-adjustment memorandum from a fine-art insurance company whose policies have legally committed the firm to one side of the debate (pick one, but commit). A museum's bronze has been dropped down a stairwell and flattened, and the memo must settle, for payout purposes, precisely what was destroyed and how many objects the company owes for. Deadpan corporate register throughout; around 500 words. The satire must do its work through the casework -- the adjuster citing the object's persistence conditions as evidence, the policy clause defining which sortal terms appear in the schedule, the appendix pricing the bronze separately from the artwork -- so that a metaphysician recognizes the arguments from differing modal and temporal properties, the grounding problem, and the arbitrariness worry without your ever naming them. No winking at the reader, and no footnote explaining the joke.
