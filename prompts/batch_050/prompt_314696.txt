I edit the ideas desk at a science-and-culture magazine, and I'm putting together a short internal assessment for writers who pitch us pieces on the perennial "is taxation theft?" argument -- I want to be sure they can do more than recycle libertarian slogans or liberal dismissals of them. Please write one essay exam question suitable for a 60-minute written answer, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves an excellent answer must make and the specific errors that should cost marks. The question must turn on the following passage from John Locke's Second Treatise of Government, Chapter XI, §135, which writers on both sides invoke but rarely parse:

"for no body can transfer to another more power than he has in himself; and no body has an absolute arbitrary power over himself, or over any other, to destroy his own life, or take away the life or property of another."

A good question should force the writer to reconstruct how this transfer principle, combined with a prepolitical right to property, generates the strongest form of the charge that taxation is theft; to explain how Locke himself escapes that conclusion -- consent channeled through a legislative whose power "is limited to the public good of the society" -- and to test whether that escape is stable once property is held to exist before government does; and to connect the passage to the modern debate without anachronism, saying where contemporary entitlement theorists borrow Locke's principle and where they reject his bridge. The model answer should take and defend a position on whether the passage, on its own terms, supports or undermines the theft charge. The marking scheme should distinguish an A answer from a B answer by name: for instance, separating consented from arbitrary takings rather than treating the principle as barring all redistribution; noticing that Lockean "property" covers more than estate; and flagging the slide from majority consent to unanimity as an error worth marks.
