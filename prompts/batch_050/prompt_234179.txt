I'm working through a popular intro to aesthetics and its capsule summary of Tolstoy goes like this: "Tolstoy held that art is simply the transmission of emotion, and that art is good in proportion as the emotions transmitted are morally good ones -- above all, feelings of universal brotherhood. Beauty drops out of the picture entirely: a work's worth is measured by whether it makes its audience morally better, which is why he ranked a peasant song above Beethoven and disowned his own novels. In short, his test for art is moral uplift."

Then I actually opened What Is Art? (the Maude translation) and right in his introduction he's saying things like:

"A false and antiquated view of life is supported by means of art, and is inseparably linked to some manifestations of art which we enjoy and prize. If the false view of life be destroyed this art will cease to appear valuable. Is it best to screen the error for the sake of preserving the art? Or should the art be sacrificed for the sake of truthfulness?"

That reads less like "art's job is to improve people" and more like he's worried art actively props up bad ideologies. And a page later he complains that artists "produce their spiritual food for the cultured only," which sounds like a class complaint, not a point about moral content. So how fair is the capsule? Please seperate what's accurate, what's a common simplification, and what Tolstoy would flatly object to. I'm especially unsure about "his test for art is moral uplift" -- I vaguely recall his famous definition turning on whether a work transmits feeling at all, with the moral worth of the feeling being a further question, but I could easily be misremembering.
