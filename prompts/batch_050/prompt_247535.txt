I am a PhD student in jurisprudence helping to run a graduate seminar on the rule of law (roughly 14 students, one 80-minute session), and I want an activity that makes Fuller's eight desiderata of legality felt rather than merely listed. My idea, which you should refine or replace if you have a better one: students in small groups govern a simple simulated village by issuing written rules that the facilitator's agent characters must follow, and across rounds the facilitator forces specific defects into their legal output -- retroactive commands, contradictory statutes, unpublished rules, rules changed faster than conduct can adjust, impossible demands -- so that compliance breaks down and the group experiences, one failure at a time, what each desideratum was doing. Give me concrete rules, a timeline, what the agents are instructed to do when the law is defective (obey literally, disobey, guess?), and how to sequence the defects. Then a debrief that maps each breakdown onto a Fullerian principle and onto the central dispute: whether these failures are merely bad instrument design (Raz's view, law as a tool that works poorly) or a distinctively moral failure (Fuller's internal morality, Waldron's emphasis on dignity and agency). Take a committed position on whether the activity, as designed, actually supports the stronger Fullerian reading or only the Razian one -- I suspect simulations like this smuggle in the moral conclusion through the debrief rather than earning it in the play -- and say what would have to happen in the room for the stronger reading to be genuinely earned. Finally, note what will predictably go wrong (clever students who treat defective law as a puzzle to route around) and which feature of real legality the simulation cannot reproduce.
