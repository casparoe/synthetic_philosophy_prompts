Please give me an intuitive, gut-level explanation of why causation by omission is such a headache for counterfactual theories of causation. I get the basic setup -- the gardener fails to water the plant, the plant dies, and we say the gardener's failure caused the death. What trips me up is the overgeneration problem: if it's true that the plant would have survived had the gardener watered it, it's equally true that it would have survived had the Queen of England watered it, or had I happened by and watered it. So on the naive counterfactual test, all of our omissions "caused" the death, which seems nuts. Please explain (a) why exactly this problem arises structurally for counterfactual accounts -- what is it about treating absences as causes that makes counterfactual dependence so cheap?; (b) how the standard fix in terms of "normal" or "expected" courses of events is supposed to distinguish the gardener's omission from the Queen's, and what that fix costs -- in particular, does it end up making causation partly a matter of our expectations or conventions, and is that a bug or a feature?; and (c) whether rival theories of causation dodge this or just trade it for a different problem -- e.g., does a process or energy-transfer view have an easier time here, given that an absence doesn't transfer anything to anyone? Keep it in American English please.
