So I write novels for a living, but to keep the lights on I also teach a little upper-level undergrad seminar on philosophy of biology at the college in town. Finals are coming up and I need one essay question on the Gould & Lewontin spandrels critique of adaptationism, plus a model answer and a marking scheme I can grade against.

Here's the wrinkle: since I'm a novelist, I want the question built around a made-up creature. Use this one: the "lantern moth" of a fictional Pacific island, whose wings give off a faint blue glow on warm nights. In the scenario, three biologists disagree about the glow. One says it's an adaptation for attracting mates. One says its a spandrel — the wing scales were selected for heat regulation, they happen to fluoresce, and the glow just rides along for free. The third says it started as a by-product but has since been co-opted for mate signaling.

The question itself (students get 45 minutes, it's worth 30 marks, expecting something like 600–800 words) should ask them to: (1) state precisely what each of the three claims says about the evolutionary history of the glow, and in particular how the co-option claim differs from the straight adaptation claim given that both agree the glow is useful now; (2) for each claim, say what evidence would actually discriminate it from the other two — comparative/phylogenetic data, heritability and selection measurements, developmental details about the scales, the metabolic cost of glowing, whatever they can come up with; (3) connect it back to Gould & Lewontin: what would they say is wrong with how the first biologist is working, and does the spandrel claim need positive evidence of its own, or is it just what's left when the adaptation story falls apart?

The model answer should genuinely take positions on the hard bits, not just wave at them. The marking scheme should name the specific moves a top answer has to make and the specific confusions that cost marks — e.g. treating "spandrel" as meaning "functionless, end of story," conflating "currently useful" with "evolved because useful," or thinking Gould & Lewontin were claiming that selection doesn't matter much or that most traits aren't adaptations. Also include something in the scheme about the move where the adaptationist just swaps in a fresh adaptive story whenever the last one fails, since that's a big part of what the critique is aimed at.
