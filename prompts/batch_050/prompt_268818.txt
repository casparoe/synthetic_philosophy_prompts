I've got a slot in our department's philosophy of mind reading group next month (everyone reads the paper in advance, then I get about ten minutes of talk and a long Q&A, which is the bit I'm dreading). The paper argues a middle position on dreams that I'm worried will get squeezed from both sides. Against Malcolm, I want to say dreams really are experiences had while asleep, not just dispositions on waking to tell a story. But against the naive realist picture I want to concede something to Dennett: the content of the dream is often indeterminate at the time, and the waking report fills in detail that was never there. My illustrative case: someone dreams of a tiger chasing them through a station, and on waking reports it vividly -- but there's no fact of the matter about whether the dreamt tiger had stripes, or whether the station had a clock, and the report's definiteness on those points is construction, not recollection. So I end up saying: the experience was real and temporally located, but its determinacy is patchy, and the report is good evidence for the coarse content and poor evidence for the fine detail. My claim is that this gives Malcolm the scepticism about reports without the absurdity of denying that anything happened, and gives Dennett the confabulation point without the cassette-theory implication that nothing is experienced until recall.

The audience is the problem. There's a committed Dennettian who will push the heterophenomenology line hard and thinks the "was it actually like something at the time?" question is ill-formed. There's a lecturer with a Wittgensteinian streak who defends Malcolm and will go after the idea that an experience can have indeterminate content at all -- for her, if nothing in the episode settles stripes-or-not, then "the experience" I'm positing is a wheel turning nothing. Two members are empirically minded and will bring up lucid dreaming and the eye-signalling studies, where sleepers report dream content in real time that seems to match later reports, which they think cuts against both Malcolm and Dennett and maybe against me too. And there's a general sceptic about first-person reports who'll ask why my view is any more than Dennett's with extra metaphysical decoration.

What I want: the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come up, phrased the way each of these people would phrase them (not in neutral seminar-speak), each with what I should concede, what I should hold firm on, and a two-or-three sentence reply I can deliver on my feet. Flag where my replies are weakest rather than letting me find out live. And include the objection I'm quietly hoping nobody raises -- which I suspect is the charge that "indeterminate content at the time" collapses into "no determinate experience at the time", i.e. that I've just redescribed Dennett's view while keeping the realist label. Close with a last line of the form "Biggest risk:" followed by one sentence naming the objection most likely to sink the paper and why.
