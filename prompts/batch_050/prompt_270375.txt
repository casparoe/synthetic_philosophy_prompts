A colleague of mine who teaches a survey course on American philosophy recently emailed me the following summary of John Dewey's account of democracy and inquiry, which he says he condensed from an encyclopedia entry:

"Dewey's central claim was that democracy is not fundamentally a form of government but a method of inquiry: the same experimental logic that drives the natural sciences should govern collective life. A policy is best understood as a hypothesis, its enactment as an experiment, and its consequences as evidence that confirms or refutes it; a democratic society must therefore institutionalize experimentation and remain perpetually ready to revise even its settled ends in light of results. Because inquiry of this kind is a technical business requiring trained intelligence, Dewey held that social questions should increasingly be settled by experts, whose findings the public's role is to ratify at the ballot box. And since, on his pragmatism, an idea is true just in case it works, the test of a democratic decision is ultimately whether the majority is satisfied with its outcomes — which is why he treated majority rule as the essence of democracy and minority protections as instrumental and revisable rather than fundamental."

I want to believe this is roughly right, because Dewey really does talk constantly about experiment, hypotheses, and consequences, and the summary sounds like the sort of thing a competent reference work would print. But two things make me uneasy: I seem to recall that Dewey was sharply critical of the idea of rule by experts, and I also thought he defined democracy as something broader than either government or voting. I don't know his work well enough to say whether these memories are accurate or whether I'm confusing him with his critics.

Please give me an accuracy audit of the summary: separate what Dewey actually held from what is a fair simplification and what is a genuine distortion, with attention to (a) the proposed role of experts relative to the public, (b) the gloss on pragmatist truth as whatever satisfies the majority, and (c) the identification of majority rule as the essence of democracy. Where the summary goes wrong, explain what the correct view is and why the error is a natural one to make. Finally, my colleague concludes from the summary that Dewey was "basically an early technocrat" — tell me whether that conclusion follows from the accurate parts of the summary or only from its errors. Please write your answer in American English.
