I am a novelist planning a sequence of biographical fictions, each meant to leave the reader not merely entertained but ethically changed. I keep returning to this claim from Plutarch’s *Life of Pericles*:

“Such objects we find in the acts of virtue, which also produce in the minds of mere readers about them an emulation and eagerness that may lead them on to imitation. In other things there does not immediately follow upon the admiration and liking of the thing done any strong desire of doing the like.”

I am trying to determine what that claim permits and forbids in the construction of a life. My tentative principle is this: a biography may be shaped for moral instruction when every factual assertion is supported, inventions merely dramatize already established traits, and omissions do not distort the answer to the question the work has explicitly undertaken to answer. Please test that principle against my intuitive verdicts:

1. In a short profile explicitly presented as a study of an explorer’s navigational judgment, I omit a well-attested episode in which he defrauded a business partner. This seems permissible because the piece does not purport to assess his whole character.

2. In a full biographical novel about the same explorer, I feel obliged to include the fraud, even though doing so complicates the example of perseverance I want readers to admire. Omitting it there seems indefensible.

3. I invent a quiet scene in which the explorer spends an evening repairing a sledge strap, although no such scene is recorded, because his letters repeatedly show just that sort of patient attention to equipment. This seems permissible.

4. By contrast, I would not construct a chapter entirely out of genuinely documented acts of generosity while suppressing the equally well-documented fraud. That seems worse than the invented sledge scene, even though every incident in the chapter is true.

5. In writing about a locally venerated saint, I may recount a miracle as something the villagers still tell one another, without asserting that it occurred. Preserving the legend in that transparent form seems compatible with historical responsibility.

6. I would not omit a grandfather’s serious domestic abuse from a book intended to give his grandchildren a morally usable picture of him. The wish to provide them with an encouraging ancestor does not seem to justify the omission.

7. In portraying a charismatic demagogue, I think it is permissible to make his physical courage genuinely attractive, provided the novel also shows what that courage serves. Readers ought to understand why people followed him, and an artificially repellent villain would teach them less.

8. I would not invent petty cowardice for the demagogue merely to prevent readers from admiring him. Guarding readers against the wrong imitation by falsifying his character seems manipulative.

Cases 3 and 4 are the pair I find hardest to reconcile: why should an invented scene that faithfully represents an established trait be acceptable, while a selection composed wholly of true events but producing a misleading moral portrait is not? Cases 1 and 6 also trouble me, since both involve omissions justified by the intended use of the life.

Please interpret the Plutarch passage carefully—especially the distinction between admiring a work and wishing to imitate its doer—and either repair my tentative principle or replace it with a better one. State the resulting principle precisely, with no more than four clauses, identifying which cases motivate each clause. Apply it to an ending I have not yet settled: the explorer is documented as having rescued an injured companion, but I have invented an earlier scene in which reading another traveler’s memoir gives him the moral resolution to do so. The invention makes the rescue imitable rather than merely admirable, but perhaps it also turns contingent conduct into an artificially tidy lesson. Finally, say what your principle would imply about two cases I have not considered: a hostile biography whose individually true facts collectively create a false impression, and a wholly invented saint whose legend nevertheless teaches a genuine virtue. If no reasonably simple principle can preserve all my verdicts, identify the verdict that should be abandoned rather than multiplying qualifications indefinitely. Please answer in approximately 1,000–1,200 words.
