I help run a philosophy-of-language reading group that grew out of the rationalist community, and I've noticed that our discussions of fictional names keep degenerating into people talking past each other: one member says obviously Sherlock Holmes doesn't exist, another says obviously there are fictional characters and Holmes is one of them, and a third insists the real question is whether "Holmes is a detective" is true or just true-in-the-fiction, and after an hour nobody has located an actual disagreement. I want an instrument that fixes this.

Please write a decision tree that sorts a person into a position on the semantics of fictional names and truth in fiction. The starting point is the bare datum that competent speakers freely say things like "Sherlock Holmes is a detective," "Sherlock Holmes doesn't exist," and "Sherlock Holmes is more famous than any real detective," and the tree should end at positions such as: the Millian or direct-reference view on which the name has no bearer and the sentences are literally false or gappy; descriptivist treatments; pretense or make-believe accounts of what we're doing when we utter them; realist views on which fictional characters exist as abstract artifacts created by authors; Platonist variants on which they exist independently of the stories; and Meinongian views granting non-existent objects properties. The tree should be built from the logical space first: identify the genuinely independent questions (does the name refer at all; if sentences about Holmes are true, is the truth literal or parasitic on a fiction-operator; do fictional characters belong in the ontology, and if so as what kind of object), work out which combinations of answers those questions permit, then locate the actual positions in that space.

Some specific requirements:

- Each diagnostic question must be a concrete case or claim someone can react to without knowing any of the labels -- for instance, whether a given sentence strikes them as true, false, or neither, rather than "do you accept descriptivism." Aim for six to nine questions.
- Every leaf gets the position's name (or "unnamed" if the combination is unoccupied) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment -- the bullet a holder of that view has to bite.
- For each unoccupied combination, say briefly whether it's empty because it's incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.
- Flag any pairs of positions the tree cannot separate, and say what further question would be needed to split them.
- This is the part I care about most: for each branching point, note whether the question is tracking a substantive disagreement or whether it looks like a merely verbal dispute about words like "exist" or "refer" -- i.e., whether two people who answered it differently could be brought to agree by tabooing the contested term. If a branch dissolves under tabooing, mark it and reroute.

The audience is smart laypeople: patient, comfortable with formalism, allergic to jargon, and prone to declaring every dispute a language game, so the instrument has to earn its distinctions rather than assume them. If you want to apply the tree to a worked example, trace how two hypothetical group members -- one who insists "Holmes doesn't exist, full stop" and one who keeps saying "of course Holmes exists, he's a fictional character" -- get sorted, and whether they end up at different leaves or the same one.
