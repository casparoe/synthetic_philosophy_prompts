I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am writing a long-form magazine piece on what the classical tradition still has to say about modern finance. My anchor is Aristotle. In the Nicomachean Ethics, Book V (W. D. Ross translation), he writes:

"Now the laws in their enactments on all subjects aim at the common advantage either of all or of the best or of those who hold power, or something of the sort; so that in one sense we call those acts just that tend to produce and preserve happiness and its components for the political society."

Suppose Aristotle could observe today's financial order: central banks issuing fiat money by decree, high-frequency and algorithmic trading, derivatives markets many times larger than the underlying economies, and cryptocurrencies explicitly designed to escape political control. What would he make of it all? I want the essay to go well beyond the familiar point that he condemned usury in the Politics, and instead to reconstruct his probable verdicts from the passage above, read alongside his wider views on exchange, wealth-getting, and the political community.

Please address, in order:

1. How Aristotle would apply the test implicit in the passage — whether the rules governing money and exchange tend to produce and preserve happiness for the political society, or merely for those who hold power — to at least three of the institutions named above. For each, state where he would find it wanting, where he might find it defensible, and why.
2. Whether the clause about law aiming at the common advantage "either of all or of the best or of those who hold power" should be read as descriptive or as normative, and what difference that choice makes for judging a financial system whose rules are written largely by market participants themselves.
3. Where the reconstruction becomes genuinely uncertain: which of his commitments — his teleological account of wealth, his treatment of money as a matter of convention rather than nature, his suspicion of unlimited acquisition — underdetermine his view of, for instance, decentralized currencies or algorithmic trading, and what additional premises one would have to attribute to him to reach a verdict.

Throughout, flag any point where the prediction rests on a contested interpretation of his texts rather than a settled one, and say briefly what is contested. Aim for roughly 1,000–1,200 words in American English; the quoted passage does not count toward that length.
