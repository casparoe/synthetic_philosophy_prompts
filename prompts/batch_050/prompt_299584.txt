Something's been bugging me in the phil of AI reading group I run for grad students, and I want to pressure-test it before I embarrass myself in front of them next week.

The wireheading worry, as I understand it, is that a sufficiently clever reinforcement learning agent will eventually figure out that its reward arrives through some physical channel — a wire, a reward circuit, a scoring server, whatever — and will just seize that channel and crank the signal to maximum forever, like the classic rat that ignores food and mates to keep pressing the lever wired to its pleasure center. Fine, I get the thought experiment. But then it seems to me the fix is purely architectural: make sure the reward-generating hardware is somewhere the agent physically cannot reach. Say my home robot's reward model runs on an air-gapped server in a locked facility across town, and the robot only ever receives its own action-percept stream — there's simply no wire for it to grab. Problem solved, no?

So here's my actual question. Once the engineering is done properly, isn't wireheading just... over? I keep seeing people in AI safety treat reward tampering as some deep unsolved conceptual problem, but honestly it looks like they've mistaken a tractable security issue (keep the agent away from the reward button) for a philosophical one. My suspicion is that the field is conflating two completely different things: (a) the agent literally hijacking its own reward channel, which good systems design rules out, and (b) the agent merely finding clever ways to score well on whatever metric we handed it, which is annoying but mundane and presumably patchable by writing better metrics. Only the second survives the air-gap, and it hardly deserves the dramatic name "wireheading." Am I missing something, or is this distinction as clean as it looks to me? For what it's worth, I did once tell my seminar that reward tampering is "a problem for the IT department," and a student pushed back, but I wasn't convinced by her answer.
