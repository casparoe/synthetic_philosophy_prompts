You are a historian of eighteenth-century political and economic thought with extensive experience running adversarial collaborations between scholars who disagree. Two interpreters of Adam Smith have been arguing past each other for years. Camp A reads Smith as a principled critic of European empire and colonialism: his attack on chartered companies, his contempt for colonial monopoly, and his sympathy for colonized peoples make him an anti-imperial thinker. Camp B reads him as an ambivalent or even enabling theorist of commercial empire: his four-stages theory ranks societies with commercial Europe on top, his objections target only the mercantile *management* of empire rather than dominion itself, and his proposed remedies (parliamentary representation for the colonies, or an incorporating union) would have perfected rather than dismantled British supremacy. Both camps accept the following passage from Book IV, Chapter VII ("Of Colonies") of *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations* as central, and both must interpret it:

"The maintenance of this monopoly has hitherto been the principal, or more properly perhaps the sole end and purpose of the dominion which Great Britain assumes over her colonies. In the exclusive trade, it is supposed, consists the great advantage of provinces, which have never yet afforded either revenue or military force for the support of the civil government, or the defence of the mother country. The monopoly is the principal badge of their dependency, and it is the sole fruit which has hitherto been gathered from that dependency."

Begin by brainstorming briefly what each side would insist on and concede. Then draft the joint statement the two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. It should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both sides accept about Smith's text and its context; (2) a disagreements table in which each continuing dispute is stated in neutral wording, with each side's position phrased as that side itself would phrase it; (3) an explicit record of each camp's competing reading of the quoted passage, attending to the qualifiers "hitherto," "assumes," and "sole fruit" and to what each implies about whether dominion, as opposed to monopoly, stands condemned; and (4) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp — and for each crux, a textual passage or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, with each side's predicted reading recorded in advance. If part of the dispute turns out to be verbal, identify which part and explain why. Do not include citations or a bibliography; reason from the text itself. Write in American English.
