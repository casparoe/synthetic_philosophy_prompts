I teach an undergraduate seminar on ethics and political office, and for the final session I pair Machiavelli with Melville's Billy Budd, Sailor. The seminar text is a short passage from chapter 15 of The Prince, in W. K. Marriott's translation:

"Hence it is necessary for a prince wishing to hold his own to know how to do wrong, and to make use of it or not according to necessity.... for if everything is considered carefully, it will be found that something which looks like virtue, if followed, would be his ruin; whilst something else, which looks like vice, yet followed brings him security and prosperity."

I'd like a critical reading of the novella against this passage that I can use to frame the discussion, doing four things.

First, interpret the passage on its own terms, paying attention to what Machiavelli's recommendation conspicuously lacks: wrongdoing appears as a learnable technique governed entirely by necessity and success, with no role for guilt, no conflict of duties, and no cost to the agent. Note too that his closing sentence trades on what looks like vice, which is a claim about reputation rather than about doing wrong. Contrast this with the modern problem of dirty hands -- the problem political theorists discuss under that name following Michael Walzer's influential essay -- which precisely requires that the necessary act remain genuinely wrong and that the agent own the wrong.

Second, apply the passage to Captain Vere clause by clause. In one sense he fits: aboard a warship in 1797, with the recent naval mutinies fresh in memory, he hangs a morally innocent man for the security of the fleet, and the hanging arguably does bring security. Yet he does not treat the deed as technique: he neither conceals it nor disowns it, he seems to hold the sentence simultaneously required and terrible, and his dying murmur of Billy's name registers a wound the Machiavellian frame has no category for. Is Vere a Machiavellian success, a dirty-hands agent in the modern sense, or a case that exposes the limits of both frames?

Third, show where the novella resists the reading: the surgeon's suspicion that Vere is unhinged; the genuine doubt that the necessity was real, given his haste and his refusal to refer the case up the chain of command; the dishonest official newspaper account and the ballad "Billy in the Darbies" as rival public records of the same death.

Finally, say what the fiction contributes to the dirty-hands debate that philosophical argument alone does not -- for instance, the meaning of Billy's forgiveness, and the way institutions launder a necessary wrong into legend.

Please mark clearly what is settled -- the plot, the content of the Machiavelli passage, the standard shape of the dirty-hands problem -- and what is genuinely contested, such as whether Melville endorses or indicts Vere (the long-running acceptance-versus-resistance dispute among critics), Vere's mental state, and the historical accuracy of the naval law portrayed. Do not present any contested reading as the consensus; where you take a stand, flag it as one. Aim for 900-1,200 words; the passage above does not count toward the length. Work from your knowledge of the novella's scenes rather than attempting verbatim quotation of Melville.
