For roughly two decades, fundamental physics has hosted an unusually public dispute over whether string theory and the cosmological multiverse should still count as science. On one side stand physicists such as Peter Woit and Lee Smolin, who since the mid-2000s have argued that the enterprise has failed: after decades of work there is no confirmed prediction; the apparent landscape of possible vacuum solutions, often estimated at 10^500 or more, seems to permit almost any low-energy physics and thus to predict nothing; and multiverse scenarios built on eternal inflation risk explaining any observation whatsoever, which is to say nothing. On the other side stand figures such as Leonard Susskind, who helped introduce the landscape idea, and the philosopher Richard Dawid, who argues that a theory can acquire tentative confirmation even in the absence of new empirical tests. Dawid's proposal has three components: a no-alternatives argument (if a capable research community, despite sustained effort, finds no rival theory that solves the same problems, the existing candidate's probability should rise); a meta-inductive argument (earlier fundamental theories that initially outran the data later found empirical footing, so the pattern itself warrants trust); and an argument from unexpected explanatory connections (a theory not built to explain some phenomenon that nonetheless comes to explain it gains credibility thereby). Critics respond that the no-alternatives argument converts a sociological fact about one community at one time into evidence; that the meta-induction cherry-picks its historical cases; and that loosening testability dissolves the line between physics and metaphysics. The debate crested around 2014–2015 — including a widely discussed comment piece by two prominent cosmologists warning that the scientific method itself was under threat, and a dedicated workshop on whether a theory can be trusted without empirical confirmation — and it has continued since at about the same temperature, with about the same arguments.

My working hypothesis is that the debate is stalled because it is not really about the physics. Both sides largely agree on the evidential situation; what they disagree about is whether confirmation by theoretical virtues is legitimate at all. But I am not confident in this, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict on string theory itself. Is the operative disagreement empirical (say, about whether relevant observations are genuinely out of reach — possible cosmological signatures of other universes, or conjectural constraints on which low-energy theories can arise from quantum gravity)? Is it a normative dispute within confirmation theory about whether arguments like Dawid's are valid? Is it verbal, a fight over who gets the honorific "science"? Is it a sociological dispute about how research resources should be allocated, conducted in epistemological vocabulary? Or are the sides answering different questions — is string theory likely to be true versus is it rational for the field to keep investing in it — under one heading?

Ground your diagnosis in what the two sides actually argue, as described above, and say which kind of disagreement is doing the real work. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a defensible formal treatment of no-alternatives reasoning or a demonstration that it is invalid; a specific class of observation that would move both camps; a change in how the community organizes itself; or an abandonment of the current question in favor of a better-posed one. If you think no resolution should be expected, argue for that and explain what structural feature of the disagreement makes it intractable. Assess my hypothesis along the way, and correct it where the diagnosis requires.
