I got into a long argument with my roommate last week about when it's rational to just go with the crowd, and I realized I don't actually have a view, I just have a pile of gut verdicts. So here they are, and I want to know what principle, if any, is generating them. Case one: I'm in a new city, two restaurants next to each other, one packed, one empty, I pick the packed one. Totally rational, no hesitation. Case two: I'm third in line to speak in a meeting, the first two people endorse the proposal, I had private doubts but nothing concrete, so I endorse it too. Feels rational, maybe slightly sheepish but rational. Case three: same meeting, but this time I've actually read the underlying report and it clearly points the other way -- I still stay quiet and go along. That one feels wrong, like a real failure, not just prudence. Case four: everyone's rushing to withdraw money from a bank that I have no private reason to distrust, and I join the queue. Rational, even if I know the run itself might kill a healthy bank. Case five: a stock keeps climbing, my own analysis says it's overvalued, but I buy in because the market presumably knows something I don't. This one I keep flip-flopping on, but my gut says irrational, or at least more irrational than the restaurant. Case six: I'm the very first person to speak in some deliberation and I have a weak hunch -- I think I should just state my hunch honestly rather than stay neutral, even though I barely trust it.

Here's the tension I can't resolve: structurally, the bank run and the stock purchase look identical to me -- in both I abandon my private information because aggregate behavior implies others know better -- yet I judge them differently. And cases two and three differ only in how good my private evidence is, but I have no idea where the cutoff between them is or what would justify one. So: is there a single principle that delivers all six verdicts, something about when your own signal should outweigh the information contained in others' actions? If yes, state it precisely and tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed, like whether I should trust a product with thousands of glowing reviews when I suspect some are fake. If no principle gets all six, tell me which verdict you think I have to give up -- I suspect it's five -- and what the surviving principle says about where the meeting cases split. Please write your answer as continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold text, like you're writing me a letter.
