I teach game theory to undergrads and do applied micro work, so repeated games, signaling, and mechanism design are basically my native language. A while back I got into an argument with a sociologist friend about this three-way typology she kept using -- honor cultures, dignity cultures, and victimhood cultures -- and I walked away thinking the whole thing was just game theory wearing a hat. She insists it isn't. I want you to adjudicate, sort of.

My rough understanding of the typology: in honor cultures your standing depends on a reputation you have to defend yourself, so insults demand a public response and backing down is costly. In dignity cultures everyone is taken to have inherent worth that doesn't depend on others' opinions, so insults are beneath notice and real disputes get routed to impartial third parties like courts. Victimhood cultures -- the newer and more contested addition -- supposedly combine dignity's reliance on third parties with honor's sensitivity to slights, so publicly appealing to your status as someone who's been wronged becomes a way to recruit powerful protectors to your side.

So here's the job. Restate this typology in my vocabulary: players, strategies, payoffs, repeated interaction, reputation as an asset, costly signaling, first-party vs. third-party enforcement, equilibrium selection, comparative statics. For each culture, tell me what the game actually is -- what sustains the equilibrium, what punishes deviation -- and what has to change in the underlying game for a transition between cultures to happen (cheap and accessible third-party enforcement seems like the obvious lever, but maybe not). Be precise; hand-wavy "it's like a coordination game" stuff will annoy me.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about:

1. The moral psychology -- shame vs. guilt, the felt sting of an insult, why some people literally can't let a slight go. Does that reduce to payoffs and beliefs, or does something real get lost?
2. The normative fight my friend keeps having -- whether any of these cultures is morally superior. Game theory tells you what's stable, not what's good. Is that a gap in my framework or a sign the normative debate is confused?
3. Whether victimhood culture is genuinely a distinct equilibrium or just what a dignity culture looks like when third-party appeal costs drop. Can the restatement settle this, or does it just relabel the disagreement?

And flag anywhere the translation smuggles something in -- e.g., if "inherent dignity" can't be expressed as a payoff without going circular, I want that said out loud, not papered over.
