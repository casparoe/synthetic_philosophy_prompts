I am a novelist revising a philosophical science-fiction manuscript, and I need help settling a disagreement between my editor, Mara, and my friend Theo, who studies ethics. Please act as a professional philosopher specializing in population ethics, procreative obligations, and the ethics of literary thought experiments. Think carefully about the arguments as they were actually made, not merely about the positions they resemble. Begin with a brief brainstorm of at least four relevant distinctions or possible interpretations before giving your adjudication. Do not provide citations or a reading list. Please write in polished American English.

The disputed premise is this: In the novel, a device offers three costless options. Pressing neither button creates no one. Pressing the blue button creates a child who is certain to have an exceptionally happy and fulfilling life. Pressing the gray button creates a different child who will have a life that is barely worth living because of chronic but manageable suffering. There are no effects on anyone else, no scarcity of resources, and no possibility that either child exists before the relevant button is pressed.

Mara argues that the device exposes the procreation asymmetry as morally arbitrary. She says that if the world containing the exceptionally happy child is vastly better than the empty world, then there must be a strong moral reason to press blue. Conversely, if the gray child’s life would be worth living, albeit barely, then pressing gray should also be at least somewhat good. She rejects the idea that morality could be indifferent to creating enormous happiness while remaining highly concerned about creating hardship. In her view, the fact that no one exists to complain if blue is not pressed is a technicality: the agent should compare the available worlds and bring about the better one.

Theo answers that Mara is confusing two separate questions. The first is whether there is a reason or duty to create someone rather than no one. The second is whether, if one does create someone, there is a reason to create the better-off rather than the worse-off possible child. He says there may be no duty to press either button, because no existing person is wronged by remaining childless; nevertheless, pressing gray rather than blue would be wrong, since an agent who chooses to create should avoid foreseeably creating a life dominated by unnecessary suffering when an excellent alternative is available. He calls this a conditional obligation rather than a reason to procreate.

Mara replies that conditional obligation is a rhetorical loophole. If the superiority of the blue outcome is morally significant enough to prohibit pressing gray, she asks, why does the same superiority cease to matter when the alternative is pressing neither button? She also notes that the blue child, once alive, would presumably be grateful to exist. Although she admits that retrospective gratitude is not prior consent, she thinks it reveals that declining to press blue deprives someone of an extraordinary benefit.

Theo responds that the gratitude argument proves too much. A happy person may be grateful for having been born without it following that anyone who declined to conceive that person acted wrongly. He also insists that Mara treats nonexistence as if it were a deprived condition undergone by a ghostly possible person. On his view, the gray child could have a genuine complaint about being created in avoidable suffering, whereas no one has a complaint if the blue child is never created. He therefore endorses a strongly asymmetrical principle: preventing bad lives can furnish a moral reason not to create, while creating good lives does not by itself furnish a comparably strong reason to create.

Mara then accuses Theo of changing cases. A complaint about the gray button might depend not on the child’s life being bad overall, but on the fact that blue was available. If so, the relevant wrong is selecting the worse possible child, not creating a child whose existence is intrinsically objectionable. That would support a principle of choosing the best possible child once one chooses to procreate, but not the broader asymmetry Theo claims. She summarizes his position as optional perfection: one may permissibly do nothing, but if one acts, one must produce the best available result. She considers that combination unstable.

Theo accepts that Mara has identified an important ambiguity, but denies that it defeats him. He distinguishes between pressing gray when gray is the only available way to create a life and pressing gray when blue is available. The first may raise a question about whether a barely worthwhile life is good enough to create; the second raises a distinct comparative question about selecting a worse-off possible person. He says Mara has collapsed those questions into a single ranking of worlds, thereby assuming the impartial consequentialist framework that the asymmetry is meant to challenge.

Please adjudicate the disagreement point by point. In particular:

1. Explain the strong and weak versions of the procreation asymmetry in your own words, and identify which claims in the exchange presuppose each version.
2. Determine whether Theo’s distinction between a reason to create and a reason governing whom to create is coherent, or whether Mara is right that it merely conceals the underlying comparison of outcomes.
3. Assess the significance of retrospective gratitude, the absence of prior consent, and the idea that only an actually created person can have a complaint.
4. Decide whether Mara’s charge of optional perfection is a reductio, a fair restatement of a defensible position, or both.
5. Say whether the three-option device genuinely supports the procreation asymmetry or instead demonstrates only the narrower principle that prospective creators should select the better-off possible child when selection is possible.
6. Identify any important distinction that both Mara and Theo are missing, including any ambiguity concerning lives worth living, lives worth starting, harm, benefit, and comparisons between existence and nonexistence.
7. Conclude with an overall verdict about who is more nearly right, while also specifying which particular points each disputant wins and loses.

The goal is not advice about how to revise the novel. The goal is a careful philosophical refereeing of this exact exchange.
