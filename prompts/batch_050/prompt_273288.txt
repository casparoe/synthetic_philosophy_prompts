I'm taking an undergraduate course in Hellenistic philosophy, and our professor has been circulating excerpts from a popular book on "the wisdom of the ancient world" as optional background reading. One passage has been bothering me, and I'd like a careful verdict on it. The author writes, roughly, the following: "Epicurus held that death cannot possibly be bad for the person who dies, because anything bad for you must be something you experience, and a dead person experiences nothing. It follows that fearing death is simply irrational, and that the sage is therefore indifferent between dying tomorrow and dying in forty years -- neither prospect involves her in any harm. Epicurus sealed the point with his celebrated symmetry argument: the eternity before our birth troubles no one, so the eternity after our death shouldn't trouble us either. Modern philosophers who object that death is bad because it deprives us of future goods are just refusing to accept Epicurus's logic."

My instinct is that this is mostly faithful but gets something wrong -- the bit about indifference between dying tomorrow and in forty years seems too strong, and I have a vague memory from lecture that the symmetry argument might not be Epicurus's own. But I can't tell whether I'm remembering correctly or just projecting modern intuitions backward. Could you audit the passage for me? Please go through it claim by claim: which parts accurately represent the Epicurean position as developed in his surviving writings, which parts are defensible popular simplifications, and which are actual misrepresentations -- whether of Epicurus's logic, of the scope of his conclusion, or of what he thought a rational person should feel about mortality? I'd also like to know how the strongest modern versions of the deprivation objection engage the real argument rather than the passage's version of it, and whether the author's final sneer -- that critics are simply refusing the logic -- survives once the summary is corrected. Finally, given everything, how close is the passage's conclusion that fearing death is irrational to something Epicurus actually established, as opposed to something he asserted within a larger ethical framework?
