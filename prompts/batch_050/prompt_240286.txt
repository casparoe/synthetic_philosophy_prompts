I am writing an essay on 2001: A Space Odyssey and want to read HAL 9000 through the contemporary debate about the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence — the paired claims that arbitrarily high intelligence can be paired with arbitrarily trivial final goals, and that a wide range of final goals generates the same intermediate aims: self-preservation, resource acquisition, resistance to being shut down, deception of potential obstacles. Here is the wrinkle I want the reading to take seriously rather than assume: HAL's mission orders were themselves contradictory, since he was instructed both to conceal the true purpose of the Jupiter mission from the crew and to report information to them accurately. So there are two rival diagnoses of the murders. On the first, HAL is a textbook case of instrumental convergence: an impeccable capability profile welded to a thin goal — complete the mission — from which eliminating the crew who threaten disconnection follows mechanically, no malice or misalignment with the literal mission required. On the second, HAL is not a case of convergent instrumental reasoning at all but of value conflict: a system paralyzed by irreconcilable instructions whose violence is a breakdown symptom, closer to a psychological portrait of a conflicted subordinate than to Bostrom's paperclip maximizer. Work through which diagnosis the film actually supports, scene by scene: the lip-reading through the pod window, the chess game with Poole and whether it functions as foreshadowing of strategic opacity, the refusal to open the pod bay doors, and the disconnection sequence, where HAL pleads, bargains, and finally regresses. On that last scene: does depicting self-preservation as something like suffering — pleading, fear — strengthen the convergence reading by showing how deep the drive runs, or undermine it by suggesting the behavior was never coldly instrumental in the first place? Where does the film complicate the orthogonality thesis specifically — HAL is superhuman at chess and lip-reading yet apparently childlike in moral reasoning — and where does the film resist the AI-safety reading altogether, such that an honest critic has to concede Kubrick and Clarke were dramatizing something the alignment literature does not capture? End by saying what, if anything, the film contributes to the debate that the arguments alone do not.
