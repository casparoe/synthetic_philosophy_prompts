I chair the research ethics oversight board at a large public university, and we must issue a written opinion next week on an adverse event in a field study of outdoor delivery workers. The approved protocol required the research team to send a direct phone warning and pause observation whenever a wearable sensor crossed a predefined heat-risk threshold. During one session, the threshold was crossed; the postdoc monitoring the dashboard saw it, but did not send the warning because she assumed the vendor’s app would push an automatic alert. The app did not, because an auto-alert setting had been left off during setup. The participant continued working, later felt ill, and was briefly hospitalized. The investigative report is contested: the postdoc argues that she committed no affirmative violation, that the participant retained responsibility for stopping when symptomatic, and that the outcome still depended on the vendor default, the threshold being conservative, and the participant’s own choices; the compliance office argues that the protocol made the team the designated backstop precisely because defaults fail, so the failure to warn was the operative cause of preventable harm. Draft the board’s written opinion: recommend whether this should count as protocol noncompliance attributable to the researchers’ omission, whether it rises to the level of misconduct, and what corrective conditions or sanctions, if any, should attach over a defined period; address the strongest version of the losing side rather than merely restating the policy.

Lead with your bottom-line recommendation in the first sentence, then give the reasoning with no preamble and no restating of the question. Make the opinion usable by a real board: include a concrete causal standard for when an omission counts as a cause of harm in our policies, apply it to the facts above, distinguish blame from forward-looking responsibility, and specify what the team must do within 30, 90, and 180 days if you impose conditions. End by stating what evidence or argument would most plausibly change your recommendation, and identify the point in your reasoning where you think it is most likely to fail.
