So I'm in this world history class and last week our teacher dropped this idea that the agricultural revolution might have been a huge mistake, like hunter-gatherers actually had more free time and better diets than early farmers, and farming only won because of population pressure and grain being easy to tax and store. Most of my class thought that was ridiculous because why would people pick a worse life? But then my friend (who's really into game theory from a summer camp thing) said it's like a prisoner's dilemma: each group individually does better by farming because you can support more people and outbreed the foragers next door, but everyone collectively ends up worse off, working longer hours, living in disease-y crowded villages, getting bossed around by whoever controls the surplus. So nobody had to "choose" the bad outcome, the bad outcome just wins anyway.

I want you to actually test that framing against what we know about how the transition to farming happened, not just nod along with it. Like: who exactly are the "players" in this game when it unfolds over thousands of years and the groups never even meet? What are the payoffs supposed to be -- calories per hour worked, health, number of surviving kids, and do those even point the same direction? And where does the prisoner's dilemma story break down? For example it seems weird to me that foragers kept "defecting" back to foraging for centuries in lots of places, and some societies apparently mixed farming and hunting, which the model shouldn't allow if farming is a dominant strategy. Also if farming spreads because it supports more people, is that "defection" or just... a different game entirely where the winner is whoever has more soldiers?

Finally, what does the messy reality do to the prisoner's dilemma explanation -- is it a genuinely good lens for explaining how a mistake can outcompete a good life, or does it just dress up the puzzle in fancy terms without explaining anything? And if it's not the right model, is there a better game-theoretic one (my friend mentioned something about arms races and ratchets but I didn't follow)? Dont just summarize both sides, I want to know whether the "agriculture as a trap" story survives contact with the actual history.
