I run a small philosophy reading group (mostly grad students plus a couple of engineers who wandered in and stayed) and we've spent four sessions on antinatalism and Benatar's asymmetry and gotten absolutely nowhere, which is strange because everyone in the room happily grants the basic setup. Quick version for anyone who needs it: the asymmetry says that for someone who never exists, the absence of pain is good (or at least not bad), while the absence of pleasure is not bad, since there's nobody around to be deprived of it -- whereas for someone who does exist, pain is bad and pleasure is good. Hence, on this view, coming into existence is always worse than never existing, no matter how good the life.

Here's what happened in session three. I drew the thing as a 2x2 table on the whiteboard -- rows for "exists" and "never exists," columns for pain and pleasure -- and asked everyone to fill in the cells. The "exists" row was unanimous in about ten seconds. The "never exists" row was a bloodbath. Some people wrote "good" and "not bad" (the Benatar reading), some wrote "neutral" in both cells, and two people refused to write anything at all on the grounds that a cell without a subject can't carry a value, and that pretending it can is exactly where the argument goes wrong. What fascinates me is that the second and third groups don't disagree with Benatar about how bad suffering is, or about any empirical claim whatsoever -- they disagree about whether an empty cell can even have a value written in it. My working hypothesis is that the whole antinatalism debate is secretly a disagreement about comparability -- whether "better off" requires a subject who is better off -- and that everything else (the consent arguments, the quality-of-life surveys, the extinction implications) is downstream noise. But I might be wrong and there might be a genuine axiological dispute under there that neither side has articulated cleanly.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal, with everyone meaning something different by "good for"? Is it a substantive dispute in value theory about whether value needs a bearer? Or are the sides answering different questions -- one about the value of worlds, one about the value of lives -- while sharing a vocabulary? Diagnose it using what each side actually says, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a distinction, a new argument form, an intuition pump that does more than restate the standoff. And if your honest diagnosis is that the impasse is permanent because both ways of filling in the table are internally coherent and nothing internal to either can force a move, then argue for that instead.
