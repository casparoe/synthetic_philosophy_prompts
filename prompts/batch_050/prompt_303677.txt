A bit of background, since it shapes what I'm asking. I spent forty years as an experimental chemist, retired about three years ago, and have spent a fair chunk of my retirement falling down the philosophy rabbit hole. Lately I've landed on advance directives and the authority of the earlier self, partly because a close friend is in the early stages of dementia and the whole family is now wrestling with what his stated wishes should count for as he changes. From what I've read so far, the debate seems to run through a few recurring fault lines: whether a directive's authority rests on precedent autonomy or just on promoting the person's good, whether severe cognitive decline disrupts personal identity enough to sever the tie between the author of the directive and the patient in the bed, whether a healthy person can even know what life with dementia is actually like (and so whether their earlier instructions were ever properly informed), and when, if ever, a patient's current refusal should override their own earlier instructions. I daresay I'm missing some.

Here's the thing I keep chewing on, and it's probably the scientist in me. An advance directive looks to me a lot like preregistering an experiment: you lock in a decision rule in advance precisely because you know your future self will be biased, or confused, or motivated to fudge. In science we treat that pre-commitment as binding even when, mid-experiment, you'd dearly love to change the analysis plan. But I suspect the analogy misleads as often as it illuminates — in preregistration the person being bound is the same person in every relevant respect, which is exactly what's in dispute with dementia. Anyway, what I'd like is a defended prioritisation, not a survey. I'm one retired amateur with maybe two or three years of serious reading and writing left in me, and I'd like to end up producing a long essay that contributes something rather than just summarising everyone else. So: of the open questions in this area — the ones I've listed or others you think I've missed — which are most worth someone in my position working on, and in what order? I'd like the ordering to be dependency-aware (which questions are upstream of which others, so that getting one wrong poisons the rest), and I'd like you to be explicit about the criteria you're using — philosophical importance, tractability for a non-professional with no graduate seminar to lean on, how crowded each question already is — and about what would change the ranking.

Two requests about how you frame it. First, please seperate what is genuinely settled in this area from what is still contested among people who actually work on it — I don't want a contested view presented to me as though it were the consensus, since I won't be in a position to spot it. Second, could you answer in British English? I've been reading so much American philosophy that my own spelling is starting to drift.
