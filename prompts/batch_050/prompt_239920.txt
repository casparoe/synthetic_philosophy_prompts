I'm a second-year graduate student in philosophy of mind preparing a two-page proposal for a ten-week summer research grant, and I need the proposal document itself. The topic is the color phi phenomenon and the distinction Dennett draws between two ways a misreported experience could have been produced: a Stalinesque revision, in which a false content is fabricated before it ever reaches experience and is then sincerely reported (the show trial, staged for the observer), and an Orwellian revision, in which the experience occurred veridically but the memory of it was rewritten before report (history falsified after the fact). Dennett's well-known claim is that when the two stimuli in the apparent-motion display are separated by a sufficiently short interval, there is no fact of the matter about which kind of revision occurred, because nothing in the subject settles whether the second content briefly entered experience and was then tampered with or was prevented from entering experience at all.

My half-formed idea is this. Standard responses to Dennett either insist the distinction remains factual (appealing to the timing of neural processing) or accept its dissolution and conclude that experience has no determinate temporal location. Both sides, I suspect, treat "the time at which a content is experienced" as a single question. I want to argue that it splits into at least two: when a content first becomes available to guide the subject's behavior and dispositions, and when it becomes available for verbal report. My conjecture is that the two notions come apart in exactly the window the color phi phenomenon occupies, and that once they are distinguished, the Stalinesque/Orwellian dispute decomposes into a part that is genuinely verbal (a dispute over which notion of "experienced" deserves the name) and a part that is empirical (a dispute about which stage of processing the second stimulus disrupts). If that is right, Dennett is correct that there is no fact of the matter as the question is usually posed, but for a subtler reason than he gives.
