So for context: I got into a long argument with my roommate (a physics PhD student) about whether chaos theory "matters philosophically" or is just a footnote to the determinism debate, and I got annoyed enough that I drafted a short essay defending the strong view. I'm pasting the draft below. What I want is comments, not a rewrite — specifically: (1) flag anywhere the physics is wrong or handwavy, (2) tell me whether the position I stake out is actually a position or just instrumentalism in a trench coat, (3) identify the single weakest inferential step, and (4) one concrete suggestion for making the last move land. If you think the whole thing rests on an equivocation, say so plainly.

Draft, working title "Determinism Is a Claim About the World; Predictability Is a Claim About You":

Para 1: Everyone treats chaos as deterministic-but-unpredictable, with "unpredictable" doing no philosophical work — a practical limitation, like not owning a good enough telescope. I think this badly undersells it. In a system with sensitive dependence, the measurement precision you need to predict the state at time t grows exponentially with t. So for any predictor embedded in the physical universe, there's a horizon: a time beyond which prediction would require storing more digits than the observable universe has particles, or making a measurement fine-grained enough that it perturbs the thing measured. Past the horizon, prediction isn't unfeasible, it's physically excluded.

Para 2: Now here's my move. Within the horizon, determinism is an empirical hypothesis — it can be confirmed by successful prediction and refuted by failure. Beyond the horizon, determinism becomes empirically idle: no physically possible observation could distinguish a deterministic chaotic system from a genuinely stochastic one. I'll call the resulting view horizon relativism: claims about what the world "really does" beyond the predictability horizon are formally statable but physically empty, the way people say absolute simultaneity is statable but empty after relativity.

Para 3: Consequence: the live question for free will, responsibility, planning, whatever, is never "is the universe deterministic" but "is this system, for this agent, inside or outside its horizon." Weather: outside. A billiard table: inside. The brain: genuinely open, and maybe that's where the interesting debate actually lives, rather than in the metaphysics.

Para 4: Anticipated objection: determinism can be metaphysically true even where it's empirically idle, and I'm just bullying people with verificationism. Reply: I'm not saying the claim is meaningless; I'm saying it can do zero work in any explanation a bounded agent could actually give, and a claim that can do no explanatory work for anyone, ever, is not one anyone has reason to assert or deny.

Some specific worries I already have, in case you want to target them: the "measurement perturbs the system" bit in Para 1 is doing a lot of work and I'm not sure it's even true classically rather than quantum-mechanically; the simultaneity analogy in Para 2 might be load-bearing in a way I haven't earned; and Para 4's "zero work" test might prove too much (does it rule out all metaphysics?). But don't limit yourself to these — if something else is the real problem, I'd rather hear that.
