I'm a philosophy PhD student (working on self-knowledge and confabulation, mostly) and I've been pulled into our department's AI safety reading group. I drafted a short essay trying to import the human confabulation literature into the debate over whether chain-of-thought outputs from LLMs are faithful to the models' actual reasoning. Condensed version below. I want comments, but please be opinionated: in particular, tell me which stance I should actually take on the objection in §4, because I keep flip-flopping and I need to commit before I present this.

---

1. Setup. The faithfulness debate usually asks: does the CoT accurately describe the process that produced the answer? The standard evidence of unfaithfulness is stuff like models failing to mention the hints or biases that actually swayed them, or giving the same answer even when you mess with their reasoning steps.

2. My thesis. Faithfulness should be cashed out as counterfactual dependence, not descriptive accuracy. A CoT is faithful iff the answer depends on it in the right way: had the reasoning gone differently, the answer would have differed accordingly. I propose an intervention suite to test this: (a) corrupt a step mid-CoT and see if the answer shifts; (b) transplant in a CoT arguing toward a different conclusion; (c) insert a plausible but irrelevant step and check the answer ignores it.

3. Why descriptive accuracy is the wrong target. Human verbal reports are famously inaccurate as descriptions of cognition, but they can still be evidence about it. What matters is whether the report is causally hooked up to the process, not whether it resembles the mechanism — like how a speedometer tells you nothing about how the engine works but still faithfully tracks what it's doing. (This is the part where I wave at the Nisbett-and-Wilson-style literature.)

4. The objection I can't settle — the "obedient scribe." Imagine a model that first settles on an answer in one opaque forward pass, then generates a CoT rationalizing it, and then actually reads its own CoT back and outputs whatever conclusion the CoT reaches. Every intervention in my suite now passes: corrupt the CoT and the answer changes accordingly. So my criterion declares this maximally faithful. But intuitively it's the worst case of post-hoc rationalization — the CoT is causally efficacious only because the model obediently follows its own confabulation. So dependence apparently isn't sufficient for faithfulness.

---

Questions: (i) Does the obedient scribe case really defeat the counterfactual criterion, or am I wrong that the relevant dependence holds there (maybe the dependence that matters has to run through the first pass)? (ii) If it defeats it, is my only retreat to demand the CoT track the first-pass computation — and doesn't that just collapse back into the descriptive-accuracy view I rejected in §2? Pick a side and defend it, don't just map the option space. Also, if you think the human-confabulation analogy in §3 is doing real work rather than decoration, tell me; if it's decoration, tell me to cut it.
