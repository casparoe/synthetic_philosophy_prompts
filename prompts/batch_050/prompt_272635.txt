So I'm teaching my grad seminar on irrationality again this spring and I've been asked to circulate my positions in advance so the students can take shots at them. Before I do that I want an audit: which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and what the cheapest fix is for each real tension. Rank them by severity, and if there's one revision that cleans up the most, tell me which it is. Here's where I've landed:

(1) Strict, clear-eyed akrasia is possible: an agent can freely and intentionally do x while judging, all things considered, that it would be better to do y. (Socrates was wrong, Davidson was right.)

(2) The best empirical explanation of most real-world akrasia is hyperbolic discounting: the predictable preference reversal as the tempting reward gets closer in time.

(3) Rationality is a matter of internal coherence: you're irrational roughly when you fail to respond correctly to your own judgments and preferences. An agent who at each moment acts on what they genuinely most prefer at that moment is not irrational, just built differently from us.

(4) Ainslie-style personal rules genuinely work: treating each choice as a precedent in an ongoing bargaining game with your future selves changes the payoff structure, and that's why resolutions can stave off temptation.

(5) Precommitment is rational, and a sophisticated agent -- one who predicts they'll give in later -- is irrational if they decline available precommitment devices and then, predictably, give in.

One thing I specifically want flagged if it's a problem: I've had a nagging feeling that (2) and (3) together do something funny to (1), but I cant quite pin it down, and I'm also not sure (5) is entiteled to the "irrational" verdict given what (3) says. Don't just flag everything -- tell me which worries dissolve on inspection.
