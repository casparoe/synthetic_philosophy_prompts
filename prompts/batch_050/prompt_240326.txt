My reading group on the history of economic thought has just spent three sessions on George Berkeley's The Querist (published in parts between 1735 and 1737), written while he was in Ireland and preoccupied with the country's poverty. Our shared understanding of his position, after a fair amount of argument, is roughly this: Berkeley denies that gold and silver have any privileged status as money; money, in its true idea, is better understood as a token or ticket whose value lies not in its material but in its power to set labour and industry in motion; bullion hoarded in a chest does nobody any good; the genuine measure of a nation's wealth is the employment of its people rather than the quantity of metal it holds; and on that basis he proposed, among other things, a national bank issuing paper credit to mobilise what he saw as Ireland's idle hands. He also frames much of this morally, treating idleness rather than lack of specie as the real disease. Some historians of monetary thought consequently describe him as an early monetary nominalist, almost a forerunner of chartalism.

Our group split over how far this position extrapolates, and I would like a reasoned conjecture to take back to them. Suppose Berkeley were confronted with today's monetary arrangements: flat fiat currencies managed by independent central banks; quantitative easing programmes that created trillions of pounds, dollars and euros essentially from nothing; and cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin, whose tokens are underwritten by deliberately costly computation and which no bank issues at all. Where would he land? In particular: (a) would he greet modern fiat money as the straightforward vindication of his ticket theory, or would he think we have kept the form of his proposal while abandoning its point — and would he distinguish, as I suspect he would, between credit creation that employs the idle and money-printing that does not, so that his verdict on quantitative easing might turn entirely on where the money went? (b) Would he see proof-of-work mining as the perverse inversion of his theory, real labour expended to manufacture a token whose scarcity is the whole attraction, and hence a relapse into the commodity-money fetishism he was arguing against — or is there a reading on which mining's sunk cost does the same trust-establishing work he elsewhere assigns to the issuing authority? (c) Given that his yardstick for wealth is employed industry rather than any monetary aggregate, would he find more to like in modern functional-finance arguments, which judge deficits by real resources and unemployment, than in the bullionist orthodoxy of his own day?

Please answer in British English, and structure the reply as a disciplined piece of counterfactual intellectual history rather than free speculation: state plainly which conclusions follow fairly directly from the Querist's commitments, which require borrowing from his wider outlook (his moralising about industry and luxury, his clerical and hierarchical assumptions about social order), and which genuinely cannot be settled from the texts and so must be flagged as guesswork. Where his views pull in two directions at once — as I suspect they do on Bitcoin — say so rather than resolving the tension artificially.
