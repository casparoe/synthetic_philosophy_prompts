I retired three years ago after 35-odd years as an exploration geologist, and like an idiot I took up philosophy as a hobby. Lately I've been reading around the topic of disagreement and what it should do to your confidence, and I've built an argument that seems to prove far too much. Please take on the role of a professional epistemologist who works on higher-order evidence. Think carefully, step by step, and before you commit to a diagnosis, do a quick brainstorm about which steps are actually load-bearing — I want to see your working, not just a verdict.

The motivating case. In the field, if a colleague and I ran the same assay and got different numbers, neither of us just stood by his own reading. The disagreement itself was a reason to recheck — not because he was smarter, but because he was about as likely to be right as I was. Generalizing: learning that someone roughly as competent as you, with the same evidence, reached the opposite conclusion is itself evidence you've slipped up, and you should move your confidence a good way toward theirs. Now the argument:

(1) When an epistemic peer disagrees with me about p on the same evidence, I should substantially lower my confidence in p, no matter how compelling my own reasoning still looks.

(2) On nearly every interesting question — politics, nutrition, whether the '94 flood at our field camp was the dam's fault or the rain's — such peers exist and disagree with me. So by (1), I should hover near 50/50 on almost everything interesting.

(3) The prinicple in (1) is itself contested — I gather plenty of philosophers are "steadfast" and deny it. Applying (1) to that very dispute, I should drop my confidence in (1) to about half as well.

(4) Worse, a flat-earther disagrees with me about the shape of the earth. I'd like to say he's not my peer, but that judgement is mine, it's contested (by him, if no one else), and (1) told me not to rely on my own contested judgements. So either I grant him standing and give the flat earth real credence, or I break (1).

(5) So (1) forces either near-universal suspension of judgement, itself included, or equal weight for cranks. Both are absurd, hence (1) is false: disagreement carries no evidential weight of its own.

(6) But the assay case shows disagreement plainly does carry weight — and the reasoning that justifies rechecking the assay looks exactly like (1). So something between (1) and (5) has to go, and I can't see what.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? Something feels off around (4), but each step looks fine when I stare at it on its own. Which premise or step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If I can only keep one thing, it's the assay intuition; the general principle can go if it must. Two side questions. First, is the self-application move in (3) even legitimate? I've seen that apply-it-to-itself trick used to refute relativism, utilitarianism, everything under the sun, and I've never trusted it. Second, this whole picture seems to require me to believe p while also believing that my evidence doesn't really support p — believing something while judging you shouldn't believe it, which a friend called epistemic akrasia. That sounds flatly incoherent, so if the view demands it, isn't that alone a refutation?
