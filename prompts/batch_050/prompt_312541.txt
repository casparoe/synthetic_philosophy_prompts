I am a novelist, and once a year I lead an advanced fiction workshop at our local arts center: twelve participants, mostly published short-story writers working across literary fiction, science fiction, and fantasy, meeting for a single two-hour session. This year I want to devote the session to a problem that haunts my own drafting -- the difference between what can be vividly imagined and what is actually possible. My writers are superb at rendering the imaginable; almost none of them have ever asked whether the imaginable is any guide to the possible, or noticed where that guide misleads.

Design a structured in-session activity that makes them feel the force of this before I ever name it. They should run into the trouble themselves: on one side, scenarios that are effortlessly, vividly conceivable but arguably impossible (a world in which someone validly proves a true mathematical theorem false; a world in which the water in this very glass is not H2O); on the other, states of affairs that strain the imagination to the breaking point and yet are actual, for which I can supply counterintuitive physics examples if needed. The heart of the activity should have them generate imagined worlds of their own and then stress-test one another's under some constraint you devise, so that the failure of conceivability-as-evidence emerges from their own work rather than from a lecture by me.

Please specify the setup and rules, the sequence and timing across the full two hours, the materials I need, and how to keep the literary-fiction writers from dismissing the whole exercise as a genre matter that only concerns the fantasists. Include a debrief that maps what happened in the room onto the philosophical distinctions (prima facie versus ideal conceivability; necessary truths we can only discover a posteriori) without my naming any of this vocabulary until late in the session. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- I anticipate at least one participant insisting that in fiction anything goes and therefore the question is empty -- and how to handle it. And be frank about which feature of the philosophical issue a room full of writers cannot reproduce and I will simply have to state outright.

Finally, commit. Where you face design choices, make them and defend them rather than handing me a menu of options. And in the debrief, take a definite position on how much evidentiary weight conceivability deserves -- in metaphysics, and separately in worldbuilding -- and give reasons for it, so I am presenting a view to be argued with rather than a survey to be nodded at.
