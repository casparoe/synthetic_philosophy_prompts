Last night a friend and I argued for hours about whether we may judge historical figures, and she walked me into a conclusion I cannot accept but could not cleanly refute. Please respond as a professional philosopher who works on moral responsibility and historical memory. Think carefully, and before giving your diagnosis, privately run through the strongest case for each step, since I suspect the argument survives a casual reading.

Her argument, as best I can reconstruct it: (1) It is unfair to blame someone for violating a moral standard they could not reasonably have been expected to recognize, given the concepts and evidence available to them. (2) What a person can reasonably be expected to recognize is fixed by their historical and cultural context. (3) Jefferson's context did not make the grave injustice of slaveholding something he could reasonably have been expected to recognize. (4) So it is unfair to blame Jefferson for slaveholding. (5) But (1) and (2) are perfectly general: they govern every agent in every period, including us, since future generations will owe us the same charity regarding whatever we presently fail to see. (6) So no one is ever blameworthy for a wrong their own time does not condemn, which dissolves most of moral blame, including blame of our contemporaries. (7) That is absurd, so (1) must be false: there is no epistemic condition on blame, and we may judge the dead entirely by our own lights.

I find (7) nearly as hard to swallow as (6). If forced to choose, I would rather keep some version of (1) than keep (3), and I am not willing to give up blaming present-day people who profit from practices their victims plainly protest. Tell me exactly where this argument goes wrong, if it does; if the flaw is a slide between steps rather than a false premise, name the two readings it trades on and explain why each looked acceptable on its own. Then price the alternatives: what does it cost to reject (1), to reject (2), and to reject (3), and which rejection leaves a workable account of blame across time. If you think the argument is actually sound, say so and defend (7) against my resistance.

Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write me a long letter: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold text, and no tables.
