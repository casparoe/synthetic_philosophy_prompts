I'm a journalist who covers evolutionary biology and the behavioral sciences for a general-audience magazine. Several times a month I receive embargoed papers and press releases claiming that some trait -- a facial feature, a mating preference, a cognitive bias, a disease susceptibility -- evolved "for" some purpose, and my editors want a fast verdict on whether the story is worth running and how much credence to give the adaptive claim. Too often I've watched colleagues relay a plausible-sounding functional story as though identifying a current benefit settled the question of evolutionary origin, which is precisely the conflation the Gould--Lewontin critique of adaptationism warned against decades ago. I want to stop making that mistake systematically rather than relying on my instincts each time.

Please write me an evaluation procedure I can run on any such claim in under an hour, without specialist training in the relevant subfield. It should be a sequence of tests with explicit pass/fail or undetermined outcomes at each step, and it must operationalize, in plain language, the distinctions that the spandrels critique insists on: between a trait's current utility and its historical origin; between a genuine adaptation, a byproduct of selection on something else, and a co-opted trait whose function changed; between a hypothesis that makes risky predictions and one that can absorb any observation. Concretely, I want the procedure to force me (1) to generate at least one non-adaptive rival explanation -- developmental constraint, phylogenetic inertia, genetic drift, architectural byproduct -- before assessing the adaptive story on offer, and to state what evidence would discriminate between them; (2) to check whether the paper actually tests the adaptive hypothesis or merely reports a correlation consistent with it; (3) to ask whether the claimed selective environment is independently evidenced or simply reverse-inferred from the trait; and (4) to produce, at the end, one of three editorial verdicts -- report the adaptive claim as supported, report it as speculation with the rivals named, or decline the story -- with rules for when each verdict is mandatory rather than discretionary.

Where a step depends on a judgment call that cannot be proceduralized, mark it explicitly and tell me what a reasonable range of judgments looks like, rather than hiding the discretion. Note also where the procedure will systematically misfire -- for instance, cases where the byproduct alternative is itself untestable, or where convergent evolution makes the adaptive case strong but the paper in hand contributes nothing to it.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical pitches: first, a study from a prominent lab, under embargo with enthusiastic press materials, arguing that the human chin is an adaptation for resisting mechanical stress during chewing; second, a paper claiming that a widespread cognitive bias is an adaptation for an ancestral social environment, where the bias's cost in modern settings is documented but its ancestral benefit is modeled rather than measured; third, a comparative study across forty bird species showing that a plumage trait tracks a specific predation regime in the predicted direction. Treat the first case honestly even though it is exactly the kind of story my editors will pressure me to run, and show me what I write back to them if the procedure says no.
