I am a retired chemical physicist who, in retirement, has begun auditing an undergraduate philosophy of mind course at my local university. The instructor runs a peer-grading exercise: each week one anonymized student answer is circulated, and we auditors submit a provisional grade with written justification before she reveals her own assessment. I would like your help producing mine. Please grade the answer below out of 20, and give the point-by-point comments I would need to submit: which parts deserve full credit, where marks should be lost and precisely why, and whether each flaw is a slip of expression or a substantive misunderstanding. I have not formed a settled view of the answer's quality, so please do not pitch the grade toward leniency or severity on my account.

Exam question: Daniel Dennett distinguishes the physical, the design, and the intentional stances, and argues that the patterns the intentional stance tracks are genuinely real. (a) Set out the three stances and the kind of prediction each affords. (b) State Dennett's criterion for when a pattern counts as real. (c) On Dennett's view, are beliefs real things, useful fictions, or something in between? Defend your reading.

Student's answer: (a) The physical stance predicts a system's behavior from its physical constitution plus the laws of nature, as when we calculate where a falling object will land. The design stance predicts on the assumption that the thing will do what it was designed to do: I expect my alarm clock to ring at seven without knowing anything of its wiring. The intentional stance goes further: we treat the system as a rational agent with beliefs and desires and predict that it will do what it would be rational to do given them. Playing a chess computer, I predict it will move its rook because it wants to save its king and knows the rules. Since the design stance requires knowledge of a designer's purposes, it is unavailable for organisms, which have no designer; for the spider and the chimpanzee we must therefore ascend to the intentional stance and attribute the beliefs appropriate to each creature's situation. (b) A pattern is real, for Dennett, if tracking it yields a predictive and compressive advantage: pattern-talk lets us describe the data more economically than an exhaustive point-by-point description would. The reality of the pattern thus consists in its usefulness to an observer — a pattern is real for those who find it profitable to track, and unreal where no one bothers. (c) Beliefs are accordingly useful fictions. When I say the chess computer believes its queen is threatened, I report no inner item but adopt a stance that works. Since a pattern's reality is observer-relative usefulness, belief-talk stands with talk of centers of gravity: handy, but not part of the world's furniture.

One further request. If you think the exam question itself is ill-posed, or that a comment the grading calls for would rest on a contested or mistaken reading of Dennett, say so plainly rather than grading around the problem. I would rather be corrected than pass a confusion on to the student.
