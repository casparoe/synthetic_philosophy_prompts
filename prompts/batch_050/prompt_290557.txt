I sit on the three-person committee at a research institute that decides, roughly fifteen times a year, whether to fund pilot projects whose success chances are far less pinned down than an ordinary grant's: sometimes there is a solid base rate from comparable projects, and sometimes the only number anyone can offer is an investigator's hunch. We keep having the same argument -- one member says a probability estimate is a probability estimate and we should just maximize expected value, while another says that when the estimate is a guess we should demand a premium before committing, the way subjects in Ellsberg-style urn experiments prefer a known 50/50 draw to one from an urn of unknown composition. Write us a decision procedure, runnable in under half an hour during a meeting, that settles when ambiguity about the probability may legitimately move a decision beyond what our best point estimate already implies, and when it must be ignored. The procedure should include a test for whether the ambiguity actually discriminates among the options on the table (if every option is equally ambiguous, or the uncertainty is symmetric around the estimate, it presumably cannot justify switching), a test for whether we are being offered the ambiguous option by someone who plausibly knows more than we do, a rule for how reversibility and the option of buying more information change the answer, and an instruction for what to do when no one will commit to any number at all. Apply it to three worked cases: one with a real base rate, one where the figure is pure judgment, and one where the procedure tells us to treat the guess as if it were a measured probability even though the committee will hate doing so. End with a short statement of what the procedure cannot settle. Begin with the procedure itself, with no preface or restating of this request.
