I'm a novelist (literary fiction, two books out, third underway) and I keep leaning on the same cluster of ideas both in my writing and in the pop-econ I read on planes, and before I bake them into this new book I want to know whether they actually cohere. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Character is revealed through choice, especially costly choice. What a character says about themselves is close to worthless; what they do when it costs them something is the truth. This is a craft rule but also, I think, just true of people.

2. The economists are basically right that preference-talk means nothing beyond what choice reveals. If someone insists they value X but never picks it when they could, then they don't value X. There's no little inner preference hiding behind the behavior.

3. Utility numbers are just bookkeeping. Only the ordering is real -- nobody wants A "twice as much" as B, and you definately can't compare how strongly two different people want something.

4. And yet people genuinely act against their own preferences all the time. Akrasia, self-sabotage, the alcoholic reaching for the bottle he truly doesn't want to want. Readers recognize this instantly as real life, and so do I.

5. In my fiction I give readers direct access to wants that never become action -- the suppressed longing, the plan never attempted -- and I take that to be truthful about the person, not a cheat.

6. When I decide which of two characters gets the scarce thing (the kidney, the one seat on the boat), it seems obviously meaningful that one would be destroyed by losing and the other only mildly disappointed, and that this should matter for what's fair.

So: does this set hang together? Which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like it? Please rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each one tell me the cheapest fix and what giving it up would cost me. I haven't decided which commitment I'd drop -- that's partly what I'm asking you to help me see.
