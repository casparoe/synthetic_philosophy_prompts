I'm a third-year philosophy student in a seminar on population ethics, and this term we've spent several weeks on the problems that arise for moral evaluation once you take seriously the possibility that the universe contains infinitely many welfare-bearing beings. In a recent session our group got stuck on what the tutor called infinitarian paralysis: if total welfare is already infinite (positive, negative, or both), then nothing I do seems to change the total, and ordinary moral deliberation looks pointless. I came away with a rough set of views that felt reasonable in the room, but the longer I sit with them the more I suspect they don't cohere, and I'd like an audit before I commit to them in my term paper.

Here is roughly where I've landed. (1) The goodness of an outcome is given by the total welfare it contains, with no discounting for spatial or temporal distance -- I regard any such discounting as arbitrary parochialism. (2) As an empirical matter, I think there's a substantial chance, say around a third, that the cosmos contains infinitely many beings with infinite total welfare, possibly both positive and negative. (3) Despite (2), my actions genuinely make the world better or worse overall, and this is what licenses ordinary cost-benefit reasoning in ethics. (4) I proposed in class what I've been calling the causal shadow principle: when comparing two options, we should quantify only over their respective causal futures, holding fixed whatever lies outside the agent's causal reach, and be indifferent between options that differ only in regions disconnected from both. (5) I reject the averaging fix for infinite populations, because there is no non-arbitrary measure over an infinite cosmos to average with respect to. (6) I also believe infinite ethics is mostly a theoretical sideshow and that day-to-day moral reasoning survives it intact.

My questions: which of these commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be? In particular, I can't tell whether my causal shadow principle in (4) is just spatiotemporal discounting under a different name -- which (1) forbids -- or whether it's doing something categorically different, and whether it can actually deliver (3) given (2) and (5). Please rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging everything equally, say for each one what would have to give, and identify the single least costly revision to the set -- including what it costs. If some apparent tension dissolves on inspection, I'd like that shown explicitly rather than left as a worry. Finally, is my confidence in (6) consistent with whatever repair you recommend, or does the cheapest fix quietly concede that infinite ethics reshapes more of ordinary moral reasoning than I want to admit?
