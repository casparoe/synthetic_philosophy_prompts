Some background so you know where I'm coming from: I'm a retired biochemist, spent forty years doing enzymology, and only got into philosophy and social theory in the last few years. Having read a fair bit around it, I've come to hold the view that Weber was basically right in his protestant ethic argument -- or at least that the generalized version of the thesis is right: values held for non-economic reasons (a religiously motivated attitude toward disciplined work, saving, keeping your word in business) can be an independent cause of economic development, and aren't just a post-hoc rationalization of material conditions that were going to produce capitalism anyway. I find this plausible partly because it fits how I think about enzyme catalysis, where the structure does causal work you can't read off the energetics alone, but I know that analogy may be doing more work than it deserves.

What I want is a map of what would count against this position. In my old field we were trained to ask what experiment would kill your hypothesis, and I can't figure out what the equivalent is here, since we obviously can't rerun European history with the doctrines changed.

Some specific things I'd like sorted, roughly from fatal to merely awkward:

1. Timing problems. If capitalist practices show up before the Reformation in the same regions (northern Italy, the Low Countries), does that refute the thesis, or does a defender just say the ethic accelerated or consolidated something rather than originating it -- and if the latter, has the thesis been weakened into something trivially true?

2. The mediation problem. As I understand it, some economists argue the Protestant advantage runs almost entirely through mass literacy, because Protestants pushed Bible reading and that raised education levels generally. My question is whether this counts against Weber or for him. If the chain is religious motive -> behavior (teach everyone to read) -> growth, is that a Weberian mechanism with a different middle step, or does the whole "ethic" part drop out? I'd like an actual adjudication, not both-sidesing.

3. The portability test. It seems to me the cleanest evidence available is emigrants: if values do causal work, people who carry the values into completely different institutional settings should keep showing the relevant behaviors, and if the values wash out in a generation or two, that looks bad for the thesis. Is that reasoning sound, and what does that kind of evidence actually show?

4. The unfalsifiability worry. Defenders often say the religious ethic mattered at the origin and then became unnecessary, because the attitudes persist in secularized form after the theology drops away. Is "the cause can disappear after doing its work" a legitimate causal structure -- scaffolding that gets taken down -- or is it an evasion that lets the thesis survive any later evidence? As a scientist this move sets off alarm bells for me, but maybe historical causes genuinely can work that way?

Finally: what would committed defenders of the thesis themselves concede as counting against them, as opposed to what critics say? I gather even Weber said he wasn't offering a one-sided spiritual determinism -- does that concession protect the thesis or quietly gut it? And if it turns out the position is very well insulated, is that a defect for a causal claim about history, or just what you'd expect given the kind of evidence history leaves us?
