Exam question: "State Bell's theorem as precisely as you can, identifying the assumptions from which a Bell-type inequality is derived. Explain what the theorem, together with the relevant experiments, does and does not establish, with particular attention to: (i) the status of hidden-variable theories; (ii) Einstein's demand that there be no 'spooky action at a distance'; (iii) whether the result implies the possibility of superluminal signalling. [25 marks: 8 for the statement and assumptions, 6 for (i), 5 for (ii), 6 for (iii)]"

Student's answer:

"Bell's theorem arose from the debate opened by Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen, who argued that quantum mechanics must be incomplete, since it ascribes no definite properties to systems prior to measurement. Bell asked whether a completion by hidden variables could restore locality and determinism, and showed that it could not. Considering pairs of particles prepared in an entangled state and sent to distant experimenters who each choose a measurement setting, Bell derived an inequality bounding the correlations any local hidden-variable theory can produce. Quantum mechanics predicts violations of this inequality for certain settings, and experiments since those of Aspect in the early 1980s have confirmed the violations.

The derivation rests on two assumptions. The first is realism: that measurement outcomes reflect pre-existing properties of the particles, encoded in the hidden variables. The second is locality: that the outcome at one wing cannot depend on the setting chosen at the other. Since the hidden variables supply definite values, the argument as Bell gave it presupposes determinism; strictly speaking, therefore, the theorem rules out only deterministic local hidden-variable theories, and a genuinely stochastic local theory is untouched by the inequality.

On (i): the theorem and the experiments together rule out hidden-variable completions of quantum mechanics. Bohm's pilot-wave theory is the natural case in point: although it reproduces the statistical predictions of quantum mechanics, it is a hidden-variable theory and is therefore empirically excluded by the observed violations.

On (ii): Einstein demanded locality. Since the experiments force us to abandon one of the two assumptions and realism is the more dispensable, we may retain locality by giving up the idea that outcomes are determined in advance. Quantum mechanics itself is thereby shown to be a local, indeterministic theory.

On (iii): the correlations cannot be used to transmit signals faster than light, because each experimenter's own sequence of outcomes is statistically random regardless of the distant setting. There is therefore no conflict with special relativity, only a tension between the correlations and a naïve picture of independent local events."
