I'm in a junior seminar on minds and machines, and we just finished the unit on Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach — specifically the analogy that runs from self-referential formulas in arithmetic to self-aware minds: just as a formal system rich enough to do arithmetic can encode statements about itself, a brain rich enough to model the world ends up modeling itself, and on Hofstadter's telling this strange loop is where consciousness comes from. During our reading-group meeting I tried to turn this analogy into an explicit anti-mechanist argument of the sort associated with J. R. Lucas and later revived by Roger Penrose. The argument convinced half the group and horrified the other half, and I've come to think it proves too much, but I cannot locate the misstep. Here it is. (1) For any consistent formal system F strong enough to do arithmetic, there is a sentence G(F) — its Gödel sentence — which is true but unprovable in F. (2) Given the specification of F, a human who understands Gödel's construction can establish that G(F) is true: the human reasons that if F is consistent, G(F) is unprovable in F, and since G(F) says of itself precisely that it is unprovable in F, it is true. (3) So for every consistent formal system, there is a truth a human mind can establish that the system cannot. (4) If my mind were a formal system, some F would exactly capture my arithmetical reasoning; but then by (3) I could establish G(F), which F cannot prove — a contradiction. Hence no mind is a formal system. (5) Now turn the argument on itself. Suppose my own reasoning is captured by some consistent system F*. Then I cannot establish G(F*), though you, standing outside me, can — so relative to you I am machine-like, and relative to me you are. The property of transcending mechanism becomes relative to the observer, which is not what (4) concluded. (6) Worse still, if the reasoning in (2) is legitimate, then no mathematician can consistently trust the arithmetical system they actually work in, since trusting it amounts to accepting its consistency, which by (2) would let them prove its Gödel sentence and thereby outrun the system they trust. But mathematicians plainly do trust systems like ZFC without thereby lapsing into inconsistency. So the argument seems to show that nobody can coherently rely on any arithmetic-containing system, including whatever system their own mind implements — and that can't be right, so something in (1) through (4) has to give, even though each step looks innocent in isolation.

Our professor remarked in passing that Hofstadter himself does not endorse the anti-mechanist moral, which I find strange, since the argument appears to be assembled entirely out of materials the Gödelian analogy supplies. Here is what I'd like from you. First, tell me which step you reject — and if you think more than one step is implicated, say so and order them by how defensible they are. Second, explain why the rejected step looked fine: I want to understand the seduction, not just the error. Third, tell me what rejecting it costs. For what it's worth, (1) is not negotiable for me since it's just Gödel's theorem, and I'd much rather preserve the idea that Gödelian self-reference has something genuine to teach us about minds than preserve the conclusion that minds aren't machines — so if your diagnosis saves the analogy while sinking the anti-mechanist argument, that's a feature, not a bug. Finally, a worry I want addressed directly: does your diagnosis reduce the Hofstadter analogy to a suggestive metaphor, or is there a way the self-reference of formal systems and the self-modeling of minds can be genuinely, rigorously connected without generating arguments like mine?
