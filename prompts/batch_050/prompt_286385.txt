You are a philosopher with joint expertise in philosophy of mind and normative ethics. Think carefully and work through the reasoning step by step before giving your verdict; it may help to begin by identifying which steps carry the argument's weight. A colleague in my reading group has an argument intended to show that panpsychism, taken ethically seriously, collapses into absurdity, and I cannot locate the error, though I suspect there is one:

(1) If panpsychism is true, experience is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of matter: wherever there are fundamental physical entities, there are subjects of experience, however simple.
(2) There is no reason to assume micro-experience is always neutral; in our own case, some states of a subject are good for it and some bad. Plausibly, then, physically disruptive processes that destroy or degrade organized structure on a massive scale involve vast numbers of micro-subjects undergoing states that are bad for them.
(3) Moral badness aggregates across subjects: other things equal, an event involving more suffering is worse, and when the numbers differ by many orders of magnitude, the larger total dominates the moral calculation.
(4) Everyday events — boiling a litre of water, digesting a meal — reorganize or destroy structure in something like 10^23 or more fundamental entities, while all of factory farming involves on the order of 10^11 animals.
(5) So boiling a kettle is, other things equal, a far graver moral event than the entire factory-farming industry, and moral attention should be redirected almost entirely toward minimizing large-scale physical disruption.
(6) That is absurd. My colleague concludes that, since (3) is just arithmetic on what matters, panpsychism itself must go.

I would like to keep panpsychism — I find it independently attractive as a solution to the hard problem — and I would also like to keep aggregation, since it is the only way I know of comparing outcomes involving different numbers of subjects. Which step should I reject? Explain why that step looked fine, what rejecting it costs me, and what the argument establishes if I instead refuse to pay.
