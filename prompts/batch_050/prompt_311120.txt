I design and facilitate professional-development workshops for secondary-school history teachers and museum educators, and I keep running into the same problem: students, visitors, and occasionally public figures draw analogies between contemporary events and the Holocaust or Nazi Germany, and the adults in the room have no shared standard for how to respond. Some teachers shut down every comparison on principle; others let anything stand, including comparisons that are plainly trivializing. I want to turn the substance of the scholarly debates about the Holocaust's uniqueness and comparability -- the German historians' dispute of the 1980s over whether comparison normalizes and relativizes, and Bauman's argument in Modernity and the Holocaust that what distinguishes the Holocaust is not cruelty or scale alone but its specifically modern means, above all bureaucratic division of labor and rational, goal-directed planning -- into a decision procedure a teacher can actually run, in real time, when an analogy comes up in class.

Please write that procedure. It should take the form of ordered questions with, for each, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a determination that the comparison must be challenged or reframed, and where it hands the decision back to the teacher because the question is one of pedagogical judgment rather than analysis. Assume no philosophy background, a decision window of about two minutes in front of a live class, and the constraint that each step needs a one-sentence rationale the teacher could give a parent or administrator afterward.

The procedure must preserve, at minimum, these distinctions: comparison as an instrument of understanding versus comparison as normalization (placing the Holocaust in a series of modern atrocities so as to make it ordinary); structural comparison at the level of mechanisms and institutions versus comparison at the level of victims' experience or of outcomes; and the fact that a single analogy can be apt at one of these levels and grotesque at another. It must also remain usable by teachers on both sides of the uniqueness debate -- those who hold the Holocaust to be singular and those who worry that singularity claims make it pedagogically inert.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a student comparing a contemporary immigration-detention policy to the 1930s; a speaker calling industrial animal farming a Holocaust; and a structurally careful comparison deployed to argue that every nation has its atrocities, so guilt is relative. For at least one case, show the procedure delivering a verdict I will find uncomfortable, and close by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do -- including how it should treat comparisons made by survivors or their descendants, where I suspect its ordinary rules need suspension.
