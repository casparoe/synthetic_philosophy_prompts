My reading group got stuck on this last week and it's been bugging me. We were talking about Nomic (Suber's game where the moves are rule-changes) and constitutions whose amendment clauses can amend themselves, and someone produced this:

(1) In a genuinely self-amending system, the amendment procedure applies to every rule, including the rules that constitute the procedure itself.
(2) A change enacted by following whatever procedure is in force at the time of enactment is valid.
(3) So the procedure could validly be used to enact "this amendment procedure is hereby repealed" or "no further amendments shall be valid".
(4) Once the repeal passes, the procedure no longer exists — but a change can only be valid if enacted under an existing procedure, and there is no procedure under which the repeal stands, so the repeal is both valid (by (2) and (3)) and not valid.
(5) That's a contradiction, so genuine self-amendment is impossible, and every constitution with a self-applicable amendment clause is a latent incoherence just waiting for someone to pull the thread.

(5) strikes me as proving way too much — amendment clauses like Article V exist and the world hasn't exploded. I suspect something goes funny in (4) but I can't pin down whether it's a tense/indexical trick about when validity gets assessed or whether (2) needs qualifying. Which step should I reject, why does it look plausible, and what does rejecting it cost me? If I have to sacrifice something I'd rather give up (3) than (1), but tell me what I'm actually paying either way. Also, do cases where amendment clauses really have been used to entrench themselves or restrict future amendments (and the legal fights over whether that worked) change the diagnosis?
