On a linguistics blog I follow, the author defends generic statements about social groups -- sentences like "boys don't cry" or "women are nurturing" -- with an analogy to stereotypes. Her argument: a generic works exactly like a stereotype of the kind it concerns. A stereotype says what is prototypical or characteristic of a group, and everyone understands that it tolerates exceptions without being false. Likewise, she says, "ducks lay eggs" is true even though male ducks and ducklings lay nothing, because the sentence expresses the stereotype-like prototype of duckhood rather than a universal claim. On this basis she concludes that generic claims about people are harmless descriptions of typicality, and that objecting to them as misleading or pernicious is pedantry -- nobody is confused about the exceptions, just as nobody thinks every single duck lays eggs. A philosopher friend of mine replied that the stereotype analogy collapses on the very examples that motivate it: "mosquitoes carry malaria" is a perfectly good generic even though only a small minority of mosquitoes carry malaria, so whatever generics express, it cannot be typicality, and the social-group case inherits the problem.

Please audit the analogy. Lay out the correspondence explicitly -- what plays the role of the stereotype, the prototype, the tolerated exceptions, the person who "knows nobody is really confused" -- and then identify where the mapping breaks. In particular, assess whether the features that make stereotypes tolerable or intolerable (their statistical basis, their social effects, their resistance to counterexamples) actually transfer to generics, and whether the low-prevalence cases like the mosquito one are fatal to the analogy or can be absorbed by a more careful version of it. I want a committed verdict: does the blogger's conclusion -- that generics about social groups are as innocent as our grasp of their exceptions -- survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the stereotype analogy doing all the work? Give your position and your reasons, not a menu of options, and if the analogy fails, say what a better one would be.
