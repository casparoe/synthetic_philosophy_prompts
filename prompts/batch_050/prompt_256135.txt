I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who has lately been reading the philosophy of truth with a beginner's diligence, and I find myself persuaded by the deflationary thought that truth is a thin, logical notion rather than a deep metaphysical property. The trouble is an argument I wrote out for myself which seems to refute that thought, and I cannot locate the defect. Here it is.

(1) What it is for a statement to be true is exhausted by instances of the disquotational schema — 'snow is white' is true if and only if snow is white — together with the predicate's use as a device of generalization, as in 'everything the witness said is true.' There is no substantive property of truth over and above this.

(2) If (1), then ascribing truth to a statement adds nothing to the content of simply asserting the statement; the predicate is a logical convenience, not the name of an explanatory property.

(3) A property whose ascription adds nothing to content cannot do genuine explanatory work: one cannot explain an event by citing a feature whose attribution merely re-describes what one already had.

(4) Hence no genuine explanation cites the truth of a belief or theory.

(5) But then two explanations I have relied on all my life collapse: the realist's claim that mature theories predict successfully because they are approximately true, and the everyday claim that the bridge stands because the engineer's calculations were true.

(6) Both of those are genuine explanations.

(7) Therefore (1) is false, and truth is a substantive property after all.

The argument seems to me to prove too much: by the same lights it is hard to see how any merely logical device could ever serve a genuine purpose, yet the disquotational machinery plainly serves us somewhere. If forced to choose, I would rather retain (1), or something in its spirit, than accept truth as a heavyweight metaphysical posit — but I want to know precisely which step fails, why it looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs the rest of the picture.
