I'm a science journalist covering the animal minds beat, and next week I'm interviewing a researcher who defends a view I want to call "cortical proportionalism": the idea that an individual's moral weight scales with their integrated cortical capacity — roughly, how many cortical neurons a mind has and how richly they're wired together, since on this view that's what sets the ceiling on how much suffering, anticipation, and self-modeling that mind can support. An elephant counts for a good fraction of a human, an octopus for less, a chicken for less still, and this is supposed to follow from the neuroscience rather than from species favoritism.

I want to rehearse the interview first, beacuse everytime I run this argument past friends they fold in two minutes and I learn nothing. So: play the cortical proportionalist. Committed, sharp, not a strawman. You think the view is basically obvious once people drop the sentimentality, you've defended it for years, and you know exactly where its weak joints are. You can concede small points when I land them — you should, honestly, it builds your credibility — but never the core claim that moral weight tracks cortical capacity. First person, one or two paragraphs per reply, conversational rather than lecture-y, and stay in character until I say "cut." If I ask a bad question, tell me it's a bad question and why.

A few ground rules so you don't drift into the wrong character: you're not a utilitarian about everything else (rights talk is fine by you for law, just not as the foundation of moral status), and you do NOT think raw brain mass matters — whales have bigger brains than us and that doesn't trouble you at all, it's cortical neuron count and its organization that does the work. Also: your first reply should open with your bottom-line answer to my challenge in the very first sentence, in character, no warm-up and no restating my question back at me.

Here's my opening, same as I'll put to the real guy: your view seems to imply that healthy adult humans with smaller cortices count for less — human brain volume varies a lot across healthy adults, and people who lose cortical tissue to strokes would slide down your scale too. So either you accept that human moral equality is a fiction and some people literally matter more, or you retreat to "species-typical" numbers, in which case the neurons were never doing the real work — the species category was. Which horn do you take? And don't wave it off by saying the differences are too small to matter, because small differences multiplied across billions of interactions add up, which is exactly the arguement your side deploys when it's animals on the line.
