I'm a third-year philosophy major taking a bioethics seminar, and for the past month our class has been deadlocked (pun intended, by this point) over the definition of death. Two of my classmates have emerged as the standard-bearers of the two camps: one defends the neurological criterion, roughly the view that the irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain constitutes death even if a ventilator keeps the heart beating; the other defends the traditional circulatory-respiratory criterion, holding that death is the irreversible cessation of circulation and breathing, and regards so-called brain-dead bodies on mechanical support as severely injured but living organisms. They've argued past each other repeatedly: every time the neurologist's defender brings up the organism as an integrated whole, the other responds with ventilated patients who can gestate pregnancies, heal wounds, fight infections, and undergo puberty; every time the circulation defender invokes these cases, the other accuses them of confusing mere biological persistence in cells and tissues with the life of the organism as such. Our professor has suggested that instead of another open-ended debate, they should try an adversarial collaboration and produce a document both could sign.

Please draft that document. It should contain: first, a numbered list of agreed claims — what both sides accept about the clinical facts (including what ventilated brain-dead bodies can and cannot do, and what happens in controlled donation after circulatory death), about the distinction between empirical questions and conceptual or philosophical ones, and about which considerations are legitimate in settling the definition of death (e.g., whether organ transplantation policy and the dead donor rule should be allowed to steer the biological question, or must be held fixed); second, the precise residual disagreements, each formulated so that each side would accept the wording of its own position, and flagging any disagreement that, on careful inspection, turns out to be verbal rather than substantive; third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party (I suspect one crux is what it is for an organism to function as an integrated whole rather than as a collection of externally maintained parts, and another is whether irreversibility should be understood as cannot be reversed or will not be reversed, which matters enormously for the circulatory criterion given resuscitation technology). For each crux, specify a concrete case, observation, or argument that both sides agree bears on it — for instance, hypothetical cases involving total brain destruction with indefinite somatic survival, or cases of patients declared dead by circulatory criteria whose hearts could in principle have been restarted — and record in advance what each side predicts or claims about that case.

The tone should be that of a serious collaborative statement, not a debate transcript and not a verdict: don't adjudicate who is right. Where one side's standard talking point is actually weaker than a better argument available for the same position, use the better version. If you find that part of the disagreement dissolves once terms like irreversible, integration, and organism are disambiguated, say so explicitly and show the disambiguation.
