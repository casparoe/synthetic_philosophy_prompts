After a three-hour argument with a friend last night about Newcomb's problem, we agreed on almost nothing except that the debate had stopped being about the boxes. Every time I pressed her on why two-boxing is mandatory, she appealed to dominance; every time she pressed me, I appealed to the fact that one-boxers reliably walk away rich. We kept trading the same four or five cases and the same two slogans, and it became clear we had no shared standard for what a decision theory is even supposed to accomplish. So before I concede anything, I want the specification written out properly.

Please write the desiderata that any adequate decision theory must meet, where adequate means a theory you would actually want an agent to follow, not merely one that formalizes our intuitions after the fact. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and state explicitly whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a prejudice of one camp in the debate. The requirements should at least cover: the verdicts it must deliver in the core cases (standard Newcomb with a highly reliable predictor; versions where the predictor is only moderately reliable; medical cases like the smoking lesion, where the correlation runs through a common cause rather than through prediction; a prisoner's dilemma against a close psychological twin; and at least one case where your choice is merely evidence of something, without causing it); the distinctions it must respect (between prediction-based and common-cause correlation, between the good news an act constitutes and the good outcomes it produces, between acting rationally at a moment and being the kind of agent who does well over time); and the structural constraints it must obey (determinate verdicts, no appeals to what a rational agent would simply be disposed to do, and sensible behavior as the predictor's accuracy varies continuously). Where a requirement corresponds to a standard principle or argument in the literature, note it, but the motivating case matters more than the label.

The part I care most about is the consistency check: identify any pairs or subsets of the desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied — for example, unrestricted dominance together with the verdict that one-boxing maximizes expected payoff — and say which requirement you would relinquish and why, treating the list itself as the deliverable rather than a prelude to scoring. Only at the end, and briefly, indicate how standard causal decision theory, standard evidential decision theory, and any view that selects policies or dispositions rather than individual acts fare against the list. A table for the requirements is fine, but the reasoning behind each one should be in prose.
