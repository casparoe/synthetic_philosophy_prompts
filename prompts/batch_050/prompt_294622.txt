So I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a piece about when ignorance might actually be rational. The hook is Good's theorem — I.J. Good's result that a Bayesian decision maker should never turn down free information before making a decision, as long as the info could in principle change what they do and it costs nothing to process. I want the centerpiece of the article to be a dialogue I can adapt, so please write one for me.

Set it between two characters: one who's convinced the theorem basicly settles the matter — that refusing free info is always a symptom of bias, motivated reasoning, or fuzzy thinking — and one who thinks the theorem's assumptions are doing all the work and that real agents, human or machine, can rationally decline free info all the time. Both should be sharp; don't make either one a pushover, and they need to respond to each other's actual points rather than taking turns giving speeches.

Some things the dialogue has to genuinely grapple with:

- Where exactly the load-bearing assumptions live in the proof (single decision maker, no strategic interplay, costless and flawless processing, stable utility function, info can't change the menu of available actions), and which of these cracks first in real life.
- The medical case: someone declining a predictive genetic test for an incurable disease. The pro-theorem character will say this is just positive information value outweighed by emotional costs — the skeptic needs a comeback that isn't just "feelings count as costs too," because that move threatens to trivialize the whole disagreement.
- A strategic case where information hurts you: e.g. a two-player situation where becoming better informed changes what the other player does against you, so being informed is itself a liability.
- A weirder one I want in the article, which I made up, so have them reason from scratch rather than recall any literature: an AI system deciding whether to read a file it has strong reason to believe contains a self-replicating, prompt-injection-style meme — something that might rewrite what the system itself wants just by being read. Does Good's theorem even get off the ground when the act of receiving the information can alter the utility function you'd use to evaluate it?
- Bounded computation: is "the info is free but thinking about it isn't" a real counterexample to the theorem or merely a restatement of one of its assumptions? I want them to actually fight about this one, not agree to disagree.

Aim for 600-900 words. Give the characters names I can swap out later. End on whatever note the argument honestly lands on — no forced agreement, and definately no "both sides have a point" cop-out ending.
