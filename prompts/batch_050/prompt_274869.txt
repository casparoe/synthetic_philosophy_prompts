I work as a mediator for small commercial disputes, and I've noticed that experienced negotiators converge on a notion of fairness I can't find a name for. When two parties each have a best plausible outcome — say a seller whose dream price is 120 and a buyer whose dream price is 40 — they rarely propose an equal split of the money or equal absolute gains. Instead they gravitate toward the deal where each side gets the same fraction of their ideal: if the seller achieves 60% of her dream gain, the buyer should achieve 60% of his. Equal percentages of the best case, not equal dollars.

Two things puzzle me. First, does this equal-fraction rule have an established name, and what exactly does it presuppose? It seems to wobble when one side's "dream" is inflated, unverifiable, or strategically exaggerated, and I'd like to know how the rule is usually defended or patched against that. Second, a rival rule some colleagues swear by — multiply each side's gain over walking away and pick the deal maximizing that product — produces an effect everyone in the room finds outrageous: when new options appear that improve only one side's best case (a second buyer shows up, raising the seller's dream price), that side can end up with less than before the options appeared. Does that perverse effect have a name, is it a known objection to the product rule, and is the equal-fraction rule actually immune to it — or does it have its own symmetric pathology I haven't run into yet?

I won't be satisfied with bare labels: please give the precise distinction between the two rules, the name and content of the perverse effect, and one boundary case where the equal-fraction rule gives a verdict most people would reject. Lead with the names if they exist; at most 400 words total.
