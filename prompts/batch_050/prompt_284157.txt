I'm on my university's animal research ethics committee (the body that approves or rejects lab protocols involving animals). Our charter says we oversee "all vertebrates and cephalopods" -- that wording was copied from somewhere years ago and nobody thought hard about it. Now it's blown up in our faces.

There's an entomology lab here that studies learning in fruit flies. One of their paradigms is basically a learned helplessness setup: flies get repeated inescapable electric shocks, and the lab measures how long it takes them to stop trying to escape. Another line of work involves gluing flies to tiny mounts for days so they can image brain activity while delivering heat pulses. They go through a LOT of flies -- tens of thousands a year, maybe more. Under our charter none of this needs our approval, and historically we haven't touched it.

A new committee member (a postdoc rep) has demanded we take it up. Her argument, roughly: recent-ish work on insect nociception, motivational trade-offs, and flexible behavior means fly sentience is a live possibility rather than science fiction; even a small probability of suffering, multiplied by the sheer number of animals, means we can't just shrug; and "the charter says vertebrates" is an accident of drafting, not a principled line. The lab's PI countered hard: no one has a test for sentience in flies, the evidence she cites is equally consistent with sophisticated reflex, and if a 2% credence in sentience is enough to trigger oversight, then logically we'd have to regulate crop spraying, the campus pest contract, and undergrads swatting mosquitos, at which point the committee becomes a laughing stock and loses the authority it needs to protect the animals that clearly matter. A third member floated a middle option: keep flies formally exempt but require the lab to follow some cheap practices anyway (minimizing sample sizes, using CO2 before disposal, shorter immobilization stints) as a "precautionary tier."

The chair has asked me to draft the written opinion we'll vote on at next month's meeting. It needs to recommend ONE concrete position -- full oversight, continued exemption, or the precautionary tier (or a better fourth option if you have one) -- with reasoning the losing side could actually respond to, and it has to say what kinds of evidence the committee should even treat as relevant to sentience in an organism this distant from us, since we can't just wait for science to settle it.

Please act as a moral philosopher who specializes in animal minds and has actually sat on commitees like this, so you know the difference between a clean argument and a workable policy. Think carefully about this before drafting -- maybe brainstorm the strongest case for each of the three options first, and pressure-test the "small probability x huge numbers" argument especially, since it's doing a lot of work here. Then write the opinion itself, ~600-800 words, ending with a clear motion we can vote on.
