I spend too much time in a couple of decision-theory-adjacent corners of the internet, and there's a move people make there that's started to bug me. Whenever someone raises a worry about tiny probabilities of astronomical payoffs -- Pascal's mugging type cases, fanaticism in population ethics, that whole cluster -- somebody drops the line "just maximize expected value" (or its ruder cousin, "shut up and multiply") and treats the conversation as over. I nodded along for years. Then I actually sat down with the Pasadena paradox and now I genuinely don't know what the slogan is even claiming.

Quick recap of the game so we're on the same page, correct me if I botch it: a fair coin gets flipped until it first lands heads; if that happens on flip n, your payoff is (-1)^(n+1) times 2^n divided by n dollars, where negative amounts are money you owe. The expectation series is 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which converges only conditionally, so by the Riemann rearrangement result you can reorder it to "sum" to literally anything, and the game's expected value just doesn't exist. Not infinite, like St. Petersburg -- undefined.

Here's what I want from you. Take the slogan "maximize expected value" and do a proper teardown. Seperate out the distinct precise theses it might be expressing: maybe it's a claim about what rationality requires in cases where expectations exist and are finite; maybe it quietly presupposes that expectations always exist; maybe it's a criterion of rightness rather than a procedure you're supposed to actually run in your head; maybe it's really a long-run claim about doing better on average over repeated plays; maybe it covertly slides between expected money and expected utility. For each reading, give me a verdict: platitude, genuinely contentious, or flat-out false. Then the part I care most about: which reading is doing the work when someone deploys the slogan to shut down a Pascal's-mugging worry, and does the Pasadena case damage that specific reading, or only some of its stronger or weaker neighbors? My current hunch is the paradox mostly threatens the slogan as a universal conversation-stopper while leaving a restricted comparative version standing, but I keep flip-flopping on whether the restricted version is strong enough to justify the mugging dismissal.
