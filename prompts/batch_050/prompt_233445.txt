Write an essay on a problem in conceptual engineering that I'll call the veto problem. Quick background so we're on the same page: conceptual engineers (think Haslanger-style ameliorative analysis) say that when a concept is defective -- vague where it should be sharp, or doing oppressive work, or just scientifically useless -- we shouldn't just analyze how the concept is actually used, we should propose a better successor concept and try to get it adopted. Fine. But here's the wrinkle I want the essay to engage with: most of the interesting cases involve concepts that belong to a community of speakers, not to the philosophers doing the engineering. So suppose a community uses some concept C in a way that philosophers (or scientists, or activists) judge defective, and suppose the engineers propose a replacement C*. And suppose the community, after hearing the proposal explained perfectly clearly, just says: no thanks, we like our concept. Who has the authority here?

To keep it concrete, feel free to invent your own example (a legal term, a folk biological kind, an everyday social category, whatever) but it has to be a case where the engineers' objections are *genuinely* reasonable, not a case where they're obviously wrong busybodies. Otherwise the essay is too easy.

Some sub-questions you should probably engage: Does the answer change if the defective concept harms outsiders rather than the speakers themselves? Is there a difference between a community rejecting an engineered concept because they misunderstand it, versus rejecting it because they correctly understand it and value the very feature the engineers want to remove? And does the "no thanks" verdict hold if the community's own usage is already fractured -- i.e., there's no single concept C to defend, just a mess of overlapping uses, and the engineers are really just picking one strand and polishing it?

The essay should weigh the main positions on both sides -- roughly, "concepts are collectively owned, so engineering without uptake is just a philosopher's daydream" versus "concepts are tools, and tools can be improved by anyone with a good design, uptake be damned" -- but don't end on a wishy-washy both-sides note. Reach an actual verdict about who has the authority in the veto case (or under what conditions each side wins) and justify it. Somthing like "the engineers win when the harm falls on non-consenting third parties, the community wins otherwise" is allowed, if you can defend it.
