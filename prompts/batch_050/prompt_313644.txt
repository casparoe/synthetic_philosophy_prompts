So my reading group (a mix of econ PhD students and a couple of stray philosophers) has been working through Keynes's Treatise on Probability alongside chapter 12 of the General Theory, and we've run into a wall that I keep hitting in this whole literature. Roughly, one camp — call them the fundamentalists — holds that Keynesian uncertainty is a categorically different thing from risk: for some future events there simply is no probability, numerical or otherwise, that a rational person could assign, probability theory just doesn't get a grip, and long-term expectations instead rest on convention, confidence, animal spirits, all that. The other camp — the Bayesians — insists this dissolves on inspection: Savage-style, any agent whose choices satisfy some minimal coherence conditions behaves as if they had subjective probabilities, so "uncertainty" is at most vague priors, unstable priors, low confidence, maybe second-order probability — nothing that escapes the calculus in principle. As far as I can tell both sides have been saying exactly this, in one vocabulary or another, since Ramsey first took aim at the Treatise, and nobody has moved an inch.

My working suspicion, which I'd like stress-tested rather than confirmed: the two camps are answering different questions under one label. The fundamentalists are doing descriptive psychology of how investors and entrepreneurs actually form expectations (and why those expectations are so fragile), while the Bayesians are doing normative theory of what coherence demands — so "can you assign a probability here" means "do people, stably, in fact" for one side and "must a minimally coherent agent, even only implicitly" for the other, and every argument sails past its target. But maybe I'm wrong and there is a real disagreement underneath — say, about whether Keynes's notion of the weight of argument is a genuine second dimension of evidence that probability can't absorb, or whether partially ordered, non-numerical probability comparisons (which Keynes clearly countenances in the Treatise) are even coherent.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Give me a diagnosis — verbal, substantive, cross-purposes, or a real open question dressed up as a terminological one — that engages with what the two sides actually claim rather than the textbook cartoon versions. And given your diagnosis, what would move it: a formal result, experimental work on choice under ambiguity in the Ellsberg mold, a distinction everyone should adopt, or is this one of those debates that can't be ended and I should just accept that? Also, please be careful to mark what's basically settled among people who actually work on this (what the Treatise says vs. what the General Theory says, what Ramsey's objection was, how far Keynes conceded to it) versus what's still genuinely contested — don't present one camp's reading of Keynes as the received view, becuase from what I can tell there isn't one.
