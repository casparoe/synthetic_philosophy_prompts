I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in metaphysics — fourteen students, one 75-minute session — and we are about to spend two weeks on dispositions. The target material is the failure of the simple conditional analysis: the claim that an object has a disposition just in case it would produce the manifestation if it received the stimulus (the glass is fragile iff it would shatter if struck). I want students to encounter, in their own reasoning, the three classic troublemakers: finkish cases, where the disposition vanishes the instant the stimulus arrives; masking cases, where the disposition remains but its manifestation is blocked; and mimic cases, where the manifestation appears in an object that never had the disposition at all. I do not want to lecture any of this. I want them to discover that "would manifest if stimulated" is neither necessary nor sufficient for possessing the disposition, by having them rely on that biconditional and lose something by it.

Design an in-class game that does this. The basic conceit I have in mind is a certification lab: teams of students act as inspectors evaluating a shipment of enchanted objects, each with a stated disposition and a stated stimulus, and the group stakes points on two distinct questions per object — will it manifest when tested, and does it have the disposition we must certify on the manifesto. The point of the game is that these two questions come apart, and I need the rules to force them apart rather than merely assert that they do. Hidden modifier cards, operated either by me as facilitator or by a designated saboteur role, would represent finks (the disposition is removed at the moment of testing), masks (the manifestation is suppressed though the disposition persists), and mimics (a counterfeit manifestation is produced without the disposition).

Please specify:

1. Setup and materials — the full card inventory, what information is public versus hidden, and exactly how a test is resolved so that the outcome is legible to everyone.
2. Rules and a scoring scheme that uses no real money but makes certification errors cost more than prediction errors, since certification is a claim about the object and prediction is only a claim about the episode.
3. A round-by-round timeline across the 75 minutes. I expect a baseline round with no modifiers in play, so that the conditional inference earns the group's trust, followed by rounds in which finks, masks, and mimics are secretly active, and a reveal phase. Tell me how many rounds and how many objects per round this group size can actually sustain.
4. A solution to what I take to be the central design problem: how to make the difference between losing the disposition at the moment of testing (fink) and retaining it while its manifestation is blocked (mask) visible in the game state rather than something I narrate afterwards. The entire philosophical weight sits on that distinction, and if the players experience both cases as simply "it didn't shatter," the activity has failed.
5. A debrief plan that maps each round onto the philosophical moves — why the conditional analysis fails in both directions, the natural repairs (hedging the antecedent to exclude finks and masks; relocating dispositions in intrinsic causal bases) and why those repairs threaten circularity or regress — and that is structured so the students generate the objections themselves before I name anything. The vocabulary of finks, masks, and mimics should be introduced only in the debrief; no prior reading should be assumed.
6. An honest forecast of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — for instance, teams collapsing certification into outcome prediction, a saboteur deploying modifiers so often that inference becomes impossible, or the scoring rewarding lucky guesses over sound reasoning — with a concrete fix for each.
7. A frank note on which features of the metaphysics the game cannot reproduce and that I must supply in discussion. I suspect these include the intrinsicality of the disposition's causal basis, the instantaneous timing of finkish removal, and the sense in which a masked object is still poised to manifest; tell me whether that list is right and how to compensate for each gap in the debrief.
