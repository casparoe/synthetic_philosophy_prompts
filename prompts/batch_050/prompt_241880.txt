Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend about pain asymbolia -- those patients who, after damage to the insular cortex or related structures, report that they still feel pain but simply don't mind it. She thinks these cases show that pain is never intrinsically bad, only contingently disliked. I resisted, and in the course of resisting I committed myself, perhaps carelessly, to the following positions. Before I write any of this up, I want to know whether my own set of commitments is coherent, because the argument made me suspect I was agreeing to things in passing that don't sit well together.

Here is what I currently hold:

(a) The badness of pain is intrinsic to the experience itself. Pain is bad because of how it feels, not merely because we desire to be rid of it, and not merely because of the avoidance behavior it produces. Pure desire-based accounts of pain's badness get something fundamentally wrong.

(b) Pain asymbolia is genuine in the strong sense: these patients really do undergo experiences with a recognizably painful sensory character -- they can locate, describe, and grade their pains -- while being wholly indifferent to them. They are not simply failing to feel pain, nor merely reporting it oddly.

(c) Sensory intensity and unpleasantness are dissociable dimensions of pain experience. The morphine cases support this: patients under morphine often say the pain is just as intense but no longer bothers them.

(d) The asymbolia patient is nonetheless harmed by her condition, and not merely instrumentally. Even setting aside the unnoticed burns and untreated injuries that follow from indifference, she is worse off than she would be with ordinary, aversive pain. Her indifference is a deprivation, not a lucky exemption from suffering.

(e) Contemplative acceptance can make pain less bad without reducing its intensity, which is why mindfulness-based interventions genuinely help chronic pain patients rather than merely teaching them to report differently.

(f) On well-being, I lean hedonist: what ultimately matters for how well a life goes is the character of the experiences in it, and bodily damage matters insofar as it affects experience.

Which of these actually conflict, and which only appear to? I suspect at least one pair of my commitments is motivated in ways that can't both be right, but I can't see where. Please rank the tensions you find by severity, and for each one state what revision would restore consistency and what that revision would cost me -- in particular, which of my answers to my friend I'd have to concede. If some apparent tension dissolves on closer inspection, say so and show why, rather than treating the whole set as suspect.
