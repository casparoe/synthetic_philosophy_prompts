I listen to this philosophy podcast and last week the host tried to explain what he called the standard picture of what a musical work even is, the "type-token" view. His summary went roughly like this: Beethoven's Fifth Symphony is not any physical thing you can point at, not the manuscript, not any concert -- it's an abstract type, and every individual performance is a token of that type, the same way each printed copy of a novel is a token of the novel's type. Because the work is abstract, he said, it exists eternally and necessarily, so Beethoven didn't actually create the Fifth, he just discovered a sound-structure that was already sitting there in Platonic heaven waiting to be found -- which supposedly shows the whole view is mysticism dressed up as analysis. He also said a token only counts as a performance of the work if it conforms to the score, so once you've got a note-perfect studio recording, that recording is basically the work made audible, and live concerts are just imperfect attempts at approximating it. Finally, since jazz improvisations and heavily studio-edited pop tracks don't correspond to any score, he concluded they aren't musical works at all on this view, which he took as proof the theory is really only about classical music and should be junked.

Something in all this smells off to me but I cant pin down exactly where. Which parts of his summary would a defender of the type-token view actually accept, and which parts distort it or smuggle in commitments the view doesn't have? Are his two refutations (composers as mere discoverers, and the theory only fitting scored classical music) any good once the view is stated carefully? And is he even right that a recording is the best or most "authentic" token of a work?
