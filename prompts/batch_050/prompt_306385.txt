So in my AI safety & governance elective we just did the unit on "control" approaches, i.e. safety measures that are supposed to work even if the model is secretly misaligned — stuff like having a smaller trusted model monitor the big model's outputs, sandboxing, least-privilege tool access, and leaving fake "opportunities to defect" lying around to see if the model bites. My prof's whole framing for the unit rests on one analogy: he says deploying a frontier model is like hiring a brilliant employee who you have specific reasons not to trust. You don't refuse to hire them, you just never let them work unmonitored, have trusted colleagues double-check everything, limit what systems they can touch, and occasionally dangle bait. His conclusion is that this shows control can substitue for alignment, at least for a while, because companies and banks have run for decades on useful-but-untrusted insiders and mostly don't get destroyed.

I have to write a short paper on this and I'm suspicious the analogy is smuggling in all the load-bearing parts. Can you lay out the mapping explicitly (what's the embezzlement, who's HR, what's the exit interview), and then tell me where it breaks? Specifically I keep coming back to: the employee has one body, a career and a life they care about, roughly human speed, and motives you can sometimes read — are those exactly the features the analogy quietly assumes the model has? Does the prof's conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if not, is there a better analogy I could offer instead?
