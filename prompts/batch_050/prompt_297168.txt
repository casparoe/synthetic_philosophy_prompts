Exam question: Mises and Hayek are often treated as offering a single "calculation" objection to socialism. State each thinker's core argument in your own words, explain how the two arguments differ, and assess whether Lange's market-socialist proposal answers both, one, or neither of them.

Student answer: Mises's argument is that economic calculation requires money prices for the means of production, and such prices can only arise from the exchange of privately owned capital goods. Under socialism the state owns everything, so there are no markets for capital goods, no prices for them, and therefore no way to compare the costs of alternative production plans. Socialism is not merely inefficient on this view; rational allocation of resources is impossible.

Hayek's argument is essentially the same point restated: without prices there is no way to do the arithmetic of production. He added that the price system also aggregates information, but this is a secondary extension of Mises rather than a distinct objection.

Lange's response is that market socialism can reproduce prices: a Central Planning Board announces prices and adjusts them by trial and error, raising them where shortages appear and lowering them where surpluses appear, while managers of state firms are instructed to produce up to the point where price equals marginal cost. Because this procedure supplies planners with everything needed for calculation, it answers Mises. It answers Hayek as well, since the planners can in principle collect the relevant statistics centrally, and modern computing power only strengthens that point: the "calculation problem" was really a claim about computational tractability, which computers have now resolved.

Critics objected that Lange's managers lack the incentives of real entrepreneurs, but incentives are a separate issue from calculation, so the core of the socialist project survives.
