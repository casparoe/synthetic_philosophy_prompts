I'm giving a department colloquium next month defending a version of the contour theory of musical expressiveness: roughly, that a passage of instrumental music is sad in virtue of structurally resembling the vocal, gestural, and gait patterns of sad people, and that we hear the sadness in the music the way we see a face in a cloud — perceptually, not by inference, and without anyone having to be sad. The audience will be mostly analytic philosophers of art who cut their teeth on the Kivy–Davies–Levinson debates, plus a few empirical music psychologists and one committed formalist. I am not defending the rival arousal theory, so don't prep me for its objections. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the seven or eight objections this particular audience will actually raise — as distinct from what a general philosophy audience would ask — ordered by likelihood and damage, each phrased the way that person in the room would phrase it (including the psychologists' empirical pushback and the formalist's opening move), each with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is weakest. Flag which objections I should concede partially rather than fight, and end with the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
