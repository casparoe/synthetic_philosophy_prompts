I got into it with a coworker last week about whether our company should keep its big contract with a defense contractor. I was laying out what I thought were decent arguments and he basically waved them off with "look, you're on the wrong side of history on this one." I've heard the same move from the other direction too -- people saying they're on the right side of history about AI, about diet, about basically everything. And it bugged me because I realized I have no idea what that phrase is even supposed to *mean*, but it sure is effective at ending conversations.

Can you break down the different precise claims someone could be making when they say you're on the wrong side of history? Like, sometimes it seems to be a straight prediction: this outcome is coming whether you like it or not. Sometimes it seems to be a claim about moral convergence, like future people will all agree with me. Sometimes it sounds almost like a threat about how you'll be remembered, and sometimes I suspect it's just a fancy way of saying "I'm confident I'm right" with a borrowed air of inevitability.

For each reading, tell me: is it a platitude, is it a substantive claim that's actually defensible, or is it probably false? And then the part I really care about: which of those readings, if any, would actually justify using the phrase to *dismiss an argument* rather than respond to one? Because it seems like there's a dilemma hiding in there -- if the outcome really is inevitable, my arguing against it doesn't matter and neither does his campaigning for it, and if it's not inevitable, then the phrase is just assertion dressed up as prophecy. But maybe I'm missing a reading where the slogan does real work. Lay them out as a menu I can keep in my back pocket for the next time this comes up at lunch.
