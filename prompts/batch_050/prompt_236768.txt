So here's my position, and I want it stress-tested. Background: longtime LW/SSC reader, and every time Zeno comes up in the comments somebody drops the standard line and the thread dies. The standard line is the one I hold: Zeno's paradoxes of motion are *dissolved*, not merely answered, by the modern continuum. Once you've got the real numbers, limits, and the at-at theory of motion (the arrow moves by being at successive points at successive instants, full stop, nothing further required of an instant), there's no residual puzzle left for philosophy. The paradoxes were a symptom of trying to reason about infinity with finite intuitions, the math fixed the concept, end of story.

But I recently went back to the actual primary source and noticed something that bugs me. In Physics Book VI, Part 1, Aristotle writes:

"Nor, again, can a point be in succession to a point or a moment to a moment in such a way that length can be composed of points or time of moments: for things are in succession if there is nothing of their own kind intermediate between them, whereas that which is intermediate between points is always a line and that which is intermediate between moments is always a period of time."

The thing that bugs me: this looks like Aristotle *already agreeing* with the modern move (time is dense, no next instant, so no instant-by-instant motion) -- while in the same breath denying that a line is composed of points at all, which modern math flatly asserts in the set-theoretic sense. So my position seems to be half Aristotle, half exactly what Aristotle argues against. Which raises the question of whether the standard resolution is actually one coherent position or a patch that survives by being vague about which half it's committed to.

So: what would count against my position? I'm not asking for the usual freshman philosophy recap of the paradoxes themselves. I want:

1. A careful reading of the passage first. What exactly is Aristotle's argument here, does it go through on its own terms, and does the modern treatment of the continuum vindicate it, refute it, or just change the subject?

2. The defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Candidate categories I'd want covered, but don't limit yourself to these: (a) anything in the Arrow specifically that the at-at theory allegedly fails to capture -- like whether an instantaneous state of a moving and a resting arrow are genuinely indistinguishable, and whether that's a problem or a feature; (b) supertask-style cases as evidence the continuum model licenses descriptions with no physical correlate; (c) the possibility that spacetime is actually discrete at some scale, and whether my position secretly depends on the continuum being *physically real* rather than just a good model; (d) rival formalizations (infinitesimals, other non-Cantorian setups) and whether their mere consistency weakens the "dissolved" claim; (e) the charge that the standard answer redescribes motion in Zenonian terms rather than explaining it.

3. For each defeater: would a committed defender of the standard resolution concede it counts, or bat it away, and is the batting-away legitimate?

4. An honest verdict on whether my position has been insulated into unfalsifiability -- i.e., does "the math handles it" function the way motte-and-bailey defenses do, where any residual puzzle gets reclassified as not-the-real-paradox?

No citations or reading list needed, I'll track down sources myself -- just name arguments and positions clearly enough that I can find them.
