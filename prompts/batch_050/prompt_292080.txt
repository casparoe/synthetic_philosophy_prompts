Here are my considered reactions to four cases involving higher-order evidence, and I want to know what single principle, if any, lies behind them.

(1) I compute a restaurant bill split by long division and get $43; my friend, equally careful and equally good at arithmetic, gets $45. I should substantially reduce my confidence in $43 -- roughly suspend judgment between the two answers until someone rechecks.

(2) I have just taken a drug that I know, from solid clinical evidence, reliably distorts probabilistic reasoning while leaving the feeling of clarity intact. I should distrust the conclusions I reach while on it, even the ones that feel airtight.

(3) I produce what seems to me a watertight proof of a theorem, then learn that nine of ten expert logicians who have reviewed it judge it invalid, though none has yet pointed to the flaw. I should stop believing the theorem, even though I cannot see where the proof goes wrong.

(4) I believe a politically charged empirical claim on what I know is strong first-order evidence. I also have excellent higher-order evidence that people with my background are systematically biased on exactly this topic. Yet when I re-examine the first-order evidence, it still clearly points to the claim. Here my verdict flips: I should keep believing it. Withholding belief merely because of the general bias statistic, when the specific evidence before me is manifestly strong, seems to require the irrational state of judging that the evidence supports p while refusing to believe p.

Is there one principle about how rational agents should respond to higher-order evidence that delivers all four verdicts -- for instance, some principled line between treating higher-order evidence as simply more evidence of the ordinary sort and allowing a genuine split between what the first-order evidence supports and what one should believe? If no principle accommodates all four, say which verdict should be overturned and why. Then state what your favored principle predicts for a case I have not listed: a weather forecaster learns that her model has been miscalibrated in her favor for months, but on this particular morning she has double-checked every input and can find no error. Finally, tell me which of my verdicts your account is least comfortable with, and what consideration or argument would lead you to abandon the account altogether rather than defend it.
