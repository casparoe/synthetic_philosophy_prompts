I am the commissioning editor for history at an independent press. We publish narrative history for general readers and for first-year university survey courses, roughly a dozen titles a year, and each manuscript goes to a single external reviewer — a working historian, but usually a generalist rather than a specialist in the book's period — who has about three weeks and a fixed fee per report. Twice in the past three years we have had books savaged in review as, after Butterfield, 'Whig history', and in both cases the label stuck and cost us course adoptions. At the same time I am wary of overcorrecting: the last reviewer who took the injunction against presentism too seriously complained that a book about the repeal of the corn laws never established its relevance to the present, which is not a standard a trade history list can live by. My reviewers need something between those two failure modes, and at the moment they have nothing but their own instincts.

Please design a diagnostic procedure a reviewer can run on a manuscript to detect and grade Whiggishness. The procedure has to distinguish between things I take to be unavoidable or even desirable — selecting topics because they matter to us now, writing so that present-day readers can follow the stakes — and the genuine vices: teleology, where the past is arranged as a series of steps towards us and actors are valued only as precursors or forerunners of what came later; anachronistic moral judgement, where people are condemned for failing to hold values that were not available to them; and the tendency to write the story from the winners' side, so that the losing positions never appear as live options. I want an ordered set of tests a generalist can actually apply in a few hours — for instance something like a counterfactual-terminus test, asking whether the same material could have been narrated towards a different present than ours — with, for each test, what counts as a pass, what counts as a fail, and what counts as inconclusive. Mark explicitly the points where the procedure must stop and hand the decision back to me because the question is one of editorial values rather than of fact about the manuscript, and state plainly what the procedure cannot settle at all.

Then run the procedure on three titles currently under consideration, summarised as a reviewer would encounter them. First, a history of vaccination from variolation to mRNA vaccines, framed as reason's long victory over fear, in which Jenner's critics figure mainly as obstacles; note that smallpox really was eradicated, so the 'progress' here is not merely projected onto the past, and I want to know whether the procedure convicts the book anyway. Second, a history of abolition in the British Empire told largely through parliamentarians, with the resistance of enslaved people themselves confined to a single chapter described as pressure from below. Third, a manuscript I personally championed: a history of religious toleration from the Reformation to the present whose closing chapter argues the story is unfinished but moving in the right direction. Flag in advance that this third book is the one I most want the procedure to pass, and if it fails, say so directly and tell me which clause convicts it. Finish by telling me which of my two past failures — the books that got savaged, and the overcorrecting reviewer — this procedure would have prevented, and which it would not.
