So background: I'm a retired condensed matter physicist, spent 35 years thinking about measurement, reference frames, emergent properties, that kind of thing. Since retiring I've fallen in with a philosophy reading group at the local library (long story) and we just spent three sessions on Jain philosophy, specifically anekāntavāda -- the many-sidedness doctrine -- and the syādvāda method of conditional predication, the sevenfold scheme where every statement gets qualified as something like: in some respect it is, in some respect it is not, in some respect it both is and is not, in some respect it's indescribable, and so on. The group keeps comparing it to the blind men and the elephant parable and leaving it there, which I find unsatisfying.

Here's what I actually want. Take the anekāntavāda/syādvāda position -- that reality is too multifaceted for any single absolute description, that apparently contradictory claims about a thing can each capture a genuine partial truth, and that assertions only work when relativised to a standpoint (a naya) -- and restate it, as rigorously as you can, in the vocabulary I actually think in: measurement outcomes, frames of reference, coarse-graining, complementarity in the quantum sense, model-dependence, the map/territory business. I'm fluent in all of that, don't water it down.

Then, and this is the part I care most about, give me a residue report. Which parts of the Jain position translate cleanly into measurement/frame talk (I suspect the standpoint-relativity does), which parts you had to distort or quietly drop to make the translation work, and which parts have no physical counterpart at all -- for instance, does anything in physics do the work of the "indescribable" predicates, or the claim that the partial views are each *true* rather than just each *useful*? And does the doctrine's ethical/soteriological payload (non-absolutism as a route to non-attachment) survive translation or evaporate?

Finally, a verdict: is the residue a defect of my physicist's vocabulary -- something real that science-talk can't say -- or is it a sign that the Jain position, stripped of its religious context, collapses into something a decent experimentalist already believes? Defend your answer, don't just split the diference.
