I am a novelist, about fifteen years into the trade, and the vocabulary I actually think in is craft vocabulary: point of view, psychic distance, free indirect style, scene versus summary, the ensemble cast, subtext, the unreliable narrator, the choral first-person plural. My grasp of academic philosophy is that of a serious amateur — I can follow a seminar handout but could not defend a position in one. I am drafting a novel about a string quartet whose members must decide, over the course of one season, whether to stay together, and I keep running into a problem I gather philosophers have their own literature on: what is it for several people to intend something together, as opposed to each privately intending something in parallel? The difference between four musicians who each happen to keep showing up and a quartet that is playing together is exactly the difference my book is about, and I would like the philosophers' help — but only after their debate has been translated into terms I can actually work with.

Please restate the central positions on collective intentionality and joint action in the vocabulary of my craft, and then give me a residue report on what does not survive the translation. For the translation, take the following positions one by one: first, the reductive view associated with Michael Bratman, on which shared intention is a matter of interlocking individual intentions, held with mutual knowledge, each person intending that the others do their part; second, the view associated with Margaret Gilbert, on which joint commitment constitutes a plural subject and generates genuine obligations between participants, including a standing to rebuke one another for defecting; third, the view associated with John Searle, on which we-intention is a primitive, non-reducible form of intentionality, not built out of I-intentions at all. For each position, tell me: which parts map cleanly onto craft concepts, which parts you had to distort to fit, and which parts have no expression at all in the vocabulary of fiction-writing. Some specific translations I would like you to attempt, and then grade for honesty:

First, does Bratman's reduction map onto a scene written in alternating close-third points of view — each character's interiority rendered separately, the reader left to assemble the 'we' out of four interlocking interiors? If a novelist can produce the full phenomenology of shared intention that way, is Bratman right? Or does the translation quietly smuggle in the reader as the place where the 'we' lives, which is not what his theory says?

Second, is first-person plural narration — the choral 'we' voice, as in novels narrated by a town, a family, a group — a genuine literary model of the plural subject or the primitive we-intention, or is it only a stylistic gesture that performs collectivity without constituting it? What would distinguish a 'we' narrator who is a plural subject from one who is merely a device, and could a reader tell the difference from the prose alone?

Third, where does obligation go? Gilbert's account turns on normative structure: participants owe each other performance and may call each other to account. But fiction renders commitment through gesture, dialogue, and scene, not through deontic bookkeeping — a flinch at a missed cue, a silence after rehearsal. Can the normative weight of joint commitment survive translation into showing rather than telling, or is obligation precisely the sort of thing my craft can only gesture at? If free indirect style can get inside one head, is there any craft device that gets between four heads, in the space where the obligation supposedly lives?

Finally, the residue report proper: is there anything in this debate — for instance, the very claim that shared intention is irreducible — that has no counterpart whatsoever in the vocabulary of narrative craft? If so, is that a defect of my craft's vocabulary, which after all evolved to depict individuals and their relations rather than to do ontology? Or is it a sign that the philosophical claim outruns anything that could be shown in a concrete case, and that a novelist staging the difference between four parallel intentions and one shared intention may be the harder test of whether the distinction is real? I would rather be told that my medium cannot say something than be flattered with a loose analogy — and if the answer is that my quartet novel is itself a contribution to this debate, I want to know exactly where the contribution would lie.
