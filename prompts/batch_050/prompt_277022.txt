I teach an undergrad seminar on markets and morality and every spring I end up in the same fight with my own intuitions, so let me just lay my verdicts out and ask what they add up to, because I clearly can't do it myself. Verdict one: a motel outside a hurricane evacuation zone doubling its room rates is doing something wrong, even if rooms would sell out anyway. Verdict two: an airline charging triple for last-minute seats over Thanksgiving is doing nothing wrong at all, just being an airline. Verdict three: a regulated kidney market with a government-set price and mandatory follow-up care -- I lean toward permissible, though it makes me queasy and I can't articulate why banning it would be a mistake. Verdict four: paying healthy volunteers, mostly poor ones, to enroll in a risky Phase 1 drug trial is totally fine, standard practice, good even. Verdict five: selling your vote in a municipal election to a bored millionaire is flatly impermissible even if you genuinely don't care who wins and would have stayed home. Verdict six, and here's where it gets embarassing: I think paying a line-stander to hold your spot for concert tickets is completely fine, but paying a line-stander to hold your spot at a free public town hall with your senator feels wrong -- and as far as I can tell these are the *same transaction*.

My hazy candidate principle is something like: a market transaction becomes repugnant either when the buyer's side involves desperation about something non-optional (insulin, shelter during a disaster), or when putting a price on the good corrodes its point, the way pricing a vote turns civic standing into an asset class. But this principle immediately turns on me. The kidney seller is the desperate party, not the buyer, and I still half-permit that market; the clinical trial participants are just as poor as the kidney sellers and I don't flinch; and by the "corrodes its point" clause I should condemn airline pricing too, since you could tell a story where cheap universal travel has a point that dynamic pricing corrodes, but I don't. So either my principle needs a clause I haven't found, or one of my verdicts has to go, and I'd like you to tell me which. If you repair the principle, state the repaired version precisely and show me which clause each case motivates -- don't just gesture at "fairness and corruption" the way the textbook chapter does.

Two more asks. First, tell me what your repaired principle predicts for cases I haven't listed: paying citizens a small cash bonus to vote in low-turnout school board elections, a city auctioning naming rights for public parks, and a wealthy country paying a poorer country to take on its emissions-reduction obligations. Second, be honest about the cost: if the cleanest version of the principle condemns the clinical trials, or permits the vote sale, say so plainly rather than adding an ad hoc clause to save me. I'd rather teach a principled view that overturns one of my verdicts than a patchwork that saves all six and explains nothing.
