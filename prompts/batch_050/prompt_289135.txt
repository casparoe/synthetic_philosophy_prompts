I work at a national statistics office on a team piloting new well-being indicators, and in three weeks I'm presenting at an internal seminar for our subjective-well-being unit: mostly happiness economists and a few affective scientists, people who spend their days with life-satisfaction scales and experience-sampling data and who regard felt happiness as the thing policy should ultimately track. My talk argues that these measures, however refined, cannot capture everything that makes a life go well, and that our indicator set should include non-experiential goods (accomplishment, knowledge, meaningful relationships) even when those goods don't show up as positive affect. My centerpiece is a passage from Robert Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia, in the experience machine discussion, where he writes of the person plugged into the machine: "Someone floating in a tank is an indeterminate blob...Is he courageous, kind, intelligent, witty, loving? It's not merely that it's difficult to tell; there's no way he is. Plugging into the machine is a form of suicide." My reading is that Nozick isn't merely reporting an intuition that machine life is worse; he's claiming that a life consisting only of experiences cannot instantiate certain properties at all, so no amount of pleasant experience could compensate for their absence -- which, if right, is a problem for any measurement program that treats experience as the whole of well-being.

Prepare me for the question period. I want the objections this particular audience will actually raise -- not the ones I'd get at a philosophy colloquium -- ordered from most likely and most damaging downward, each phrased the way a happiness economist or affective scientist would put it (they will know about status quo bias worries, adaptation and set-point research, and the interpersonal-comparability case for subjective measures, and at least two of them will think thought experiments are bad evidence about welfare). For each objection, tell me what I should concede up front, give me a two-minute reply, and flag where the reply is softest. Please also address three things directly: whether my reading of the passage survives the point that Nozick's claim about the blob might be doing less work than I need (a property-instantiation claim rather than a well-being claim); whether the suicide line overreaches in a way my audience will pounce on; and whether granting that subjective measures are the best *available* proxy undermines my case for supplementing them rather than replacing them. Finally, include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and why I can't answer it cleanly.
