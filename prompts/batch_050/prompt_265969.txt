Since retiring from my physics department three years ago I've been trying to get a proper grip on philosophy of science (forty years of doing science, zero years of thinking about what I was doing), and I've gotten properly stuck on what people call the problem of old evidence. As I understand it: if you're a Bayesian and confirmation means that conditionalizing on E raises your credence in H, then evidence you already know can't confirm anything, because P(E) = 1 and the posterior equals the prior. But that seems clearly wrong. Einstein already knew about the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion when he finished general relativity, and everyone -- including Einstein himself -- took the fact that the theory accounted for it as strong evidence in its favor. So how can old evidence confirm?

Here's my list of proposed fixes, assembled from my reading and from a very patient gradstudent who humors me over coffee every second Tuesday. (1) The counterfactual fix: ask what your credence in H would have been had you not already learned E, and check whether conditionalizing on E from that hypothetical position raises it. (2) The nothing-is-certain fix: P(E) is never literally 1, since you might be misremembering or the original observation reports might be off, and even credence 0.999 leaves enough room for confirmation to happen. (3) The logical-learning fix: what does the confirming isn't E itself but your discovery that H entails E, which is genuinely new information even when E is old news. (4) The confirmation-isn't-updating fix: confirmation is an atemporal relation of evidential support between H and E that held all along, and the whole problem only arises if you conflate it with the dynamics of how you should change your beliefs upon learning things, which is a separate matter.

What I'd like from you is a fifth proposed solution that isn't on this list -- and I mean genuinely distinct, not a rewording, a hybrid, or a special case of one of the four. Explain how it handles the Mercury case specifically, and then give me your honest assessment of whether it survives scrutiny or quietly collapses back into one of the four under pressure. Finish with \answer{...} containing a short label for the solution you proposed.
