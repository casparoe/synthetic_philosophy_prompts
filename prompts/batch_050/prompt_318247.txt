Alright, so here's my situation. I'm a retired chemist (40 years in polymer research, so ethics boards are not exactly my home turf) who got talked into joining the citizens advisory panel attached to our state's parole review board, mostly because I kept bugging my neighbor who's on it with philosophy questions after getting into this stuff in retirement. Now I've actually got to produce something. We've got a case: an inmate up for parole after serving 14 years for a string of calculated investment frauds that wiped out a lot of people's retirement savings. He's been diagnosed with psychopathy using the standard checklist thing, and his lawyers have submitted neuropsych evaluations arguing he should get credit for diminished culpability, because while he can state perfectly well that fraud is illegal and that society calls it wrong, he apparently lacks whatever deeper grasp of wrongness the rest of us have. One evaluator compared it to a colorblind person who has memorized which shade everyone calls red. The kicker: the inmate himself is articulate, passed every "did you know this was wrong" test, and says things like "of course I knew it was wrong, I just didn't care." Half the panel says that settles it, he knew it was wrong, end of story. The other half says reciting the rule isn't the same as knowing the wrongness, the way knowing the speed limit isn't the same as understanding why it exists, and if he genuinely can't grasp moral reasons then maybe he's less blameworthy even if he's more dangerous (which complicates the parole question, since the dangerousness cuts the other way).

I got voluntold to draft the decision memo. What I need is a memo recommending one of three things to the full board: grant parole, deny parole, or deny the diminished-culpability argument specifically while leaving the dangerousness assessment to the experts. I want the reasoning to actually engage with the question of whether this man knows what wrongness is -- if someone can label an action "wrong" the way a foreigner uses a phrase book, does that count as moral knowledge, and does it matter for blame either way? Please take seriously the strongest argument for whichever side the memo rejects, because the board members on that side will read it closely and I have to sit next to them at the next meeting. And since I come from chemistry, spell out any philosophy concepts you lean on rather than just name-dropping them.

Format-wise: the memo should open with its recommendation in the very first sentence ("The panel recommends that...") and then get straight into the reasoning -- no preamble, no restating the facts of the case back to me, no "certainly, happy to help" business. Just the memo itself.
