I am writing a survey chapter on theories of humour and have grown dissatisfied with how the standard candidates — superiority, incongruity, relief, and benign violation — are usually presented: each is introduced with the examples it handles well and tested against nothing else. Before I draft the chapter I want the specification written first, independent of any theory. Please produce the desiderata for an adequate general theory of humour: the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, and the observations from psychology and everyday practice it must not contradict — with, for each desideratum, the case or consideration that motivates it.

The list should cover, at minimum, the following pressure points, but should not be limited to them: (i) the difference between finding something funny and judging it to be a good or appropriate joke — cases where someone laughs and is ashamed of laughing, and cases where someone concedes a joke is excellent without being amused; (ii) failed humour as an explanatory target in its own right — a joke that falls flat is not merely a non-joke, and a theory should say what failure is; (iii) the wear-out phenomenon — why a joke is rarely funny twice for the same person, why children tolerate repetition adults cannot, and what this implies about whether surprise is necessary or merely instrumental; (iv) tickling, nervous laughter, and laughter in the absence of anything joke-like, as boundary cases a theory must either accommodate or principledly set aside; (v) moral limits — the phenomenon of an amusing joke that is also objectionable, and whether the offensiveness and the funniness interact or sit side by side; (vi) the role of the audience's inferential work — why a joke that must be explained typically dies, and why jokes that require the listener to complete a step are often funnier than fully explicit ones; (vii) humour about the self and one's own misfortunes, including gallows humour by the person on the gallows, which strains theories that locate humour in the amusement of the unthreatened observer.

For each desideratum, state whether you take it to be non-negotiable for any adequate theory or a demand specific to one camp — and where the honest answer is contested, say why. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, since if the list itself is inconsistent, that is a finding worth having. Conclude with two things: first, an assessment of which single desideratum does the most work in excluding the standard contenders; second, a brief scoring of superiority, incongruity, relief, and benign violation accounts against the list — brief, because the criteria are the deliverable, not the verdict. A table is acceptable for the scoring, but the requirements and their motivating cases should be in prose.
