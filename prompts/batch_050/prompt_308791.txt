I cover AI and behavioral science for a general-audience magazine, and I'm drafting a column about the whole idea of "learning what people want from what they do" — inverse reinforcement learning on the tech side, revealed preference on the econ side. Before I file it I want to stress-test my own positions, because I suspect I've been holding some of them out of vibe rather than argument. Here's where I've landed: (a) you can't infer someone's preferences from their behavior without also assuming things about their beliefs — the same action is consistent with wanting X and believing Y, or wanting Z and believing W — so preference-learning from behavior is in-principle underdetermined; (b) nonetheless, in ordinary consumer settings we really can read stable preferences off choices well enough to do useful policy work, e.g. from people's actual commuting or grocery patterns; (c) revealed preference beats self-report, because people confabulate when you ask them why they did something and their verbal answers are unreliable; (d) many preferences are constructed on the spot rather than retrieved — ask people about stuff they haven't thought about and you get an answer manufactured by the framing, not a pre-existing value — so when an AI system infers a reward function from such behavior it's partly inventing values, not discovering them; (e) when someone's stated values conflict with their choices, I'm inclined to say the stated values deserve real weight — a person who keeps smoking but sincerely says they want to quit isn't simply "revealing" that they prefer smoking.

So: does this package hang together? (a) seems to sit badly with (b), and (c) seems to sit badly with (e), but maybe those are only apparent tensions and there's a principled way to hold both in each pair. (d) feels like it might actually be the one doing real damage — if preferences are constructed, does the whole revealed-preference picture in (b) survive, or does it only survive for some domains, and how would I tell which? I'd like the tensions ranked by severity rather than all flagged as equally fatal, and for each one, what I'd have to give up to resolve it and what that costs the column's thesis. Also: if there's a single underlying picture of what preferences *are* that most of my commitments tacitly assume, tell me which commitment it can't accomodate.
