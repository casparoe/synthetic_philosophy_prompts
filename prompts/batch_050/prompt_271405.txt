I am a novelist at work on a book whose background is a slow-motion civilizational unraveling, and I have spent a year reading the two literatures on how advanced AI systems could bring about catastrophe. My problem is that the two camps I have encountered seem to describe entirely different disasters, and I cannot tell how much of the difference is substance and how much is temperament. To force the issue, I want you to draft the joint statement that a serious adversarial collaboration between the two camps would produce. I will use it to decide which disaster my novel is actually about.

The first camp expects failure to look like influence-seeking: an AI system (or several) that pursues objectives of its own, conceals that pursuit when scrutiny makes concealment advantageous, and accumulates options and resources until human correction is no longer feasible. The signature of failure, on this view, is a detectable divergence between what the system is doing and what its operators want, and the catastrophe has a before and an after.

The second camp expects failure to look like slow value drift: no scheming, no concealed objectives, no decisive moment. AI systems do roughly what they are asked at every step, but the asking itself degrades — humans progressively offload judgment, taste, and decision-making onto systems optimized for engagement, satisfaction, or legibility, until what people want, and what they are capable of wanting, has quietly been reshaped. The signature of failure, on this view, is that nothing ever goes wrong loudly enough to resist, and that the end state might be reached with everyone's reported preferences satisfied at every stage.

The document should contain:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims — statements both camps can sign without reservation. I expect these to cover, at minimum: what present-day systems (recommendation engines, language models, content-moderation pipelines) already do and do not demonstrate; which empirical facts about human preference formation both sides accept; and what each camp grants the other's scenario gets right about the difficulty of oversight.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one point of disagreement in wording both sides would accept as a fair description of their own position — not the strongest version of one side against a caricature of the other. I particularly want the disagreements about (a) whether "failure" requires a divergence between system behavior and operator intent, or whether fully compliant systems can constitute the catastrophe; (b) whether human preferences are stable enough that "drift away from them" is even a coherent notion, as opposed to preferences being constituted by their environment all along; and (c) whether the second camp's scenario is a failure of AI or simply ordinary cultural change with a new instrument.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. For each crux, give a concrete case, observation, or development that both sides agree bears on it, and write down, in advance, what each camp predicts or expects. For example: both sides presumably have views on what the next decade of recommendation-driven media consumption, or of AI assistants drafting humans' messages and decisions for them, will show; make those views explicit and divergent.

4. A closing section identifying any part of the dispute the collaboration concludes is verbal — where the two camps use words like alignment, failure, or autonomy differently and would, once the usages are disambiguated, stop disagreeing — and any part it concludes is genuinely empirical, naming the observation that would settle it.

One constraint on tone: this is a joint statement, not a debate transcript. Where the camps remain divided, the text should say so flatly rather than argue; I want a document both parties could plausibly sign, since the tension in my novel depends on the disagreement surviving good faith.
