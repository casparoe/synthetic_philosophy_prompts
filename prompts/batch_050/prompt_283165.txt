I am putting together the syllabus for a graduate seminar on normative ethics, and one week is devoted to the demandingness objection to consequentialism. In preparing the session I have been struck by how repetitive the literature is: the same handful of moves get recycled under different labels, and I want to push my students past them. I would therefore like your help generating a genuinely additional demandingness-style objection that is not merely a rewording, special case, or combination of the ones already on my list.

Here is what I already have, so please do not repeat any of it. First, the integrity objection associated with Williams: consequentialism can require an agent to treat her own ground projects as one item among others in the ledger, and thereby alienates her from the commitments that make her life hers. Second, Railton's moral-schizophrenia worry: even a sophisticated consequentialist who takes alienation into account must maintain a split between her motives and her actual justificatory standard. Third, Wolf's moral saints argument: a life maximally devoted to the impersonal good would be lacking in non-moral excellences and would be, on that account, unattractive. Fourth, the supererogation objection: consequentialism has no principled room for the good-but-optional, classifying every achievable good deed as obligatory. Fifth, Scheffler-style complaints about the sheer magnitude of the sacrifice demanded of affluent agents, and the proposal to blunt this with an agent-centered prerogative. Sixth, Murphy's point about partial compliance: when others fail to do their share, consequentialism loads the uncompensated shortfall onto those who do comply, and the marginal demand grows without a principled floor. What I want is one further objection that targets consequentialism's demandingness but does not ultimately rest on costs to the complying agent's own well-being, projects, motives, or psychic economy, since every entry on my list bottoms out there. A demandingness objection with a fundamentally different locus -- say, one concerning third parties, the structure of the theory itself, or something I have not thought of -- would be ideal.

You are a specialist in normative ethics with a good eye for what counts as a new argument versus an old one in a new jacket, so please think carefully rather than reaching for the first thing that comes to mind. Begin by brainstorming several candidates briefly, then select the strongest one and present it as a fully stated objection: its target, its structure, and how it differs from each item on my list (it should be obvious from your presentation that it is not a variant of any of them). Conclude with an honest assessment: is this objection actually any good, how would a consequentialist push back, and does it survive the standard rejoinder that any plausible moral theory must make substantial demands and so the complaint proves too much?
