ok so quick background in case these terms are fuzzy: counterfactual mugging is the case where a predictor flips a coin, and only if it lands tails do they ask you for $100, on the condition that if it had landed heads they'd have given you $1000 iff you would have paid when asked. tails came up, predictor asks for the money. updateless theories say pay, because "you" before the coin flip would want to commit to paying. XOR blackmail is the one where a predictor sends you a letter saying "i've predicted whether you'll pay me $10; my prediction and whether you have termites in your house have different truth values (an XOR). pay up and you won't have termites, refuse and you will." since you can't causally affect the termites but the predictor's accuracy makes paying correlate with no termites, evidential-ish reasoning says pay, which seems insane.

I work with a tiny research group (like 4 of us) doing conceptual decision theory, mostly aimed at what agents should do when predictors/simulators of them exist, and we have maybe a year of person-time to allocate. Help me prioritize. Some candidate open questions: (a) whether counterfactual mugging-style commitment requires actual causal pre-commitment or just the right dispositions, and what that even means for agents without self-modification ability; (b) whether XOR blackmail shows something is wrong with evidential reasoning in general or only with a specific way of formalizing "evidential"; (c) whether these cases are just edge cases that a good theory handles however it handles them, or whether they're load-bearing diagnostics that should shape theory choice from the start; (d) whether the right response to both is to bite the bullet and pay, or to revise the theory.
