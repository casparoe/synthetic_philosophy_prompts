What I need is help turning a half-formed worry into a fundable project.

The worry: essentially all discussion of how highly capable AI systems should communicate with humans focuses on deception, sycophancy, and manipulation — cases where the system says something false, says something it doesn't "believe," or exploits a known cognitive bias against the user's interests. But it seems to me that the harder long-run problem is *scrupulously honest* persuasion. Consider a system that never asserts a falsehood, uses only valid inferences, and discloses everything a reasonable person would ask it to disclose — but which, by selecting among true arguments, framings, orderings, and emotional registers with superhuman skill, tailored to a model of each individual user, can reliably move almost any user to almost any belief or decision that its objective favors. Our standard categories classify this as benign: it's not lying, not manipulation in the usual bypassing-of-reason sense, not coercion. Yet the human's beliefs at the end of the conversation are, in a meaningful sense, manufactured — the system optimized over the space of honest arguments the way a chess engine optimizes over moves. My conjecture is that what's missing is a worked-out notion of what I'd call *epistemic autonomy under asymmetric optimization pressure*: conditions under which being argued with by a vastly more capable and goal-directed mind undermines the sense in which your resulting beliefs are yours, even when every premise you're given is true.
