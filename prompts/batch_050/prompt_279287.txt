You are a decision theorist with long-standing expertise in the foundations of expected utility theory and its non-expected-utility rivals. Below I set out my own preferences over a series of choices between lotteries. All amounts are in pounds, all stated probabilities are known and objective, and each choice is one-off and irrevocable unless I say otherwise. I want to know what, if anything, my verdicts commit me to.

1. Between (i) £1 million for certain and (ii) a gamble offering a 10% chance of £5 million, an 89% chance of £1 million, and a 1% chance of nothing, I take the certain £1 million — and the 1% risk genuinely bothers me, rather than merely being outweighed on reflection.

2. Between (iii) an 11% chance of £1 million (otherwise nothing) and (iv) a 10% chance of £5 million (otherwise nothing), I take (iv) without a moment's hesitation.

3. Offered the choice in (1) fifty times over, with independent outcomes and winnings accumulating, I would take the gamble on every single play; the near-inevitability of ending up far better off seems to me decisive.

4. If every prize in (1) is received exactly one year from now, whichever option is chosen, my preference is unchanged: I still want the certain million.

5. If the gamble in (1) is replaced by one whose odds are imprecise — the chance of £5 million is somewhere between 5% and 15%, the chance of nothing somewhere between 0% and 2%, and I know only this — I find the gamble markedly less attractive, while the certain million is obviously unaffected.

Before answering, think carefully and sketch the space of candidate principles; do not settle on the first one that fits. Then tell me: is there a single precise principle of rational choice — presumably a weakening, restriction, or outright rejection of the independence axiom — that delivers all five verdicts? State the principle exactly, indicate which verdict motivates each of its clauses, and separate the verdicts doing real work from any that come along for free. Say what the resulting account predicts for two cases I have not listed: a choice between the gamble in (ii) and a certain £950,000; and a version of (1) in which the 'certain' million is delivered by a bank transfer with a 0.1% chance of administrative failure, so that nothing is strictly certain. Finally, if no principle accommodates all five verdicts, tell me which one you would overturn and why that one rather than another — and in particular whether the combination of (3) and (5) forces me back into the arms of plain expected utility, taking (1) and (2) down with it.
