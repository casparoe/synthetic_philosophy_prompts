I build consumer AI features for a living and I keep running into the same move in internal discussions and on podcasts: people say the assistant should be like a good doctor or a good lawyer, not like a vending machine. A doctor doesn't hand you opioids just because you ask, a lawyer won't help you structure a deal she thinks will ruin you, and the point of hiring a professional is precisely that they know your interests better than your momentary requests do. This analogy gets wheeled out to justify assistants that refuse or gently override user requests -- nudging you away from a fad diet, declining to draft the angry email, insisting on a caveat you didn't ask for. My cofounder, who is skeptical of all this, counters that the user is the AI's *boss*, not its patient, and that the doctor comparison is basically a licensing scheme for the lab's values getting smuggled in as "expertise."

Can you audit the analogy for me? I want the full correspondence first: what plays the role of the physician's training, the medical facts the patient lacks, the oath, malpractice liability, informed consent, and the patient's right to refuse treatment and seek a second opinion -- and then where each of those has no real counterpart on the AI side. Two things I specifically want pressed on: (1) the doctor's epistemic advantage is about *means* (which treatment cures which disease), whereas the override cases I listed are mostly about *ends* (whether your diet or your email is a good idea), so does the analogy even reach the cases it's used for; (2) historical medical paternalism was rolled back precisely *by* the informed-consent doctrine, so is the analogy actually borrowing its authority from a version of the profession we decided was wrong. Also flag any features the analogy quietly imports that the AI lacks -- like the fact that my doctor doesn't get paid by a third party with its own agenda for how I behave.

Format: give me a table with exactly these columns -- "Professional element", "Supposed AI counterpart", "Mapping status (holds / partially / breaks)", and "Relevance to when an assistant may refuse or override". After the table, a JSON object with these fields: "verdict" (a string: whether the analogy licenses broad paternalistic override, narrow override only, or neither), "strongest_disanalogy" (string), "disanalogy_that_does_not_matter" (string, one that looks damning but isn't), "better_analogy" (string, what comparison would actually fit if any), and "what_the_analogy_was_really_doing" (string, one sentence).
