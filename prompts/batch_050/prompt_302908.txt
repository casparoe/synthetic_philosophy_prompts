Having spent a career in empirical science and turned to philosophy only in retirement, I find the centipede game the sharpest illustration I know of how a chain of impeccable local reasoning can arrive at a conclusion that feels wrong. I would like a poem, of no more than thirty lines, that takes this seriously. My constraint on the form: the stanzas must shrink as the poem proceeds -- say, stanzas of eight, six, four, and two lines -- so that the poem's very shape enacts the backward unraveling from the generous terminal payoffs to the miserly first-move defection. The early, longer stanzas should give the cooperative temptation its genuine due: both players do better by passing than either does by grabbing early, and that fact should feel like an argument, not a mere sentiment. The later, shorter stanzas should let the logic of the final node reach backward and hollow the temptation out, so that the closing couplet lands on defection at the first node with something of the chill the conclusion actually carries. Please do not resolve the poem by simply siding with either the game theorist or the cooperator; but do commit the poem to a definite reading of where the trouble lies -- whether in the assumption of common knowledge of rationality, in what it means to reason about a future one is helping to produce, or elsewhere -- and let the imagery serve that reading. Append a brief prose note, four or five sentences, stating which reading you chose, why, and where the poem takes liberties a game theorist would object to.
