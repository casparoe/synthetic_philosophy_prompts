So my film-philosophy seminar is doing personal identity this semester and I want to build a session around The Prestige (the Nolan one, with the two rival magicians). Read it through the lens of teleportation/duplication cases and the question of whether survival is about psychological continuity or about being the *same* body/brain -- the Parfit-style stuff, but keep the reading anchored in the actual film.

Here's what I want you to grapple with specifically. Tesla's machine is deliberately ambiguous: does it transport Angier across the theater, or does it copy him and leave the original standing where he was? The film gives us that scene with the hats scattered in the woods as the first hint, and then the horrible nightly arrangement -- one man falls through the trapdoor into the tank and drowns, the other appears at the back of the hall to take the bow. The kicker is that even Angier doesn't seem to know which one he is each night, and the last line about not knowing whether he'd be the man in the box or the one at the prestige basically hands us the philosophical problem on a plate.

So: which theory of personal identity best describes what Angier is actually doing? On a psychological-continuity view, he survives every night and the man in the tank is just a copy who suffers a few minutes of terror -- but then the man in the tank has exactly the same memories of stepping into the machine, so why does his death feel like murder? On a bodily-continuity view, Angier committed suicide on night one and everything after is an impostor taking bows. Also dig into the twist that the first time he used the machine he shot the duplicate on sight -- is that self-defense, suicide, or murder, and does the answer change depending on who pulled the trigger relative to when the machine fired?

And be honest about where the film resists the reading. Nolan clearly wants the dread, not a verdict, and the drowned-men warehouse reveal is doing emotional work that might not survive philosophical scrutiny. Does the film actually take a position, or does it just exploit the fact that the question feels unanswerable? Bonus points if you compare it to Borden's solution to the same trick, since the film sets up a pretty clean contrast between two ways of paying for the illusion. I'll be using this in class so I need it faithful to both the movie and the philosophy.
