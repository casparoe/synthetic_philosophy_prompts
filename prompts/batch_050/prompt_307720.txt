I'm a PhD student working with an interpretability team at a small AI lab, and I keep getting pulled into the same argument and need to end it. Our deployment setup runs what we call mechanistic anomaly detection: we validate that some behavior of a model (say, refusing a certain kind of request, or doing multi-hop arithmetic) is produced by a specific internal mechanism we've characterized -- roughly, which attention heads and MLP features carry the relevant information and where interventions on them change the output. Then during continued fine-tuning or deployment we monitor whether "the mechanism" is still intact. The problem nobody can settle: what counts as the SAME mechanism? Last month a refusal behavior that used to depend on one head now runs through that head plus a backup head that had quietly grown in; behavior on every test input is identical, but activation patching gives different results than before. Half the team says the monitor should fire, half says nothing important changed. Then a seperate case where the features implementing a computation rotated to different neurons within what looks like the same subspace. We argue about this every two weeks and it's blocking sign-off on whether models need re-auditing after updates.

So here's what I want: act as someone fluent in both the philosophy-of-science literature on mechanisms and multiple realizability AND the practical side of interpretability (activation patching, causal scrubbing style tests, interchange interventions). Think carefully, and before writing the procedure, spend a short section brainstorming the candidate notions of "same mechanism" (same causal graph of components? same algorithm under some abstraction mapping? same behavior plus same intervention profile?) and kill off the ones that are unusable in practice. Then give me a decision procedure we can actually run on a case in a day or two of GPU time: the tests in order, what result on each test settles it as "same" vs "different" vs "retrain the monitor," and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure -- I want those marked honestly, not hidden. Some things I'm pretty sure the procedure has to preserve or it's useless: the distinction between a component being necessary and being sufficient, the fact that "same mechanism" is relative to a grain of description (and the procedure should force us to state the grain BEFORE running tests, since we keep smuggling it in afterwards), and some way of handling components that are redundant backups.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) the backup-head case above, (2) the subspace-rotation case, and (3) a case where the internals are clearly different but behavior is identical on our whole test suite and would only diverge off-distribution -- I suspect the procedure will call (3) "same" or "different" in a way half my team will hate, and I want to see that consequence stated plainly rather than papered over. End with a short section on what the procedure can't do -- i.e., which of our recurring disputes it will definately not settle and has to be kicked up to a human decision about what we actually care about (behavioral guarantees vs. understanding).
