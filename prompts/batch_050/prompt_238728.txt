Here are the solutions I know of to the puzzle of imaginative resistance — our apparent inability or unwillingness to imagine a story in which, say, cruelty for its own sake really is virtuous: (1) the impossibility account — moral truths are necessary, so the fiction asks us to imagine an impossibility, no easier than imagining a round square; (2) the fictionality account — the deviant moral proposition simply isn't true in the story, because authors can't make moral claims fictional by fiat the way they can make it fictional that the butler did it; (3) the psychological contingency account — the resistance is just a quirk of human imaginative habits, calling for no deeper explanation; (4) the contagion account — imagining a deviant morality risks letting deviant attitudes leak into one's actual evaluative outlook, so refusing is a rational safeguard rather than a puzzle; and (5) the desire-like imagining account — fully imagining a moral truth requires taking up a conative stance toward it, and we cannot make ourselves imagine the deviant as to-be-wanted. Can you offer a sixth explanation that is genuinely different — not a rewording, a special case, or a combination of these five (so don't just say the puzzle conflates several of them)? Tell me also how it relates to the closest item on the list and what would count for or against it.
