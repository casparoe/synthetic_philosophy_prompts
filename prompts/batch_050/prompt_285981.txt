In many common-law property systems, a movable object can become a fixture: once it is sufficiently attached to land or a building, it is treated as part of the real property rather than as an independently owned chattel. The tests vary, but officials commonly consider how firmly the object is attached, whether it was adapted to the building, whether removal would damage either one, and what the installer intended. Thus a pile of bricks beside a plot remains so many bricks, while the same bricks assembled into a house are treated as parts of the house; a shelving unit standing freely may remain the tenant’s property, while closely similar shelving built into a wall may pass with the building.

Treat this as a stylized description of the practice rather than a question about the exact doctrine of any one jurisdiction. What does it presuppose about the special composition question—the question of when several things compose one further thing? Does treating bricks, timber, wiring, and fixtures as a building implicitly posit a composite object over and above its arranged parts, or is the building merely a legal and linguistic bookkeeping category? Does the doctrine favor a functional-integration answer, on which things compose a whole when they jointly play a suitably unified role, or can intention, permanence, and potential damage explain the classification without any further ontological commitment?

In your answer, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well. In particular, consider three rival views: mereological nihilism, according to which there are only simples arranged in various ways; unrestricted composition, according to which every plurality whatsoever composes a sum; and a restricted, function-sensitive view. Which of these would make the fixture rules easiest to justify, which would make them merely pragmatic conventions, and which would be in genuine tension with them? Pay special attention to the role of the installer’s intention: can a fact about someone’s plans help bring a new object into existence, or can it only determine how existing things are legally classified?

Finally, say how the practice would have to change if its administrators explicitly adopted each of the three rival views. Where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, say so. Please separate conclusions that follow from the practice as described from interpretations that remain genuinely contested, and do not present a disputed metaphysical reading as if it were the consensus.
