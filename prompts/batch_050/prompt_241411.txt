I sit on the awards committee of a mid-sized scientific society (fictional-ish, think a national physics or biology society) that gives out a Founders Medal each year. Our charter, written in the 1920s, says the medal goes to "the discoverer" of the most important result in the field that year. Singular. This year's obvious result was arrived at independently by two teams. Team A submitted their paper to a journal in February but told no one else about it until it was published in May. Team B posted a preprint in March and gave talks about it before their journal version came out in June. So A has first submission, B has first public disclosure, and both swear they had no idea about the other. To make it messier, a grad student at a third institution had a conference poster eighteen months ago sketching the core idea and why it should work, but without the actual derivation or data, and she is now (reasonably) pointing out that nobody would have looked for the result if people like her hadn't made it seem findable.

The committee is split three ways. One faction says first-to-submit wins because that's the only rule you can apply without getting into judges' metaphysics. Another says split the medal, since both teams did the full work and the result was, as one member put it, "in the water supply" after the enabling instruments came online last year — if neither team had existed, someone else would have had it within two years. The third faction, including our chair, thinks the whole affair shows the charter's premise is broken: if discoveries are more or less inevitable products of the state of the field, then awarding "the discoverer" rewards being fastest to a finish line everyone was already running toward, and we should push to amend the charter to reward contributions instead of priority. (A minority within the minority wants to give the grad student something, though nobody can say what.)

Draft the memo I'll circulate before our vote. It should: recommend a concrete outcome for this year's medal (not just "form a subcommittee"); say what general priority rule the society should adopt going forward, since this will not be the last time; engage seriously with the inevitability argument rather than waving at it — i.e., either explain why priority still matters even if discoveries are inevitable, or bite the bullet and design an award that doesn't rest on priority; and give the strongest version of the losing position(s) fair treatment, since whichever way we go, roughly a third of the committee will think we got it wrong. Keep it under ~1200 words and write it so the committee can actually act on it.
