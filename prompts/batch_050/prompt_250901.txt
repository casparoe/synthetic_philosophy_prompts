I serve on the disciplinary committee of a professional licensing board (roughly a dozen cases a month, sanctions ranging from a formal reprimand up to license revocation, with decisions taken in a half-day hearing by a mixed panel of practitioners and lay members, reviewed by legal counsel, and subject to appeal). Our current rubric is frankly retributive in its language: we ask what sanction a violation "deserves," we weigh aggravating and mitigating factors as inputs to desert, and we record remorse as mitigation.

I have been reading the exchange between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso over free will, just deserts, and punishment. As I understand it, Dennett rejects "basic desert" yet wants to keep most of our responsibility practices -- blame, graded sanctions, mitigation for those whose decision-making competence is impaired -- on the grounds that they shape the behavior of reasons-responsive agents; Caruso, as a free will skeptic, wants blame and desert out of the system entirely and would rebuild sanctioning around a public-health-and-quarantine model: incapacitate only as much as safety requires, address the social causes of misconduct, and treat severity of sanction as a burden to be justified forward, never backward. Our statutes don't require desert; they require proportionality and public protection, so we have room to change our method.

Write a decision procedure our panel could actually follow in a hearing: an ordered sequence of questions or tests, in language a lay member can use without the philosophy, that produces a defensible sanction without any covert appeal to what the offender deserves. In constructing it, please:

1. Identify which distinctions from the Dennett-Caruso dispute must survive translation into the procedure (for example, reasons-responsiveness and competence, deterrability, incapacitation versus rehabilitation, and the status of blame and of moral emotions like indignation), and which can safely be dropped at our level of decision-making.

2. Flag each point where Dennett and Caruso would pull the procedure in different directions -- for instance, whether expressions of blame have any legitimate role, and how much weight general deterrence may carry against a sympathetic individual -- and mark those explicitly as policy calls the board must settle in advance, not questions the procedure can answer.

3. Build in the hard cases the procedure must handle rather than assume away: the remorseless repeat offender who is fully rational and therefore a candidate for blame on Dennett's view; the offender whose misconduct stems from a treatable impairment; and the respected practitioner with a single serious lapse where general deterrence pressures toward a sanction harsher than any forward-looking rationale seems to need.

4. Show the procedure working on two or three concrete cases, including at least one where it yields an outcome we will find uncomfortable in either direction (harsher or more lenient than desert would give), so we can see the cost of the method before adopting it.

5. State plainly what the procedure cannot do: which of our current practices it leaves untouched, where it will misfire, and what we would lose by abandoning the language of desert even if the theory says we should.
