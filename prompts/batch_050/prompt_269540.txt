I'm a software engineer who's been reading about longtermism on the side, and I've settled on a position I want stress-tested: taking the simulation argument seriously should dampen "fanatical" concern for the far future -- fanatical in the decision-theory sense, where you accept tiny probabilities of sufficiently vast payoffs as dominating your choices.

My reasoning: if there's a substantial probability we're in an ancestor simulation, then (a) simulations presumably run on finite compute and can be switched off, which caps the total value at stake no matter what we do; (b) we have zero causal leverage over any basement-level far future, since that future belongs to a world we can't touch; and (c) the expected-value case for sacrificing now on behalf of trillions of future lives only goes through on the non-simulation branch, so the simulation branch dilutes it. Conclusion: the case for far-future fanaticism is weaker than longtermists claim -- not zero, but heavily discounted.

What would count against this inference? I don't want the standard objections to the simulation argument itself (substrate independence, limits on computing, the trilemma). I want to know what would have to be true for my move from "we might be simulated" to "fanaticism is less warranted" to fail. Things I've wondered about: whether simulated people still count morally, and whether that alone restores the stakes; whether simulation could raise rather than cap the value at stake -- say, if the simulators' decisions about whether to keep us running, or to instantiate more copies, are sensitive to what we do; whether the expected-value math actually gets diluted across branches or just gets messier. Which of these would a defender of my position have to concede as a genuine defeater, and which could they absorb with minor qualifications? Does the position survive if the probability of simulation is 10% rather than 50%? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.
