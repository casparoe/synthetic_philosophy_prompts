I am a novelist, and my writers' circle is divided over a question that follows me into every draft. On one side are those who hold that political fiction fails precisely insofar as it preaches, and who defend the novel as a mirror: its task is to render the world accurately, and whatever reform follows from clear seeing is a welcome by-product, never a duty. On the other side are those who answer — usually with W. E. B. Du Bois's name attached — that a novel is better understood as a hammer: not a surface held up to the world but an instrument for reshaping it. From this they draw a conclusion they do not hesitate to apply to me: an author who declines to place her fiction in the service of the cause has produced, at best, decoration. They take their warrant from the close of Du Bois's 1926 address to the NAACP, published in The Crisis under the title "Criteria of Negro Art," where he insists that all art is propaganda. Yet earlier in that same address Du Bois writes something that seems to me to pull in a different direction:

"You realize this sooner than the average white American because, pushed aside as we have been in America, there has come to us not only a certain distaste for the tawdry and flamboyant but a vision of what the world could be if it were really a beautiful world; if we had the true spirit; if we had the Seeing Eye, the Cunning Hand, the Feeling Heart; if we had, to be sure, not perfect happiness, but plenty of good hard work, the inevitable suffering that always comes with life; sacrifice and waiting, all that -- but, nevertheless, lived in a world where men know, where men create, where they realize themselves and where they enjoy life."

Please audit the hammer analogy as it is actually used in my circle, with this passage on the table. Begin with the correspondence table: in the analogy, what plays the role of the hand that wields the hammer, the nail, the blow, the resistance of the material, and the blueprint the hammerer presumably consults? Then locate the breaks that matter for the intended use. Does a novel have anything like a single wielder with a settled aim, or is it made by a person who discovers the aim in the making? Do readers respond to fiction the way timber responds to a nail — passively, at the point struck — and if not, what does the image quietly smuggle in about authorial control and audience docility? Can the hammer picture accommodate what Du Bois calls the Seeing Eye and the Feeling Heart, which sound like organs of perception and response rather than instruments of impact, and which he seems to treat as the whole point? Distinguish the disanalogies that are idle from the ones that bear on my circle's specific conclusion — that the novelist who refuses the cause has failed as an artist — and say whether that conclusion survives once the breaks are priced in, or whether the analogy was carrying it the entire time. Finally, if the hammer fails, say whether the address itself, in this passage or in its general argument, offers a better governing image for what political fiction is doing, and what that image would commit my circle to instead.
