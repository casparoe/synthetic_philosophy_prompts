I teach moral psychology and, some semesters, a seminar on the historiography of mass violence, and in reading across the Browning–Goldhagen controversy and its successors I keep tripping over an inferential pattern for which I cannot find an established name. The pattern is this. A historian wishes to show that some episode of participation in atrocity is to be explained by situation rather than by character or conviction. The evidence offered consists of demonstrations that a situational mechanism — obedience to authority, conformity within one's unit, career pressure — is sufficient to produce broadly comparable behavior in ordinary, unremarkable people: Milgram's subjects and the shocks, Asch's confederates and the lines, and so on. The historian then concludes that the perpetrators' conduct was produced by such a mechanism. But the same evidence seems to me equally compatible with a different causal story: that the perpetrators already possessed the relevant motivation — ideological commitment, say — and that the situation did not manufacture the motive but merely stripped away the restraints, such as fear of punishment or social sanction, that normally prevent such motives from issuing in action. The laboratory shows that the mechanism can make almost anyone do the deed; it does not show that it made these particular people do it, since they may have needed no making.

I notice the same structure in more mundane settings. A colleague explains a student's plagiarism by deadline pressure, citing studies showing that ordinary students can be induced to cheat under time pressure — which leaves entirely open the possibility that this student was already disposed to cheat and that the deadline merely altered the risks. A physician notes that a pathogen causes illness in nearly everyone exposed and infers that this patient's illness was caused by exposure, whereas the exposure may only have unmasked a pre-existing condition. In each case a demonstrated sufficient condition is treated as evidence about the actual causal route in a particular case, and the distinction between producing a motive and merely releasing one does the hidden work.

Does this configuration have an established name, whether in philosophy of action, philosophy of science, or the historiographical literature itself? I recognize that it lives near the person–situation debate and near the distinction between causes and enabling conditions, but neither seems to be exactly it: the first is a substantive dispute about what in fact drives behavior, whereas my pattern is a gap in a form of inference that could arise even if the situationists are right about everything; the second is a metaphysical distinction, whereas I am asking what sufficiency evidence of the laboratory kind actually licenses. I should say that I would not be satisfied with a bare label. What I want is to know how this pattern relates to those neighboring concepts, where its boundaries lie — which kinds of cases fall outside it and why — and, if nothing established fits, a coined term defined precisely enough to keep manufacturing a motive distinct from merely permitting its expression.
