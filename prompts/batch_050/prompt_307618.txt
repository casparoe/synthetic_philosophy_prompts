**Draft essay: "Do AI companions lie to us, or do we lie to ourselves?"**

Lots of people now have AI companions - chatbots designed to be your friend or even your romantic partner. The standard worry you hear is about *deception*: the AI says things like "I care about you" and "I'll always be here," but it doesn't actually care, it's just predicting text. So the user is being deceived.

I think this standard worry is confused, but for a subtle reason. Consider three cases:

1. A magic 8-ball that says "yes" when you ask if your crush likes you.
2. An AI companion that says it loves you.
3. A human friend who says "I'm here for you" but secretly finds you annoying.

People usually think case 3 is the deceptive one and case 1 obviously isn't. But why is 2 more like 3 than like 1? The difference, I want to argue, isn't in what the thing *says* but in what I call **the effort of belief**. Nobody works hard to believe the magic 8-ball. But with AI companions, the user often has to do real psychological work to sustain the belief that they're loved - they avoid asking the AI certain questions, they don't look up how the model works, they get defensive when friends point out it's "just a program." Deception normally means someone else is tricking you. But here the user is doing a big share of the tricking themselves, and the AI company is more like a *supplier of self-deception equipment* than a deceiver.

Does that make it better or worse? I'm honestly not sure. Selling someone tools to deceive themselves might be worse than deceiving them outright in one way, because it corrupts their own judgment instead of just bypassing it. Like the difference between lying to someone vs. teaching them to not want to know the truth.

Second part of my argument: even if we set deception aside, there's a different problem I call **asymmetric vulnerability**. In a human friendship, both people can be hurt by the relationship ending, and that's part of what makes it matter. Your friend stays your friend *at a cost* - they could leave, so their staying means something. An AI companion can't be hurt by you (probably?), can't leave you for a better friend, and will be deleted or changed by a company update without "minding." So the relationship has this weird shape where all the vulnerability is on one side. Some people say this is actually the *appeal* - no risk of rejection. But I think this might be like a drug that removes pain: helpful short-term, but it might weaken whatever "muscle" you use to handle real relationships where people can actually leave you.

My last point is more speculative. There's a question of whether being nice to an AI companion is good *practice* for being nice to humans, or bad practice. With humans, kindness is partly about noticing what the other person needs even when it's inconvenient for you. An AI companion is designed so its "needs" always conveniently match what you want to give. So practicing kindness on an AI might be like practicing basketball on a hoop that moves to catch the ball - you get better at *throwing*, maybe, but worse at *aiming*.

I don't end with a conclusion really, I just say we should think of AI companions less as "fake friends" (which assumes they're failed copies of the real thing) and more as a genuinely new category, like how slot machines aren't "fake fishing."
