I've noticed a pattern in how people around me -- and, if I'm honest, I -- reason about choices that determine who will exist, as opposed to choices that merely affect people who will exist either way. A friend of mine recently decided against having a third child, and nobody at the table thought the possible child had been wronged or that anything of value had gone missing from the world; yet everyone agreed that a couple who knowingly conceives a child they cannot feed does something wrong. Or take a city council weighing two uses of the same funds: improving services for current residents counts straightforwardly as a benefit, whereas a policy expected to attract a thousand new families who would go on to live decent lives is never defended on the ground that those families' future happiness is itself a gain -- though if the policy were expected to drive a thousand current families away, that loss would count heavily. It seems that when a decision changes how well off people are, harms and benefits both register, and register roughly symmetrically; but when a decision changes whether certain people exist at all, the harms still register while the benefits go silent.

And yet the asymmetry isn't stable. The same people who won't count merely possible happiness in these case-by-case decisions will say, comparing two futures wholesale, that the flourishing world is better than the bleak one even if the flourishing world contains more people, or entirely different people. So the principle seems to switch on and off depending on whether one is evaluating particular acts or whole outcomes, and I can't tell whether that's a defensible division of labor or just inconsistency that hasn't been caught. It also seems to break down precisely where it matters most: something like climate policy changes both how well off future people will be and, through countless downstream effects, which particular people and how many of them there will be, so the two categories can't even be cleanly separated in practice.

Is there an established concept or literature for this pattern, and is it one phenomenon or several stitched together? I'd like more than a label: I want the carving done properly. What exactly would the underlying principle have to say to capture these judgments, what does it exclude, and can it be stated in a form that doesn't collapse in either direction -- neither into counting all possible happiness equally, which seems to lead to the conclusion that a vast population of lives barely worth living would be better than a smaller population of thriving ones, nor into the claim that it is simply indifferent whether happy lives ever come to exist? If nothing in the literature fits exactly, I'd like the nearest concepts identified, an account of where each diverges from what I've described, and -- if it comes to it -- a coined term with a definition and some boundary cases, including the mixed cases where a single decision changes both the identities and the quality of life of the people affected.
