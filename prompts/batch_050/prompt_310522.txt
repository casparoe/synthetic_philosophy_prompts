At my philosophy reading group we recently spent a session on Augustine's On Lying, and two members, Marta and Jon, ended up in a running argument that the rest of us couldn't settle. Neither is a specialist, but both had read the text carefully. I'd like a point-by-point adjudication. Here is the exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Marta opened by saying that Augustine's definition of lying — deliberately uttering against what one holds in one's own mind, with the will to deceive — locates the wrong of lying entirely in the speaker's interior state. On that definition, a lie is a lie even if no one is actually deceived and even if the proposition uttered turns out to be true; and conversely, a false statement made in honest error is no lie at all. She took this to be the view's strength: it makes the wrong of lying a matter of the will rather than of outcomes.

(2) Jon replied that this cuts the other way. If what matters is speaking against one's own mind, then a technically true statement crafted to mislead escapes the definition entirely — he gave the example of telling a friend "I can't lend you anything, my account is nearly empty," while omitting that one keeps a second account elsewhere. Nothing false was asserted; nothing was uttered against the speaker's mind. So either Augustine's absolute prohibition of lying is easily gamed by careful phrasing, or the definition is not really doing the work the prohibition needs.

(3) Marta answered that Jon was reading the definition as a legal formula when Augustine plainly treats the will to deceive as the fault itself. Augustine doesn't even count jokes as lies, precisely because a joke carries on its face that no deception is meant — which shows intent is the real test, not the literal content of the utterance. So the deceptive truth-teller in Jon's example is condemned by Augustine's lights, even if we reserve the word "lie" for the stricter case.

(4) Jon said this was a patch that breaks Augustine's most famous verdict. Augustine holds that one may not lie even to save an innocent person from murderers — at most one may stay silent and accept the consequences. But if the will to deceive is what fundamentally matters, then staying silent when you know your silence will direct the murderers to the right door is deception by omission, and the absolute position forbids even silence; whereas if silence is permitted, then the view's real content is a technical rule about assertion, not about deception — which is exactly the gaming he predicted in (2). Either way, Marta's reading collapses one horn of Augustine's position.

(5) Finally they took up a case neither could place: a hospice nurse who answers a dementia patient's daily question "Is my husband coming today?" with "Yes, he'll be here soon," though the husband is dead. The patient is comforted, forgets the exchange within minutes, and would be freshly traumatized each day by the truth, with no lasting benefit. Marta held that this is still a lie and still forbidden: Augustine's ranking of goods — no bodily or emotional comfort justifies corrupting the soul's relation to truth — applies regardless of the hearer's memory. Jon argued that deception aims at installing a false belief in a mind, and a mind that cannot retain anything cannot be deceived in the morally loaded sense; the nurse's will is to comfort, not to deceive, which puts the utterance closer to Augustine's own treatment of jokes than to a lie. Marta responded that this proves too much: it would license lying to anyone expected to forget, disbelieve, or never verify — the dying, the very young, strangers one will never meet again.

Here is what I'd like from you. Adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of (1) through (5), say who is right and why, or what exactly each side gets right and wrong where the truth splits. Two more things. First, separate two questions the two of them kept running together: which side has the better reading of Augustine's actual text, and which side has the better philosophical position on lying, since I want your verdict on each even if they diverge. Second, tell me what both of them are missing — a distinction, or a strand of Augustine's thinking, that would have moved the argument forward. Please commit: definite verdicts with reasons, not a balanced menu of options for me to choose between. Where you think the text genuinely underdetermines a point, say which reading you judge stronger anyway, and what settles it for you.
