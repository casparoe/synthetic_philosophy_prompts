Take the civil-service examination system of late imperial China as a case study for the dispute between Mencius and Xunzi over human nature. For the purposes of the analysis, treat the relevant features of the institution as follows: advancement depended on success in demanding written examinations centered on canonical texts; candidates generally spent years preparing, often with substantial family support; success was rare but brought extraordinary prestige and access to office; and the system rewarded disciplined memorization, stylistic mastery, perseverance, and deference to an established curriculum. Analyze this institution twice: first through Mencius’s picture of human beings as possessing incipient moral dispositions that can be protected, extended, or starved by circumstances, and then through Xunzi’s picture of human beings as naturally inclined toward desire and contention but capable of being reshaped through ritual, education, and deliberate artifice.

Be precise about the mechanisms rather than simply saying that one thinker was optimistic and the other pessimistic. Which features of the examination system would each thinker regard as genuinely formative, which as merely selective, and which as potentially corrupting? Does the production of outwardly disciplined, classically educated officials support Xunzi’s account of artificial transformation, or could Mencius explain the same result as the cultivation and social extension of preexisting moral capacities? Pay particular attention to the gap between mastering a moral canon and becoming virtuous, the role of family ambition and status competition, and the possibility that institutionalized virtue training might teach strategic conformity. Finally, let the case test the theories: identify observations about the examination system that would count for or against each view, explain whether its longevity favors one account over the other, and conclude with your own judgment about whether the dispute is best understood as a disagreement about human nature, about the mechanisms of moral education, or about what should count as genuine virtue.
