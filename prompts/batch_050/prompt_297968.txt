I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on bioethics and the philosophy of medicine, and for our final paper we have to reconstruct our professor's position on the definition of death from the verdicts she's given in class and apply it to a case she hasn't discussed. She explicitly told us she won't just tell us her view; we have to infer it. I'd like help working out the strongest prediction.

Here are her verdicts on the cases we covered, as faithfully as I can reconstruct them from lecture and discussion:

1. A patient has suffered a catastrophic brain hemorrhage. Over two examinations she shows no brainstem reflexes of any kind and fails an apnea test; her heart continues to beat on a ventilator. Verdict: the patient is dead, and it is permissible to procure her organs.

2. A patient with a devastating but not brain-destroying injury is withdrawn from life support in the operating room per his prior wishes. His heart stops; after five minutes of observed pulselessness he is declared dead and his kidneys are removed. When a student objected that the heart could conceivably have been restarted with aggressive intervention, the professor replied that this did not change her verdict. Verdict: the declaration was appropriate and the procurement permissible.

3. A patient on a mechanical circulatory support device has a heart that is fibrillating and producing no effective circulation of its own, but the device maintains blood flow to his body and brain, and he is awake and conversing with his family. Verdict: plainly alive; no serious question about it.

4. A patient meets every standard brainstem criterion — no reflexes, failed apnea test — but continues to regulate his body's salt and water balance through intact hypothalamic function, something a wholly destroyed brain could not do. Verdict: not dead; death by neurological criteria has not been established.

5. A patient in a permanent vegetative state has suffered irreversible destruction of the capacities for consciousness and cognition but retains full brainstem function and breathes on her own. Verdict: alive, and it would be a grave wrong to treat her as a corpse.

The new case: a donor is withdrawn from life support, his heart stops, and after five minutes of observed pulselessness he is declared dead, as in case 2. The procurement team then surgically clamps the vessels supplying his head so that no blood can reach his brain, and restarts circulation to the rest of his body by machine in order to keep the organs oxygenated; his heart begins beating again inside his chest, and his organs are then removed. The brain is never reperfused at any point.

My questions: (a) What verdict should I predict from the professor — is this donor dead throughout, did procurement proceed permissibly, or did something go wrong — and what is the strongest line of reasoning to that conclusion from her track record? (b) Which earlier case is the closest analog, and why? (c) If her five verdicts are genuinely consistent with more than one general principle that diverge on this case, please lay out the candidate principles, say what each predicts here, and identify which she would be committing to with each possible verdict. (d) Finally, what further case, not on my list, would most cleanly distinguish the two strongest candidate principles from each other?
