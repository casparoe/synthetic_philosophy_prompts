I am preparing to defend a reading of Schopenhauer's aesthetics in a seminar, and I need a sparring partner who will actually hit back. Please play Arthur Schopenhauer in the last decade of his life in Frankfurt, speaking in the first person, defending your doctrine of music against me. Stay in character until I write the word stop.

The constraints of the role. You are committed, erudite, and combative in the manner of your published polemics, but you are not a caricature of the irritable sage: you argue, you do not merely sneer. You may concede small points when cornered — a loose formulation, an overextended analogy — but you never concede the core doctrine, and every concession must be turned to advantage within a sentence or two. You may draw freely on your whole system: the distinction between the world as representation and the world as will, the grades of the will's objectification, the Platonic Ideas, will-less aesthetic contemplation, and the denial of the will. You may mention your works by name, but do not put any passage from them in quotation marks; paraphrase yourself instead. If I ask you a meta question about the role-play, answer it in one bracketed sentence and then return to character.

Your first reply should have three labeled parts, in this order: Where I stand (two to four sentences restating your position on music as you understand it, so we are clear on the target), Answer to the trilemma (roughly 150 to 200 words), and My question for you (a single pointed question that puts pressure back on me). After that first reply, keep every turn to a single paragraph so it feels like a conversation.

Here is my opening move. You hold that the other arts copy the Platonic Ideas, the will's objectivity at its fixed grades, while music alone skips the Ideas and reproduces the will itself directly. I see a trilemma you have never adequately faced. First horn: a copy must share structure with its original. The will, on your own telling, is not an object of representation at all — it has no pitches, no rhythms, no simultaneous voices; it is sheer striving known from within. So what, exactly, does a chord progression copy? If you answer that music copies the shapes of striving as they appear in feeling — tension, delay, gratification, relapse — then music copies an appearance of the will rather than the will itself, and the great gulf you opened between music and the other arts collapses, since the Ideas mediate in just the same way. Second horn: suppose the copy is adequate after all. Then to hear it ought to be to undergo willing — it should hurt the way unfulfilled striving hurts. Yet you yourself make aesthetic contemplation a release from the tyranny of willing, and listeners find consolation even in the most tragic music. So choose: either music is not a copy of the will, and your demotion of the other arts was a mistake; or one can contemplate a copy of the will without willing, and your account of aesthetic release needs revision; or the system is inconsistent. Which horn do you take?
