In my philosophy of language seminar we've just started the unit on vagueness, and before I commit to a paper topic I want to check whether the views I find intuitive can actually be held together. Here's where I've landed:

(1) Vague predicates like 'heap' and 'tall' have genuine borderline cases, and this is a normal, unproblematic feature of natural language rather than a defect that needs repairing.

(2) Every well-formed declarative sentence is either true or false, and ordinary classical logic — modus ponens, excluded middle — is valid everywhere, including in borderline territory.

(3) One grain of sand never makes the difference between a heap and a non-heap, and one millimeter never makes the difference between tall and not tall. Vague predicates are, in that sense, tolerant of marginal change.

(4) In a sorites series — say, a line of people descending from seven feet to four feet, one millimeter at a time — there is no fact of the matter about where 'tall' stops applying; any assignment of a precise cutoff would be arbitrary.

(5) Whatever vagueness turns out to be, it isn't mere ignorance about a hidden sharp boundary: there is no precise height such that the semantic facts secretly classify everyone above it as tall.

Which of these are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to be? For each real tension, tell me how severe it is and what the cheapest revision would be. If you think one commitment has to go, tell me which one and what I'd be giving up by dropping it.
