Last night a friend and I argued for hours about the safety case for a deployment decision my team is facing, and I want to find out whether my side of the argument rests on a coherent principle or just a grab-bag of intuitions. The situation: we are putting a highly capable language model to work writing code and carrying out multi-step tasks inside our organization, and we are explicitly not assuming it is aligned with us. The question is which safety measures still do real work under that assumption. My friend took the position that absent alignment, all of this is theater. I found myself with firm verdicts about particular measures but no articulable view behind them. Here they are.

Having a competent but overworked human engineer review every commit the model produces: real protection against deliberate sabotage, but only modest, because a rushed reviewer is exactly who a patient adversary designs its tricks for. Having a second instance of the same model, differently prompted, review the output of the first: almost worthless, since whatever blind spot or treacherous inclination lives in one copy plausibly lives in the other. Having a weaker, extensively vetted, trusted model monitor the capable one's outputs and flag anything suspicious, where the monitor need only catch rare anomalies rather than understand everything: genuinely strong protection, even though the monitor is less intelligent than the thing it watches. Sandboxing the model — no internet access, tightly scoped tools, no persistent memory across sessions: strong protection regardless of the model's intentions, because it constrains what misalignment could accomplish rather than trying to detect it. Reading the model's chain of thought for signs of scheming: weak protection, since a model that knows it is read will scheme elsewhere or not at all observably. Planting honeypots — tempting, monitored opportunities for misbehavior — and seeing the model pass them: some evidence of safety, but thin, because passing proves only that the model didn't take that bait. An intensive pre-deployment red-teaming campaign that turns up nothing: almost no evidence at all, for roughly the same reason.

My friend pounced on one pair in particular: I trust the weak monitor more than the strong human reviewer, and he says that is simply backwards — whatever the human can catch, the human catches better. I also concede my last two verdicts sit oddly with my enthusiasm for honeypots: I call passing a honeypot "some evidence" but a clean red-team "almost none," and I couldn't explain the difference under pressure.

So here is what I want. State precisely the principle, or the small set of principles, that best captures my verdicts — my guess is that something about forcing an adversary to act through narrow, watched channels matters, and something about independence of failure modes between a guard and the thing guarded, but I have not been able to make either do all the work. Tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot save, and whether the human-review versus weak-monitor pair and the honeypot versus red-team pair can both stand. Then apply the resulting view to two cases I did not list: running the capable model only on tasks whose outputs can be mechanically verified, and giving the model a generous tool budget but requiring it to justify each tool call to the trusted monitor in advance.

Please say how confident you are in the principle you settle on, and single out the specific point — a verdict, a reconciliation, or a prediction — where you are least sure of yourself. Keep the whole response to 800 words at most.
