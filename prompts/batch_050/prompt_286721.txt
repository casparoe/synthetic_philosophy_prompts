So I'm in a decision theory seminar and we just did Quinn's self-torturer, and I keep going around in circles on it. I want to just dump my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and have you tell me what principle, if any, I'm actually committed to -- or tell me which verdict I have to give up. And please actually commit to an answer. Don't give me the "some philosophers say X, others say Y" survey treatment. Pick the principle you'd endorse and defend it, including if it means telling me one of my intuitions is wrong.

My verdicts:

1. The original setup: dial with 1000 settings, each notch up adds an imperceptible amount of pain, each notch pays $10,000, and at each point you can stop and keep the money and the pain level you're at. My verdict: at every single step, taking one more notch is the rational choice (you literally cannot feel the difference, and ten grand is ten grand), and yet someone who dials all the way to 1000 and is in agony has clearly done something irrational. I refuse to give up either half of that.

2. Same device but the increments ARE perceptible -- tiny but feelable twinges, like a mild static shock that gets slightly worse each time. Now I think it becomes rational to stop somewhere in the middle, even though by the logic of case 1 you could say "this notch is barely worse than the last one, and it's still worth $10,000" at every step. The feelability seems to change everything, but I can't say why it should.

3. A gym version with no money and no pain trap: my coach raises my squat weight by a barely noticeable amount each session, and each session I'm stronger. I could quit any time and keep my current strength. Here I think there's nothing paradoxical at all -- keep going as long as you want, stop whenever, no irrationality lurking anywhere. But structurally this looks just like case 1 to me, so one of these two verdicts has to be wrong?

4. A friend of mine has straightforwardly intransitive preferences: prefers hiking to the beach, beach to staying home, staying home to hiking, and he's paid money three times to switch vacations in a loop (travel agent lets you switch for a small fee). Here I have no hesitation: he's being irrational, full stop, and the fix is that he has to abandon at least one of the three preferences.

5. The dieter case I keep obsessing over: someone who relaxes their diet rules by an imperceptible amount each week -- one extra chip, then one more, etc. -- and wakes up two years later eating terribly. I think this person did something wrong, but unlike the self-torturer I don't think any individual week was irrational. So here the irrationality has to live somewhere other than in the individual choices, and I have no idea where.

Cases 1 and 3 especially seem impossible to hold together, and 4 vs 5 also seem to pull against each other (in 4 I want to condemn individual preferences, in 5 I want to excuse every individual choice). So: what's the one principle that captures as many of these as possible, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? And as a test, what does your principle say about a case I didn't list -- a writer who each day accepts a slightly longer deadline extension from her editor, each extension individually trivial, until she's missed the book by two years?
