I'm a PhD student in political theory, and next month I'm presenting the core chapter of my dissertation at a political philosophy workshop. The chapter defends metric pluralism about egalitarian justice: the equality-of-what question has no single correct answer, because each candidate metric — welfare, resources, capabilities — tacitly presupposes an institutional setting, so the right move is to assign metrics by domain (resources for tax-and-transfer design, capabilities for health and disability policy, welfare-sensitive measures wherever adaptive preferences are the live problem). My argument for this is that a metric of equality is better understood as a decision procedure for an institution than as a theory of the good, so demanding one global metric conflates distinct institutional tasks. The audience: two capability theorists working in the Sen–Nussbaum tradition, one Dworkinian resource egalitarian, and a welfare economist broadly sympathetic to Arneson's opportunity-for-welfare view. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the seven or so objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered roughly by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased in the vocabulary its author would use (conversion factors and adaptive preference from the capability theorists; ambition-sensitivity and endowment-insensitivity from the Dworkinian; interpersonal comparisons from the economist), with what I should concede up front, a two-minute reply to each, and a note on where each reply is soft. Finish with the one question I should most hope nobody asks.
