In a recent graduate seminar on mental causation, I set my students a thought experiment borrowed from Hofstadter's discussion of symbol-level dynamics — the "careenium" — and I was dissatisfied with the answers I received. I would like a careful explanation that I can distribute at our next meeting.

The careenium is a sealed system containing two sorts of objects. At the micro-level there are vast numbers of tiny, fast-moving particles ("simms"), whose trajectories are wholly fixed by deterministic collision dynamics. At the macro-level there are a few dozen large, heavy balls ("simmballs"). Each simmball is nothing over and above a densely packed swarm of simms; its center-of-mass motion is simply the aggregate of the motions of the simms composing it. Crucially, the system can be arranged so that the simmballs' interactions reliably track abstract, rule-governed patterns — something like symbol manipulation — and an observer working at the macro-level can state generalizations of the form: whenever simmball A strikes simmball B under conditions C, simmball D moves off in direction θ.

Here is the conceptual crux I want explained. Asked which level does the real causal work, my students split into three camps: (i) only the simms ever push anything, so simmball-level "causation" is a useful fiction; (ii) simmballs genuinely push each other around, since the macro-generalizations support counterfactual and explanatory practices that no simm-level description can economically replicate; and (iii) both levels are fully causal, which appears to court systematic overdetermination of every macro-outcome.

Please explain, as precisely as the material allows, how this puzzle replicates the structure of the causal exclusion argument against nonreductive mental causation, and where — if anywhere — the analogy breaks down. In particular, the explanation should address three points. First, whether a causal closure principle at the simm level, combined with the claim that each simmball-event is constituted by (rather than strictly identical to) some configuration of simm-events, suffices to exclude macro-level causal efficacy, or whether constitution rather than identity changes the dialectic. Second, whether counterfactual or interventionist accounts of causation vindicate camp (ii) without violating micro-determinism, and what "downward causation" could intelligibly mean in this system beyond metaphor. Third, whether the symbol level in the careenium is in a better or worse dialectical position than propositional attitudes are with respect to the neural substrate — that is, whether the careenium actually dissolves the exclusion problem for mental causation or merely relabels it.
