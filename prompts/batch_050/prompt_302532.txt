Here's the puzzle. Take how inheritance is typically taxed (I'm thinking of something like the US system, but describe it roughly as I lay it out even if the details are off somewhere). Money you earn from working gets taxed as income to you. Money you win in the lottery gets taxed as income to you. But money you recieve from a dead parent is, in most of these systems, treated totally differently: the tax (if any) is levied on the dead person's estate as a lump, only kicks in above a very high threshold, and once it's paid the heir owes nothing -- the inheritance never even counts as the heir's income. Meanwhile there are all these odd structural features: a seperate exemption for gifts made while alive, often a rule that unrealized capital gains just get wiped clean for tax purposes at death, and the whole thing only bites on estates way above what most people will ever leave. Some countries instead tax the recipient on what they recieve, more like income. So we have two designs chasing the same revenue that seem to embody completely different pictures of what's going on.

What I want to know: what does the estate-tax design presuppose about who the wealth actually belongs to? Or that it belongs to the dead person, who no longer has rights, so the state may take a cut but the heir's windfall is somehow morally invisible? Or that recieving unearned wealth is a different category of event from earning it, even though the heir did no more work than the lottery winner? Notice the tension: the lottery winner and the heir are in exactly the same position -- money fell on them from the sky -- but the tax system treats one as having income and the other as having nothing. Reconstruct the theory (or theories) that would make sense of that asymmetry. Which rival views about ownership, desert, and the family does the practice quietly rule out?
