I direct underwriting at a mid-sized specialty insurer. Our actuarial models assign each risk a probability -- say, a 4% chance that a given commercial property floods within the policy term -- and my underwriters, who see several dozen files a week and have perhaps ten minutes per file, then set premiums. The standing rule has been to trust the model, but underwriters constantly want to adjust: they visited the property and the drains looked bad, the client's records seemed incomplete, the neighboring building flooded last spring. I need a written procedure, grounded in the philosophical principle associated with David Lewis that rational credence should defer to known objective chances, that tells an underwriter when the price must simply reflect the model's number, when case-level information licenses a deviation, and -- crucially -- when the model's output stops being a chance worth deferring to at all, as when the model was trained on a decade of unusually mild weather. For each step, the procedure should state what counts as sufficient grounds to proceed, mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated, and say plainly what it cannot settle. Then apply it to three cases: an experienced underwriter with a strong hunch contradicting the model; new information the model could not have incorporated, such as the city having just rebuilt the storm drains; and two in-house models that disagree. At least one application should yield an answer my underwriters will dislike.
