I sit on the sanctions committee of my state's professional engineering society. Three years ago a bridge designed by one of our member firms collapsed during construction and two workers died. The investigation just wrapped up and the finding is awkward: nobody did anything sanctionable. Over six years, dozens of engineers and managers each made one small, individually defensible call -- trim a safety margin here, accept a cheaper inspection regime there, reorganize review so no single person ever saw the whole picture -- and the sum of those calls was a bridge that fell down.

The committee is split. Half say we have to censure the firm itself, since someone must answer for two deaths and if not the firm then no one does. Half say blame without a blameworthy person is a category mistake: a firm cant intend anything or feel guilt, and censuring it just punishes current staff and shareholders who did nothing, while the people whose decisions added up to this have all moved on.

Act as a philosopher who advises professional bodies. Think carefully, and start by laying out the strongest version of each side before you commit to anything. Then draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote: a concrete bottom line (censure the firm, decline to, or censure with conditions) with reasoning both camps could actually engage. Whatever you recommend, give me your real answer to the underlying puzzle: does it even make sense to blame an organization rather than the people inside it, and if so, what exactly is the blame attaching to -- the incentive structure, the culture, the org chart? Take the losing side's best point seriously rather than waving it off.
