I run a 3rd-year undergrad seminar on decision theory (about 18 students, one 80-minute session) and we're finishing a unit on Newcomb-ish reasoning. I want an in-class activity built around the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery -- quick refresher if needed: in the first, 20 people each privately decide whether to enter, and the prize goes to the entrant only if exactly one person enters; in the second, entries are free and unlimited but the prize gets divided by the total number of entries. The activity should make students feel the strange bind at the heart of superrationality: that "we all reason the same way" is at once the argument for restraint and the thing that makes you want to exploit the situation, since if everyone really does what you do, you control everyone's choice by controlling your own. Design it as two rounds with a twist between them (e.g. in one round students may publicly announce how they'll randomize, in the other they stay silent) so the difference between merely hoping for symmetric reasoning and having common knowledge of it becomes visceral rather than a footnote. Include rules, a points-based payoff scheme with no real money, a timeline, a debrief mapping each round onto Hofstadter's setup, what will predictably go wrong the first time (someone will just flood the lottery, I assume), and an honest note on what a classroom can't reproduce about the original thought experiments.
