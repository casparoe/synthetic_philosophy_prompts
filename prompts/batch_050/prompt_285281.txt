So a friend of mine works for a mid-sized European city (I'd rather not say which) that's piloting something they're calling a "civic aggregation platform," and we got into an argument about whether Rousseau would have loved or hated it, so I'm crowdsourcing a verdict. The idea is roughly this: instead of electing a city council every few years, residents continuously answer policy questions on an app, and a machine learning system clusters their answers, filters out what it classifies as narrow self-interest or captured-by-a-lobby preferences, and outputs what the designers literally call the synthetic general will on each issue. Participation in the deliberation phase is deliberately structured so citizens can't see each other's arguments or coordinate beforehand, which the designers pitch as preventing faction-formation. And here's the kicker: residents who repeatedly refuse to comply with the platform's outputs (say, ignoring a waste-sorting rule the system approved) face escalating nudges and eventually small fines, and one of the designers defended this to my friend by explicitly gesturing at Rousseau's notorious claim in Book I, chapter 7 of the Social Contract (The Sovereign) that someone who refuses to obey the general will can be compelled to do so by the whole community, and that this compulsion is somehow a way of making him free.

So: if you could sit Rousseau down in front of this thing, what would his verdict be? I'm not asking for a generic "he'd be skeptical of technology" take. Walk through the specific design choices -- replacing elected deputies with continuous direct input (Book III, chapter 15 seems relevant here), the algorithm doing the filtering rather than the citizens themselves doing it in their own heads, the ban on citizens communicating during deliberation (which the designers defend with the passage in Book II, chapter 3 about citizens having no communication with each other), and the fines-for-noncompliance bit. Which parts would he recognize as his own ideas, which would he recoil from, and is the system's whole premise -- that the general will is something you can compute from individual inputs rather than something each citizen has to actually will -- one he could even make sense of? He opens the book saying man is born free; and everywhere he is in chains, so presumably he has some thoughts about an app fining people into freedom.

One thing I care about: Rousseau interpretation is famously a minefield, so please be honest about which parts of your answer are solidly grounded in the text and which parts hinge on a contested reading (e.g., the whole totalitarian-vs-liberal Rousseau fight, or what he actually meant about deliberation and communication). Don't present one scholarly camp's reading as the settled consensus -- flag where people who actually know this stuff disagree.
