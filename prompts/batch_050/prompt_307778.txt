I'm in twelfth grade and I've volunteered to give the main talk at my school's philosophy club next month -- twenty minutes, then open discussion. My thesis is a negative one: I want to argue that quantum suicide and quantum immortality thought experiments prove too much, because the reasoning that supposedly shows you should expect to survive any quantum Russian roulette, given the many-worlds interpretation, would equally show that you should accept quantum gambles for trivial stakes -- a machine that kills you in most branches but pays you five dollars in the survivors. Since almost nobody accepts the small-stakes conclusion, I claim the arguments must be smuggling in a mistake about what "measure of existence" means for a persisting first-person self, and that the feeling of obviousness in the lethal case comes from the stakes, not from the physics. The audience is about fifteen students whose knowledge is mostly pop-science (many of them have seen the many-worlds idea in TV shows and will assume it's settled physics), plus our faculty advisor, who has a philosophy background and will know the real literature on personal identity and on whether probability even makes sense under many-worlds. Prepare me for the question period: the six or seven objections I'll actually get, ordered by how much damage they'd do to my thesis, each phrased the way that person would phrase it -- distinguishing what a pop-science-fluent classmate would ask from what the advisor would ask -- with a reply I can give in under a minute each, a note on what I should concede up front, and a flag on whichever reply is my weakest. Please also identify the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and tell me honestly whether my "prove too much" move actually lands or whether it just pushes the problem one step back.
