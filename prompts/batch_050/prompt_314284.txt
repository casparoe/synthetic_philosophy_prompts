I have just taken over as coordinator of a small donor circle -- seven members, roughly $400,000 to allocate twice a year, proposals nominated by the members themselves, no professional staff. At our last meeting we adopted the "importance, neglectedness, tractability" framework as our scoring rubric, and I have been asked to turn it into an actual working procedure. In preparing, I read the 80,000 Hours article "A framework for comparing global problems in terms of expected impact," and one passage keeps nagging at me:

"A challenge of any framework of this kind will be that carefully chosen 'narrow' problems tend to do better than broadly defined ones. For example, 'combating malaria' will look more pressing than 'global health' because malaria is a particularly promising health problem to work on. Similarly, improving health in Kenya is going to look more impressive than improving health in Costa Rica. There's nothing wrong with these findings -- but they could create a misleading impression if a broadly defined problem is compared with a narrowly defined one. If someone were motivated they could make a problem look more or less pressing by defining it differently -- and this is something to be aware of in interpreting these scores."

Please act as a philosopher who also has practical experience in grant evaluation. Think carefully, and before drafting anything, brainstorm the ways a rubric like ours can be gamed or misapplied by well-meaning nominators. Then address the following:

1. Interpret the passage. Explain precisely why a narrowly defined problem scores better under this framework -- which of the three factors the manipulation inflates, and through what mechanism. The same article defines solvability as follows: "if we doubled the resources dedicated to solving this problem, what fraction of the problem would we expect to solve?" Take that definition literally in your answer, and say whether the narrowness problem is a defect in the framework itself or only in how it gets applied.

2. Write the procedure: the questions my members answer, in order, when scoring a proposal; what counts as an adequate answer to each; what to do when a factor resists numerical estimation; and where the procedure must stop and hand the decision back to judgment -- marked honestly as judgment, not disguised as arithmetic. It has to be runnable by seven volunteers in a single evening.

3. State what the procedure, and the framework it encodes, presupposes philosophically: about the independence of the three factors, about diminishing returns to resources already committed, and about the comparability of good done across very different domains. Where would a member who rejects one of those presuppositions have to dissent from the outcome even while accepting every individual step?

4. Apply the procedure to two of our actual nominations: a bednet distribution proposal scoped to a single named district (well-evidenced, cheap, very narrow) and a member's cherished local arts education charity (diffuse effects, already heavily supported in our town). I expect the procedure to rank the first above the second -- show me exactly where that ranking is produced, and tell me which steps a dissenting member could legitimately contest.
