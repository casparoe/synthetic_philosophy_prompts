There's an argument that comes up constantly among historians and in my reading group and never seems to move: would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler? The great-man side points to Hitler's specific decisions -- the timing of Barbarossa, the refusal to ever retreat, the Holocaust as basically his personal project -- and says no Hitler, no Third Reich. The structuralists point to Versailles, the Depression, the stab-in-the-back myth, widespread antisemitism, and say some ultranationalist revanchist regime was coming no matter what, just with different people in charge and maybe a different name. My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because the two sides secretly disagree about what makes a historical counterfactual true at all: one side treats "what would have happened" as a question about which individual decisions were load-bearing, the other as a question about which macro-conditions constrained whoever was in charge. If that's right, the dispute is at least partly verbal and no new archival evidence ends it. So: is my diagnosis right, or is there a real substantive disagreement buried in there? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress -- a better account of historical counterfactuals, more comparative cases (Mussolini's Italy, failed fascist movements elsewhere?), or is the question just ill-posed? I want the diagnosis, not a verdict on the history. Under 350 words.
