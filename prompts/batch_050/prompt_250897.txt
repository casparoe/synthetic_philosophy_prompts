I'm a software engineer (backend, mostly infrastructure) with no formal philosophy training, but I've recently fallen down a rabbit hole on weakness of will and hyperbolic discounting. It started when I realized that every productivity system I've ever built for myself — site blockers, scheduled payouts, a cron job that locks my laptop at 11pm — has been defeated by exactly one adversary: me, twelve hours later, with full credentials. A friend pointed me to the vocabulary — preference reversal, present bias, precommitment, Ulysses contracts, the difference between a naïve discounter who trusts his future self and a sophisticated one who doesn't — and I can't stop seeing my own behavior as a distributed-systems problem where the nodes disagree about what they want.

Write the README and changelog for a fictional commitment-device app called Mast, in the deadpan voice of a real open-source project: version numbers, feature list, bug fixes, deprecations, an FAQ, a pricing section, and an "Adversary Model" section written like a security whitepaper. About 700–800 words. The humor has to come from getting the psychology and philosophy right, not from random absurdity. A few things I'd love to see handled this way: the changelog should show the same feature added, removed, and re-added across versions, so that the developers' own preference reversal is visible in the commit history; the FAQ should quietly extract each user's personal discount rate (for instance, an option to buy back a forfeited deposit at 80 cents on the dollar, with the app updating its estimate of you every time you do it); the pricing tiers should distinguish the Naïve plan from the Sophisticated plan, where the sophisticated user pays more precisely because he knows he'll defect and therefore wants locks he can't pick; and the Adversary Model section should treat the authenticated account holder as the threat actor without ever dropping the professional tone.

Two constraints. First, stay deadpan throughout — no winking, no fourth-wall jokes; the app should feel plausibly real for as long as possible, so that a specialist laughs because the satire is accurate and a layperson just thinks it's a very strange startup. Second, end with a "Known Issues" section that is, without saying so, a list of the genuinely open problems: whether the "true" preference is the earlier one or the later one, whether locking yourself out of your own account is self-governance or just one self coercing another, and whether acting against your better judgment is even a failure of rationality in the first place — but all of it phrased as bug reports the maintainers have marked "wontfix."
