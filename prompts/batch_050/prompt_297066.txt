Ok so last night I got into this long dumb argument with my friend Marcus that started over beer and ended with us yelling about math. We're both on the little committee at my job that hands out our company's annual community grants -- it's about $40k a year, we get maybe 25-30 applications from local nonprofits and individuals, and the three of us score them and average the scores. Real formal, I know.

Anyway the fight was about this: Marcus said our scoring should just be "most total good done per dollar," and I said no, a dollar helping someone who's already doing fine counts for less than a dollar helping someone in real trouble, even if the raw benefit is the same. He said that's just jealousy dressed up as ethics, and then I brought up the thing I'd read about where if you take that equality idea too far, it implies that making everyone equally miserable is in one respect BETTER than a world where some people are better off, which is obviously nuts. He hadn't heard of that and accused me of making it up. I'm pretty sure it's a real thing in the literature (the "leveling down objection"?) and that the view I was groping toward is called prioritarianism -- benefits matter more the worse off the recipient is, but dragging the better-off down doesn't count as a gain in any respect.

So here's what I actually want: turn that into a scoring procedure our committee can actually run. Constraints: we have about 90 minutes per meeting, none of us are philosophers, we get a one-page application plus a budget for each proposal, and our current rubric is basically vibes. I want:

1. A step-by-step procedure for scoring a proposal, in order, that builds in the "worse-off recipient = benefit counts more" weighting WITHOUT ever letting "this reduces inequality" score points on its own. That distinction is the whole ballgame for me -- the procedure has to make it impossible for leveling-down to look good.
2. A concrete weighting scheme (I don't care if it's rough -- tiers, multipliers, whatever) plus instructions for what to do when we can't tell how well-off the beneficiaries actually are, which happens a lot.
3. The points where the procedure has to hand things back to human judgment, flagged honestly, because I suspect there are several.
4. Run it on these three cases so I can see it working: (a) a job-training program for people leaving prison vs. a coding camp for kids at a wealthy suburban school, same cost, camp claims slightly more total "outcomes"; (b) a proposal to fund wheelchair repairs at one underfunded clinic vs. one that would cancel a popular but mediocre arts program and split the savings among food banks; (c) a case where two proposals produce identical raw benefit numbers but go to very differently situated recipients. For at least one of these, tell me if the procedure spits out an answer that feels wrong, and whether that's a bug in the procedure or in my gut.
5. A short section on what this procedure just can't do, so I can pre-empt Marcus when he inevitably finds an edge case.

Also please note where prioritarianism-as-I've-described-it might be a garbled version of the actual view, becuase I learned most of this from a podcast and one Wikipedia tab. Write it in American English, and keep it usable by people who think "utility" is the power company.
