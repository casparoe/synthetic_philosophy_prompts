I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on global priorities and the ethics of future generations, and I co-run an interdisciplinary reading group at my department (philosophers, economists, a couple of computer scientists -- about 12 regulars, all grad students). This term we've been circling the so-called hinge of history hypothesis: roughly, the claim that we may be living at an unusually pivotal moment, where decisions taken within the coming decades could lock in, or foreclose, very long-run trajectories for civilization, and that this would make our era disproportionately important to influence compared to most others. In our last session the group got stuck in a familiar pattern: half the room treated the hypothesis as almost obviously true given developments in AI and other potentially trajectory-shaping technologies, and the other half treated it as a self-flattering bias that nearly every generation could have indulged in (surely the industrial revolution, or the invention of agriculture, or the first nuclear tests, looked equally hinge-like to people at the time). The discussion stayed entirely at the level of dueling intuitions, and I came away thinking the problem is that nobody in the room had ever had to actually act on a judgment of pivotalness rather than just assert one.

I'd like you to design a structured activity I can run in a single 75-minute session that makes participants feel, in their own choices, what it is like to allocate influence across time under genuine uncertainty about which moments are hinges. My thought is that it should use a constructed fictional world rather than real history, precisely so that nobody can settle things with historical knowledge they happen to have: something like giving small teams a timeline of a fictional civilization with brief event descriptions, a fixed budget of "influence points" they can invest in particular eras, and some mechanism by which the facilitator reveals which investments actually compounded into long-run effects and which dissipated. I want the rules or payoff structure arranged so that the central difficulties arise in play before anyone names them -- the temptation to concentrate on dramatic crisis moments, the fact that hinge-like moments may only be identifiable in retrospect, the way judgments of pivotalness depend on contestable assumptions about contingency and lock-in, and the hindsight problem that makes every era's inhabitants feel pivotal. Whatever mechanism you design for determining which interventions mattered should encode those features deliberately rather than leaving them to facilitator improvisation.

Please give me everything I would need to actually run it: the setup and materials (including the structure of the fictional timeline and how detailed the event descriptions should be), the allocation and reveal rules, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments participants will have experienced onto the real philosophical debate -- including the skeptical position that the hinge hypothesis is an instance of a predictable presentist bias, and what, if anything, could distinguish a justified hinge claim from an unjustified one. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (for example, participants gaming the reveal structure, or the reveal feeling rigged to whichever outcome it produces) and how to handle it. Finally, be honest about the activity's limits: the multipliers in the fictional world are chosen by the designer, so the activity presumably cannot settle whether our actual century is a hinge -- say clearly what it can and cannot establish, and what the facilitator must supply in the debrief to connect the game back to live questions about AI, institutional design, and where a young researcher's efforts are actually best spent.
