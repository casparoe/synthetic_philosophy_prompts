In the mental imagery debate (Kosslyn vs. Pylyshyn), everyone on both sides seems to agree on the behavioral data: people scan longer across imagined maps when the imagined distances are longer, mental rotation times scale with rotation angle, and so on. Yet Pylyshyn thinks none of this settles anything because a propositional representation plus the right tacit knowledge could reproduce the timing patterns. Now Kosslyn's side got what looks like a boost when fMRI studies showed retinotopically organized activation in early visual cortex during imagery, and the size of the activated region tracked the imagined size of the object.

Here's what I want explained, at a gut level rather than with heavy formalism: does neural evidence like that actually do better against Pylyshyn's challenge than the reaction-time data did, or is it vulnerable to the exact same "format isn't shown by the data" move, just relocated from behavior to brain? And if neither behavioral nor neural evidence can distinguish a picture-like representation from a propositional one that's merely *functioning like* a picture, what would a genuinely distinguishing experiment even have to look like? Please explain whether the debate is empirically resolvable in principle, or whether it's partly a dispute about what counts as an explanation of imagery in the first place.
