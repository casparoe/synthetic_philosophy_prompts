I help run a little reading group that spun off from our local ACX/LessWrong-ish meetup crowd, and last night two members got properly stuck into each other over something that started as a joke and ended up eating the whole session. The setup: Mara was packing up her kitchen for a flat move and had swaddled her wine glasses in about six layers of bubble wrap. Dan dropped one (on carpet, it survived, no harm done) and then announced, half-seriously, that the packed glass wasn't fragile, since it wouldn't have broken when struck. Mara said that was obviously mad -- you wrap glasses *because* they're fragile. From there it escalated. Reconstructing the exchange as best I can:

(1) Dan's opening move: if being fragile just means "would break if struck," then a glass encased in bubble wrap isn't fragile while packed, and neither is one sitting safely in a display cabinet under the care of a paranoid owner. Since both verdicts are absurd, the simple conditional gloss on dispositions has to go. (2) Mara countered that this is just loose talk, not deep metaphysics: when we call the packed glass fragile we mean it would break under *ordinary* handling, and "fragile" is context-sensitive the way "tall" is. She added that Dan's alternative is circular anyway -- he wants to say fragility is "would break if struck, unless masked," but what's a mask except whatever stops the breaking? You can't define the exception by pointing back at the thing it's an exception to. (3) Dan replied that masks are perfectly identifiable on their own -- padding, antidotes, blast shields -- and the underlying causal basis (the glass's molecular structure) is untouched the whole time; the packing interferes with the *manifestation*, not the disposition. He then tried to split two cases: the bubble-wrapped glass versus a glass watched over by a guardian angel who would instantly alter its molecular bonds to make it shatterproof the moment anyone struck it. In the first case the fragility is masked; in the second it's genuinely removed (he called it "finked," a word he got from some philosophy podcast). Both glasses fail the conditional test, but for completely different reasons, which he said shows dispositions are real intrinsic features and the conditional analysis needs repair rather than the concept. (4) Mara's comeback: this all makes fragility empirically untestable, since any failure to break can be blamed on a hidden mask or an angel, and it's a bizarre consequence that whether her kitchenware is fragile depends on the dispositions of hypothetical angels. Her closing shot was that "fragile" is just useful shorthand for a vague cluster of conditionals, and the hunt for its metaphysical essence is a confusion about language. Dan's closing shot was that her shorthand mysteriously lines up with what physics says about molecular structure, and that can't be an accident. At that point the pub kicked us out and they asked me to referee, which I absolutely cannot do.

Please adjudicate. I'd like you to answer as a metaphysician who knows the dispositions literature (finks, masks, antidotes, the lot) inside out but can write for smart non-specialists -- think carefully and maybe sketch the dialectic in a sentence or two before giving verdicts. Go through moves (1) to (4) and say who wins each and why, and then say who's more right overall. I'm especially interested in: whether the circularity worry in (2) is actually fatal; whether the mask/fink distinction in (3) is as clean as Dan thinks (does the angel case remove the disposition, or does it just guarantee the stimulus never gets its normal grip?); and what, if anything, both of them are missing -- e.g. whether they should be seperating the semantics of the word "fragile" from the metaphysics of the property, or whether the conditional was ever the right thing to analyse in the first place. Also, is there any practical stake here at all, or is this the purest pub philosophy ever produced? Please write in British English.
