So here's a thought experiment. Leibniz famously hated Newtonian gravity because it seemed to demand action at a distance with no mechanism, and his own alternative was the pre-established harmony: things don't actually causally push each other around, God just synchronized all the monads at creation like a room full of perfectly wound clocks.

Now fast-forward to 1964 and Bell's theorem: quantum entanglement produces correlations that no local hidden-variable theory can reproduce, and the later experiments (Aspect and friends) back the quantum predictions. The standard menu of responses is roughly: give up locality, give up the statistical independence of measurement settings (superdeterminism), go retrocausal, or go many-worlds.

Question: if you resurrected Leibniz and patiently walked him through the EPR setup, Bell's inequality, and the experimental results, where do you think he'd land? I'd like you to actually reason through the following rather than gesture at them:

- Is pre-established harmony basically superdeterminism avant la lettre? If so, is Bell a vindication of Leibniz rather than a problem for him? Or is there a real disanalogy — Leibniz's monads don't interact at all, whereas modern superdeterminism is usually a claim about ordinary causal stuff conspiring to correlate the measurement settings with the hidden variables?
- Leibniz insisted on the principle of sufficient reason: no fact without a sufficient reason. Does Bell force us into brute correlations with no underlying mechanism, and would he find that MORE horrifying than nonlocality — i.e., might he actually swallow action at a distance to avoid unexplainable coincidences?
- He also held that this is the best of all possible worlds, chosen for maximizing richness of phenomena given simplicity of laws. On that metric, does a local-but-branching many-worlds universe beat a nonlocal single world, or vice versa?

Feel free to push back on the framing if you think I've misrepresented Leibniz somewhere — that's part of the excercise.
