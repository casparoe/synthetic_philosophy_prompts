I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I spend a fair amount of time on forums where people apply expected-value reasoning to animal welfare, and I recently drafted a blog comment defending what I've seen called the scale argument for taking insect suffering seriously. A colleague replied that my argument proves too much, and after a week of turning it over I can't tell whether she's exposed a flaw in my reasoning or just made me uncomfortable. Here is the exchange reconstructed as a single numbered argument, with her objection folded in. (1) When we are uncertain which beings are sentient, the rational policy is to weigh harms by expected value: probability of sentience, times the badness of the harm if sentient, times the number of individuals affected. (2) Insects deserve a small but non-negligible probability of sentience, given nociception, associative learning, dopamine-mediated reward, and sleep-like states; call it 1 in 1,000 to be conservative. (3) Insects exist at extraordinary scale: on the order of 10^18 alive at any moment, more than a trillion farmed annually for feed, plus vast numbers affected by agriculture. (4) Multiplying through, the expected suffering of insects exceeds that of the roughly 80 billion vertebrates we farm each year, whose probability of sentience is near 1 but whose numbers are far smaller; so insect welfare deserves serious priority. (5) But nothing in (1) through (3) is specific to insects: the same form of argument applies to any system with a nonzero probability of sentience and sufficient scale, including soil bacteria (on the order of 10^30 cells) and, on certain functionalist or panpsychist theories, the transistors in the world's server farms. (6) Since nobody can prove the probability of bacterial sentience is exactly zero, and the numbers swamp any plausible ratio of probabilities, the expected-value calculation ranks bacteria above insects and insects above chickens, so moral concern ends up monopolized by whatever is most numerous while having any chance at all of mattering. (7) That conclusion is absurd -- it is structurally a Pascal's-mugging-style result -- so the scale argument in (4) must be defective, and expected-value reasoning cannot be allowed to license it.

My problem is that I want to keep (4) and reject (7), but I can't locate the step where my colleague's extension of my own premises goes wrong, if it does. I'd like you to respond as a moral philosopher with real expertise in decision theory under uncertainty, writing for a technically literate non-specialist. Please think carefully rather than giving the first answer that comes to mind: start by sketching at least two rival candidate diagnoses of where the argument breaks (or the case that it doesn't break and one simply has to bite a bullet), then commit to the diagnosis you find strongest and explain why the rejected step looked plausible in the first place. In particular, I want you to price the alternatives honestly: what it costs to restrict or abandon the expected-value machinery in (1) given that it's the only decision procedure I trust in my day job, what it costs to deny that the inference in (5)–(6) really parallels the insect case, and what it costs to impose some floor below which tiny probabilities get ignored, since such a floor seems like it would also defuse arguments I take seriously about low-probability catastrophic risks. If forced to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and the absurdity of (6) than keep the bacteria conclusion, but I don't want the insect case rescued by special pleading, and I'd like to know exactly what I'm paying whichever way I go.
