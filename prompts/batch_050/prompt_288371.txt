Analyze the reception of continental drift and the rise of plate tectonics — Wegener's proposal in the 1910s, its widespread rejection by mid-century geologists, and its rapid acceptance in the 1960s following paleomagnetic work on magnetic reversals and the seafloor-spreading hypothesis — as a test case for Kuhn's account of scientific revolutions. Structure the analysis in three parts.

First, the mapping. On Kuhn's scheme, identify what would count as the governing paradigm in mid-century earth science, which anomalies it faced, whether the discipline ever entered a state of crisis in his technical sense, and where exactly the paradigm shift is supposed to have occurred. Be careful here: several features of the episode fit Kuhn awkwardly. The old view was arguably not a single unified paradigm but a collection of adjacent research traditions — field geology, geophysics, paleontology — with different standards of evidence, and the shift was driven by new instruments and new data types rather than by a crisis internal to the old theory's own problem-solving.

Second, incommensurability. Did drift advocates and their opponents genuinely talk past each other in Kuhn's sense — disagreeing about what counts as a legitimate question, an acceptable mechanism, or even what the relevant facts were — or were they in continuous, mutually intelligible dispute over a shared body of evidence? The episode is cited on both sides of this question, so take a position. Consider, for instance, whether the demand that drift supply a physical mechanism, and the different weight given to geodetic measurements versus paleoclimatic and fossil distributions, amounts to incommensurability or to ordinary methodological disagreement conducted at a high level of rigor.

Third, the verdict in reverse. What does the episode do to Kuhn's theory? If acceptance of plate tectonics was driven by the convergence of several independent data streams within a few years, with relatively little of the generational attrition and persuasion-by-conversion Kuhn describes, is the episode an anomaly for his account, or can it be accommodated without hollowing the account out? And if Wegener was largely right for largely inadequate reasons, what does that do to the Kuhnian claim that there is no neutral algorithm of theory choice to which we can retrospectively appeal in declaring who was being rational?

Keep the analysis at the level of the theory's actual commitments; where the historical record is genuinely ambiguous or Kuhn's concepts are simply silent, say so rather than forcing a fit.
