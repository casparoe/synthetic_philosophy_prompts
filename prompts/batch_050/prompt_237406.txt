In my AI alignment reading group, we keep running into what people call the strategy-stealing assumption: roughly, that for any strategy a misaligned AI could pursue to acquire power, resources, or influence, an aligned AI under human direction could pursue an equally effective strategy in the service of good ends, so that misalignment confers no systematic advantage and safety reduces to getting the goals right. One member defends the assumption by appealing to its pedigree. The name, he points out, comes from strategy-stealing arguments in combinatorial game theory, where the reasoning is airtight. His example is Hex. Hex cannot end in a draw, so suppose the second player had a winning strategy: the first player could open with an arbitrary move and thereafter adopt that very strategy as if she were the second player, making a fresh arbitrary move whenever the strategy calls for the cell her extra stone already occupies. Since an extra stone of one's own color can never be a liability in Hex, this yields a contradiction, and the first player must have a winning strategy. He concludes that the alignment assumption inherits this solidity: whatever a misaligned system can do, a system on our side can do.

Please audit the analogy. Begin with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the two players, the extra stone, the harmlessness of the extra move, and the impossibility of a draw. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for my colleague's conclusion from those that do not. In particular, address the fact that the Hex argument is nonconstructive — it proves that a winning strategy exists without exhibiting one — and say whether anything analogous follows for the alignment claim. Finally, state whether his strong conclusion (misalignment is no inherent disadvantage, so safety just is alignment) survives once the disanalogies are on the table; if the analogy cannot carry it, propose a better one.
