I'm a novelist, maybe two-thirds through a literary thriller, and I'm stuck on the big confrontation scene. Setup: eight years ago a detective named Mara solved a famous poisoning case, and she's certain the valet did it — confession, motive, physical evidence, the whole thing held up. By any reasonable standard she *knows* who did it. Now a young journalist shows up at her door with new lab results that seem to point somewhere else entirely. Mara's response: since she knows the valet did it, she already knows any apparent evidence to the contrary has to be misleading somehow, so she won't even open the folder. The journalist thinks that's the textbook definition of closed-mindedness. Mara thinks it's just what knowing something commits you to.

Write the dialogue between them. Please don't make either one a dummy. Mara should be able to say why her position feels like plain epistemic consistency — if knowing p really does entitle you to treat future counterevidence as misleading, why is refusing to look at it irrational rather than just efficient? The journalist should press her on the gap between being entitled to *expect* the evidence to be misleading and being entitled to dismiss it *before you've seen it*, and on whether her certainty now is really being carried by the old evidence or just by itself, bootstrapped. I want them actually answering each other's points, not two little essays stapled togther, and by the end they should have pinpointed the exact step where they disagree — nobody has to capitulate, the scene plays better unsettled.

One wrinkle that matters for the book: Mara's argument has to be genuinely tempting, not crazy-sounding, because in the next chapter the reader is supposed to half-side with her right before the twist lands. So give her the strongest version of the reasoning, not the cartoon one. And don't have either of them say the phrase "dogmatism paradox" out loud — nobody talks like that.

Hard cap of 800 words, dialogue plus minimal beats/stage directions only.
