Okay so I write novels, and my current manuscript has a main character, Marguerite, who has spent twenty years not admitting to herself that her brother's drowning wasn't the accident she's always said it was. The whole arc depends on her simultaneously knowing and not-knowing, if that makes sense -- she keeps the boathouse locked, she steers conversations away from 1987, she corrects anyone who says "drowned" with "was lost." Classic self-deception, I thought. Then at my writing group this guy Theo, who did two years of a philosophy PhD before dropping out to do standup, announced that my plot is literally impossible. Not implausible. Impossible. He walked us through it and I've been picking at it for a week and I can't find where it breaks, but the conclusion can't be right because if it is, like half of literature is about something that can't exist.

His argument, as best I can reconstruct it: (1) To deceive someone, you have to get them to believe something you yourself don't believe -- the deceiver holds the truth, the victim ends up with the falsehood, and those have to be different states in some sense. (2) So if Marguerite deceives herself, she has to believe p (in her role as deceiver) and believe not-p (in her role as victim), at the same time, about the same thing. (3) But nobody can straight-up believe p and not-p simultaneously; someone who seems to is just confused, or changing the subject mid-thought, not holding two beliefs. (4) Therefore self-deception in the full, intentional sense is impossible, and everything we call that is really just wishful thinking, inattention, or ordinary bias wearing a trench coat. (5) But then he went further -- he said the same argument shows nobody can ever strategically keep themselves ignorant of anything, since that would require knowing what you're avoiding knowing. (6) And that last part is clearly false, because I personally have not opened certain emails for months now, and I arrange my whole life around it. So the argument has gone somewhere wrong by (5) at the latest, and probably earlier.

The thing is, every time I try to poke it, I end up conceding the steps. I want to keep (1) because it seems like just what deception means, and I'd really like to keep (3) because I don't want my characters to be logically impossible objects. But then Marguerite evaporates. So: which step do I actually reject? Explain why the bad step looked fine to me and Theo in the first place, and what it costs to give it up -- like, if the fix is splitting the mind into compartments that can't talk to each other, tell me what I'm committing to about whether Marguerite is one person or a committee. And if the fix is saying self-deception doesn't require any intention to deceive, just motivated sloppy belief-formation, then tell me what's left of the difference between her and someone who's merely optimistic. I need to know which version of my character is philosophically load-bearing before I write the final chapter.
