I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums — LessWrong, the EA Forum, and their various offshoots — for the better part of a decade, and one passage in the history of ethics that I keep returning to is Henry Sidgwick's discussion of population in Book IV of The Methods of Ethics (1874). There, when defining the utilitarian end, Sidgwick considers whether we ought to encourage population growth up to the point where average happiness is greatest. He answers that we should not: on his view, the right stopping point is instead the one at which the total quantity of happiness — in effect, the number of people multiplied by their average quality of life — is as large as possible, even if pushing numbers higher would lower the average. He seems to regard this as a fairly straightforward consequence of taking happiness itself, rather than its average distribution, to be the ultimate good.

What strikes me is that Sidgwick thereby endorses, in 1874 and without apparent anxiety, the precise move that Parfit's repugnant conclusion is usually taken to render intolerable: trading away arbitrarily large losses in average quality of life for a sufficiently large increase in the number of lives barely worth living.

Please take the five standard families of population axiology — the total view, the average view, critical-level (or critical-range) views, variable-value views, and person-affecting views — and rate each one out of ten on the following compound criterion: how successfully it vindicates Sidgwick's judgement in the passage above (that the optimum lies at the maximum of the total, not of the average) whilst avoiding, or at least defusing, the repugnant conclusion. A score of 10 would mean the view fully captures what Sidgwick saw and pays nothing for it; a score of 0 would mean it either abandons his judgement entirely or keeps it only by embracing the repugnant conclusion without remainder.

For each score, give a short paragraph of justification that identifies exactly where the view parts company with Sidgwick or with anti-repugnance, and state whether the parting looks like a cost or a clarification. Then address two further questions: first, whether Sidgwick's own framing — treating the addition of happy lives as unproblematically good, full stop — survives reflection on the mere addition paradox; and secondly, whether the rationalist community's tendency to treat the repugnant conclusion as a bullet worth biting rests on an argument Sidgwick already had, or on something genuinely newer. Conclude by saying which single view you would recommend to a careful modern reader of Sidgwick, and why.

Please aim for roughly 800–1,000 words in total, and write for an audience already familiar with the standard terminology of population ethics.
