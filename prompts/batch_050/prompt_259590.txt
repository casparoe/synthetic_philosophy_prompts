I serve on my department's doctoral admissions committee, and I have a decision to make that has started to feel like a philosophical puzzle. Two senior colleagues have, over a decade, compiled nearly identical track records: the applicants each has championed have thrived, and those each has opposed have mostly washed out, at about the same rate. What differs is how they arrive at their verdicts. One reads the file closely and produces a page of reasons connecting evidence to predictions, reasons that have held up when checked. The other skims the file and announces a judgment; pressed to explain, she says she has simply learned to tell, and the explanations she does offer are clearly post hoc, sometimes citing features we know to be irrelevant. This year they disagree about one applicant, and I hold the deciding vote. The obvious move is to follow the reason-giver. But I want the harder question answered: if I stipulate that their future reliability on cases like this one is exactly equal, what does the reason-giver's method actually buy us, if anything? Please give a verdict on whom I should follow and defend it, in no more than 400 words, and close by saying what would change your mind — which consideration, if established, would lead you to recommend following the other colleague, and where your own argument is most likely to break down.
