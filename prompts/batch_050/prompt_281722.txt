In the ethics seminar I TA for, we spend two weeks on Singer's drowning child and then watch the pond analogy do essentially all the argumentative work for the rest of the unit: my students are told that walking past a drowning child to save your shoes is monstrous, that donating the cost of those shoes to an effective charity saves a life just as surely, and that therefore most of us are, morally speaking, walking past drowning children every day. The analogy gets deployed to support a very demanding conclusion -- something like: you ought to keep giving, up to the point where further giving would cost you something of comparable moral significance. I want you to audit the analogy itself, not the conclusion in the abstract. Give me the explicit mapping: what in the donation case plays the role of the pond, the child, the ruining of the shoes, the wading in, the fact that you are the only passerby. Then go through the disanalogies that my students raise and sort them into the ones that genuinely threaten the intended use and the ones that are noise -- I'm thinking of things like: the rescuer's certainty that the child will be saved versus the donor's statistical and contested confidence in aid effectiveness; the one-off rescue versus an open-ended stream of children falling into ponds forever; the fact that at the pond there is exactly one agent positioned to help, whereas global poverty has billions of equally positioned bystanders (does that dilute or distribute the duty?); physical proximity and salience versus distance; and whether the analogy covertly assumes a direct causal line between my money and a saved life that real giving never has.

For each disanalogy, say whether it merely changes how the duty feels or actually changes its content or strength, and be honest about which direction each one cuts -- some of them arguably make the donation case more demanding, not less, and I want that on the table too. Then answer the load-bearing question: once the disanalogies are priced in, does the demanding conclusion still stand, and if so, is it standing on the analogy or on independent premises the analogy was only illustrating? Finally, if the pond is the wrong picture, propose the better one: is giving more like triage in an understaffed emergency room, like a standing subscription to a rescue service, like living downstream of a factory whose runoff you could filter, or something else -- and does the better analogy support the same conclusion, a weaker one, or a differently shaped one? I'd like the answer in a form I can hand to the seminar, so a correspondence table early on is welcome, and please don't just relitigate whether utilitarianism is true; keep the focus on what this particular comparison can and cannot carry.
