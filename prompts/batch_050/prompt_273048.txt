Ok so I've been working through Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments (well, a long summary of it tbh) and something is really bugging me. As I understand it, his whole moral theory runs on the "impartial spectator" idea — you judge your own conduct by imagining how a fair bystander would react to it. But where does the bystander get their standards from? They can only be built up out of the actual reactions of people in the society you live in, just with personal bias stripped out. So the impartial spectator is basically the internalized average opinion of your community.

If that's right, doesn't Smith's theory make morality just majoritarian? Like, in 18th century Glasgow the imagined spectator would've been fine with the slave trade, since pretty much everyone respectable was. So a Glasgow merchant consulting his inner spectator gets a green light. And it seems to get worse: I thought Smithian sympathy means actually feeling what the person you're observing feels, so a spectator surrounded by slaveholders would sympathize with the slaveholders at least as much as with some fringe abolitionist, and the slaveholders are the ones whose sentiments the merchant can most easily share.

So here's my question: given that Smith's framework apparently can't distinguish "what my society happens to approve of" from "what's actually right", how could he even make sense of moral progress — the abolition movement was happening during his own lifetime, after all? Is there anywhere in the theory where someone's inner spectator could tell them that their entire community is the one that's wrong, or is Smith just committed to moral conformism and we should be honest about that?
