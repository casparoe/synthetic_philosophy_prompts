Last month a close friend and I spent an entire evening arguing about hate speech regulation, and we ended the night further apart than we started. She defends the European model -- the German and Council-of-Europe style framework in which certain categories of speech are restricted in the name of human dignity, historical memory, and proportionality balancing. I lean toward the American First Amendment approach, with its hostility to viewpoint discrimination and its very narrow incitement exceptions along Brandenburg lines. We both left frustrated, and I suspect a large part of the problem is that we were arguing in a legal-philosophical vocabulary neither of us actually works in.

So I want to try the argument again in a vocabulary I do control. I am a content moderation systems designer for a mid-size social platform: I write enforcement policy, tune and evaluate classifiers, worry about precision-recall tradeoffs, false positives and their asymmetric costs, appeals queues and human review, adversarial users who probe the edges of the rules, context collapse, enforcement at scale, and jurisdictional routing of policy. That is my native conceptual apparatus.

Here is what I want. Restate the substantive disagreement between the American and European approaches to hate speech as faithfully as you can in that vocabulary -- policies, classifiers, error rates, thresholds, enforcement costs, appeals, adversarial adaptation. Which claims on each side translate cleanly? For instance, does the American position amount to setting an extremely high enforcement threshold because the false-positive cost of restricting protected speech is treated as near-catastrophic, while the European position accepts a lower threshold because it prices certain harms to dignity and social peace differently? Or is that already a distortion?

Then give me the residue report. What does not survive the translation? My suspicion is that several things will not fit: the grounding role of dignity, the marketplace-of-ideas justification, and above all the fact that these legal doctrines presuppose a state with coercive power over citizens, whereas my systems are a private actor's rules for users -- and I am not sure that asymmetry is incidental.

Finally, the verdict: when something fails to translate, whose failure is it? Is my moderation vocabulary missing concepts it would need to capture the real stakes of the legal debate, or does the residue show that part of the American-European disagreement depends on features of state power and history that simply do not generalize -- and is therefore less substantive, as a general dispute about speech and harm, than my friend and I assumed? Please be honest about where you are fudging the translation rather than smoothing it over.
