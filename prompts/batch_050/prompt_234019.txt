Write a satirical traveler's pamphlet, roughly 550 to 650 words, issued by an invented body called the International Society for the Moral Force of Conventions. Its purpose is to instruct conscientious travelers that local conventions are not mere etiquette but genuine moral obligations, binding on visitors exactly as they bind on locals. The pamphlet should therefore explain, in deadpan earnest, why the tourist in America is morally required to tip at the going rate even though no law compels it, why the tourist in Britain must queue even when no one is watching, and -- the pamphlet's proudest section -- why the tourist in a city where jaywalking is the settled local practice is morally required to jaywalk, since conventions obligate by being conventions, not by matching the visitor's home customs.

The target of the satire is the philosophical claim that conventions, as coordination equilibria, generate real moral obligations all by themselves. The humor has to come from getting the underlying theory right and following it slightly too far: the arbitrariness of the equilibrium (we could all drive on the other side; nothing about tipping at 20 percent is intrinsically better than 15), the way each person's conformity gives everyone else a reason to conform, the role of precedent and salience in settling which equilibrium obtains, the complaint that a convention everyone treats as optional is no convention at all, and the awkward question of what a convention could add morally beyond the harm of disappointing expectations it itself created. The Society's pamphleteer should marshal these ideas earnestly -- a specialist in the literature on convention should recognize each move and wince at how little had to be exaggerated.

Keep the irony deadpan but legible: no winking, no narrator stepping outside the voice, and the pamphleteer must remain internally consistent, applying the doctrine with equal confidence to tipping, queueing, and jaywalking rather than quietly treating the last case as different. Include at least one moment where the pamphlet addresses an obvious objection -- for instance, that some local conventions are themselves immoral -- and answers it in a way that stays in character while exposing the theory's weak point. By the end, a reader should be able to state precisely where the pamphlet's reasoning goes wrong (if it does) and should feel the genuine unresolved question underneath the joke: if convention alone can oblige, why does the obligation seem to evaporate exactly where the convention becomes most arbitrary, and if it cannot, what was doing the work in the cases where it seemed to bind?
