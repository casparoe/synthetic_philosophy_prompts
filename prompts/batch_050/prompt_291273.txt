I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm writing a piece on the big development-economics fight over why some nations ended up rich: institutions vs. geography vs. culture. Here's what strikes me after sitting through a bunch of panels and reading around the litreature. Every side knows the other sides' moves by heart. The institutions people wheel out settler mortality instruments and the reversal of fortune; the critics say the instruments are leaky and the historical data is thinner than advertised; the geography camp points at disease burden and landlockedness; the culture people say everyone's measuring the wrong thing entirely. Thirty years in, nobody has budged, and the citations keep circulating in the same loop. My hunch is that this stopped being a factual dispute a while ago and is really three groups holding different standards for what counts as causal evidence when you only get one run of history and can't rerun it — in which case convergence is impossible in principle. But maybe that's too pessimistic and there's a real empirical crux still open that I've missed. So: diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, methodological, bedrock intuitions, or a hidden factual question — using what each side actually argues, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress. A new kind of natural experiment? A methodological truce? A different question altogether? Please answer in British English.
