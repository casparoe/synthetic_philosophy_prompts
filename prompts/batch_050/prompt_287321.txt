Construct a thought experiment for testing the inefficacy objection to individual duties concerning collective harms: the claim that where one person's contribution makes no difference to the outcome — the emissions occur regardless, the animal was slaughtered to meet aggregate demand rather than to fill this order, the election is not decided by a single vote — that person does no wrong by contributing. The case is intended as a diagnostic instrument: it should reveal whether the objection really rests on causal inefficacy alone or smuggles in further assumptions, so stipulating the verdicts rather than generating them from the theories would defeat the purpose.

The scenario must satisfy all of the following specifications. First, it must be set in an ordinary, realistic context — a consumer choice, a workplace decision, or participation in a routine institutional practice — with no trolleys, demons, perfect predictors, or knowledge a normal person could not possess. Secondly, the inefficacy must be genuine rather than merely improbable: it must be settled, before the agent acts, that the total harm is identical whether or not she contributes. In particular, you may not rely on the standard rejoinder that her purchase, vote, or decision might tip a threshold (a supplier's order, a procurement batch, a recount); the case must be engineered so that no such tipping is possible, even in expectation. Thirdly, despite the second constraint, the moral assessment of her act must turn on a single feature of the case which you then remove in a variant — and removing it must leave her causal contribution every bit as inefficacious as before.

After presenting the scenario and its variant (the scenario itself should be a single paragraph), do the following. State, for each of three positions — expected-consequence reasoning, a complicity or participation-based account, and universalisability reasoning — what verdict it delivers on the original and on the variant, and why. Identify which feature is doing the work: what exactly changes for the theories whose verdict changes, and which theories are insensitive to it? Then say what the case establishes about the inefficacy objection itself: does it show that the objection, to succeed, must rest on causal inefficacy alone, or does it show that the objection was never really about consequences in the first place? Finally, give the strongest line of resistance available to a defender of the objection, and note whether that resistance concedes ground — for instance, by retreating from a claim about wrongness to a claim about blamelessness, or from individuals to the structure of the practice.
