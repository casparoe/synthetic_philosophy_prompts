Okay so I write novels for a living -- three published, fourth due in March -- and the cover of each one says my name. Just mine. But honestly: my editor cut the middle third of the second book and restructured the rest, my agent talked me out of the original ending of the first one, my wife read every draft and basically invented a whole subplot, and the new book exists at all because a packager sold me the pitch. Meanwhile I know ghosts who have written entire celebrity memoirs, every single word, and their name appears nowhere, not even buried in the acknowledgments. And friends in TV tell me the guild over there runs actual arbitrations with percentage thresholds for who officially counts as a writer, while the old kids' book series just printed one fake house name (Carolyn Keene style) over whichever freelancer happened to write that volume.

So what I want to know is: what theory of joint action is my industry presupposing? When the spine says the novel is by me, what view of collective intentionality makes that even true-ish -- that there's one directing mind whose plan absorbs everyone else's contribution? That authorship tracks who had final veto rather than who typed the sentences? Does ghostwriting, where you can flat-out sell your authorship by contract, presupose something inconsistent with that -- like authorship is transferable property rather than a fact about what actually happened? And are novels, TV credits, and house-name series running three different implicit theories stapled together, or one coherent one? Which rival accounts of shared intention does the practice quietly rule out, and where is it just underdetermined -- the name on the cover being a marketing convenience that presupposes nothing philosophical at all? Finally, what would actually have to change -- credits, royalties, how readers interpret a book -- if we swapped in the view that a work is authored by whatever group of people genuinely shared the intention to produce it?
