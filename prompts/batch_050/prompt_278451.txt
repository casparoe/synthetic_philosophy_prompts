Ok so I'm in a phil of mind / philosophy of psychiatry seminar and we just did the unit on delusions, mostly Capgras and the two-factor theory stuff (anomalous experience + a second deficit in belief evaluation). After class two people in my study group got into a genuinely heated argument about whether delusions are actually beliefs, and they made me promise to get an outside opinion lol. Here's the exchange as best I remember it:

(1) Dan started: take the standard Capgras patient who insists the woman in her house is an impostor who just looks exactly like her wife. He said she obviously doesn't *believe* this, because she barely acts on it. She keeps living there, doesn't call the cops, doesn't go searching for the "real" wife, doesn't ask the impostor where the real one is being kept. If I genuinely believed my partner had been replaced by a double I'd be doing all of those things immediately. So delusions fail the basic functional test for belief.

(2) Maya pushed back: first off, some patients DO act, there are cases of people attacking family members they took to be impostors, so the behavioral criterion cuts against Dan there. And when patients don't act, that can be explained by confusion, fear, passivity, being medicated in a ward, whatever -- you don't need to deny belief. She said the two-factor framework basically presupposes the doxastic reading: the first factor gives you the bizarre experience (loved one looks right but feels wrong, no affective response), and the delusion is just the subject's best explanation of that experience, adopted and maintained because the second factor (the evaluation deficit) can't shoot it down. Explanation-formation is exactly what belief-forming is.

(3) Dan's reply: careful, two-factor actually helps *him*. If the second factor is damage to a belief-evaluation system, then what makes the delusion delusional is precisely that it's decoupled from evidence evaluation -- but responsiveness to evidence is partly constitutive of belief. A state that no amount of counterevidence can touch, that sits in its own compartment while the rest of the patient's cognition is basically intact (they don't draw any of the obvious inferences from it either), isn't a belief, it's some other attitude -- an imagining, or an "acceptance," or whatever. He's not denying the patient says it sincerely; he's denying sincerity settles what kind of state it is.

(4) Maya's last move: that responsiveness test is way too strong, it would de-belief half of ordinary human cognition. Conspiracy theorists shrug off counterevidence all day, religious believers compartmentalize constantly, everyone has beliefs they'd never act on consistently. If those still count as beliefs, the Capgras patient qualifies too, and Dan is just gerrymandering the criteria so only the delusions fail.
