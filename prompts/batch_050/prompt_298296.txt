I am a novelist at work on a book in which one character's private reading habits become a neighborhood scandal, and my background reading has led me to Amartya Sen's liberal paradox: the theorem that no social decision rule can simultaneously honor the Pareto principle and grant individuals decisive authority over even one purely personal matter. What strikes me is that the theorem itself is fifty years old and mathematically uncontested, yet the secondary literature cannot agree on what it shows. One camp holds that it exposes a genuine, deep conflict between collective efficiency and individual rights. A second holds that the conflict is an artifact of encoding rights as features of social outcomes, and that it dissolves once rights are represented as constraints on procedures or game forms instead. A third treats it as a curiosity about meddlesome or nosy preferences, of interest only to people who care whether strangers are distressed by what they read in bed. My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the dispute is not about the theorem at all but about what counts as a faithful formalization of respecting a right -- a representational disagreement wearing the clothes of a substantive one. Diagnose the impasse: is it verbal or substantive, and does a hidden question about whether social choice theory evaluates outcomes or institutions do the real work? What would count as progress? Please commit to a definite diagnosis and defend it, rather than surveying the options for me to choose among.
