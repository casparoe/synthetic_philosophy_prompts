I'm homeschooling my nine-year-old and, for reasons I can't fully explain, I've been working through Locke's Some Thoughts Concerning Education as my guide. What strikes me is that he gives verdicts on tons of little scenarios, and I'm trying to figure out the rule he's actually running. Here's my summary of his calls so far: learning Latin by just conversing and reading in it, grammar drilled later or never — fine by him. A boy who hates his book but loves mucking about in the garden — don't force the book as a set task, work with his bent instead. Beating a kid for failing to memorize his lesson — absolutely not, punishment for failures of learning just breeds hatred of the subject, and anyway the rod is for obstinate rebellion, not for mistakes. Lying, though, he treats as the one fault where real severity is warranted, even early on. A child's endless why-questions — answer them seriously and never palm the kid off, since curiosity is the engine of the whole thing. Buying lots of fancy toys — against it, better that children have few playthings and make their own. And tormenting small creatures for fun — he wants that stamped out early; his worry (I'm paraphrasing a passage in the book here) is that a habit of delighting in the suffering and killing of animals gradually hardens a person even toward other people.

Now the case I actually need the verdict on. My son is a budding naturalist. He catches insects, keeps a beetle in a jar until it dies, and has started pulling legs and wings off things to, in his words, see how they work. Here's the thing: he is not a cruel kid. He's gentle with the dog, kind to his little sister, genuinely distressed when he accidentally hurts anyone. It's all curiosity, and honestly impressive curiosity. So two of Locke's apparent rules collide: encourage-and-channel the child's inquisitive bent (never crush it, never make learning a chore) versus intervene early and firmly on anything that harms creatures (because habits of the heart form young). Tell me what you think Locke would actually advise here, and why. And notice that his little parable about animal torment is doing a lot of work: does it cover curiosity-driven harm, or only delight in suffering as such? There seem to be at least three rules consistent with his track record — something like "no harming creatures, whatever the motive," something like "only cruelty-as-enjoyment is the problem," and something like "protect the scientific impulse even at some cost." Say which one you think best fits his other verdicts and this text, what each of the losing rules would predict for my son's case, and give me one further case (invented is fine) that would actually tell your favored rule apart from the others.
