I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind organising an interdisciplinary reading group on risks from advanced AI, and I want to open the first session with a classification exercise rather than yet another survey of the literature. Please write a decision tree that takes an interlocutor from the shared starting commitment that machine learning may soon produce systems matching or exceeding human capabilities across most domains, and sorts them into a named position on the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of familiar positions: the discriminating questions come first, then the positions get located in the space those questions carve out.

The tree should have between seven and nine branch questions, and must include splits on each of the following: whether orthogonality is defended as a merely conceptual possibility claim or as an empirical claim about systems whose goals are implicit in trained weights; whether instrumental convergence is read as holding for all possible final goals or only for the overwhelming majority; whether idealised assumptions about coherent utility-maximising agency are doing load-bearing work in the argument; and whether training and deployment filters are taken to select against the power-seeking drives the convergence thesis predicts, even where those drives are granted. Phrase every branch question so that someone could answer it without knowing which position it leads to — as a concrete case or a yes-or-no claim, not technical jargon.

Each leaf should carry the position's name (or 'unoccupied', with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether that slot is empty because the combination of answers is incoherent, unattractive, or merely underexplored), followed by one sentence on the view's most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate rather than papering over the ambiguity. Around 1,200 words is plenty, and please write in British English.
