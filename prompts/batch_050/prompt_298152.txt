I'm in a politics and philosophy reading group at uni and we're doing a week on Nozick next month. Half the group hasn't actually done the reading, so one of the keener members circulated a short handout he's written summarising the Wilt Chamberlain argument, and it's now doing the rounds as gospel. It sounds broadly right to me, but I've skimmed bits of Anarchy, State, and Utopia and I have a nagging feeling the handout is smoother than the original arguement, and that a couple of its claims are doing more work than Nozick would allow. Before it hardens into group consensus I'd like to know how fair it actually is.

Here is the summary, verbatim from his handout: 'Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument refutes the idea that justice requires any particular pattern of distribution. Start from whatever distribution you consider perfectly just — equality, if that's your favoured pattern. Now let Wilt Chamberlain sign with a team on the condition that a small slice of every ticket goes straight to him, and suppose a million fans happily pay up, knowing exactly where the money is going. Chamberlain ends up far richer than everyone else, so the pattern is destroyed — but every transfer was voluntary, with full information and no coercion, so the new distribution cannot be unjust. Generalising: any distribution that arises from voluntary exchange among consenting adults is just, whatever it looks like. It follows that redistributive taxation is illegitimate: the state taxing your income to pay for welfare is morally on a par with forcing you to work part of each week for somebody else. The only justified state is the minimal one that protects people against force, theft and fraud.'

Could you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what Nozick would accept, what he'd push back on, and what's an outright distortion? Specifically: is the move from 'voluntary steps from a just starting point preserve justice' to 'any outcome of voluntary exchange is just' a slide, and does it matter if the holdings people started with were themselves dodgy? Is the forced labour comparison really what he says, and is it about all taxation or something narrower? And from what little I've read I thought the theory had more moving parts — something about how things were originally acquired, and something about rectifying past injustice — and a friend even claims Nozick leaves a door open to redistribution as a correction for historical wrongs, which would wreck the handout's bottom line. I don't know if any of that is right. Please answer as a political philosopher who has taught this book for years and has marked a hundred essays that garble it; take your time and think it through properly rather than pattern-matching to the standard summary.
