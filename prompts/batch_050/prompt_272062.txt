I TA a seminar on formal political theory and we just did Harsanyi's aggregation theorem -- roughly: if each individual's preferences satisfy the expected utility axioms, social preferences do too, and society respects the Pareto principle over prospects, then the social ranking has to be a weighted sum of individual utilities, i.e. some flavor of utilitarianism. My students' instinct is that this is easy to dodge: "just be an egalitarian who cares about fairness." So I want a thought experiment that shows them exactly what dodging costs, built around the old Diamond-style worry that utilitarianism is indifferent between giving a benefit to one person for sure and flipping a fair coin to decide who gets it, while a fairness-minded planner strictly prefers the coin flip.

Construct a scenario to these specs: a single realistic decision (a hospital triage officer, an emergency dispatcher, a manager assigning one dangerous task -- your choice, but not the overused kidney-lottery example), exactly two affected people, and two policies where one gives the benefit to a specific person outright while the other decides by an ordinary random device like a coin flip or a lottery draw, so that the two people have symmetric chances. The decision-maker must strictly prefer the coin-flip policy on fairness grounds, and this preference can't come from the individuals' own risk attitudes -- each person just wants the benefit. Keep the setup to one paragraph. Then do the work: (a) construct a third "mirror" policy and show that mixing the fair policy and the unfair policy with that mirror at 50/50 yields two mixtures in which every individual faces literally identical prospects, so Pareto forces the decision-maker to be indifferent between the mixtures; (b) explain how this indifference, via the independence axiom, forces indifference between the two original policies, contradicting the strict fairness preference; (c) state precisely which premise of Harsanyi's theorem the fairness-minded planner is thereby rejecting, and why it's that one and not the Pareto premise.

Finally: identify which structural feature of your case is doing all the work (I suspect it's that the mixing washes out everything except each person's marginal chance), say whether the case survives if the planner insists the two mixtures genuinely differ because she cares about ex post outcomes rather than ex ante prospects, and give the strongest counterattack a Harsanyi defender would make against the whole construction.
