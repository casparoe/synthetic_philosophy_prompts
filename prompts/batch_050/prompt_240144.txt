My roommate and I were messing around with the absent-minded driver problem last night (you know the one: driving home on the highway, you need to take the second exit, but you're absent-minded and can't tell exits apart; payoff 0 if you take the first exit, 4 if you take the second, 1 if you sail past both and end up at the bad motel). We ended up proving something that can't be right and we can't find the leak.

Numbers first so we're on the same page. If you commit to exiting with probability p whenever you find yourself at an exit, your expected payoff is 1 + 2p − 3p², maximized at p = 1/3, giving 4/3. And given that policy, when you're actually sitting at an exit you should figure it's the first exit with probablity 3/5 (you hit exit one once per trip no matter what, exit two only on the 2/3 of trips where you passed the first). On those beliefs, exiting is worth 0·(3/5) + 4·(2/5) = 8/5, and continuing is worth (3/5)·(1/3·4 + 2/3·1) + (2/5)·(1) = 8/5. A dead tie. Okay, now the argument:

(1) The plan with the highest expected payoff is exiting with probability 1/3 at each exit, so that's what a rational driver commits to.
(2) When the driver is actually sitting at an exit, he should do whatever has the highest expected payoff given his beliefs at that moment.
(3) Given the 1/3 policy, his beliefs are 3/5 first exit, 2/5 second, and on those beliefs exiting and continuing are worth exactly the same, 8/5 each way.
(4) If two options are worth exactly the same, picking either one is permissible. That's just what a tie means.
(5) So at the exit, the driver may rationally just exit. Full stop, no coin flip needed.
(6) But the driver at the first exit is in exactly the same situation with exactly the same information, so the same permission holds there too: he may just exit.
(7) A driver who exits whenever he's permitted to just exit is running policy p = 1, which means he takes the first exit every trip and gets 0 — the worst payoff on the table, worse than the brain-dead "never exit" policy that guarantees 1.
(8) So the rational plan, followed step by step by a driver who reasons rationally at each exit, yields the worst possible outcome.
(9) Which is absurd, so apparently expected utility maximization defeats itself for agents with imperfect recall?

Something obviously slipped between the premises but we honestly can't localize it. (4) looks like a truism, (2) looks like just what it is to be rational, and we've both checked the arithmetic in (3) twice. So: which step do we give up, why did it look innocent, and what does it cost to keep the rest? I'd especially like to hang on to (2) — I don't want a theory where you have to act against your current best judgment purely because past-you issued orders — but if that's the one that has to go, tell me straight. Bonus question: does the fix mean the driver's beliefs about which exit he's at end up hostage to his own future behavior, and is that as creepy as it sounds or totally fine?
