Here's a thing I've been chewing on and I want a case built for it. There's this idea that deliberation crowds out prediction: while you're genuinely deciding what to do, you can't treat your own forthcoming choice as just another event to forecast, because from the inside the whole point is that it's still open. If you catch yourself thinking "I'll probably cave and have the cake," either that prediction does your deciding for you, or you haven't really started deliberating yet. So the claim goes, an agent in the middle of deliberating can't coherently assign probabilities to her own options the way she can to anyone else's.

Construct a thought experiment that stress-tests this. Specifications:

- One agent, one choice with exactly two options, and a deadline. Nothing science-fictional: no perfect predictors, no brain scanners, no twins. The agent has to be someone with ordinary self-knowledge, the kind any of us has about our own habits.
- The scenario must force a moment where the agent apparently needs a probability for her own action in order to decide well -- e.g. because how good an option is depends on whether she'd stick with it, or because someone else's behaviour (which she must predict) depends on what she'll do. Make this need arise naturally from the stakes, not from some philosopher in the story demanding a credence.
- Include a second character who faces the same decision *about the agent*, from the outside, so we can compare: the friend can assign probabilities to the agent's choice without paradox, but allegedly the agent can't assign them to herself. Hold as much else fixed as you can between the two perspectives.

Then show the work: (1) walk through what happens if the agent does assign a probability to her own choice -- does deliberation collapse, change its conclusion, or carry on regardless? (2) walk through what happens if she refuses to -- is she now missing information the friend has, and if so, can that be rational? (3) name the single structural feature of your case that generates the tension, and say what happens if you remove it -- does the case dissolve into an ordinary decision under uncertainty? (4) say how someone who thinks self-prediction is perfectly unproblematic would attack your scenario, and whether their attack succeeds.

The case should be short enough to tell in a pub -- a paragraph or two -- and it has to generate the tension rather than just stipulating it. Answer in British English please.
