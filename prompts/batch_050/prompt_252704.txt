In reading history, I keep running into a kind of explanation that doesn't fit either of the two models I know -- subsumption under general laws, or plain causal tracing. Sometimes a historian explains an event by folding it into a larger whole so that it comes to look like a natural part of that whole, and the understanding seems to arrive entirely through this redescription. Two examples: a historian treats an obscure 1789 bread riot not by giving its causes but by recounting it as an early episode of the French Revolution, so that its sense and significance seem to be supplied by the larger story; and a biographer explains an apparently erratic decision by an admiral by exhibiting it as one more instance of a pattern of caution running through his entire career, after which we feel we understand it, although nothing about its causes has been added. Is there an established name for this mode of explanation, and if so, how is it supposed to work -- what makes one such larger whole genuinely explanatory rather than merely a framing the historian happened to choose? I'd also like to know precisely how it differs from explaining by subsumption under laws, from explaining by citing causes, and from merely narrating events in sequence.
