I'm a high school senior in an honors seminar on classical Athens, and my teacher has a running habit: after we study each episode, he declares whether, in his view, it counts as a genuine failure of democracy as such, as opposed to a mistake any constitution could have made, or even a quiet success. His verdicts so far:

- The Mytilenean debate (427 BC): the assembly voted in anger to execute every adult man in Mytilene, reconvened the next day, heard Diodotus answer Cleon, and narrowly reversed itself. His verdict: not a failure — arguably the assembly's finest hour.
- The Sicilian expedition (415–413 BC): the assembly voted to invade Sicily while, by most accounts, badly underestimating the size, population, and resources of Syracuse, and the armada was annihilated. His verdict: a failure of democracy.
- The trial of Socrates (399 BC): a lawfully empanelled jury, following ordinary procedure, convicted him and imposed the death sentence. His verdict: a failure of democracy.
- The amnesty of 403 BC: after the fall of the Thirty Tyrants, the restored democracy barred prosecutions for acts committed under the oligarchy, with narrow exceptions, and the settlement held. His verdict: not a failure — a success.

Next week we turn to Pylos and Sphacteria (425 BC): Cleon taunted Nicias in the assembly, got maneuvered into taking the command himself, boasted that he would settle the matter within twenty days, and — with considerable luck and Demosthenes' generalship — actually returned within twenty days bringing Spartan prisoners. Athens had stumbled, by way of a demagogic-looking process, into a famous victory.

I'd like your help preparing, and I want the prediction to rest on his track record rather than on your own view of these episodes:

1. Predict my teacher's verdict on Sphacteria — failure of democracy or not — and give your reasoning.
2. State the single rule you believe he is implicitly applying, then give at least two other rules that fit all four earlier verdicts equally well but that disagree, either with each other or with your main rule, about Sphacteria. For each rule, state what it predicts about the new case.
3. Identify which of the four earlier episodes is the closest analog to Sphacteria and defend the choice — or argue that none of them is a true analog.
4. Propose one further case, drawn from Athenian history or invented, that would best discriminate among the rules that survive step 2, and explain what each rule would say about it.
