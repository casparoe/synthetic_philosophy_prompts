I TA an undergrad moral philosophy seminar and this term we did a unit on hypocrisy and the "standing to blame" -- you know, the intuition behind things like "who are YOU to lecture me about honesty" said to a chronic liar. My students immediately got the intuition but then turned out to mean like five different things by it, and honestly I kept conflating them myself in section. Some thought the hypocrite loses a right he used to have, some thought his blame is fine in content but shouldn't come out of his mouth, one student insisted the real problem is that nobody at all is ever in a position to blame, and another thought silent private resentment is exempt from whatever rule governs public blaming. I want to hand out a decision tree next semester that sorts a person into the view they actually hold about this, based only on how they answer concrete questions -- not on which label sounds good to them.

So: please build me that decision tree. Start from the root reaction "something is off when the office gossip angrily berates a coworker for gossiping" and make each branch node a question someone could answer without knowing any philosophical terminology (cases and gut verdicts, not "do you believe blame is a sanction?"). The tree needs to at least distinguish: whether the defect lies in the blamer or in the blame itself; whether whatever's wrong with the hypocrite generalizes to all of us flawed blamers or is specific to hypocrisy; whether you can lose standing about one norm (lying) while keeping it about another (cruelty); whether the problem is insincerity of commitment versus treating equals unequally; and whether feeling resentful privately already requires standing or only calling someone out does. Each leaf should get a short descriptive name (invent them -- "right-forfeiture view", "no-standing skepticism", whatever fits), one sentence on its core claim, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. If some combination of answers on your tree corresponds to a position nobody seems to hold, leave it in as an empty branch and say briefly whether that's because the combo is incoherent, just unattractive, or genuinely unexplored. And flag any pair of leaves your questions can't actually pull apart rather than pretending they can.

Format-wise: give me the tree itself as indented text (question → answers → where each leads), and then a JSON object summarizing it with these fields: "nodes" (array of {id, question, options, next_node_or_leaf}), "leaves" (array of {name, core_claim, awkward_commitment, occupied_or_empty, if_empty_why}), "inseparable_pairs" (array of two-leaf arrays with a one-sentence note on why the tree can't split them), and "root_intuition" (a one-line restatement of the starting case). The JSON is so I can autogenerate the handout, the indented tree is for me to actually read.
