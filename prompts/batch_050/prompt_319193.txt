I am a novelist at work on a book set in a near-future city governed in part by a Bureau of Civic Contentment, an institution I invented after rereading the Grand Inquisitor chapter of Dostoevsky's The Brothers Karamazov. The Bureau's most popular service is its Office of Resolution: any citizen tormented by a hard decision -- whether to leave a marriage, change careers, forgive a betrayal -- may bring the matter to the Bureau, whose caseworkers hear the whole story in confidence and then simply decide it, issuing a binding recommendation the citizen is expected to follow. Demand is enormous and satisfaction surveys are glowing. I built the service around a line in which the Grand Inquisitor tells the silent Christ: "The most agonizing secrets of their consciences – all, all will they bring to us, and we shall resolve it all, and they will attend our decision with joy, because it will deliver them from the great anxiety and fearsome torments of free and individual decision." My writing group is split on whether my fictional Bureau is faithful to the Inquisitor's argument or smuggles in something of my own, and I realize I cannot settle that dispute, or write the Bureau's internal debates convincingly, until I understand what the practice I have described actually presupposes.

So here is my question. What view of freedom, and of happiness, does the Office of Resolution have to be committed to for the practice to make sense on its own terms? Presumably something like: freedom of choice is for most people a burden rather than a good, and relief from it is a welfare gain. But does the practice strictly require that strong claim, or is it equally compatible with weaker ones -- that decision fatigue is real, that some choices are objectively better left to experts, that outsourcing a decision is itself a free choice? Note one wrinkle I wrote into the novel and now worry about: the same Bureau also runs a public campaign celebrating authenticity and self-authorship, urging citizens to "become who they are." Is that flatly inconsistent with the Office of Resolution, or can some theory hold both? Finally, if I rewrote the Bureau to presuppose the opposite view -- that choosing for oneself is partly constitutive of a good life, not just a means to contentment -- what concretely would have to change about the Office, and would anything of its evident benefits survive? Please give your answer in American English, as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
