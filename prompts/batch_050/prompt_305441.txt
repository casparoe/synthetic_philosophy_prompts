I have been reflecting on why some philosophical thought experiments move me and others leave me feeling managed, and I would like help turning my reactions into a defensible principle. Dennett calls these devices intuition pumps, and my worry is that the genre includes both precision instruments and rhetorical traps. Here are my verdicts on six cases, with my reasons as I currently see them.

First, Nozick's experience machine: I find it genuinely persuasive against hedonism. The fantasy is outlandish, but the question it presses -- whether I would trade an actual life for a guaranteed stream of pleasant experience -- seems to survive translation into mundane terms, since people decline far milder versions of that trade every day.

Second, Thomson's violinist: I accept the conclusion it is usually taken to support, but I distrust the pump itself. Something about the setup -- a famous musician surgically attached to a kidnapped stranger -- feels engineered to make one side of the case seem absurd before the argument begins, even though I cannot say precisely what it distorts.

Third, trolley problems: I think they are fine for probing the distinction between intending and merely foreseeing harm, but misleading when treated as a general method for ethics. They stipulate perfect certainty about outcomes and delete every institutional feature that real moral life contains, and I suspect much of their power comes from those stipulations rather than from anything deep about morality.

Fourth, Jackson's case of Mary the color scientist: I felt the pull strongly on first encounter, but on reflection I think it trades on the surprise of a new experience and equivocates on what Mary is supposed to have known beforehand. Verdict: misleading.

Fifth, Searle's Chinese room: I think this one misleads by exploiting scale and speed. A person shuffling symbols by hand at a few characters per hour cannot exhibit anything like fluent understanding, so of course my intuition reports no understanding in the room; but I doubt that intuition tracks anything about what a system running at the right speed and complexity would do. Verdict: misleading.

Sixth, Gettier cases: I find them decisive against the justified true belief analysis, full stop. The lucky-coincidence structure is artificial, but the artificiality seems beside the point.

My tentative principle is this: an intuition pump is legitimate when it strips away features of a situation that are merely distracting while preserving the feature that actually bears the weight of the question at issue, and misleading when it removes, distorts, or smuggles in a load-bearing feature. The trouble is that this principle, as stated, does not obviously sort my own verdicts. The trolley cases and the Gettier cases both proceed by stipulation and artificiality, yet I want to condemn one and endorse the other; and the experience machine and the violinist both rely on biologically bizarre scenarios, yet I want to acquit one and indict the other. Either my principle needs clauses I have not articulated, or one of my verdicts has to go.

Please do the following. State a repaired principle -- or, if you think no single principle will do the work, a small set of candidate principles -- precisely enough that it could be applied mechanically to a new case. For each clause of your proposal, identify which of my six cases motivates it. Say explicitly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, if any, and argue briefly for overturning those verdicts rather than complicating the principle. Then apply the principle to two cases I have not listed -- Parfit's teletransporter and the ticking time bomb scenario invoked in debates over torture -- and report what verdicts it delivers there, including any verdict that cuts against the common philosophical reception of those cases. Finally, I would like one diagnostic question, derivable from your principle, that I could ask of any unfamiliar thought experiment to get a quick first read on whether it is a precision instrument or a rhetorical trap.
