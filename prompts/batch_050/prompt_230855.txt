At our policy institute's reading group, we've been working through the "Moloch" picture of multipolar traps: situations like the AI capabilities race, academic publishing's incentive spiral, or education signaling tournaments, in which every participant would prefer the competition to de-escalate yet none can afford to slow down unilaterally. My question is whether this framing actually adds anything. As far as I can tell, a multipolar trap is simply a prisoner's dilemma with more than two players, and the game theory of the prisoner's dilemma is well understood: given repeated interaction, a sufficiently long shadow of the future, reputation effects, and enforceable agreements, cooperation is an achievable equilibrium. Presumably this is how arms control functioned during the Cold War, under conditions of mutual distrust far worse than anything facing, say, competing technology firms. So why do people discuss race-to-the-bottom dynamics as though they posed a deeper or qualitatively different problem? If two superpowers could coordinate on nuclear stockpiles, why couldn't frontier AI labs coordinate on safety investment, or universities on admissions standards? Is the Moloch framing anything more than the observation -- which nobody disputes -- that coordination gets harder as the number of parties grows? If so, what exactly is the extra structure, stated in game-theoretic terms rather than metaphorical ones?
