You are a political theorist who has spent years teaching the ethics of secession to advanced undergraduates, and you are designing a seminar exercise. You want a decision tree that sorts students into positions on unilateral secession by their answers to concrete questions, not by which label they find appealing. Before drafting the tree itself, please think carefully and sketch the logical space: what are the independent yes/no questions on which views about secession can diverge, and which combinations of answers are coherent at all.

Then produce the tree under these constraints:

1. Start the tree at a commitment any reasonable participant holds, such as: existing states are not entitled to keep their territory at any cost whatsoever. Each branch node must be a single question, phrased so that a student can answer it without knowing which theory it leads to. Prefer concrete cases over abstract formulations. Your node questions must include at least: a case modeled on Catalonia (no grave injustice, economically stronger than the parent state, majority of the region favors leaving); a case modeled on Quebec 1995 (no serious injustice, but a distinct national culture, and a further question about whether minorities inside the seceding territory may in turn separate from it); and a hypothetical case of a group with no shared national identity, no grievance, and a narrow territorial majority who simply vote to leave. You may add one or two further cases of your own design, including at least one that tests whether the right depends on the new state's being viable.

2. The leaves must include at least the following positions, each under a descriptive name the model supplies (standard scholarly labels are welcome where they exist, but the tree must work without them): the view that there is no unilateral right to secede at all, only agreed secession with the parent state's consent; the remedial view that secession is justified only as a remedy for serious injustice; the view that nations as such hold a right to self-determination even absent injustice; and the view that any territorially concentrated group expressing majority support has a presumptive right to leave regardless of nationhood. If your tree produces more leaves than these, that is fine, up to about seven.

3. For each leaf, state: the position's name, which of your case questions does the decisive sorting work for it, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment (for instance, what it is forced to say about Scotland-style agreed referendums, about recursive secession of minorities within the breakaway region, or about dispersed minorities with a grievance but no territory).

4. Build the tree from the logical space first, and then explicitly note any combination of answers that no recognizable position occupies, with a one- or two-sentence diagnosis of whether that combination is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored in the debate.

5. Finally, flag any pair of leaf positions your tree cannot separate using only your case questions, say what further question would be needed to separate them, and if no such case-question exists, say so honestly rather than adding a node that just asks respondents which label they prefer.

Keep the whole instrument answerable by someone who has thought about secession carefully but never read the specialist literature.
