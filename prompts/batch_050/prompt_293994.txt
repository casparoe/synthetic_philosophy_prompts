I am a teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate course in social choice and formal political theory. In your comments, please distinguish carefully between claims that are technically incorrect and claims that are merely under-argued, and identify the particular sentences in which any errors occur.

**Examination question**

A three-member grants panel must decide whether to fund a research project. Funding is warranted, the panel agrees, if and only if the project is feasible (P) and scientifically important (Q). Each member submits a judgment on P, on Q, and on the conjunction (P ∧ Q). The panel may aggregate either by taking a majority on each premise and deriving the conclusion from the majority verdicts on the premises (the premise-based procedure) or by taking a majority directly on the conjunction (the conclusion-based procedure).

(a) Construct a profile of individually consistent judgments for the three members such that the premise-based procedure delivers funding and the conclusion-based procedure does not. (7 marks)

(b) Now consider a five-member panel. Show that the premise-based procedure can mandate funding even when four of the five members individually judge that funding is unwarranted. What, if anything, does this show about the premise-based procedure? (8 marks)

(c) A colleague, presented with your answers to (a) and (b), concludes that majority voting over connected propositions is simply incoherent, and that the panel should therefore always vote directly on the conclusion. Assess this recommendation. (10 marks)

**Student's answer**

(a) Let the three members judge as follows. Member 1 accepts P, accepts Q, and accepts P ∧ Q. Member 2 accepts P, rejects Q, and rejects P ∧ Q. Member 3 rejects P, accepts Q, and rejects P ∧ Q. Each member's set of judgments is consistent, since each rejects the conjunction exactly when rejecting at least one conjunct. Under the premise-based procedure, P passes two to one (members 1 and 2) and Q passes two to one (members 1 and 3), so the panel derives P ∧ Q and funds the project. Under the conclusion-based procedure, P ∧ Q fails one to two (only member 1 accepts it), so the project is not funded. Hence the two procedures diverge on the same profile.

(b) For five members: member 1 accepts P, Q, and P ∧ Q; members 2 and 3 accept P, reject Q, and reject P ∧ Q; members 4 and 5 reject P, accept Q, and reject P ∧ Q. Then P passes three to two and Q passes three to two, so the premise-based procedure mandates funding, while four of the five members (members 2 through 5) individually reject the conjunction and so judge funding unwarranted. This is striking but not, in my view, a defect: a group's judgment need not be identical to, or even shared by, any member's judgment, since the group is not a person. Indeed, because the two premises are logically independent of one another and the conclusion is then derived from the majority verdicts by a fixed logical rule, the premise-based procedure guarantees a consistent collective judgment set, and this is its principal advantage over voting on the conclusion directly.

(c) My colleague's recommendation does not solve the problem; it merely chooses one horn of the dilemma. If the panel votes directly on the conclusion, it avoids overturning what most members believe about funding, but it severs the connection between the group's verdict and the group's view of the reasons: the panel may reject funding while a majority accepts each consideration that, by the panel's own agreed standard, would justify it, leaving the group unable to state a coherent rationale for its decision. So the dilemma is not an argument for the conclusion-based procedure but a choice between two costs: responsiveness to members' views on the conclusion versus the ability to give reasons. I would also note that the underlying phenomenon here is essentially Condorcet's paradox transposed from preferences to judgments: just as pairwise majority voting over candidates can produce a cycle, majority voting over interconnected propositions can produce an outcome that conflicts with what the group collectively endorses. Because no aggregation rule can avoid this in all cases, my colleague's claim that majority voting over connected propositions is incoherent is too strong — the difficulty is not incoherence but the impossibility of satisfying all of our desiderata at once.
