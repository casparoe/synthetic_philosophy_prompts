I'm a software engineer at a mid-size company that runs a large fleet of microservices plus a few machine-learning systems, including a recommendation model and an LLM-based support agent. In design reviews and incident postmortems, my team keeps falling into the same unproductive argument: someone says "the service thinks the cache is warm" or "the agent wants to end the conversation," and someone else objects that this is loose talk for what is really just state machines and matrix multiplication. From my own reading I've picked up Dennett's three stances — physical, design, and intentional — and his claim that a higher-level description can track a real pattern when it compresses information in a way the lower-level description can't manage at any practical cost. I find this plausible, but I can't turn it into anything my team could actually use, and right now the arguments get settled by whoever sounds most confident.

What I want is a decision procedure: a checklist or flowchart-in-words that an engineer with zero philosophy background can run in under five minutes, during a design review or mid-incident, that takes (a) a system and (b) a concrete goal — predict its behavior under a novel condition, choose where to intervene, decide how to write the alert — and outputs which stance to take for that goal, plus a verdict on whether intentional-stance language about that system is tracking a real pattern or is merely decorative metaphor. The procedure needs an explicit operational test for the real-pattern question, something in the spirit of: does attributing beliefs and desires to this system yield predictions or compression that the physical description cannot feasibly match, and would the pattern survive noise and minor perturbation of the system? It needs tie-breaking rules for cases where the design stance and the intentional stance both seem to "work," and it needs at least three hard cases built into its scope — a thermostat or load balancer, a chess engine, and a modern LLM — with an explanation of where each lands and why. It should also directly address the failure mode I actually care about: an engineer over-trusting an intentional-stance description during an outage and concluding "the system is confused" when a physical-stance fix was needed, or the reverse, where someone refuses stance-shifting and wastes hours reading logs that a design-stance hypothesis would have short-circuited.

Please work the procedure through two or three applications, including at least one where it returns an answer I won't like — either denying real-pattern status to a system I find natural to anthropomorphize, or granting it to something I'd rather treat as a dumb mechanism. Mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure rather than mechanical tests, and say plainly what the procedure cannot settle: I assume consciousness and anything about moral status are out of scope, but tell me if I'm wrong about what else it leaves open.
