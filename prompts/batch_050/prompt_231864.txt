Compare two of the standard complaints about effective altruism: the institutional critique (EA focuses on individual donations and career choices, which lets unjust structures off the hook and crowds out political action aimed at changing them) and the measurability bias critique (EA's demand for quantifiable evidence steers money toward whatever can be easily counted, like bednets, and away from things like governance reform or long-run political change). Are these actually the same objection wearing two costumes, or do they cut in different directions? Test your answer with two hypotheticals: first, a version of EA that goes all-in on funding political and institutional reform but keeps its strict GiveWell-style evidential standards -- which critique does that answer, and which does it make sharper? Second, a version of EA that drops the measurement standards entirely but stays focused on individual donations rather than collective action. Which critique is more fundamental, in the sense that fully answering it would force you to answer the other one as well? Or is neither more fundamental, and if so, where exactly do the two objections come apart?
