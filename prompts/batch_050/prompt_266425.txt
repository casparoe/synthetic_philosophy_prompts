Write a dialogue between two friends, Priya and Marcus, who each donate a chunk of their income every year but to totally different causes: Priya is convinced corporate campaigns against factory farming are by far the best use of a dollar, Marcus thinks malaria prevention is. It starts when Marcus's employer announces a donation-matching program that only covers global health charities. Marcus proposes a deal: Priya routes some of her giving through the malaria match (so it doubles), and in exchange Marcus redirects the same amount of his own money to Priya's animal charities. Both causes end up with more money than if each had just donated to their favorite. Priya agrees that one's a no-brainer.

The argument starts when Marcus proposes they turn this into a standing arrangement: every year, they negotiate how much of each other's giving to "trade." Priya balks, and the dialogue should have her press at least three objections and have Marcus actually answer them:

1. The counterfactual problem: the trade only benefits Priya if Marcus genuinely would have donated that money to malaria otherwise. But that's unverifiable, and Marcus has every incentive to overstate what he's "giving up." Priya compares it to carbon offsets that fund trees that were never going to be cut down anyway.
2. The exchange rate problem: how many nets for how many hens? They disagree about exactly that comparison, so any ratio they settle on either smuggles in one person's values or devolves into bargaining where each side exaggerates their sacrifice.
3. The integrity worry: if Priya really believes factory farming is a moral emergency, then letting Marcus spend on bednets "on her behalf" feels like being bribed to look away, like her judgment is just a preference to be haggled over.

At some point Marcus should reveal that he's actually morally uncertain and gives real credence to Priya's view, which Priya pounces on: if he's uncertain, his own uncertainty already tells him to shift money toward animal welfare, so the "trade" is just him charging her for doing what his own epistemic state recommends. Give Marcus a genuinely good reply to that (moral uncertainty doesn't mechanically dictate an allocation; it depends on how you weigh credences, and anyway her objection would dissolve all cooperation, since you never know what the other party would have done).

Both characters should be equally sharp, respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talking past each other, and neither should win outright -- though they can reach a partial agreement about which kinds of moral trade survive Priya's objections and which don't. Write it in American English, maybe 700-900 words, natural sounding dialogue, no narration beyond minimal speaker tags.
