The exam question quotes Kant at the outset, from the Preamble to the Prolegomena to Any Future Metaphysics (1783), in the old Paul Carus translation: "My object is to persuade all those who think Metaphysics worth studying, that it is absolutely necessary to pause a moment, and, neglecting all that has been done, to propose first the preliminary question, 'Whether such a thing as metaphysics be at all possible?'" The question then asks: Kant does not answer this preliminary question directly, but instead asks how pure mathematics and pure natural science are possible, taking their actuality as given and reasoning back to their conditions of possibility. (a) Why does Kant think this indirect procedure can answer his preliminary question? (b) Is the inference from the actuality of experience to its necessary conditions a transcendental argument in the sense later debated by Stroud and Strawson, and does it matter that Kant's own version is embedded in his idealism? (c) Can such an argument answer the sceptic about the external world, or does it, as Stroud objects, establish at most what we must believe? Assess Stroud's objection and at least one reply to it.

Here is the student's answer, verbatim, typos and all:

"Kant's preliminary question looks as though it calls for a straight yes or no, but his actual procedure is deliberately indirect. As he explains, the Prolegomena was written two years before the Critique of Pure Reason as a kind of trailer for it, and its method is analytical or regressive: rather than building metaphysics up from first principles, it starts from two bodies of cognition -- pure mathematics and pure natural science -- that everyone already accepts as actual, and works backwards to whatever makes them possible. If it turns out that synthetic a priori principles supplied by the understanding are what make these sciences possible, then the preliminary question is answered not with an abstract proof that metaphysics is possible but with a demonstration that we already rely on it every time we do physics.

This is a transcendental argument in the later sense: it moves from an undisputed starting point (there is experience, of objects ordered in time) to a substantive conclusion about what must hold for that starting point to be so much as possible. What is distinctive of Kant's version is that the inference runs through transcendental idealism: the conditions it uncovers are conditions our own faculties impose on appearances, not conditions of things in themselves. Stroud's famous objection is aimed at versions of the argument that drop this idealism. He points out that 'we must think that p' does not entail 'p', so a bridge is needed between necessity of belief and truth; and he shows that Kant's bridge was a verification principle, roughly that a claim means nothing beyond what could be checked in possible experience, so Kant escapes the objection only because he was a verificationist avant la lettre. Strawson's austere reconstructions (in Individuals and The Bounds of Sense) avoid this by needing nothing more than the claim that our conceptual scheme hangs together such that experience of an objective world is a condition of self-ascription. So Stroud's charge misfires against Strawson, and Stroud himself later withdrew the objection anyway.

Does the argument answer the sceptic? Yes. Once we grant that it concerns only appearances, things in themselves drop out as beyond our concern, so the sceptic's doubt about them is idle and the external world of experience is secured. Overall, then, the method answers the preliminary question by quietly changing it: not whether metaphysics is possible, but how the synthetic a priori cognition we already have is possible. Whether that counts as answering or evading the sceptic depends on how much one is willing to pay for idealism."
