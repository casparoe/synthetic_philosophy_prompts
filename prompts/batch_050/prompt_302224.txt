A friend who's a committed nominalist-adjacent type keeps pressing this on me and I keep failing to block it, even though the conclusion seems mad. (1) If x grounds y, then y is nothing over and above x -- that's the whole selling point of grounding, it tells you how you get the derivative reality for free once you've got the base. (2) If y is nothing over and above x, then a complete inventory of what exists doesn't need to mention y once it has mentioned x; y makes no further demand on being. (3) Anything that makes no further demand on being isn't an extra existent; strictly speaking there's no such thing as y, just x. (4) So nothing grounded strictly exists -- only the fundamental exists. (5) Grounding, though, is meant to be a relation between an existent ground and an existent grounded; you can't relate Socrates to a singleton that strictly doesn't exist. (6) So there's no grounding either, and the apparent cases -- whole from parts, set from member, disjunction from true disjunct -- were all confused. (7) But obviously the singleton exists and exists because Socrates does, so something between (1) and (6) has to give. He says the culprit is (1), that "nothing over and above" talk was always marketing copy. I really don't want to drop (1), since without it I can't see what grounding was supposed to buy us over supervenience. Which step do you reject, what did it get right enough to look plausible, and what does rejecting it actually cost me?
