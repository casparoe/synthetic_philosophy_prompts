Below is a draft essay I am preparing for an undergraduate philosophy of art course I teach. My aim is to introduce students to the ontology of musical works through a contrast that standard treatments often leave blurry: the contrast between works-for-performance (the classical case) and works-that-are-recordings (the rock case), and to propose a unified picture. I would like comments rather than copyediting. Specifically: (1) correct any inaccuracies in how I characterize the standard positions — misattributions bother me more than any other flaw; (2) identify the weakest link in the central argument of sections 3–4 and state the strongest objection available to someone who rejects my proposal; (3) tell me whether the analogy with photographs in section 4 genuinely does argumentative work or is decoration; (4) note any place where a student encountering this literature for the first time would be misled into thinking a contested claim is settled. Please organize your comments under those four headings.

---

**Two Kinds of Musical Work, or One?**

1. Ask what Beethoven's Fifth Symphony is, and the intuitive answer is: an abstract pattern of sounds, a type whose tokens are performances. On the type-token view associated with Julian Dodd, musical works are eternal, uncreated sound-structure types, and a performance is a token of the work when it complies closely enough with the score's instructions. This explains why the Fifth survives the burning of every score copy and why two orchestras can play the same work. Its well-known cost is that composers become discoverers rather than creators, and Jerrold Levinson's alternative — works as sound-structures-as-indicated by a particular composer at a particular historical moment — restores creation at the price of making works dependent on historical context in ways that can seem ad hoc.

2. Now ask what the Beatles' *A Day in the Life* is. There is no score whose faithful tokening counts as an instance. The work, on the view associated with Theodore Gracyk and developed by Andrew Kania, is the track itself: the mastered recording. Performances of the song on stage are not instances of the work but performances *of* it in a looser sense — renditions, references, tributes. Kania helpfully distinguishes tracks (the recorded artifact, which is the work in rock) from songs (thin structures that can be performed and recorded many times) and from the studio performances that produced the tracks. On this picture, rock inverted the classical order: there, the work is a type and recordings are mere documentation of tokens; here, the work is a concrete, historically produced artifact and live performances are the documentation.

3. I want to resist the tidy conclusion that we have discovered two ontological categories of musical work — types for the classical tradition, artifacts for the recorded tradition — and that the ontology of music is simply pluralist in this way. Consider the following. The reason we deny that a classical recording is an instance of the symphony is not that recordings are the wrong ontological kind; it is that the classical work's correctness conditions concern what is *done* (the performance event, in Stephen Davies's spirit), not what is *heard on playback*. Conversely, the reason a live rendition of *A Day in the Life* is not an instance of the rock work is that the rock work's correctness conditions concern what is *heard on playback*, not what is done. Both works, I propose, are best understood as types: they differ only in whether the type's tokens are performance-events or playback-events of a master. Call this the *playback-token proposal*: a recording is a work whose instances are its playbacks, just as a symphony is a work whose instances are its performances.

4. The proposal has one advantage worth stating carefully. It explains a datum the two-categories view handles awkwardly: we speak of *listening to the work* when we play a record, with the same grammatical directness as when we attend a concert, and we hold individual playbacks answerable to the work (a scratched pressing is a defective instance of the work, just as a flubbed entrance is a defective instance of the symphony). The analogy I want to lean on is photographic. A photographic negative and its prints: the print is an instance, the negative is not the work but its enabling artifact, and Ansel Adams burning and dodging a print is doing something to an instance, not to the work. The master tape, I claim, is to the rock work as the negative is to the photographic work: the production template, not the artwork. This preserves the intuition that recordings, unlike symphonies, are *made* in a strong sense — since which token-events count as instances is fixed by a concretely produced master — while denying that the work *is* a concrete artifact.

5. Two objections I anticipate. First, fidelity direction: a playback of a record reproduces the master, whereas a performance of a symphony approximates the score, so the two "tokenings" are not symmetric — playback is closer to copying than to interpreting. Second, the destruction test: destroy the master tape and all copies, and the rock work is gone, whereas the symphony survives the loss of all scores; this, the objector says, shows the rock work was the artifact all along.
