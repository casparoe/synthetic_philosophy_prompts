I teach an introductory metaphysics seminar for first-year undergraduates, about 20 students, with a 90-minute slot. We are approaching the Ship of Theseus and, more generally, the puzzle of material constitution and persistence through change. In past years I have presented the classic cases (the ship, the sock with successive patches, the statue and the lump) and the students have nodded along politely without feeling any real pull in either direction.

Design me a classroom activity in which the students generate the paradox themselves before it is named. The core mechanic I have in mind is this: early on, the group must draft and ratify, in one or two sentences, their own criterion for when a valued object survives a change of parts (I will supply the object -- our departmental common room has a battered ceremonial gavel with a story attached to it, and there can be a small real stake, such as which physical item gets entered in the faculty showcase as "the original"). The activity should then proceed through a sequence of concrete, individually reasonable modifications, chosen so that each step is defensible under the students' own ratified criterion, but the accumulation of steps forces them either to amend the criterion at a cost they can articulate, or to endorse a verdict they find absurd. Ideally the sequence should branch at some point, so that two rival continuers exist simultaneously and the criterion must pick one. The students should only hear the name "Ship of Theseus" and meet the historical case after the activity has run.

Please provide: the full facilitator script and rules, including the cover story (I do not want them to suspect a setup in the first ten minutes); the branching structure and how to steer votes without rigging them; the debrief, mapping specific moments of the activity onto named positions in the literature (mereological essentialism, spatiotemporal continuity accounts, four-dimensionalism, closest-continuer or best-candidate theories, sortal-relative identity), with a one-paragraph statement of each position's central claim that a first-year could follow; the two or three ways this activity predictably goes wrong when run with real students and how to recover; and a candid note on which feature of the philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce and that I must supply by other means.

In the debrief section, please distinguish carefully between points on which there is broad agreement among metaphysicians working on persistence and points that remain genuinely contested live options, and do not present any of the positions above as the settled solution.
