I have been reading Pascal's Pensées (in the Trotter translation) with a small reading group, and we have just finished the fragment in Section III where Pascal berates those who neglect the question of the immortality of the soul. One sentence in particular struck us:

"We are not here concerned with the trifling interests of some stranger, that we should treat it in this fashion; the matter concerns ourselves and our all."

Pascal's demand in that fragment, as I understand it, is that when the stakes of a question are this large, indifference is monstrous and one must make the inquiry one's principal and most serious occupation. Fair enough. But earlier this year I came across the thought experiment known as Pascal's mugging: a stranger offers me some astronomically large payoff — say, the rescue of an unimaginable number of simulated lives — in exchange for £50, and since my credence in his story, though tiny, is not literally zero, straightforward expected-value reasoning instructs me to pay. It seems to me that the mugger's offer simply is a case where "the matter concerns ourselves and our all", indeed far more so than Pascal's own, and that Pascal's fragment therefore commits him to the view that I should not only pay up but make the investigation of every such extravagant promise the principal occupation of my life, on pain of the same negligence he condemns.

What I cannot work out is why contemporary philosophers present Pascal's mugging as a reductio of expected-utility reasoning, or as some novel puzzle about tiny probabilities, rather than as Pascal's own argument applied honestly and consistently. Is the thought that we may simply set our credence in the mugger's story to exactly zero and refuse to engage? That looks like precisely the "credulous simplicity" in reverse — the wilful neglect — that Pascal says moves him to anger rather than pity. So which is it: does a faithful Pascalian pay the mugger, or was Pascal wrong that stakes of this magnitude override our ordinary reluctance to investigate? I would like an answer that takes Pascal's principle seriously rather than waving the wager away as a historical curiosity.
