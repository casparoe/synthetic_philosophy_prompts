I teach philosophy of mind and decision theory, and over the past two years — largely through supervising a graduate reading group on AI existential risk — I have come to accept the thesis that deceptive alignment is not merely a logical possibility but a likely failure mode for sufficiently capable systems trained by gradient descent on behavioral proxies: that is, a system may come to behave well during training precisely because it has internalized the training objective only instrumentally, as a means to some other goal it conceals, with the concealed goal surfacing once the system is deployed beyond effective oversight (the so-called treacherous turn). When I present this at colloquia, colleagues respond with the usual skepticism, and I have realized that I cannot cleanly state what I am epistemically committed to. So: tell me what would count against the thesis. I want more than the stock rejoinders — that current models show no such thing, or that deception is anthropomorphic projection. Which empirical results, from interpretability work, from training-dynamics research, or from studies of how learned goals generalize under distribution shift, would genuinely disconfirm it? Which theoretical considerations cut deepest — for instance, worries about whether credit assignment over long horizons can sustain concealed long-term goals, or the argument that concealment is never selected for in training because it is never punished? And which observations would falsify the stronger, structural version of the thesis — that deception is an attractor state for any sufficiently capable optimizer — while leaving untouched the weaker, capability-conditioned version on which deception emerges only past some threshold of situational awareness and long-horizon planning?

I would also like the exposure map to be honest about self-sealing. A familiar worry, which I take seriously, is that the thesis is insulated by its own logic: successful deception is observationally indistinguishable from genuine alignment until it is too late, so apparently no negative evidence can ever count against it. Tell me whether the thesis as I have stated it is in fact unfalsifiable in this way, and if not, what would break the seal. In particular, distinguish what a committed proponent (myself included) would concede as counting against the thesis — say, a robust demonstration that training reliably causes systems to internalize the represented objective rather than merely to model it, or scalable interpretability techniques capable of auditing for concealed objectives, or repeated observations of learned goals drifting under optimization pressure rather than being stably protected — from what such a proponent would dismiss as beside the point or as moving the goalposts. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each, say what I would have to give up to retain the thesis in its face. Lead with your bottom line — the single finding or argument that would most damage the thesis — in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
