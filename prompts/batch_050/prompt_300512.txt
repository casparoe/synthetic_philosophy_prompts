So in history class we've been doing the rise and fall of Rome, and separately I fell down a wikipedia hole about Ibn Khaldun, this medieval Arab historian who had a theory of history that seems weirdly relevant. His idea, as I understand it: dynasties go in a cycle. A rough group from the margins with really strong internal solidarity (he called it asabiyyah) conquers the cushy civilized people, sets up a dynasty, and then within about 3-4 generations the rulers get rich, soft, and decadent, the solidarity rots, and some new tough group from the margins knocks them over. Rinse and repeat.

Ok so: analyze the rise and fall of Rome through Khaldun's theory. Like actually do the mapping, don't just say "yes rome got decadent lol." Where does it fit well (early Rome vs late Rome, the citizen-farmer-soldier thing, whatever) and where does it break? Because it seems like it should break a lot: Rome lasted wayyyy longer than 3 or 4 generations, it kept absorbing conquered peoples and extending citizenship instead of just being one tribe ruling, and the "fall" took centuries and happened to only half the empire. A theory where the exception is 500 years long seems like a problem.

Then the other direction: what does the Rome case do to Khaldun? Does it just falsify him, or is there an easy patch (like maybe asabiyyah can be renewed, or empires and dynasties cycle differently) that saves the theory but makes it less interesting? And be honest about whether Khaldun's cycle actually explains Rome better than the boring linear story of "it got too big and declined," or whether he's just a cooler-sounding version of the same thing. I'd rather you say "the theory is silent here" than force it to answer.
