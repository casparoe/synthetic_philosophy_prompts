I'm in an intro epistemology course and we just covered Moore's paradox — sentences like "it's raining but I don't believe it's raining," which are somehow defective even though they contain no outright contradiction, since it could genuinely be raining while I fail to believe it. Fine, I follow that. But later I was arguing with my roommate about intellectual humility and something clicked in a bad way: isn't "at least one of my beliefs is false" just the same move, generalized? If I can't coherently think "P, but I don't believe P" for any particular P, how can I coherently think the general version, that some belief of mine is wrong? Doesn't believing the generalization commit me to one of those instances being true, and wasn't the whole lesson that the instances are unthinkable?

My roommate now thinks this proves that a fully rational person has to be dogmatic — they'd have to believe that every single one of their beliefs is true, on pain of falling into the paradox. That conclusion seems insane to me but I honestly can't find the hole in the reasoning. I also have a vague memory of some case about an author who admits in the preface of their book that, given their track record, something in it is bound to be wrong — and this is supposed to be a perfectly rational attitude, which sounds like exactly the "incoherent" position from above. So which is it? Is rational humility actually ruled out by Moore's paradox? And if not, what seperates the humble general claim from the paradoxical specific ones? Please don't just wave at "context" or "pragmatics" — I want to understand exactly where the inference from "each instance is defective" to "the generalization is defective" breaks down, assuming it does.
