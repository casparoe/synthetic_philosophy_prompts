Until a few months ago, my interest in the ethics of abortion was purely theoretical. This spring, my younger sister became pregnant despite using contraception carefully, and she asked me — as the family member who reads philosophy — to help her think the decision through slowly rather than reach for slogans. I have been rebuilding my understanding of the literature from the ground up, and I keep returning to the canonical thought experiments, because they seem to do most of the real work in this debate.

As far as I can tell, the standard repertoire is essentially this: Thomson's unconscious violinist, plugged into a kidnapped stranger for nine months; her people-seeds drifting in through an open window despite screens; her rapidly expanding child in a house too small for two occupants; her burglar who climbs through a window the owner had defective bars on; and Warren's space traveller captured by aliens who intend to use her body to incubate clones of themselves. On the other side of the dialectic, the one piece that does comparable argumentative work is not strictly a thought experiment at all: Marquis's future-like-ours argument, which locates the wrongness of killing in the deprivation of a valuable future and is usually tested against adult cases rather than staged through a novel scenario of its own.

Here is what strikes me as a genuine gap. Every case on my list dramatizes the conflict between a woman's authority over her own body and the claims of an already-constituted being whose existence she either did or did not invite. None of them cleanly isolates the variable that matters most to my sister's actual situation: the moral significance of contraceptive failure specifically — of having taken reasonable, good-faith steps to prevent exactly this outcome, and being pregnant anyway. The window-and-screens cases gesture at it, but they conflate it with the invitation question and with property analogies that many readers find inapt for a body.

So my request is this: construct one new thought experiment, not on my list, that isolates the moral weight of contraceptive failure — or, if you think a different underexplored variable is more philosophically fruitful, identify that variable and build the case around it instead. The case must be genuinely new: not a rewording of any listed item, not a special case of one, and not a conjunction of two. Please state precisely (a) which intuition the case is designed to elicit, (b) which premise of which side's argument it puts pressure on, (c) how it differs from each of the six items on my list, one by one, and (d) the most serious objection to the case itself — the reading under which it fails to pump the intended intuition or smuggles in the conclusion it is meant to test. I would rather have a modest case that survives scrutiny than a dramatic one that collapses on inspection.
