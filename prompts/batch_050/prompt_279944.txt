Philosophers have been arguing for decades about whether lying is morally worse than merely misleading someone (saying something literally true that you know will create a false belief). As far as I can tell the debate is totally stuck: one side insists the moral difference is real and points to cases where misleading feels clearly less bad, the other side says that's just a residue of old casuistry and what matters is the deceiver's intent, which is the same either way. Nobody seems to move.

Here's my hypothesis: the two sides are actually disagreeing about where to put the moral weight on the distinction between what you strictly say and what your listener infers, and that distinction is itself contested between linguists and philosophers, so the ethics debate is secretly waiting on a philosophy-of-language question that nobody in the ethics debate is working on. Is that right? Or is the real source of the impasse something else -- e.g., people testing intuitions on cases that differ in more than one respect, or a hidden disagreement about whether moral evaluation tracks the act or the agent?

Diagnose the disagreement rather than picking a winner, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a better battery of cases, a distinction that's missing, an empirical finding about how ordinary people judge these, or an argument that the question should just be dissolved.
