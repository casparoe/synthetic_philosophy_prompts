I got into a running argument with my roommate last week about whether self-deception is even possible, and I want to settle it properly. Her position is basically that the very phrase borrows its sense from ordinary interpersonal lying, and if you take the analogy seriously it collapses. In a normal lie, person A knows that p and deliberately gets person B to believe not-p. In self-deception, A and B live in the same skull, so the deceiver has to know p while the deceived believes not-p -- one person, one head, two flatly contradictory beliefs, plus an intention to hide the truth from someone who is himself. Her conclusion: nobody literally deceives themselves, and people we call self-deceived are really just putting on a performance for others, or wishfully hoping out loud. But that seems to erase something real. My uncle has been told by two doctors his drinking is destroying his liver and he will, completely sincerely as far as I can tell, describe himself as a social drinker who got unlucky with his bloodwork. He doesn't seem to be acting.

So: take her analogy as an argument and audit it. Lay out the mapping explicitly -- deceiver, deceived, the intention to mislead, the false belief installed, the concealment -- and say which elements have a plausible home in a single mind and which don't. A philosophy tutorial I sat in on once distinguished a static puzzle (can one person hold p and not-p at the same time without dissolving into mere ambivalence?) from a dynamic puzzle (how could anyone successfully execute the project of getting themselves to believe what they currently know is false, given that they'd have to keep the plan hidden from the very person running it?). Tell me whether the analogy's breakdown tracks one of these, both, or neither, and which disanalogies are just incidental -- for instance, you can walk away from a liar but not from yourself, and lies usually involve speech while this mostly doesn't.

The part I care about most: does her deflationary conclusion actually survive the audit, or does it only follow if you demand that self-deception match interpersonal deception feature for feature? If there's a weaker model that keeps the phenomenon -- motivated evidence-gathering without a deceptive intention, some kind of functional partition in the mind, a gap between what someone avows and what their behavior betrays -- spell out which phenomenon it saves and which it quietly drops (does it still cover my uncle, or only milder cases?). And if the lying analogy turns out to be beyond repair as a model, propose a better one -- I'd welcome one stolen from outside philosophy entirely -- and say exactly what it gets right that the lying model gets wrong.
