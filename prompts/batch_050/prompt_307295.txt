I am a literary novelist, and my new novel is set in a deindustrialized town and narrated in alternating first person by two characters on opposite sides of its central conflict: a laid-off plant worker who becomes a right-populist city councilor, and an environmental organizer who has fought for years to keep the plant closed. I spent two years interviewing people in such towns before writing it, and I tried to make each narrator the more persuasive one while on the page. On the strength of the book I have been invited to give the annual public lecture at a flagship state university in a region that votes heavily against my own politics. The series draws honors students, faculty, and a good number of local residents, and the question period is famously long and unmoderated. Both the campus activist groups and the College Republicans will be in the room, and my publisher has made it clear the evening will be recorded.

In the lecture I intend to defend the following thesis: that most political disagreement is not a contest between liars and truth-tellers, because each side inherits a different network of trust — different authorities, different testimonies, different lived evidence — and given those inputs, each side reasons roughly as well as the other. On this picture the echo chamber is a problem of environment rather than of character, and accusations of bad faith are themselves mostly symptoms of the phenomenon they claim to describe. I also want to argue that fiction's distinctive contribution is precisely this: it lets a reader spend three hundred pages inside a trust network they would never voluntarily enter, and that this does something an op-ed cannot. What I do not want to do is retreat, when pressed, into the claim that it is only a novel and I make no empirical assertions — I am making one, and I want to defend it honestly.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections I should expect, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage each does if it lands, phrased the way each audience segment would actually phrase it: the activist students for whom a both-sides framing launders injustice and treats a factually false position as merely a different trust network; the conservative students and town residents who suspect a coastal novelist has studied them like specimens and will want to know which narrator I privately agree with; and the political science faculty who will press me on the empirical literature — motivated reasoning, affective polarization, and the finding I have seen summarized that people's actual online news diets are more ideologically mixed than the echo-chamber metaphor implies — knowing I cannot defend the studies themselves under questioning. For each objection, tell me what I should concede up front, what the one-sentence reply is, and where that reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and say what the single answer is that has to land for the lecture's thesis to survive the evening. Finally, tell me where this prep sheet itself is most likely to be wrong: what you could learn about the audience, or what piece of evidence or argument, would lead you to reorder the objections or change your verdict on which one is most dangerous to me.
