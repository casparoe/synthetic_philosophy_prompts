I serve on the seven-member Commons Allocation Chamber of the first permanent settlement on an uninhabited, resource-poor planet. The Chamber’s charter permits it to recognize exclusive property claims when first appropriation creates a genuine improvement and does not unjustly prejudice later arrivals. One claimant, the Meridian Cooperative, has spent twelve years drilling into a deep subterranean ice field and building the reactors, pumps, filtration plant, and access tunnels that alone make its water usable. Meridian now seeks permanent and exclusive ownership not merely of the water it extracts and the facilities it built, but of the entire forty-square-kilometre field, including deposits it does not expect to reach for another sixty years.

Two other ice fields remain unclaimed. Together they contain substantially more water, but reaching them would cost later settlers roughly twice as much, and one contains minerals requiring more intensive purification. Meridian’s pumping has also produced a brine discharge that, unless captured, will gradually contaminate an estimated fifth of the unclaimed portion of its field. The Chamber may grant the claim, deny it, or impose conditions concerning duration, extraction limits, compulsory access, environmental remediation, compensation, and future settlers.

Draft the written opinion I should circulate before our vote. It should recommend a concrete disposition and timetable, then defend that recommendation through a careful application of Locke’s labour theory of acquisition and the Lockean proviso. Distinguish among ownership of extracted water, ownership of productive infrastructure, and ownership of the untouched field; explain what kind of labour creates which entitlement, if any; and assess whether physically abundant but materially less accessible reserves count as leaving enough and as good for others. The opinion should also address spoilage and waste, the relevance of Meridian’s having created the opportunity it now seeks to monopolize, and the claims of settlers who do not yet exist. Give the strongest argument for the losing side an honest and non-caricatured statement before answering it. The opinion should be approximately 900–1,200 words and suitable for adoption as the Chamber’s decision.
