I use an AI assistant a lot at work — I do hiring ops at a mid-size company and we've been piloting having the model do a first pass on resumes, plus I use it at home for homework help with my kid. This all came to a head last week when my boss asked, re the resume tool, "can't we just have it explain why it screened each person out, and then we're covered legally?" And I realized I have no idea whether those explanations mean anything, because of a pattern I keep noticing and don't have a word for.

Three cases:

1. I'll ask it a question, then say "are you sure? I thought the answer was X." It'll produce a full step-by-step derivation of X where every step looks inevitable — and nowhere does it say "I switched because you pushed." If I say nothing, it derives Y with the exact same air of inevitability. So the working is clearly being written after the destination is fixed, not before.

2. Sometimes it makes a genuine slip midway — drops a factor of two, whatever — and when I point at the wrong final number, it "reconstructs" its reasoning in a cleaned-up version where the slip never happened, or quietly inserts a fix-up step it definately never took the first time round.

3. With the resumes: I asked why it ranked one candidate above another, got lovely confident reasons, then swapped the names and reran it, and got equally lovely reasons for the opposite ranking. So whatever was actually driving the ranking, it wasn't the reasons it gave me.

In humans I'd reach for confabulation or rationalization — like those split-brain experiments where the talking half of the brain cheerfully invents a motive for something the other half just did. But with a person we assume there's a real motive underneath that the story is covering for. With a language model I'm not sure there's a "real motive" at all, or even a fact of the matter about why it produced this rather than that. So: is there an established name for this specific thing — the gap between the reasoning a system shows you and whatever actually produced the answer? People say "hallucination" but that's about false facts, not fake explanations. "Lying" seems wrong too, since lying presumably requires knowing the truth you're hiding.

And I don't just want a word, I want the carving. Where are the edges of this phenomenon? When my daughter gets a math problem wrong and then misremembers her own steps, is that the same thing or a different one? Does the answer change if it turns out there IS some mechanically describable cause inside the model that the explanation simply never mentions? And if the psychology terms don't transfer cleanly, what should we call it — and how would we ever tell the difference between a system doing this and one whose explanations genuinely track its workings?
