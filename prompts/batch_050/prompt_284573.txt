At my university's debating society, a member of the pro-life society ended a debate with a single move: "A person's right to life outweighs the mother's right to decide what happens in and to her body, so abortion is impermissible." When pressed on what the outweighing claim meant, he simply repeated it, as if repetition made it clearer. I have been reading Judith Jarvis Thomson's "A Defense of Abortion" (Philosophy & Public Affairs, 1971), where she reconstructs the standard argument from the fetus's personhood in just these terms:

"Every person has a right to life. So the fetus has a right to life. No doubt the mother has a right to decide what shall happen in and to her body; everyone would grant that. But surely a person's right to life is stronger and more stringent than the mother's right to decide what happens in and to her body, and so outweighs it. So the fetus may not be killed; an abortion may not be performed."

I suspect the slogan compresses several quite different claims, and I want them separated. Distinguish the precise theses it could express: about what the right to life is a right to (not being killed? not being killed unjustly? whatever one needs to survive?), about what "outweighs" amounts to (absolute priority? a defeasible default? a comparison of the gravity of wrongs rather than of rights?), and about whether the argument really turns on the stringency of the right to life or on its content. For each reading, state the thesis exactly and say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or simply false — and whether it is the reading Thomson's reconstructed argument actually needs. Then commit: which reading is the most defensible, does the violinist case defeat that reading or only cruder ones, and was my opponent's slogan-deploying move any good? I would rather have your verdict with reasons than a neutral survey.
