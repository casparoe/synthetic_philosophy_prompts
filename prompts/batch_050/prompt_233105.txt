I'm in the second term of a graduate seminar on AI safety, and our current unit is on iterated amplification and its idealized limit, the construction often called HCH: a human answering a question by consulting copies of themselves, each of whom answers sub-questions by consulting further copies, with the tree allowed to run to arbitrary depth and the human given unlimited time. I've found myself defending a fairly strong claim in discussion, and I'd like you to stress-test it rather than help me defend it. The claim is that HCH, taken as a limit rather than as a practical scheme, is a faithful idealization of idealized human deliberation — that is, the answer the tree converges to is the answer the seed human would themselves reach if they could reflect with unlimited time and cognitive capacity — and that this is what makes it an attractive alignment target, since it preserves the human's values and judgment while scaling capability.

So: what would count against this view? I'm granting the idealization itself, so "you can't actually build the tree" doesn't interest me. I want the conceptual and empirical defeaters. Some candidates we've touched on in class, which I'd like you to develop and to rank from potentially fatal to merely awkward: the possibility that some questions don't decompose — that arriving at a correct answer requires a holistic insight no decomposition into sub-questions can reconstruct; instability or path-dependence in the amplification process, so that the tree's output depends on arbitrary features of how questions get split rather than on anything the human would endorse on reflection; value drift across the depth of the tree, where small distortions at each layer compound; and the worry that the seed human would endorse the tree's output not because it is right but because they lack the capability to detect where the decomposition went wrong, making the endorsement test toothless. For each, tell me what the world — or what formal or experimental results — would have to look like for it to actually sink the view, and which of these a committed defender of HCH-as-idealization would themselves concede as a defeater versus dismiss as a mere implementation concern. I'd also like an honest verdict on whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if every apparent failure can be redescribed as the tree being run incorrectly or not deeply enough, is there anything left that the view risks, and is that a problem for an idealization of this kind, or is insulation from refutation exactly what an idealization is supposed to have?

Finally, please be explicit about your own epistemic state throughout: mark how confident you are in each of the main claims you make — especially your verdicts on which defeaters are decisive — and at the end, single out the single point in your answer you are least sure of and say what would change your mind about it.
