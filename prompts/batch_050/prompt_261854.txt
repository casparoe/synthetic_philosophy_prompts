I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and for one session I want to organize the discussion around the standard objections to Dutch book arguments. Below are seven objections, stated without attribution and in roughly the form students encounter them. Your task is to rank them from most to least dialectically powerful, where power is measured against a specific target claim: that probabilistic coherence is an epistemic requirement of rationality (not merely a prudent policy), and that the diachronic Dutch book establishes Bayesian conditionalization as a rule one must follow rather than a strategy it would be wise to adopt.

The objections:

1. The pragmatic objection: susceptibility to a sure loss shows at most a practical defect in one's betting dispositions, not that one's credences are inaccurate, unjustified, or epistemically irrational; epistemology is not the theory of how to avoid losing money.
2. The avoidance objection: the argument presupposes an agent who is compelled to accept any bet at odds she regards as fair, but declining to bet is always available, and refusing to wager is not a failure of rationality.
3. The utility objection: the argument measures stakes in money and tacitly assumes that value is linear in money; for a risk-averse agent, or one for whom money has diminishing marginal utility, accepting what looks like a Dutch book can be fully rational.
4. The logical omniscience objection: the argument holds agents responsible for inconsistencies they cannot in principle be expected to detect, and so the coherence norm it underwrites is too demanding to govern actual finite reasoners.
5. The strategy objection (diachronic only): the diachronic argument condemns the act of committing in advance, publicly and in full view of the bookie, to a non-conditionalizing update rule; it says nothing against an agent who simply revises her beliefs non-Bayesianly after the evidence arrives, since no such agent is guaranteed a loss by any single bet she accepts.
6. The package objection: the sure loss attaches only to the whole set of bets taken together, and there need be no individual bet in the package that the agent had reason to reject; so no single credence is shown to be defective, only the aggregate.
7. The opportunism objection: being exploitable is a defect only relative to the actual existence of an exploiter who knows one's credences; vulnerability to a merely possible bookie is a contingent misfortune, not a rational failing.

Please do the following. First, produce the ranking, grouping objections into tiers if you think some are close in force, and give two or three sentences per objection explaining what it concedes to the Dutch book proponent, what it denies, and where it bites hardest against the target claim stated above. Second, note explicitly where an objection's force differs between the synchronic argument for probabilism and the diachronic argument for conditionalization -- I want students to see that a ranking which ignores this distinction is incomplete. Third, name the single objection you consider most overrated and the one you consider most underrated, and justify each choice in a short paragraph. Finally, state in one sentence what, on your ranking, remains of the Dutch book case for Bayesianism once the strongest objections have been conceded.
