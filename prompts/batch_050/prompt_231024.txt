I am a novelist at work on a historical novel set in England in the 1840s, during the final collapse of duelling. For texture, I need a document that could plausibly have existed in that world, and I would like you to write it for me: a chapter from an invented etiquette manual for gentlemen, of the kind that explained the code of honour to young men, advising readers on how to conduct themselves now that the duel has begun to fall into disrepute. Around 650 words, in British English, deadpan throughout, with a chapter heading and the slightly pompous cadence of a conduct writer who does not realise he is a comic figure.

The satire should work on two levels, and this is where I must be exacting. On the surface it should mock the practice itself: the elaborate casuistry of what constitutes a challengeable slight, the seconds negotiating apologies with the precision of conveyancing solicitors, the punctilio about pistols at dawn over an insult muttered at a card table. Beneath that, it should enact a thesis I care about, associated with Bernard Williams and developed at length by Kwame Anthony Appiah, about how moral revolutions actually happen. The thought, roughly, is that duelling was not argued out of existence: every serious argument against it (its irrationality, its impiety, its unfairness, its tendency to punish the truthful and reward the touchy) had been stated plainly for well over a century, and men duelled anyway. What killed it, on this view, was a shift in honour: the practice came to seem absurd, then provincial, then embarrassing, until the reputational cost ran the other way.

So the chapter should dramatise that mechanism without ever stating it. Some devices I would welcome: the author reciting the familiar arguments against duelling only to wave them aside as long-answered trifles, revealing that everyone has known them all along; his growing preoccupation not with whether duelling is wrong but with whether it is still done, with what they now think in London clubs, with the distressing levity of the younger generation; a fretful passage on how to preserve one's honour when one's opponent simply laughs; perhaps a melancholy footnote lamenting that a certain sort of man no longer regards being shot at as a compliment. A clergyman character quoted approvingly within the text, whose decades of sermons achieved nothing, would land the point nicely against a single passing remark that mockery has done more in a season than sermons did in fifty years.

The irony must stay legible but unwinking: the author never suspects what he is documenting. Do not have him endorse or refute the thesis; let a reader who knows the debate recognise it, and a reader who does not simply enjoy a funny period piece. No real historical persons need appear; invented names and invented manual titles only. And please keep the piece funny through accuracy rather than exaggeration for its own sake: the comedy should come from the code's genuine logic, pursued earnestly, one step past the moment the world stopped taking it seriously.
