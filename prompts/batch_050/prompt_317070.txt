The argument between Americans and Europeans about inequality has been running for decades and seems completly frozen. Roughly: Europeans tend to treat large inequality as a sign something went wrong and want redistribution; Americans are more tolerant of it and more likely to see outcomes as earned. The standard story is that the difference rides on beliefs -- Americans supposedly overestimate social mobility and how much effort determines success, Europeans the reverse, and redistribution preferences track those beliefs. My working hypothesis is that the debate only looks empirical: both sides talk as if fixing the facts about mobility and the role of luck would settle it, and I doubt that's true. I suspect underneath there's a bedrock disagreement about what a society owes you when you lose, or about whether desert is even the right currency for thinking about this, and the factual disputes are just where it's easiest to fight. But maybe I'm wrong and it really is mostly a factual disagreement plus genuinely different exposures to what luck does to a life. Diagnose it rather than adjudicate it: which parts are factual, which are about standards of evidence (what counts as luck vs effort, whose data you trust), and which are starting intuitions neither side can argue the other out of? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress -- better mobility data, a cleaner conceptual distinction, a reframed question, or nothing? And tell me what would change your mind about the diagnosis itself, i.e. where it's most likely to fail.
