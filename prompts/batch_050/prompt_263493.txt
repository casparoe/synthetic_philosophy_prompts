I am preparing the final examination for my upper-level undergraduate seminar in normative ethics, and I need one essay question on the aggregation of tiny harms — the "torture versus dust specks" problem — together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The exam is seventy-five minutes and closed-book; students are expected to write roughly 700–900 words; the question is worth 50 marks; no citations to the literature are required, though students may invoke standard arguments by name if they wish.

The question must be built around the following scenario I have devised, and it must be constructed so that an adequate answer has to engage all three of its variants rather than reciting the general point that consequentialists aggregate harms and their critics refuse to.

The city of Aldermarsh must replace its water treatment plant, and the council can afford only one of two systems. System A will, with near certainty, cause one specific maintenance worker a severe and permanent injury: chronic pain and the loss of her vocation. System B eliminates that risk but imposes a tiny cost on each of the city's four million residents. The complication is that the engineers have issued three incompatible reports about what that tiny cost actually is, and the council does not know which report is correct. Under Report One ("the Flicker"), each resident will, on exactly one occasion, experience a barely noticeable moment of irritation — consciously registered, immediately forgotten, and smaller than the displeasure of stepping in a puddle. Under Report Two ("the Cell"), each resident will lose a single retinal cell in one eye, a change that makes no difference whatsoever to anyone's experience, capacities, or prospects — undetectable from the inside, even in principle. Under Report Three ("the Dice"), each resident's lifetime risk of one specific serious misfortune rises by one chance in ten million, so that the expected number of additional victims citywide is well under one: very probably, no one is ever actually harmed.

The exam question should ask: for which of these reports, if any, may the council permissibly choose System B on the ground that the aggregated tiny costs fall short of the single severe harm? Please phrase the question so that students must state a verdict and defend it, not merely survey positions.

In the model answer, identify the specific moves a top answer must make, including at minimum:

1. A distinction between a cost that is tiny but genuinely bad for the person who bears it and an effect that is not bad for that person at all, together with the observation that the naive summation argument silently assumes the former — if the Cell report describes something wholly neutral for each person, then aggregating it across four million people is summing zeros, and the comparison with the worker's injury never gets going. The genuinely hard case is the Flicker, where each cost is real but trivial.
2. A clear statement of the spectrum or continuum argument for the Flicker case: each pairwise comparison between a slightly more severe harm to one person and a slightly lesser harm spread across many seems to favor the latter, and transitivity of "at least as choiceworthy as" then forces a verdict. The answer must then present at least two principled points of resistance — for example, a lumpy or vague threshold below which harms cannot be traded against severe ones, a denial that the relevant comparative relation is transitive across the whole spectrum, or a separateness-of-persons objection holding that the intrapersonal trade-offs we accept within one life do not transfer to trade-offs across lives, where someone is always left bearing the uncompensated cost.
3. Separate treatment of the Dice report, since aggregating minuscule risks is an ex ante matter rather than an aggregation of actual harms. The question there is whether exposing everyone to a vanishingly small risk is permissible even when the expected number of victims rounds to zero — and whether the reasoning that licenses it would equally license a "statistical" version of the severe harm, which would collapse the very distinction the council is trying to preserve.
4. A defended verdict on each variant, hedged if necessary, with the reasoning that ties the three verdicts together into a coherent general view of aggregation.

The marking scheme should allocate the 50 marks across components, state what earns full credit within each, and — just as importantly — name the specific errors that should cost marks. These should include at least the following: treating imperceptibility as a merely epistemic limitation (we cannot measure the effect) rather than as the claim that the person is not affected in any way that could matter to them; conflating the Cell report with the Dice report, i.e., confusing an imperceptible effect with a small risk of a perceptible one; asserting a lexical threshold at serious harm without confronting the spectrum argument that pressures any such threshold; invoking the repugnant conclusion as a label or a refutation rather than as an argument whose structure must be engaged; and answering entirely in the aggregate — total disvalue — without ever addressing what can be said from the standpoint of the one severely injured worker.

Finally, calibrate the difficulty so that a median student can describe the dilemma and rehearse the standard positions, but only a genuinely strong student can locate where the pairwise-comparison reasoning actually breaks down, or argue convincingly that it does not. In the model answer, quote or paraphrase the two or three sentences you would expect to find only in the very best scripts, and note in the marking scheme how many marks hinge on them.
