I teach an upper-level seminar on consciousness, and every year we hit the same wall. We spend three weeks on Dennett's multiple drafts model -- the idea that there is no single place or moment in the brain where content becomes conscious, that at any time there are multiple parallel 'drafts' of narrative content at various stages of revision, and that probing the system at different times yields different reports without any fact of the matter about which draft was 'really' conscious first -- and then three weeks on the critics, who respond that Dennett has not explained consciousness but explained it away, that he treats introspective reports as exhaustively constitutive of phenomenology when they are at best fallible evidence for it, and that his 'fame in the brain' metaphor quietly concedes the very thing it denies, since fame still happens somewhere and to someone. Dennett's counter, in turn, is that his critics remain in the grip of what he calls the Cartesian theater: the picture of a inner screen where contents are presented to a witnessing subject, at a definite time, in a definite place. Thirty years on, as far as I can tell, nobody has moved. Both sides claim the other has simply failed to understand the position.

This year I want to anchor the unit in the historical text itself. In the Sixth Meditation, Descartes writes that the mind is not immediately affected by all parts of the body, but only by the brain -- or perhaps only by one small part of it, the part where the 'common sense' is said to reside -- and that whenever this part is disposed in one and the same way, the same thing is presented to the mind, whatever may be happening in the rest of the body. I have two questions about this passage. First, as a matter of interpretation: does it actually commit Descartes to the theater picture Dennett attributes to him -- a privileged terminus where everything 'comes together' for the subject -- or does it say something weaker and more defensible, merely that consciousness supervenes on some neural state rather than being distributed over the whole causal chain? Dennett himself has conceded that everyone, including himself, accepts some version of the weak claim; the question is whether the strong one is anyone's actual view. Second, and this is the question I really want to put to my students: does the fairness of the attribution matter for diagnosing why the larger debate has stalled?

So here is my request. Diagnose the impasse between multiple drafts and its critics rather than adjudicating it. Is this a substantive disagreement about the structure of consciousness, or a verbal one sustained by the fact that each side defines 'consciousness' by what it takes to be the explanandum -- phenomenology as what it's like, versus phenomenology as whatever is fixed by the subject's dispositions to judge and report? My own hypothesis, offered tentatively, is that the dispute is kept alive by an equivocation on what counts as data: the critics treat the felt temporal order of experience as a datum any theory must save, while Dennett treats the reports and the neural timeline as the data and the felt order as a construction the theory is free to dissolve. If that is right, then experiments like the color phi phenomenon or the cutaneous rabbit do not decide between the sides at all, because each side reads them through its own stipulation of what would need explaining. Tell me whether you think that diagnosis is correct, what would count as progress if it is -- a stipulated distinction, a constraint on what theories of consciousness may treat as given, or something else -- and whether the passage from the Sixth Meditation shows that the impasse was baked in from the start or is instead a distinctly modern artifact of how the problem came to be framed.
