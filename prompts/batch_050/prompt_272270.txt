A friend in my metaphysics seminar has been defending an argument, adapted from the sea-battle reasoning in Aristotle's discussion of future contingents, that seems to prove far more than anyone in the room is willing to accept. Here it is as he states it:

(1) Either there will be a sea battle tomorrow or there will not be. (Excluded middle.)

(2) If a proposition is true at all, it is true now: truth does not wait for the events that make it true, since a statement about tomorrow is either correct or incorrect as things presently stand.

(3) If it is now true that there will be a sea battle, then there is nothing anyone can do between now and tomorrow to make it false. What is already the case is settled; the present truth of the proposition cannot be undone by future action any more than a past event can be undone.

(4) What cannot be prevented or altered by anything we do is necessary, at least relative to us: an outcome that no possible course of action could avert is, for all practical and deliberative purposes, unavoidable.

(5) So whichever outcome will occur is necessary relative to us, and the other outcome was never genuinely possible.

(6) Since (1) guarantees that one disjunct is now true, every future event is necessary relative to us in this sense.

(7) Deliberation is rational only when more than one outcome is genuinely open. So deliberation about anything future is irrational, and there are no contingent truths about what has not yet happened.

The conclusion strikes me as absurd -- it would make fools of admirals, voters, and everyone packing an umbrella -- but I cannot find the step I want to reject. Diagnose where the argument goes wrong, if it does. If the flaw is an illicit move between steps, say exactly which inference is bad and why it can look valid. If the argument is valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to surrender, and explain what the main alternatives cost: I gather some have denied that future contingents have truth values at all, and others have distinguished a proposition's being true now from its being settled now, but I do not know what each side has to give up. For the record, I would most like to keep (1) and (7); I am willing to get more subtle about (2) through (4).
