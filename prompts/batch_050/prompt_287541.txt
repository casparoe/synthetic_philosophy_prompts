I'm TAing a discussion section for an undergrad metaphysics course and we've landed on dispositions -- finks, masks, the conditional analysis, all that. In prepping I realized my own intutions are a mess and I can't figure out what principle I'm running on, if any. So here are my verdicts on some cases. Tell me what principle unifies them, or if no clean one does, tell me which verdict is cheapest to give up and what the repaired principle then predicts for cases I haven't thought of.

1. A wine glass sits on a shelf. Unbeknownst to anyone, a demon is watching it: the moment the glass is struck and begins to break, the demon instantly alters its molecular structure so it becomes the sort of thing that wouldn't have broken anyway. Verdict: the glass is fragile right now, sitting there. The demon doesn't change what it is, only what would happen.

2. An identical wine glass ships encased in fitted styrofoam. Struck with the same force, it doesn't break. Verdict: still fragile the whole time. The foam masks the fragility, it doesn't remove it.

3. A rubber band is cooled to near absolute zero, where rubber goes brittle and snaps rather than stretches. Verdict: while frozen, it is NOT elastic. The cold doesn't just mask the elasticity, it takes it away.

4. A sugar cube is sealed inside a solid block of lucite. Verdict: still water-soluble. Masked, not gone.

5. A person has been given a permanent, irreversible antidote in their bloodstream that neutralizes a certain poison on contact. Verdict: the poison is still poisonous, period. It just won't ever poison her.

6. A flash of magnesium ribbon sits in a vacuum chamber with no oxygen. Verdict: still flammable. Though I'm less sure here -- maybe the vacuum isn't a mask but just the absence of something burning needs?

7. A styrofoam cup that would never break on impact, except the same demon from case 1 now shatters it whenever it's struck, to make it look fragile. Verdict: not fragile. Being made to break isn't the same as being breakable, if that makes sense.

8. A magnet is heated above the point where it stops attracting iron filings. Verdict: while hot, it's genuinely not magnetic -- this one feels like case 3, not case 2.

So the trouble: 2, 4, and 5 are "masked, still has it"; 3 and 8 are "genuinely lost it"; and 1 says a standing threat of intervention doesn't touch the disposition. But structurally, freezing the rubber band looks a lot like encasing the glass in foam -- in both, some condition external to the parts and arrangement of the object prevents manifestation, and in both you can restore manifestation by undoing that condition (take off the foam, warm up the band). So why do I call one a mask and the other a loss?

My tentative principle was: a disposition is masked rather than lost iff you could restore manifestation by changing only the object's surroundings, leaving the object itself as is. But warming the band up changes the surroundings more than it changes the band's parts or structure -- the rubber molecules are all still there, arranged the same -- so the principle says the frozen band is still elastic, contradicting verdict 3. And heating the magnet arguably changes it less, structurally, than extracting the sugar cube from lucite would. So my principle seems to give exactly backwards results on the pair I care most about.

So: what's the best principle (or small set of candidate principles) that captures most of my verdicts, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing which work? Which verdict does the simplest decent principle force me to drop, and what does each candidate say about fresh cases -- e.g., a person who's taken a fear-suppressing drug, are they still brave, and a match that's wet but will dry in ten minutes, is it flammable now? Also, please be upfront about which parts of this are basically settled territory in the literature (I gather the simple conditional analysis is dead?) and which parts are still genuinely contested among people who work on dispositions, rather than presenting one side as the consensus.
