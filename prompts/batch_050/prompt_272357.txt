A health policy newsletter my coworker forwarded me explains medical cost-effectiveness like this: 'When budgets are tight, health economists rank treatments by QALYs — quality-adjusted life years — which assign each patient's life a value based on how long they'll live and how healthy they'll be. So triage by QALYs means a young patient beats an old one, and a non-disabled person beats a disabled one, because their lives are worth more on the scale. Governments use a related figure, the value of a statistical life, about $10 million in the US, which is the price the state puts on a citizen's life when it decides whether a safety regulation is worth the cost. Critics call this discriminatory, but economists reply that some lives just produce more QALYs than others and the math doesn't lie.'

This rubbed me the wrong way but I can't articulate why, and I don't want to dismiss it if it's basically right. Go through it and tell me which parts a health economist would accept, which are oversimplifications they'd let slide, and which are actually misrepresentations — especially the stuff about lives being 'worth more' and the $10 million 'price of a life.' And does the concluding claim (that the discrimination objection is just innumeracy) follow from the accurate parts, or does it smuggle something in?
