I coordinate a reading group on ergodicity economics, and after a term working through the time-average versus ensemble-average literature we have a standing exercise: each member circulates a written statement of their own commitments so the group can stress-test it before discussion. My turn is next week, and I would like a consistency audit before I send mine out. I am reasonably confident about the individual pieces, but I suspect I may have quietly combined claims that were each defended on different grounds in the sources I read, and I would rather find out now than in front of the group. Here is where I have landed:

(1) For decisions under multiplicative dynamics repeated over time, the time-average growth rate of wealth is the rational criterion, and ensemble-average (expected value) reasoning misleads because such wealth processes are non-ergodic. In the geometric Brownian motion picture, the ensemble average grows at the drift rate while the typical trajectory grows at drift minus half the variance, and an individual lives one trajectory, so it is the second rate that matters.

(2) Maximizing the time-average growth rate of wealth is equivalent to maximizing the expected logarithm of wealth, which means Bernoulli's resolution of the St. Petersburg paradox is recovered from the mathematics of multiplicative dynamics alone — no appeal to diminishing marginal utility, risk psychology, or any other contingent feature of human preferences is needed.

(3) The framework thereby vindicates behaviors that look irrational under expected-value theory: buying insurance at actuarially unfair prices, and refusing one-shot bets that have positive expected value but fat left tails.

(4) People who maximize expected value in repeated gambles are not expressing a different, equally legitimate risk preference; they are taking the wrong average, which is a mathematical error. The dispute between the two camps is technical, not normative.

(5) In my own investing, however, I size positions at roughly half-Kelly rather than full Kelly, because the drawdowns full Kelly implies would be psychologically unbearable for me.

Please go through this set and tell me whether it hangs together. For each genuine tension you find, tell me which commitments are involved, how severe the tension is, and what the cheapest fix would be and what that fix costs; rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. Also tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I would like you to discriminate rather than flag everything: I suspect at least one pair of these commitments only looks inconsistent but actually dissolves on inspection, and a good audit should say so and explain why. One thing I ask you not to do is simply defend or attack ergodicity economics as a whole — I am not asking whether the framework is true, only whether my particular package of commitments is jointly tenable.
