The question we set was:

"State the Principal Principle as precisely as you can, and explain what it is meant to govern. Then consider the following worry: sometimes we know the chance of an event but also possess other information that seems directly relevant to whether it will occur. Does the principle tell us to set our credences by the chance anyway? If so, defend that consequence; if not, explain how the principle survives the apparent counterexample."

Here is the student's answer:

"The Principal Principle, due to David Lewis, says that a rational agent's credence in a proposition, conditional on knowing that the proposition's objective chance is p, should itself be p. So if I know a fair coin has a one-half chance of landing heads, I should believe to degree one-half that it will land heads. The principle is meant to capture the special authority that objective chance has over rational belief: chances are the kind of thing that, once known, settle what you ought to think.

The principle is best understood as an empirical generalisation about what rational agents actually do, confirmed by the observation that people's betting behaviour tracks known frequencies. Indeed, objective chance just is long-run relative frequency: when we say the chance of heads is one-half, we mean that in a long run of tosses the coin lands heads half the time, and so the principle amounts to the sensible advice to match your credences to the observed frequencies.

As for the worry in the question: the principle is strict, and it should be. If I know the chance of heads is one-half, then no other information can change what I ought to believe, since the chance already summarises everything relevant. For example, suppose the coin has been tossed and a friend has seen it land heads, but I have not. Even if my friend tells me he saw the outcome, I should keep my credence at one-half until I see the coin myself, because the chance of the toss was one-half and the principle instructs me to defer to the chance. Biting this bullet is the price of taking chance seriously, and the alternative — letting any scrap of information override the known chance — would collapse the distinction between chance and mere opinion.

The principle therefore survives the counterexample by denying that it is one: other information is only ever relevant to credence by informing us about what the chances are."
