I am a high school junior, and my school has three practices that seem to me to pull in different directions, and I would like help figuring out what they jointly assume about morality.

First, graduation requires forty hours of verified community service. The paperwork records only what you did and where; no one asks why you did it, and hours count the same whether you loved the work or resented every minute. Second, the school's annual citizenship award is explicitly different: nominating letters emphasize that the winner helped others when there was nothing to gain, and students who are visibly padding their college applications are quietly passed over, even if their service record is longer. Third, my civics teacher defends the service requirement by arguing that many students who begin volunteering only for the hours end up genuinely caring, and that manufacturing the habit is exactly the point of the requirement.

Consider the standard menu of views about moral worth: the broadly Kantian view that an action has moral worth only when done from the right motive rather than from self-interest; the view that only the good produced matters, motives being neither here nor there; and the broadly Aristotelian view that character is built through repeated action, so that doing the right thing for bad reasons can be a step toward doing it for good ones. Which view does each of the three practices presuppose, if any? Can a single coherent theory of moral worth license all three at once, or is my school committed to inconsistent positions? And tell me what would concretely have to change in each practice if we replaced its presupposed view with one of its rivals — for instance, what the service requirement or the award would look like under a strict Kantian who held that coerced or incentivized service has no moral worth at all. Finally, say where the practices are underdetermined: which of their features are compatible with any of the three views and therefore teach us nothing about what the school really assumes.
