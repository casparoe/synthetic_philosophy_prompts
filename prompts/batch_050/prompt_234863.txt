I chair the five-member environmental review committee of a mid-sized municipality, and we must issue a recommendation next month on whether our water utility may switch to a newly developed algaecide for treating the reservoir that supplies most of our drinking water. The background is roughly this. The utility's engineers argue for approval: every required laboratory study shows the compound to be safe at the concentrations that would actually be used, it is far cheaper than our current method (mechanical harvesting combined with an older, less effective treatment), and the summers are getting warm enough that algal blooms are becoming a genuine threat to water quality, so delay itself carries costs. One committee member, a biologist, objects that the studies only run a few months and test standard endpoints, while the compound will persist at low levels in the reservoir for decades; she argues that where a potential harm would be effectively irreversible and very difficult to detect until it is widespread, the fact that nothing bad has shown up yet in short trials is close to no information at all. The engineers reply that this standard is unworkable — literally any new substance fails it, since one can always imagine an undetected long-term effect, and on her logic we could never adopt anything new at all.

The committee is split two-two, and I am the swing vote, so I need to write the decision memo myself. I am stuck on the deeper structure of the disagreement, not just the facts: both sides seem to agree on what the studies show, but disagree about who bears the burden of proof under uncertainty, and about whether "no demonstrated harm" should count the same as "demonstrated safety." I also suspect there must be some principled middle position — something that takes the biologist's worry about irreversibility and detection limits seriously without collapsing into the paralysis the engineers predict, perhaps involving the availability and cost of alternatives, or the possibility of conditional approval with monitoring and a defined trigger for reversal.

Please draft the committee's decision memo. It should contain a clear, actionable recommendation (approve, reject, or approve under specified conditions), explain the reasoning in terms a skeptical council member could follow, and give an honest statement of the strongest argument on whichever side loses, along with why it does not ultimately carry the day.
