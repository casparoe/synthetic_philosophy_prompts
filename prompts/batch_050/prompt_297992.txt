Anyone who has spent years reading rationalist blogs has met the Dutch book argument early and often: if your credences violate the probability axioms, a cunning bookie can offer you a package of bets you will happily accept and that loses you money no matter how the world turns out. The argument is usually presented as showing that incoherent degrees of belief are practically self-defeating. I have come to suspect it shows something narrower — that incoherent degrees of belief *held by a sufficiently obliging trader* are self-defeating — and I want a thought experiment built to probe exactly where the standard argument leans on the obligingness.

Construct a scenario meeting the following specifications:

1. The agent's credences violate at least one axiom of probability. Exhibit the violating assignments numerically and show the violation, rather than gesturing at it.
2. The incoherence must arise from a psychologically plausible policy the agent follows — something a reasonable person might actually do — and must not simply be stipulated into existence.
3. The agent must accept at least some bets at their stated fair prices. A policy of declining all wagers is cheating: the interest of the case lies in an agent who trades and still cannot be booked.
4. No demons, no perfect predictors, no exotic physics. Stakes should be sums of money an ordinary person might plausibly handle.
5. The bookie is allowed to know the agent's dispositions perfectly and to offer any finite package of bets, one package at a time; the agent accepts or declines according to a single explicit rule, stated up front, that covers every contingency.

Then demonstrate that the case works. State the agent's acceptance rule precisely; show that the credences it generates are incoherent; and show that for any finite package of bets the rule permits the bookie to get accepted, some possible outcome leaves the agent no worse off than before — so no sure-loss book exists. (This last step should be argued from the structure of the rule, not merely illustrated with two or three sample packages.)

After that, the analysis. Identify the single premise of the standard Dutch book argument that your case violates — candidates include the assumption that an agent's fair price is a price at which they will both buy and sell, that they price a package of bets as the sum of its parts, that their willingness to trade is independent of what they already hold, or that their utility is linear in money at these stakes — and pinpoint where in the usual proof that premise is silently doing the work. Then give the probabilist's best reply: whether the argument can be re-run against your agent with utility rather than monetary payoffs, or via a diachronic construction or money pump, and whether that reply concedes something about how much of probabilism the practical argument actually secures. Finally, note which feature of your case carries the load and confirm that removing it collapses the scenario back into a standard, working Dutch book.
