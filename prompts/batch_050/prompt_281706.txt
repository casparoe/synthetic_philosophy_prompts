ok so I'm on the hiring committee for our department (mid-sized public uni, hiring a tenure-track ethics person) and we use this scoring rubric that I've started to find philosophically weird. Every candidate gets scored 1-5 on four things: research, teaching, service/citizenship, and "fit." We sum the four scores and the highest total basically gets the offer. But there are two rules bolted on: (1) anyone who gets a 1 on teaching is out no matter what the total says — the official line is that bad teaching can't be compensated for by anything else, and (2) if two candiates finish within one point of each other, the tie gets broken by open committee discussion with no rubric at all, just "overall judgement."

So my question: what theory of value does this setup presuppose? The summing part seems to assume all four goods are measurable in one common unit, but the teaching-veto seems to deny exactly that, and the one-point tiebreak seems to deny it in a different way again (if a one-point gap counts as a tie, that's suspiciously close to that small-improvement argument — I think Ruth Chang's? — that two options can be neither better, worse, nor equal but merely "on a par"; don't worry about getting names right though, the idea matters more than the attribution).

What I want: reconstruct the implicit theory of value the rubric runs on. Say which view of value comparison each of the three parts smuggles in (full commensurability, lexical priority/trumping, incomparability or on-a-par-ness, whatever you think fits). Then tell me whether the three parts can be made consistent with each other or whether the committee is quietly committed to contradictory things. Finally: what would the rubric actually have to look like if we took seriously the claim that research excellence and teaching excellence are genuinely incomparable — and is any functioning committee rubric forced to just pretend values are commensurable, or is there a coherent way to run one without that pretense?

End your answer with a single line: Verdict: does our rubric presuppose full commensurability — yes, no, or only in parts.
