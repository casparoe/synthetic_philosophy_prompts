As a novelist developing a piece of literary speculative fiction, I want you to write a short story of roughly 2,000–2,500 words about sentience as a matter of degree.

Set the story in a near-future city where public institutions use a Sentience Allocation Index to distribute protection among animals, artificial systems, and human-built environments. The Index does not claim to detect consciousness directly. Instead, it combines behavioral evidence of nociception, associative learning, memory, self-maintenance, flexible goal pursuit, integration across time, and responsiveness to novelty. Although these are conceptually distinct dimensions, the law compresses them into one public score because courts, insurers, and infrastructure agencies need a workable threshold.

The protagonist is the caretaker of a failing research conservatory that houses both a managed colony of native insects and an old environmental-control system that has been incrementally upgraded for decades. During a prolonged power shortage, the caretaker can preserve the conditions needed by only one of them. The insects exhibit learning, avoidance, and altered behavior after injury, but their individual capacities are difficult to distinguish from the adaptive intelligence of the colony as a whole. The control system anticipates failures, revises its model of the building, protects its own continuity, and behaves differently depending on its history, yet it may have no valenced experience at all. Do not resolve the conflict by revealing that either side is secretly humanlike or obviously nonsentient.

The story should dramatize several conceptual problems without turning into a philosophical essay: whether degrees of sentience can be meaningfully represented by a single score; whether moral status attaches to individuals, colonies, functional systems, or patterns maintained over time; whether sophisticated self-preservation without pleasure or suffering creates morally significant interests; and how uncertainty should affect decisions when the available tests themselves risk harming the entities being tested. Include at least one scene in which the Index appears publicly authoritative while seeming privately inadequate to the protagonist, and one scene in which an attempt to gather more evidence changes the outcome being investigated.

Use close third-person narration, concrete sensory detail, and dialogue that arises naturally from the characters’ practical situation. The protagonist should face an irreversible choice with genuine costs on both sides. End with the decision and its aftermath, but leave the underlying moral question unresolved rather than having a character deliver the correct answer. Give the story a title.
