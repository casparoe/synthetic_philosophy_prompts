Next month I'm presenting a paper at our department's aesthetics colloquium defending the view that games are a distinctive art form whose medium is agency itself: where a painter manipulates pigment and a novelist manipulates prose, the game designer sculpts the practical reasoning of the player — the stipulated goals, the permitted means, the deliberately inefficient obstacles — and what the player aesthetically appreciates in play is not the game's audiovisual surface but her own acting within that designed structure. On my account, chess, basketball, and a well-crafted video game all belong to this art form, and a game's artistic quality is a matter of the struggles it affords and the temporary practical identities it lets us inhabit.

The audience will be about a dozen colleagues: mostly traditional aestheticians trained on Kant, Collingwood, and the institutional theories of the last century, none of whom work on games and some of whom regard them as mass entertainment at best; one philosophy-of-sport scholar who will be broadly sympathetic but protective of his own territory; and two philosophers of mind who will press on the notion of agency without caring much about the art question.
