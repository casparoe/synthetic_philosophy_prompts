So I'm taking this econ history / phil of social science seminar on path dependence, and our prof is obsessed with one particular distinction: cases that are genuinely locked in (a worse option won early, and now we're stuck with it because switching is too costly or uncoordinated) versus cases where something just persisted for boring reasons (it was fine all along, or it won fair and square). Over the term he's given his verdict on four cases:

1. VHS beating Betamax = genuine lock-in. Early lead, rental shops stocked VHS, feedback loop, and the arguably better format died.
2. Brunel's broad gauge (7ft) losing to Stephenson's narrower gauge = NOT lock-in. His line was basically: Stephenson's gauge was cheaper to build and good enough, so getting outcompeted on cost isn't a trap, it's just losing.
3. The US never switching to metric = lock-in. Possibly everyone would be better off after the switch, but nobody can move first, classic coordination failure.
4. AC beating DC in the war of the currents = not lock-in. The better technology won, and the transition happened as soon as AC was properly available — that's what a healthy technology market looks like, he said.

Homework is to classify three new cases, but I honestly care less about the answers than about reverse-engineering the rule he's running. The cases are: (a) the seven-day week, which has survived every revolution and calendar reform attempt; (b) the fact that some countries drive on the left and some on the right, and no country has switched sides in decades even though having one global standard would surely be convenient; and (c) gasoline cars beating the early electric cars around 1900, after which the whole fueling infrastructure, supply chains, and urban design built up around the winner.

Can you work out what rules are consistent with his four verdicts? I suspect there are at least two, maybe three (e.g. something about whether the loser was demonstrably better, something about whether rerunning history would give a different winner, something about whether a coordinated switch would actually be worth it). For each candidate rule, tell me what it predicts about cases (a), (b) and (c) — and where they disagree, tell me which prediction you think he'd actually give and why. Also: if one of his four verdicts already sits awkwardly with one of the natural rules, flag that. And finally, name one more historical case where the candidate rules diverge so sharply that his verdict on it would definately settle which rule he's running.
