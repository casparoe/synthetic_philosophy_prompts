So here's something that's been bugging me for years, and I figure I'd rather ask than keep arguing about it at the department happy hour. I teach a seminar on philosophy of history every other year, and every time we get to historical materialism the same thing happens: the Marx-sympathetic students insist the productive forces really do drive history -- technology changes, relations of production strain, revolution, repeat -- and the skeptics wheel out the usual counterexamples (the Russian revolution happened in a backward country, the superstructure seems to do plenty of causal work of its own, the whole thing reads more like a Hegelian just-so story than a science). Cohen's defense gets us to a cleaner version of the view, sure, but then we just relocate the fight: now it's whether functional explanations without a stated mechanism are legitimate, whether "fettering" is a precise enough notion to ever be wrong about, whether the primacy thesis is a falsifiable claim or a methodological preference. Twenty years of my own reading later and I honestly cannot tell whether anyone, on either side, has ever been moved by an argument they hadn't already half-accepted.

So my question isn't which side is right. It's: what kind of disagreement is this, structurally? My working hypothesis -- offered as a hypothesis, please push on it -- is that the two camps are answering different questions under one label: one side is defending a research program (look first at material conditions and you'll usually find the explanation), the other is evaluating a law-like causal thesis, and a research program can survive almost any counterexample in a way a thesis can't, so the sides keep talking past each other because nothing either side offers could possibly count as evidence for the other. But maybe I'm wrong and there's a hidden empirical question doing the work -- something about, I don't know, how often technological change precedes institutional change rather than following it -- and the debate is stalled only because nobody can agree on what would even count as measuring that. Or maybe it's a third thing: a disagreement about what an explanation in history is allowed to look like, dressed up as a disagreement about history itself.

Diagnose it for me, using what the positions actually say rather than a survey of who wrote what. And then the harder part: given your diagnosis, tell me what progress would even look like. Is there a distinction that, if both sides accepted it, would dissolve or sharpen the debate? A body of historical evidence that would actually settle something? Or should I just tell my students this is one of those disputes where the impasse is the finding, and the honest move is to downgrade the question rather than answer it?
