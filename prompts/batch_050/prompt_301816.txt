I'm a PhD student working on Hume's practical ethics, and I'm stuck on something in his essay Of Suicide (the posthumous 1783 one). In the section on our duty to ourselves, Hume argues — I'm paraphrasing, my edition is at the office — that sickness, age, or misfortune can make life a burden worse than annihilation, and he adds the remark that nobody ever throws away a life while it's still worth keeping. Rereading it, I think his whole argument quietly leans on a phenomenon I can't find a name for anywhere, and it's actually what my dissertation chapter is about.

The phenomenon: in pallative care contexts, patients often report that simply having a reliable means of exit — a stockpile of medication, eligibility for assisted dying — makes their current suffering bearable, even when they never use it. They endure because they could stop. And there's a mirror image: when the option is time-limited or politically threatened, people report acting earlier than they otherwise would have, because the window is closing. So the mere availability of exit transforms the suffering that continues, without the exit ever being taken. This isn't quite option value in the finance/decision-theory sense, since nobody plans to exercise the option and the benefit isn't flexibility under uncertainty — it's that the suffering gets redescribed as chosen. It's also not Hirschman's exit thing, since nothing is exited.

So: does this have an established name, in the assisted dying literature or elsewhere? Hume seems to presuppose it — his reassurance that no one abandons a life worth keeping only works if keeping it is up to you — so maybe it predates the Oregon-style debates by a lot. If there's no existing term, coin one, with a precise definition and some boundary cases (does it apply to an emergency fund or a return ticket home, or only to exit from existence?). Please also flag how confident you are in each part of the answer and single out the claim you're least sure about.
