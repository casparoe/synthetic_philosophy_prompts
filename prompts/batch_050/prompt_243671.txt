I TA'd an aesthetics course last semester and the paradox of tragedy unit frustrated me more than any other. For anyone rusty on it: tragedy and sad art reliably produce grief, pity, fear etc., which feel bad, and yet people seek these works out and say they love them. The standard menu of answers is well known. One camp says the painful emotions are genuinely there but outweighed by some payoff (the pleasure of the craft, the insight, Aristotle-style catharsis). Another camp, following the Hume line, says the negative affect gets converted into pleasure by the artistry, so there isn't really suffering to explain. A third camp says we don't actually feel the negative emotions toward fictions at all, only quasi-emotions, so the paradox dissolves. And a fourth says the negative response is itself intrinsically rewarding in the right frame, so there's nothing to convert or outweigh.

My question: why hasn't this gone anywhere in decades? Everyone seems to accept the same data points (people cry at films and go back for more, nobody wants actual grief in their life, spoilers don't always ruin it, etc.), and yet the four camps keep talking past each other. Is this a real empirical disagreement that's waiting on psychology (like, can we just test whether viewers are in genuine distress?), or is it a verbal dispute where each camp uses 'pleasure' and 'enjoyment' differently, or is it four answers to four different questions (what motivates us to attend vs. what we feel during vs. why we value it after) all wearing the same hat? Give me a diagnosis with reasons, engage with what each camp actually claims rather than a survey of who's who, and say what would count as progress given your diagnosis -- a distinction, an experiment, a reframe -- or argue that no progress is coming and why. Also, please flag which parts of what you've said are basically settled among people who work on this and which parts are your own contested read, and don't dress up a minority view as the consensus.
