I'm a journalist who has covered online misinformation and political polarization for about a decade, and I'm applying for a nine-month fellowship that funds reporters pursuing a single ambitious long-form project. My pitch is a reported essay series on why so many well-intentioned efforts to de-polarize audiences -- bridge-building events, cross-partisan reading apps, "both sides" formats, fact-checking units -- seem to accomplish so little, and sometimes backfire. My working hypothesis draws on a distinction I keep returning to from recent philosophy of social epistemology, associated with C. Thi Nguyen: an epistemic bubble is a failure of exposure, where contrary voices simply never reach you, while an echo chamber is a failure of trust, where contrary voices reach you but arrive pre-discredited, so that hearing them can actually tighten your convictions. The standard prescriptive conclusion is that these pathologies need different remedies -- bubbles need more contact with the other side, chambers need something closer to a restoration of trust -- and that misdiagnosing one as the other explains why exposure-based interventions fail.

Here's my half-formed idea, which I want the proposal to sharpen rather than just adopt. The bubble/chamber distinction, as I've seen it deployed, assumes that exposure and trust vary independently: either you don't hear the other side, or you hear them and distrust them. But my reporting suggests a third configuration that the framework may not have a clean place for. On algorithmically curated platforms, people are constantly exposed to the other side -- but to its worst representatives, selected for outrage value, and encountered inside a running commentary from their own side that supplies the discrediting interpretation in real time. This looks like exposure doing the work of a chamber: the chamber is maintained not despite cross-cutting contact but by means of it. If that's a real phenomenon rather than a verbal stretch of the existing categories, it would mean the prescriptive literature's core advice (diagnose bubble versus chamber, then apply the matching remedy) is operating on a taxonomy that's missing the dominant case, at least for platform-native audiences.

Write the two-page fellowship proposal. It should state the central research question precisely enough that the series could in principle answer it; explain why the existing bubble/chamber framework, and the intervention literature built on it, leaves this question open rather than already settled; lay out the method -- which I imagine combines conceptual work on whether the taxonomy needs a third category or a revision of the trust axis, with reporting on how specific audiences actually encounter opposing views online -- broken down into a plausible month-by-month plan over nine months; describe what the deliverable (a three-part essay series plus a short conceptual memo for practitioners designing interventions) would accomplish and for whom; and say candidly what would count as the project failing, including the possibility that my "third configuration" collapses into the ordinary chamber on inspection. Please don't include citations or a literature review; I'll add those myself, and I'd rather the proposal argue from positions and mechanisms than from names.
