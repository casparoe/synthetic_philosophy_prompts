I'm a journalist who covers the science of belief and social cohesion, and I'm writing a long feature on a question that keeps surfacing in my reporting: does religion actually make people more moral, and can highly secular societies sustain the trust that complex institutions depend on? Rather than interviewing two opposing scholars and stitching their quotes together, I want to try something I've seen done in physics: an adversarial collaboration, where the two camps co-author a single document. Draft that document for me.

The two parties are as follows. Camp A holds the "religion as social technology" view: religious belief and practice, through mechanisms like belief in watchful moralizing gods, costly communal rituals that screen out free-riders, and dense congregational networks, historically built and still sustains the cooperation and generalized trust that modern societies run on; on this view, secularization is living off inherited moral capital. Camp B holds the "secular sufficiency" view: prosociality and trust are products of effective institutions, rule of law, and existential security rather than religion as such; the high-trust, low-corruption Nordic countries show that religion is at most one historical scaffolding among several, and its individual-level association with generosity is small, context-dependent, and partly an artifact of how religiosity and charity are measured (giving to one's own congregation counts as charity, religious respondents self-report more generously than behavioral measures show).

Draft their joint statement. The test of quality is that both camps could sign it without feeling misrepresented: agreed claims must be substantive rather than diplomatic filler, and each side's position must be stated in words that side would itself choose. Structure it in the following sections, in this order:

1. Preamble (about 150 words): what question the collaboration addresses, and what each side concedes it does not yet know.
2. Signed claims (about 400 words): a numbered list of propositions both camps endorse. These should include claims about the empirical record (what the cross-national correlations do and don't show, the distinction between individual-level religiosity and society-level secularization), about the mechanisms both accept as real even if they weight them differently, and about the conceptual difficulty of defining "more moral" when the two camps' measures partly diverge.
3. Statement of disagreements (about 400 words): a table with three columns — the residual dispute, Camp A's formulation of its own view, Camp B's formulation of its own view. Cover at least: whether secular trust is parasitic on a religious past or self-sustaining; whether the Nordic cases generalize; whether religious prosociality extends beyond the in-group or mostly stops at it.
4. Cruxes and advance predictions (about 400 words): the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp, and for each, a prediction recorded in advance from each side — for instance, what each predicts about the trajectory of trust in societies now in the middle stages of secularization, or about how trust behaves when strong institutions in a secular society are temporarily removed.
5. Verbal residue (about 150 words): identify which part of the public dispute you judge to be terminological rather than substantive — I suspect something about what counts as religion and what counts as morality belongs here — and say why.

If at any point you cannot draft a formulation both camps would accept, flag the sticking point explicitly rather than smoothing it over.
