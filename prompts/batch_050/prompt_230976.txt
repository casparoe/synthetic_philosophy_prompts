I'm TAing a philosophy of biology seminar this fall and the prof wants me to build a little grading tool. Students have to hand in a short paragraph 'explaining' some biological phenomenon (things like the action potential, E. coli chemotaxis, protein synthesis, predator-prey population cycles, that kind of stuff) and I need to sort each answer into what kind of thing it actually is, in roughly the Machamer-Darden-Craver sense of mechanism.

What I want from you: a decision tree where every internal node is a question I can answer just by reading the student's paragraph, without needing to know any theory labels. So questions like: does the answer identify entities actually doing things (activities, not just properties or correlations)? Does it show how the product of one stage sets up the next, or does it just list steps? Does it say anything about setup conditions and termination conditions? Are there black boxes or filler phrases ('and then the signal is processed') hiding gaps? Does it explain the phenomenon by appeal to mathematical structure or regularities rather than componential organization?

The leaves should be: a complete how-actually mechanistic explanation, a mechanism schema (abstract but no real gaps), a mechanism sketch (has black boxes / filler terms), a how-possibly model, and not mechanistic at all (purely descriptive or mathematical). Give each leaf one sentence on the diagnostic feature and a one-sentence example of what a student paragraph landing there would look like.

Two extra things. First, build the tree from the questions outward, not from the labels inward, and if some combination of answers has no natural home (say: full entities-and-activities but nothing about setup or termination conditions), say whether that's a schema, a sketch, or just incoherent. Second, flag anywhere the tree fails to separate things I might care about -- e.g. I suspect whether the 'adequacy' of a complete explanation is understood ontically or epistemically won't show up in text-based questions, and I'd like that marked rather than papered over. Also note where population-level cases like Lotka-Volterra style answers should land and whether pointing to a mechanism at a different level rescues them.

Keep it usable for a grader with 40 papers to get through.
