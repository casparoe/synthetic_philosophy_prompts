I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character, Marta, staked everything on a single act of self-binding: she told her entire firm, at an all-hands meeting, that if she was not made partner by year's end she would resign. The managing director responded the following week by announcing a formal, board-ratified rule that partnership decisions would never be made under pressure, with no exceptions. I took the bones of this from a real dispute I watched unfold between two friends at a dinner I hosted, and I need a referee before I can write Marta's chapters honestly, because I cannot tell whether my character is shrewd or merely reckless.

Daniel's position was that Marta played her hand correctly. His argument: a bare threat to resign is cheap talk, because the firm knows she would rather stay than leave; by committing publicly and irrevocably, she destroyed her own option of quietly staying, and thereby made the threat credible. Once staying is off the table for her, the firm's calculation changes -- it must now weigh promoting her against actually losing her, whereas before it could call her bluff. He cited the logic of burning one's boats: the general who removes the option of retreat makes his army's resolve undeniable, and Marta did the same to herself.

Sofia's counter was that this reasoning ignores what happened next. The managing director did not weigh anything; he counter-committed. A board-ratified no-exceptions rule is itself a boat-burning: it destroys the firm's option of caving without humiliating itself. Now both sides are locked and the collision is guaranteed. Her claim was that commitment strategies only work when the other side retains the freedom to yield -- the moment both parties bind themselves, you get not leverage but a wreck, and the one who committed first is simply the one who crashed first.

Daniel conceded that dual commitment is disastrous but said this changes the lesson rather than refuting him: the rational play is to commit first, fully, and fast enough that the other side has no time to counter-commit. Marta's error was tactical -- she announced her deadline in September, giving the firm three months to build its barricade. Had she resigned-effective-immediately conditional on an answer within the week, the firm would have had to decide before it could entrench.

Sofia replied that this actually proved her deeper point: the rationality of a commitment move depends entirely on facts about the other party's option set that you cannot reliably know in advance -- whether they can commit back, how fast, and at what cost. A strategy whose success turns on information you lack is not a rational strategy but a gamble wearing strategy's clothes. Daniel said by that standard all negotiation is gambling, since you never know the other side's reservation price either. Sofia said there is a difference between uncertainty about what the other side prefers and deliberately closing off your own exits before finding out.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: who is right at each step, what each is missing, and who is more right overall. I want a definite verdict with reasons, not a menu of positions for me to choose among -- my Marta must turn out to be one thing or the other, and I would rather be argued out of my own indecision than left with it.
