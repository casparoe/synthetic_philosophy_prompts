I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and last month I attended a small workshop on the foundations of probability where I watched two participants — Ada, a decision theorist, and Ben, a physicist with philosophical training — argue for nearly an hour about the Principal Principle: the doctrine that, absent special circumstances, your credence in an event should equal the objective chance you take that event to have. I took notes and would like the dispute adjudicated properly, since both struck me as smart and neither conceded anything. Here is the exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it. (1) Ada opened by defending the principle as close to non-negotiable: if you know a die is fair and set your credence in a six at anything other than one-sixth, you are straightforwardly out of step with the world, vulnerable to sure losses in betting scenarios, and you have no business calling your degrees of belief rational. (2) Ben replied with a counterexample: suppose a demonstrably reliable time traveler tells you the die will land six. The objective chance of six is still one-sixth — the die is still fair — but your credence should plainly be near one. So the principle as stated is false, full stop; chance-deference must sometimes be overridden. (3) Ada answered that this doesn't touch the principle, because it was always implicitly restricted to admissible evidence: information that bears on the outcome only by way of the chances. The time traveler's report is paradigm inadmissible information — it bypasses the chances and speaks directly to the outcome — and besides, insofar as the future is genuinely settled, the chance of the settled outcome is arguably one, not one-sixth, so the case dissolves either way. (4) Ben then pressed two harder points. First, 'admissible' looks circular: evidence is called admissible exactly when deferring to chance given it works, and no one has given an independent, non-question-begging characterization. Second, he described a puzzle about self-undermining chances: certain physical processes (his example was radioactive decay modeled over time) imply, by their own lights, that the future chance distribution will differ from the present one — so the current chances themselves tell you that deferring to current chances can lead you astray. He said the standard repair is to defer not to the chances simpliciter but to what the chances say conditional on the entire chance theory being true, and that once you make this repair, the principle Ada is defending is no longer the simple thing she started with. (5) Ada closed by granting the technicality but insisting the repaired principle vindicates her: the core idea survives, the repairs are footnotes, and no working scientist should worry about oracles or undermining when setting credences from physical probabilities.
