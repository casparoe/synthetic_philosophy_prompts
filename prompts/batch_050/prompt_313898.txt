For several years I have distributed a handout in my graduate seminar on population ethics titled "Responses to the Non-Identity Problem." The handout is meant to be exhaustive with respect to the standard moves in the literature, and I now want to stress-test it. The problem, as the handout frames it, arises whenever a choice determines which people will exist: since the people who exist in the worse outcome would not have existed at all had the better choice been made, they cannot complain that they were made worse off than they otherwise would have been, and yet the choice strikes nearly everyone as wrong. The handout lists five responses:

1. Reject the person-affecting intuition outright. An act need not be worse for anyone in order to be wrong; outcomes can be better or worse impersonally, and morality directs us to bring about the better one. In choices where the same number of people would exist either way, this becomes the principle that it is worse if those who live have lives of lower quality, regardless of who they are.

2. Distinguish harming from wronging. Concede that, for instance, the child conceived by the fourteen-year-old is not made worse off than she otherwise would have been, since the alternative for her is non-existence; but insist that she may nonetheless be wronged, because wronging someone does not require making her worse off in the comparative sense.

3. Reject the comparative analysis of harm. On a non-comparative account, being brought into an existence marked by sufficiently serious suffering or deprivation is itself a harm, even when the only alternative is never existing; so the premise that harm requires being worse off than one otherwise would have been is what should go.

4. Recast the problem in contractualist terms. Wrongness consists in being disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject, and the reasons bearing on such principles attach to generic standpoints rather than to the fate of identifiable individuals; whether the same individuals exist in the compared outcomes therefore drops out of the analysis.

5. Bite the bullet selectively. Distinguish choices that fix the number of future people from choices that also fix their number and identities on a larger scale; accept some version of (1) or (2) for the former while conceding that in the latter, where the choice determines how many people ever live, impersonal comparison fails and the theory must simply be restricted.

As far as I can tell, this list covers every response that appears regularly in the literature, which is exactly my worry: the field may have settled on a closed menu too early. I want a sixth response for the handout. It must be genuinely different from all five — not a rewording, special case, or conjunction of items already listed — and it must engage the problem's actual structure, namely that the choice changes who exists, rather than dissolving into a general affirmation that future generations matter. If the literature contains a response I have forgotten, give me that; otherwise construct one yourself. In either case, state the response, identify which premise of the problem it rejects and how, explain how it relates to the five listed responses, and then give me your candid assessment of its merits: what its strongest version looks like, where it is most vulnerable, and whether it deserves a place on the handout.
