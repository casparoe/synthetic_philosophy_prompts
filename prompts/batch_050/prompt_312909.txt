i run a small philosophy reading group, mostly philosophy postgrads plus one physicist, and in two weeks it's my turn and i picked the hole argument. they've all read a bit about the philosophy of space and time (substantivalism vs relationalism, the bucket, that stuff) but none of them actually know general relativity beyond pop-science level, and i don't want to teach them GR. plan the session for me -- not the explanation itself, the plan for how i should build it up in about 40 minutes with a whiteboard.

what i want to know:

- the order the pieces have to come in. my instinct is spacetime as a manifold with fields on it -> what an active diffeomorphism is (and how it's not just a coordinate change) -> the actual construction with the hole -> why this is supposed to be a problem rather than just a math fact. is that right, or does something else need to be in place first? in particular, do i need them to understand anything about the field equations and determinism before the construction lands, or can that come after?

- the one example to carry the whole thing. i was thinking of building it on a plain flat plane with a temperature field on it, so nobody has to think about curvature or Einstein's equations at all -- does that actually work, and what does the example need to have and need to NOT have so that the lesson transfers to the real spacetime case rather than misleading them?

- the misconceptions, roughly in the order i should expect them. my guesses: that this is just "coordinates don't matter" dressed up, that the hole is a literal hole in spacetime, that the conclusion is "determinism is false so physics is broken," and that the obvious fix is to just say spacetime points aren't real. tell me which of these i'm right about, which i'm wrong about, and where in the sequence each one should be headed off rather than answered after it's already taken root.

- how to handle the responses without the session becoming a literature survey. i want them to see the difference between "these two models are the same physical situation" (Leibniz equivalence) and "points only get their identity from the metric" and whatever the physicist in the room is going to say, but framed as moves you can actually evaluate, not names on a slide.

- one question to ask at the end that would show me whether they've understood the argument -- e.g. i'm thinking of asking whether the same construction works against a scalar field living on a fixed, non-dynamical background spacetime, and why or why not. is that a good discriminator or does it test the wrong thing?

and tell me what to explicitly leave on the table at this level (i assume the whole cauchy problem machinery, and probably quantum gravity) and say that i'm leaving it, rather than letting them think what they got was the whole story.
