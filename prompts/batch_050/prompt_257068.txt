I coordinate a monthly philosophy-of-probability seminar for adults with mixed mathematical backgrounds. The topic has recently become practical for me: during a planning meeting, my department was told that if enrollment falls, the small seminar is very likely to be preserved. Some of us treated this as evidence about whether enrollment would fall, while others insisted that it said only what would happen under that condition. The disagreement made me appreciate how slippery the Judy Benjamin problem becomes once the conditional information is delivered by an institution with its own reasons for speaking.

Design an original 75-minute classroom activity for 12–16 participants that makes this difficulty experiential before it is named. Use a self-contained version of the Judy Benjamin setup: four initially equiprobable regions formed by the Red/Blue distinction and the headquarters/elsewhere distinction, followed by a reliable announcement that the probability of headquarters given Red is 3/4. Participants have not learned that they are in Red. They should have to make pre-announcement and post-announcement decisions with visible stakes, such as allocating limited tokens, pricing simple wagers, or choosing rescue actions, rather than merely stating numerical probabilities. Ideally, the same conditional wording should be used under two different announcement protocols—for example, one in which the messenger is required to report that conditional fact and one in which the messenger could have reported a different fact—so that participants encounter for themselves why the source’s selection policy may matter.

Provide a complete facilitator’s plan: preparation, materials, group roles, the precise script, phase timings, payoff rules requiring no money, a method for recording commitments before discussion begins, and a debrief. Participants should not hear the name Judy Benjamin, receive a lecture on conditioning, or be shown the standard analysis until after their decisions have created a genuine disagreement. The debrief should connect moments in the activity to at least three possible reconstructions of the post-announcement state: preserving the probability of Red, revising it because the announcement is treated as evidence, and concluding that no unique revision follows without further assumptions about why that conditional was announced. It should also distinguish learning a conditional probability from learning that the corresponding condition actually obtains.

Please be concrete about implementation. Identify two predictable ways the activity will fail the first time—such as participants treating the conditional as information favoring Red, gaming the payoff scheme, or focusing only on their individual quadrant—and explain how the facilitator should respond without prematurely giving away the analysis. State clearly which aspect of Judy Benjamin the game cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied during the debrief.
