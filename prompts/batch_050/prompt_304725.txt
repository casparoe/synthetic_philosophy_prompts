Read Dickens's A Christmas Carol as a thought experiment about L. A. Paul's problem of transformative experience, and assess where the novella succeeds and fails as one. Paul's worry is that certain choices -- becoming a parent, undergoing religious conversion -- cannot be made rationally in the standard way, because the person who emerges from the experience values things differently from the person who must decide, and the deciding self cannot know in advance what the transformed life will feel like from the inside. Scrooge looks at first like a counterexample to the epistemic half of this problem: the Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come quite literally shows him the phenomenal content of his unredeemed future, and the earlier spirits give him something like lived access to past and present joys he has foreclosed. So press the reading in both directions. On one side: does Scrooge's night solve the epistemic problem, or only dramatize why it is unsolvable -- is what the spirits give him knowledge of what transformation would be like, or is it itself the transformation, so that no decision ever occurs? If the visions do the work by changing him rather than by informing a chooser who then chooses, does the story covertly concede Paul's point while appearing to refute it? On the other side: where does the decision-theoretic frame distort the book? Scrooge's defect is arguably not that he lacks evidence about outcomes but that he cannot imaginatively occupy perspectives (his younger self's, the Cratchits', his own grave-side) -- which may be a failure of a different kind than missing information, and the story's machinery of memory and vicarious witnessing may model a route to transformation that Paul's framework, built on first-personal experience of the outcome itself, does not obviously capture. Be honest about where the reading strains: the novella's moral psychology is sentimental, its timeline incoherent if pressed, and Marley's chain suggests a theory of character formation (habits forged link by link) in some tension with the overnight conversion the plot requires. Conclude by saying what, if anything, the story contributes to the philosophical debate that Paul's own examples do not -- for instance, whether it identifies imagination and memory, rather than future experience, as the real bottleneck in transformative choice. I am writing a criticism seminar paper and want a reading that is faithful to both the text and the argument, not one that flattens either.
