I teach a master's module on science and policy, roughly 18 students, and I've got a single 2-hour slot on climate-model ensembles. I want a game or simulation I can run in class that makes them *feel* the difference between model agreement and actual robustness — specifically the trap where an ensemble looks reassuring because six models point the same way, but half of them share the same underlying assumptions, so the "agreement" is partly borrowed. I don't want to lecture them on model interdependence first; they should get stung by it themselves and only hear the vocabulary afterwards.

Concretely what I'm after: students play some kind of decision body (a coastal council, an infrastructure fund, whatever works) that has to commit real resources under uncertainty — e.g. how high to build flood defences given a handful of "model" projections, with a budget that makes overbuilding costly and underbuilding catastrophic. The setup should secretly include models whose projections are correlated because they share a component, so a naive count of agreement overstates confidence, and there should be a reveal partway through where that becomes visible and the group has to decide whether to revise the decision they already made. I'd like a scoring/payoff scheme that doesn't use real money, the full rules, a timeline for the two hours, and enough structure that students have to argue about *which* uncertainty is actually decision-relevant (does the spread in storm-surge projections matter if the defence height is the same either way?).

Then a debrief plan that maps what happened onto the real concepts: ensemble spread vs. structural uncertainty, why weighting models as if they're independent misleads, and the point that "wait for the models to agree more" is itself a decision with costs. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (students gaming the payoffs, anchoring on the first projections, etc.) and how to handle it. And be honest about which feature of real climate-ensemble practice the game can't capture — presumably genuine deep uncertainty, where you can't even list the shared assumptions you're missing.

British English please, and keep the whole thing under 850 words — I need to actually be able to prep from it.
