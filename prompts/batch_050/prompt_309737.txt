Right, bit of background first. I'm a retired chemist, spent forty years in a lab, and about two years ago I fell down the philosophy rabbit hole and haven't climbed out. I now help run a little discussion group at our local library -- eight to ten people, mixed ages, mostly no philosophy background, we meet for ninety minutes on Thursday evenings. Lovely lot, but they glaze over if I just talk at them, so I try to make them *do* things.

Next month I want to tackle an idea I find completely delicious: the strategy-stealing argument. As I understand it, for certain games (Hex is the famous one) you can prove the first player must have a winning strategy without having the faintest idea what that strategy actually is. The trick is roughly: suppose the second player had a winning strategy. Then the first player just makes any old move, and thereafter pretends to be the second player, following "the second player's" strategy -- and having that extra piece sitting on the board can't possibly hurt. Contradiction, so the second player can't have a winning strategy, so the first player must. The thing that bakes my noodle is that it's a proof that a strategy *exists* which tells you absolutely nothing about *what it is*. In chemistry if I tell you a reaction pathway exists I can at least point at the molecules.

So: design me an activity that makes the group feel the force of this themselves, before I ever say the words "strategy-stealing". Some things I have in mind, but push back on any of it if you think I'm wrong:

- I'd like them to first actually play some games in pairs, so they get a feel for what it's like when having an extra piece on the board is never a disadvantage -- and ideally also stumble on a game where an extra piece CAN hurt you, so they realise the argument has real teeth and doesn't just apply everywhere. (I dimly remember chess positions where you'd rather not move at all, zugzwang or something?)
- Then I'd love some way for them to construct the stealing idea themselves -- maybe one pair role-plays a player who mimics another's strategy and something goes wrong, or they try to imagine the second player winning and keep hitting a wall. I don't want to hand them the argument on a plate.
- The debrief should connect what happened in the games to the shape of the proof, and be honest about where the argument works and where it breaks. I'd also like them to sit with the weirdness for a minute: we've proven something exists and couldn't find it if our lives depended on it. Is that a problem for mathematics, or just a funny fact?

Practical constraints: ninety minutes total, about ten people, tables and paper available, no phones needed (some of them wouldn't thank me), and nobody has played Hex before so keep whatever games you use very quick to learn. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run this -- with this crowd, someone will definitely try to apply the argument to noughts and crosses and announce that noughts and crosses is a first-player win, which I'm fairly sure is wrong since it's a draw. I want a plan for handling that sort of thing.

Oh, and please don't just give me a lecture script with a game tacked on. The whole point is they trip over the idea before it has a name.
