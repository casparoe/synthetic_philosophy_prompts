I'm a high school student taking a civics elective, and we've been doing a unit on political ideologies. Something that's been bothering me (and that I've argued about with friends at lunch) is how strangely well-packaged political opinions seem to be: knowing where someone stands on gun control tells you a suspicious amount about their views on abortion, environmental regulation, and immigration, even though it's not obvious these issues have anything in common logically. So the question we keep circling is whether the left-right distinction actually tracks something real about human beings and their values, or whether it's just a historical accident that happened to get frozen into our institutions.

Please write me a taxonomy of the competing explanations for what, if anything, the left-right divide fundamentally is, organized as a tree where each branch is labeled by the question or claim that separates it from its siblings. For instance, one early fork might be whether the axis is supposed to describe a stable feature of individual psychology at all, or only a pattern in how political coalitions happen to form; further forks might then separate accounts of what the psychological feature would be (attitudes toward change versus preservation? toward equality versus hierarchy? differences in temperament that precede any political content?). The tree must include as leaves, at minimum: the view that left and right reduce to attitudes toward change; the view that they reduce to attitudes toward equality or hierarchy; the view that they express underlying temperaments or dispositions that political content merely drapes itself over; the view that the axis is a contingent coalition of convenience with no deeper unity; and the deflationary view that the whole distinction is a distortion we should discard, plus any multidimensional rival that keeps some structure but splits the single axis into two or more.

A few specific requests. First, phrase each branch question so that a classmate could answer it without already knowing which theory it leads to. Second, if two positions on my required list can't actually be separated by your tree's questions, flag that openly instead of forcing a split. Third, note any combination of answers your tree allows that nobody seems to occupy, with a sentence diagnosing whether that spot is empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Finally, end each leaf with one sentence on the most awkward fact that explanation has to swallow, since that's what I want to raise in class discussion. I'd like roughly six to nine leaves total, and I'd rather the tree be built from the logical space of possible answers than from a list of famous thinkers' names.
