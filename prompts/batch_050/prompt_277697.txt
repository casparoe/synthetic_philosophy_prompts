Last night a friend and I spent hours arguing about Fitch's paradox of knowability — the derivation purporting to show that if every truth is knowable, then every truth is in fact known — and we parted without conceding anything to each other. She concluded that the paradox refutes the knowability principle outright and that some truths are in principle unknowable; I suspect the principle survives once it is stated more carefully. Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers: an anti-realist who defends the knowability principle by restricting it to truths that can be known without contradiction (thereby excluding propositions like "p, and nobody knows that p," since knowing such a proposition would make it false), and a realist who contends that any such restriction is an ad hoc retreat that quietly abandons the principle's original motivation. The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth: the realist should press on whether the restricted principle still captures what made knowability philosophically attractive in the first place, and the anti-realist should answer by challenging whether the realist's permanently unknown truths do any explanatory work beyond being posited out of reach by definition. Neither side should be a strawman, and each should respond to the other's actual points rather than talk past them. Roughly 600–800 words.
