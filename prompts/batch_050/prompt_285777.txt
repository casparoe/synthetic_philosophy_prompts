Three months ago my wife died. We were together for twenty-six years, and for the last four of them I was her full-time carer while a degenerative illness took her apart piece by piece. She knew she was dying long before she did, and we talked about it often. One of the last things she asked of me was that there be no black clothes and no solemn music: she wanted people to eat well, tell the embarrassing stories, and dance at the end. That is what we did.

Here is my problem. Her family were appalled, and they have not been quiet about it. Her sister told me that I buried her as though I were glad to be rid of her; that anyone who could laugh at his wife's own memorial, only weeks after her death, plainly never loved her as a wife deserves; that grief has a shape, everyone knows what it is, and if mine does not have it, that says something about me and about what our marriage really was. I should say: I do not feel nothing. I miss her every day. But the overwhelming thing I feel is not devastation. Part of it is relief — that she is no longer suffering and, if I am honest, that I am no longer watching her suffer and measuring out my life in medication times. And part of it is something harder to describe: a settled sense that this is simply what happens, that everything that lives does this, and that raging against it would be like raging at the weather.

The relief is the part that worries me. I can defend the celebration to her family — I was honouring her explicit wishes. I can even defend the calm. But I cannot decide whether the relief makes me a bad person, or whether it is exactly what a clear-eyed person ought to feel when a long dying finally ends. Her sister thinks feelings are the measure of love: the right ones, at the right intensity, for the right length of time. I suspect feelings are more like weather, and that what I actually did for her over those four years is the truer measure. But I am not sure, and I notice that my view is also the convenient one.

So: is there a right way to grieve, such that grieving wrongly — or recovering too quickly, or feeling relief — is a genuine moral failing rather than a mere breach of etiquette? I am not looking for bereavement counselling. I want to know whether my sister-in-law's position, harsh as it is, rests on something defensible that I ought to take seriously, or whether mine does, and why.
