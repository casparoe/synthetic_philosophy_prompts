I'm teaching a phil of science seminar and we just did structural realism -- the idea (Worrall's pitch, roughly) that what survives scientific revolutions isn't the ontology but the mathematical structure: Fresnel's equations for light got carried straight over into Maxwell's theory even though the luminiferous ether they were about got tossed out entirely. I want to open the next session with a poem that enacts this rather than just describing it. Write a poem of two stanzas, exactly 8 lines each: the first about Fresnel-era optics, light as waves rippling through the ether, the second about Maxwell's electromagnetic field. The constraint is that both stanzas must have the identical rhyme scheme and meter, and as far as possible the same syntactic scaffolding line by line -- same sentence shapes, verbs and nouns falling in the same slots -- while sharing essentially no content words. A reader flipping between the two should see that only the form carried over, the way the equations did. A specialist should clock the Fresnel/Maxwell reference without you naming either of them. After the poem, add one section titled "Structural notes" (~150 words) explaining how you made the form do the philosophical work.
