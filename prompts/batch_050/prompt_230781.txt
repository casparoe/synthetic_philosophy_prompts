My starting hunch comes from William James in A Pluralistic Universe, paraphrased: a bunch of separate experiences can be close together, coordinated, even mutually responsive, without there being one extra subject who lives all of them at once. I want to turn that into a usable principle for the combination problem, so here are my gut verdicts: two friends finishing each other's sentences do not make a third mind; an ant colony running a fungus farm does not have colony-level experience; an octopus with weirdly distributed neurons still seems like one octopus having a day; a split-brain patient is one person in ordinary life but maybe two subjects during the trick experiments; a brain organoid wired into a little robot that learns to avoid shocks is the case I keep flip-flopping on; a company full of employees, dashboards, and incentives is definitely not phenomenally conscious; and a locked-in patient with no outward behavior still has experience. My tentative principle is no-seam integration: parts form a bigger subject only when each part's activity is available to the others as part of its own perspective, not merely as data about them. Test that against my verdicts, repair it, and tell me which verdict has to go if the repaired principle stays simple -- especially how to keep octopus yes and ant colony no without basicly defining integration as whatever brains have.
