Next month I am presenting a paper at a decision-theory workshop defending the claim that money-pump arguments do not establish a rational requirement of transitivity. The paper's core argument runs as follows. First, a money pump is a diachronic sequence of individually acceptable trades, and whether an agent counts as "exploited" by the sequence depends on how the sequence is framed -- the pump only looks like a strict loss if the agent's end state is compared to her start state across choices she made at different times, and I argue this cross-temporal comparison smuggles in the very requirement (a stable, transitive preference ordering persisting across the sequence) that the argument is supposed to prove. Second, an agent with intransitive preferences who can foresee the pump can rationally decline to enter it, or adopt a standing policy that blocks it, without thereby giving up her intransitive preferences; being steerable into a bad sequence by a clever trader is a practical liability, not an inconsistency. Third, I conclude that money pumps show at most that intransitive agents need foresight, self-binding, or policy-based choice to navigate the world -- a need they share with plenty of agents we regard as perfectly rational -- and not that intransitivity is itself irrational.

The audience: roughly fifteen people. Most are formal epistemologists and decision theorists trained in the tradition where representation theorems are taken to ground the axioms of rational choice -- for them, transitivity is not one norm among others but part of what makes preferences attributable to an agent at all. Two economists from the business school will attend; both think in revealed-preference terms and tend to treat "you can be turned into a money pump" as a perfectly good sense of "irrational" without further argument. There is also a contingent of graduate students, one of whom I know is writing a dissertation on diachronic rationality and is better read on the recent money-pump literature than anyone else in the room, including me. Nobody in the audience shares my conclusion, though two or three are at least open to the idea that the standard arguments are weaker than advertised.

Please prepare my Q&A sheet. Give me the eight or so objections this particular audience will raise -- not the objections a general philosophy audience would raise -- in the order I am likely to get them, and for each one: (a) phrased the way the person raising it would actually phrase it, noting where the economist's version and the formal epistemologist's version of the same worry would differ in vocabulary and emphasis; (b) a reply I can deliver in about two minutes without slides; (c) an explicit note on where that reply is weakest or merely defers the issue; and (d) what I should concede immediately, if anything, so the objection doesn't cost me more credibility than it has to. I especially want to be ready for the objection that my "foresight" defense proves too much and would equally vindicate obviously irrational choice patterns, and for the objection that declining the pump or binding oneself to a policy is just the intransitive agent paying a tax that transitive agents don't pay. Finally, tell me the one question I am hoping nobody asks, why it is dangerous given my third premise, and what my least-bad answer to it is.
