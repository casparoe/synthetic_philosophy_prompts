I've been persuaded by illusionism about phenomenal consciousness: roughly, the view that phenomenal properties as we naively conceive them don't exist, and that introspection systematically misrepresents purely functional, physical states as having them. What would count against this position? Please don't rest on the retort that the illusion is introspectively undeniable, since the view predicts precisely that. Instead, I want a serious map of the position's exposure: which empirical results would trouble it (say, about the accuracy or structure of introspective reports), which philosophical arguments would (for instance, a successful argument that representing a phenomenal property requires instantiating it, or that the view covertly reintroduces what it denies), and which would be merely awkward. I'm also interested in two structural questions. First, the view owes us a positive account of why the illusion arises and why it takes this specific form; at what point does failure to deliver that account count against the view rather than merely against its current defenders? Second, since any apparent experience can in principle be redescribed as an illusory appearance, is there any possible state of affairs that would falsify illusionism, and if not, is that a defect or just what one should expect of a view whose job is to explain appearances away? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely inconvenient, and distinguish what committed illusionists would themselves concede as damaging from what only their critics take to be damaging.
