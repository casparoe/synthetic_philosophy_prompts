I'm putting together materials for a workshop on decision-making under uncertainty, and I've been going back and forth with a friend who works in risk management about whether the ensemble average ever actually misleads a single individual. Her view is that the whole time-average-versus-ensemble-average distinction is just expected utility theory wearing a false moustache, and I want a case sharp enough to test that. Construct a decision problem meeting all of the following specifications: (1) the gamble on offer has positive expected value on every single play, computed in the standard way over possible outcomes; (2) an agent who repeatedly plays with her entire bankroll, reinvesting all proceeds each round, converges to ruin with probability approaching one as the number of rounds grows; (3) nevertheless, a large population of agents each playing the same gamble independently with their own bankrolls ends up, on average, strictly richer -- so that a scheme which pools and shares the proceeds across many players flips the rational verdict from refuse to accept. Constraints: the scenario must be realistic -- a freelancer's income strategy, a small business reinvesting profits, a retirement portfolio, something of that sort -- not a casino or a stylized coin flip; the stakes must be ones an ordinary person could face; no demons, predictors, or exotic physics; and the setup itself should be statable in one paragraph, with the analysis following after.

After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it meets each specification: write out the ensemble-average calculation showing the gamble is favorable in expectation, then the time-average (multiplicative growth-rate) calculation showing individual ruin, and show precisely why the population average and the individual trajectory diverge. Then identify the single structural feature of the case doing the work -- whether it is the multiplicative dynamics, the absorbing state at or near zero, the path-dependence of outcomes, or some combination -- and describe what happens if that feature is removed: does the case collapse into one where ensemble and time averages agree, and if so, show that it does. Be honest about whether the gamble you describe is really one anyone would be offered, and flag any idealization you had to smuggle in to make the numbers come out.

Finally, anticipate how my risk-manager friend will attack the case. Her line, I expect, is that an expected utility maximizer with logarithmic (or otherwise risk-averse) utility already refuses the gamble and accepts the pooled scheme, so the example demonstrates nothing that standard theory doesn't already handle. Give the strongest version of that objection against your own construction, then say what, if anything, the ergodicity framing adds beyond it -- and if the honest answer is that the case only shows that expected *wealth* is the wrong maximand rather than showing anything about time and ensembles per se, say so plainly rather than overselling it.
