Last night I had a long and unresolved argument with a friend about superrationality, and I would like an outside opinion with the reasoning shown. The case we debated is the Platonia dilemma: twenty strangers are each told that the others are intelligent, are working from identical information, and face the same one-shot choice; each may post a single entry, and if exactly one entry arrives its sender wins a fortune, while if none or more than one arrive nobody wins anything. My friend held that rationality simply is the symmetric mixed equilibrium — each player enters with probability one in twenty — and that any talk of the other players mirroring one's reasoning is confusion. I held that a player who expects the others to reach the same conclusion as themselves has grounds to choose as one member of a coordinated group, that this vindicates the one-in-twenty answer for deeper reasons than equilibrium best-response does, and that the two approaches genuinely diverge in related cases such as the one-shot prisoner's dilemma among known-similar agents. Please give me your credence, as a number, that the superrational account is the correct theory of rational choice for symmetric one-shot games among known-similar reasoners, decomposed rather than bare. Present the answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: the first stating your overall credence; the second, third and fourth each isolating a sub-question on which that credence depends, with a probability and brief justification for each (candidate sub-questions include whether one's own deliberation is evidence about the deliberations of similar agents, whether rational choice should respond to such non-causal evidence at all, and whether superrationality actually prescribes anything beyond standard game theory in the Platonia case itself); the fifth naming the sub-question to which your final number is most sensitive and what argument or evidence would shift it most.
