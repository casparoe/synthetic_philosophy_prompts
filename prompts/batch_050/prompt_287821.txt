The question: "State the Identity of Indiscernibles and the Indiscernibility of Identicals. Which of the two is controversial, and why? Then describe Black's two-sphere thought experiment and evaluate whether it succeeds as a counterexample to the Identity of Indiscernibles."

The student's answer:

"The Identity of Indiscernibles, often just called Leibniz's Law, says that if x and y share all the same properties, then x and y are numerically identical -- one thing, not two. This direction is fairly uncontroversial. Its converse, the Indiscernibility of Identicals (if x and y are identical, they share all properties), is where the real metaphysical disputes live, since it depends on contentious questions about what counts as a genuine property.

Black's counterexample: imagine a universe containing nothing but two qualitatively identical spheres, same size, same composition, one mile apart, with nothing else in the universe. Every intrinsic property of one is a property of the other, so by the Identity of Indiscernibles they would have to be one sphere, yet there are clearly two.

One might think the spheres are discernible after all, since sphere A has the property of being one mile from sphere B, while sphere B does not have the property of being one mile from sphere B. So the two-sphere universe doesn't actually contain two indiscernible objects, and Black's example fails as stated. Even setting that aside, though, the thought experiment conclusively refutes the Identity of Indiscernibles, since the scenario it describes is possible and in that scenario the principle gives the wrong answer."
