So I'm teaching my philosophy of mind seminar this spring and we do a couple weeks on consciousness and levels of explanation, and I always assign the careenium stuff from Hofstadter's I Am a Strange Loop because students love it. This year, in a fit of laziness, I grabbed a chapter from some recent popular book on the science of consciousness (won't name it) that has a section on Hofstadter, thinking I could hand it out as a warm-up before they read the real thing. Reading it more carefully last night, something felt off, but it's been maybe six or seven years since I actually read Strange Loop cover to cover and I can't put my finger on what's wrong versus what's just compressed for a general audience. Here's the gist of what the chapter says:

It describes the careenium pretty accurately as far as the setup goes -- a big vat full of thousands of tiny marbles (the "simms") careening around and bouncing off each other, and then much larger balls (the "simmballs") that get knocked about by the collective buffeting of swarms of the little marbles, colliding with each other, merging into bigger compound simmballs and so on, where the marbles are supposed to be like low-level neural events and the simmballs like symbols or thoughts. Fine. But then it says Hofstadter's central claim is that the simmballs reach down and shove the marbles around -- that the symbolic level literally exerts causal force on the substrate, which is what he thinks happens when your thoughts cause your neurons to fire. And it presents this as a straightfaced scientific proposal about how brains work, then lands the punchline: Hofstadter is thus committed to the thesis that mind pushes matter around, which is just interactionist dualism with a fresh coat of paint, and that most philosophers have accordingly moved on.

My inclination is that this makes Hofstadter sound much spookier than he is -- my recollection is he's at great pains to deny any extra physical forces, and the whole point of the parable is something subtler about which level of description you should explain behavior at. But maybe I'm the one misremembering and he really does plant his flag on downward causation? What I want is an audit: which parts of this summary would Hofstadter recognize as his view, which parts twist it (and how -- is it an overstatement, a conflation, a strawman?), and in particular does the dualism punchline survive once you state his actual position on whether symbols push the substrate around? Also, if the chapter is wrong, is there a nearby criticism of Hofstadter that's actually fair -- like, what SHOULD the popular book have said is the weak point?
