I've been a reader of rationalist blogs and forums for over a decade, so I've followed the AI alignment discourse closely, including recurring arguments about the orthogonality thesis and about whether any strategy for aligning a superintelligence can rest on pure instruction-following. Recently, in a completely unrelated reading project, I started working through a history of early Islamic theology (kalam), and I was struck by what looks like an uncanny structural parallel, and I want to test how deep it goes.

For context, as I understand the classical debate: the Mu'tazila held that moral properties attach to actions intrinsically and can be apprehended by unaided human reason prior to any revelation, such that God commands what is just because it is just, and indeed cannot command injustice, since justice belongs to His wise nature. The Ash'arites, reacting against this, denied that acts possess moral value independently of God's command: reason can discern benefit and harm, they said, but obligation and wrongness exist only through revealed legislation, and God wrongs no one because nothing is owed to creatures within His absolute dominion. So on one side, morality is a domain of truths accessible to rational intellects as such; on the other, moral status is conferred by the commands of a particular authority.

Here is what I'd like you to do. Imagine a sophisticated Mu'tazilite theologian and a sophisticated Ash'arite theologian, each fully briefed on modern machine learning, and have them weigh in on three questions. First, the orthogonality thesis: would the Mu'tazilite be committed to denying it, at least for moral truths, insofar as sufficient rational capacity should suffice to apprehend the good, while the Ash'arite could accept it comfortably? Second, alignment strategy: which of the two would favor building an AI whose morality consists in scrupulous obedience to operator instructions, and which would insist instead on an AI that reasons about good and evil directly, and what objections would each raise against the other's preferred design? Third, authority: suppose we succeeded on either approach -- would the resulting system's pronouncements carry any genuine moral authority for humans, on each view? Finally, step back and assess the mapping itself: where, if anywhere, does the analogy between this medieval dispute and contemporary alignment debates break down, and does either modern camp face a problem the medieval theologians already saw?
