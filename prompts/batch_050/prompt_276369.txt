Write an owner's manual and FAQ for a fictional consumer product called the SelfTorture 1000, a bedside device marketed to ambitious savers. The product is a transparent homage to the puzzle in Warren Quinn's 1990 Philosophical Studies paper "The Puzzle of the Self-Torturer," and the manual should take its central specification directly from Quinn's setup: the machine has settings numbered from 0 to 1000, the wearer may advance the dial by at most one notch per day, each advance is permanently wired to a payment deposited in the wearer's account, the increase in pain between any two adjacent settings is so slight that no one can tell them apart by feel, and the only way back to comfort is a factory reset that returns the dial to zero and claws back every payment ever earned. Quinn's paper presents this arrangement as a genuine puzzle about rational choice rather than a mere gotcha, and the humor of the manual should come from the manufacturer having calmly read that paper and decided it was a business plan.

Roughly 700 words, formatted with the usual apparatus of a real manual: a congratulatory opening, safety warnings, a warranty section, customer testimonials, and a troubleshooting FAQ. Keep the prose completely deadpan in the voice of cheerful corporate liability management -- no winking, no explicit mention of philosophy journals. But the content should do the philosophical work. The jokes must land the actual structure of Quinn's puzzle: that each single step up the dial is, taken on its own, plainly worth taking; that a rational agent who takes every such step ends up in agony she would never have chosen directly; and that the only apparent escapes are uncomfortable. At minimum, the FAQ should include a question from a user stuck at a very high setting who insists each individual decision to advance was rational and wants to know where it went wrong, and the manual's answer should manage to be funny while leaving genuinely open whether the answer is "one of those steps was irrational," "rationality can be intransitive here," or "stop evaluating choices one at a time." The warranty section should quietly dramatize the Sorites problem (which imperceptible notch voided your quality of life?). One testimonial should come from a satisfied customer who resolved the puzzle by embracing rational intransitivity and is doing great, thanks for asking, at setting 982.

A reader who knows the literature on this puzzle -- the money-pump worry, the debate over whether rational preferences must be transitive, the idea of evaluating whole sequences rather than pairwise comparisons -- should recognize that the satire understands exactly what it is mocking and find the mockery fair. A reader who doesn't should still find it a funny and slightly chilling piece of product documentation. Do not break the fourth wall to explain the puzzle; trust the manual format to carry it.
