So a friend of mine does stats consulting and I keep a running list of her calls on Simpson's-paradox-flavored cases, because her instincts seem principled but I can't quite state the principle. Her verdicts so far:

1. A hospital study: patients getting Treatment A recovered at a higher rate overall, but A did worse than B within both the severe and mild case strata. She said: trust the within-strata comparison, severity was driving both who got what and how they did.
2. A university's grad admissions: men admitted at a higher rate overall, but nearly every department admitted women at equal or higher rates. She said: trust the department-level numbers, the aggregate is misleading because women applied to more competitive departments.
3. A drug trial: the drug group recovered more overall, but did worse within both the high and low blood-pressure strata, where blood pressure was measured during treatment and is known to respond to the drug. She said: trust the aggregate this time -- stratifying on blood pressure blocks part of the very effect you're trying to measure.
4. A batting-average case where one player leads in each of two seasons but trails on the combined numbers. She said: both answers are correct answers to different questions, and there's no fact of the matter until you say what you're asking.

New case for her: a company compares employees who completed an optional leadership training to those who didn't. Overall, non-completers get promoted at a higher rate; within each tier of manager-rated "potential," completers get promoted more. Predict her verdict -- does the training help, hurt, or can't she say? Which earlier case is the closest analog? And if you think her prior verdicts underdetermine this one, spell out which competing rules fit cases 1-4, what each says here, and what one further fact about the case (say, about how the potential ratings work) would settle it.
