I am a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on classical Indian epistemology, and we have just finished the unit on pramāṇa theory. The topic that has stuck with me is the Mīmāṃsā doctrine of svataḥ prāmāṇya, the intrinsic validity of cognition: roughly, the view that a cognition is valid simply in virtue of arising, and does not need to be certified by any further cognition or test before it may be trusted. Our professor presented this in contrast to the Nyāya view that validity must be established extrinsically, and she extended it to the Mīmāṃsā defense of Vedic testimony, according to which the Veda's authorlessness (apauruṣeyatva) insulates it from the ordinary grounds on which we distrust human testimony, since doubt about a speaker's word ultimately traces to possible faults in the speaker. In our study group we tried to reconstruct the strongest lines of argument available to the Mīmāṃsaka, and I would like an outside assessment of how well each of them actually works.

Here are the candidate defenses we identified, in our own rough formulations. First, the regress argument: if every cognition required validation by another cognition before being trusted, validation would either regress infinitely or terminate in an unvalidated cognition, so some cognitions must be intrinsically valid anyway. Second, the default-entitlement argument: we in fact accept cognitions unless we detect a defect in their source, and this practice of presumptive acceptance is itself the more natural description of how belief and inquiry actually proceed. Third, the immediacy argument: the validity of a cognition is apprehended along with the cognition itself rather than inferred afterward, since we do not ordinarily experience a gap in which the content is entertained neutrally and only later certified. Fourth, the practical-confirmation argument: successful action guided by a cognition is not an extrinsic proof of its validity but merely the absence of discovered defeaters. Fifth, the authorlessness argument, specific to the Veda: because ordinary testimony is doubted only insofar as speakers can be ignorant, deceived, or deceitful, a text with no personal author at all escapes the ordinary epistemology of testimony and must be assessed differently.

Please rank these five defenses into tiers (or give each a score out of ten) according to how dialectically forceful you find them as answers to the Naiyāyika critic, with a few sentences of justification for each placement, and identify which single argument you would lean on if you had to defend the Mīmāṃsā position in debate. Two further requests. Where an argument's strength depends on contestable background assumptions — for instance, on what validation would even consist in, or on whether authorlessness is a coherent or well-evidenced notion — please flag the dependence explicitly rather than letting it pass unnoticed. And please keep clearly separated what is settled about the historical Mīmāṃsā position (what Kumārila and Prabhākara actually held, insofar as this is well established) from what is genuinely contested, whether among classical opponents or modern interpreters; in particular, do not present your own ranking, or any reading of how the intrinsic-validity doctrine relates to the authorlessness claim, as though it were the scholarly consensus.
