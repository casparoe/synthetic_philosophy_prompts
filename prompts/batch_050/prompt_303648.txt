I run a small philosophy reading group, and we have just spent two sessions on Dennett's distinction between cranes and skyhooks in Darwin's Dangerous Idea, along with his charge of greedy reductionism against those who think good explanations must always go straight to the lowest level. Most of us were persuaded that the crane/skyhook contrast is a useful piece of critical machinery. One member, however, who arrived sceptical and left more so, has produced the following argument and maintains that it shows the distinction collapses under its own weight. I have been turning it over for a week and cannot find the exit, though I am fairly sure there is one. The argument runs as follows. (1) A skyhook is an unexplained explainer: a posit invoked to account for the design, complexity or order we observe, which is not itself the product of any prior, gradual, crane-like process. (2) Every explanation invokes something that the explanation itself does not explain. (3) Any chain of explanation either terminates somewhere or regresses without end; there is no third option. (4) If a chain terminates, it terminates in an explainer that nothing further explains — that is, in an unexplained explainer, which by (1) is a skyhook sitting at the bottom of the whole edifice. (5) If instead the chain regresses without end, then nothing is ever fully explained at any stage, since every posit remains suspended upon something further; the entire structure then hangs in the air indefinitely, which is skyhook-suspension in spirit if not in name. (6) So on either horn, every explanatory scheme whatsoever, naturalism included, ultimately rests on skyhooks. (7) Therefore the crane/skyhook contrast does no genuine critical work, and Dennett's polemic against skyhook-positing theories condemns his own naturalism in exactly the same breath.

The conclusion strikes me as proving far too much: it would follow that no theory of any kind could ever be criticised for smuggling in a miracle, since everyone is supposedly guilty of the same thing, and that cannot be right. What I would most like to preserve are premises (2) and (3), which seem close to platitudes, and also the background thought, which I take Dennett himself to accept, that explanation has to bottom out somewhere rather than run in a circle or descend for ever. My friend insists the argument is simply valid and that naturalists must learn to live with (7); I suspect something has slipped either in the opening characterisation of what a skyhook is or in the move from (2) and (3) to the dilemma of (4) and (5), but I cannot name the slip precisely enough to satisfy myself.

So: which premise or step should I reject, why did it look innocuous, and what does rejecting it cost? If the fault lies in (1), please explain what the genuine crane/skyhook distinction actually is — I would like the answer to mark the differences between a posit's being unexplained, its being brute, and its being miraculous — and whether the distinction, properly drawn, can still do the polemical work Dennett wants it to do. If the fault lies instead in (4) or (5), please explain why a brute explanatory terminus does not count as a skyhook and why the regress horn does not have the force it appears to have. Finally, and whichever diagnosis you favour, say something about whether the argument at least succeeds as a caution against a particular way of wielding the skyhook accusation, even if it fails as a refutation of the distinction itself.
