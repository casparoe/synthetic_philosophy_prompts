So I've been reading about this view people call lexical threshold negative utilitarianism -- the idea is roughly that reducing suffering takes priority over increasing pleasure, but not in an absolute way. Mild suffering can be traded off against other goods once things are bad-but-not-catastrophic, while intense suffering is "lexical": no amount of pleasure or happiness elsewhere can outweigh it or justify causing it. The commensurability question underneath is whether pleasure and pain are even measurable on one scale, or whether the badness of agony is just a different kind of thing from the goodness of joy.

Okay, so take that view and run it against industrial egg production as it actually exists. The facts I want you to take as given, because they're common knowledge: billions of laying hens in battery cages or cage-free-but-crammed barns, debeaking, male chicks killed right after hatching, and on the other side, cheap eggs eaten by hundreds of millions of people, most of whom get modest enjoyment and some convenience and nutrition out of it, plus the industry providing jobs and profit.

Here's what I want:

1. Work out what the theory actually says about this case, carefully. Is laying-hen suffering the kind that crosses the lexical threshold, or is it chronic low-grade suffering that falls under the trade-offable kind? Note that the theory's verdict seems to hinge entirely on where that threshold sits and how you classify the suffering, so tell me whether the theory even has the resources to answer that, or whether it just pushes the hard question one step back.

2. The theory usually talks about suffering of individuals, but the case involves aggregates -- each hen's suffering might be moderate while the total is astronomical, and each consumer's pleasure is tiny while the total is large too. Does aggregation sneak commensurability back in through the side door even if you deny it at the level of a single life?

3. Then run it the other direction: what does this case show about the theory? Does it expose that the lexical threshold is doing no real work (since the answer changes with wherever you place it)? Does the case reveal a difference between causing suffering and failing to relieve it that the theory flattens? And is there a reading of the view under which buying the eggs is fine but running the farm isn't, and would that be a feature or a reductio?

Don't just give me the standard utilitarian animal ethics rundown, I've seen that a hundred times. I want the analysis disciplined about what follows from the lexical structure specifically, and honest about where the theory goes silent.
