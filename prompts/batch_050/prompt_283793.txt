I've been asked to circulate a short statement of my views ahead of a workshop on ideals of character, and before I do I'd like a consistency audit rather than encouragement. Here is where I've landed:

1. Impartialism: from the moral point of view, everyone's interests count equally. My own welfare, and that of the people I love, carries no greater weight in the justification of action than the welfare of a stranger.

2. Real supererogation: some actions are morally excellent without being required, and this category is neither empty nor reserved for freakish heroism. It includes deeds within the reach of ordinary people in ordinary circumstances.

3. Sainthood as the summit: the moral saint — someone who organizes their entire life around producing as much good as possible, subordinating every other concern to that end — represents the highest ideal of a human life, the person we should admire most and aspire, however imperfectly, to become.

4. Flourishing requires the personal: a good human life constitutively involves personal projects and partial attachments — devotion to particular friends and family, the pursuit of an art, craft, or field of knowledge for its own sake — and the value these have for the person living the life cannot be fully justified on impartial grounds alone.

5. Admiration tracks obligation: the degree to which a trait or way of life is morally admirable corresponds, at least roughly, to the strength of morality's call on each of us to realize it in ourselves. There is no large class of excellences that morality praises but never demands.

What I want is not a defense of any single item but an assessment of the package as a whole. Which pairs or triples of commitments are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to be? For each genuine tension, say how severe it is and what would have to give on each side; for each merely apparent one, show why it dissolves rather than just asserting that it does. Then answer the harder question: if I may make exactly one change to the set — one deletion, weakening, or restatement — which single revision resolves the most conflict while preserving the spirit of the position? Please be specific and rank the tensions by severity. I would rather be told that two particular commitments cannot both stand, with the argument laid out, than receive an even-handed tour of the options for each item in turn.
