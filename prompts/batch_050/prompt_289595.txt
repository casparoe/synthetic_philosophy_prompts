Two friends of mine got into a long argument over beers last night about the 2011 Libya intervention, and it's still bugging me, so I want a referee. I'll relay it as best I remember. For background: they're both reasonably informed, they agree on the basic facts (NATO bombed, Gaddafi fell, the country then fell apart into militia chaos), they just disagree about what follows.

Maya's opening position: the intervention was justified when it started, full stop. There was a credible threat of a massacre in Benghazi, the intervention plausibly prevented it, and you judge a decision by what was knowable at the time, not by how the dice landed afterwards. She said judging the 2011 decision by the 2015 aftermath is "outcome bias," the same mistake as calling a good poker play bad because the river card went against you.

Dan's reply: war isn't poker. The decision to intervene isn't just "stop a massacre now," it's "set in motion a regime change whose aftermath you can't control," and any competent decision-maker in 2011 knew that post-Gaddafi Libya had no functioning institutions and that the intervening powers had no appetite for a long occupation. So the chaos wasn't bad luck layered on top of a sound decision -- it was a predictable consequence of the decision itself. He said you can't evaluate jus ad bellum while plugging your ears about jus post bellum, because for wars of choice the foreseeable endgame is part of the gamble you're choosing.

Maya came back with two points. First, he was cheating on the counterfactual: he was comparing actual Libya (chaos) to a fantasy Libya (no intervention, no massacre, Gaddafi ages gracefully), when the real alternative was likely a long Syria-style civil war, which could easily have been worse. Second, she said his standard makes humanitarian intervention literally impossible: you can never guarantee the aftermath, so "don't intervene unless you can fix the country forever" means never intervene, which means standing by during preventable massacres, which he'd never actually endorse if pressed.

Dan said fine, he doesn't endorse never intervening, but he wants the doctrine judged the way it will actually be used, not the way it reads in a seminar. His argument (this is the part I found most interesting): any intervention doctrine will be administered by powerful states pursuing their own interests, which is why we get Libya and not a dozen worse crises elsewhere, and why "humanitarian" missions keep drifting into regime change. So the question isn't "was this intervention, evaluated as a one-off act, justified?" but "does the standing permission structure, predictably abused, do more good than harm?" He said Maya keeps defending an idealized version of a practice that doesn't exist.

Maya's last move: that's a fair point about institutions but it's not the argument he started with -- he started with "the Libya decision itself was wrong," and you can't convict a specific decision by indicting the general doctrine. A specific rescue can be right even if the rescue system is corrupt. Dan said they're connected: each individual intervention looks defensible on its own terms, that's exactly how the ratchet works, and "each one was defensible" plus "the overall record is a disaster" is a contradiction someone has to resolve.

So: who's right about what? Please go through it point by point -- the outcome-bias charge, the foreseeability of the aftermath, the counterfactual problem, the "this standard makes intervention impossible" charge, the doctrine-vs-individual-act dispute, and Maya's closing move about shifting goalposts. Tell me who wins each specific point and who is more right overall, if anyone. Also please flag anything where *both* of them are missing something obvious.
