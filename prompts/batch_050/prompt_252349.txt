Some years ago I started reading Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling on my own, and the notion of the teleological suspension of the ethical has stayed with me: Abraham suspends the universal moral law for the sake of a direct relation to the absolute, and precisely because of that he cannot justify himself to anyone — unlike the tragic hero, whose sacrifice can be explained and mourned in universal terms.

I want to test this framework against a real case: Dietrich Bonhoeffer, the Lutheran pastor who moved from pacifist convictions into the conspiracy against Hitler — deception, oath-breaking, involvement in an assassination plot — and was executed in 1945. He looks at first like a candidate for the suspension: a religious man violating universal moral rules for what he took to be a higher calling. But I keep hitting mismatches, and I want a disciplined analysis rather than a verdict. Concretely: in Kierkegaard's scheme the ethical is the universal, yet in Nazi Germany the universal — law, oath of loyalty, civic duty — pointed the other way, so what exactly is being suspended, and does the theory presuppose an intact universal? Abraham cannot speak; Bonhoeffer gave reasons to his co-conspirators and in his writings — does that demote him to tragic hero, or can one give public reasons and still stand in a private relation to the absolute? And Abraham's telos is God, not an outcome; if Bonhoeffer acted for the sake of ending the regime, is his suspension just consequentialism in religious dress?

Finally, run it in the other direction: does this case confirm the theory, expose a class of agent it cannot place — the conscientious political criminal — or show that the suspension is only usable when the agent accepts the universal as valid and overrides it anyway?
