In his second Untimely Meditation, Nietzsche distinguishes three ways in which history can serve life: the monumental, which keeps the great deeds of the past alive as models and incitements to action; the antiquarian, which preserves and venerates the past because it is ours, a tether of identity and belonging; and the critical, which drags the past before a tribunal and condemns parts of it so that the living can breathe. Each mode, he argues, has both a use and a characteristic excess, and each can turn against life when it dominates.

Take, as the case, the controversies over Confederate monuments in the United States, roughly from the Charleston church shooting of 2015 through the removals and topplings of 2017–2020, including the Charlottesville rally and the eventual removal of the Robert E. Lee statue there and of the large Lee monument in Richmond. Assume I know the basic facts: most of these monuments were erected decades after the Civil War, many during the Jim Crow era and the 1950s–60s; defenders spoke of heritage, history, and not erasing the past; opponents spoke of white supremacist symbolism and of who the monuments honored; proposed compromises included relocation to museums, added contextualizing plaques, and the erection of counter-monuments.

Analyze the episode through Nietzsche's typology, in both directions. On the theory-illuminates-case side: map the actual positions in the dispute onto the three modes, but do it carefully, because the mapping is contested in an interesting way. Defenders presented themselves as antiquarians preserving what is theirs; opponents argued the monuments were monumental history doing its characteristic work, holding up exemplars to be emulated, and that this was precisely the problem. Which reading of the monuments is correct, and does Nietzsche's framework even contain the resources to adjudicate that question, given that the same object can be antiquarian inheritance to one public and monumental exhortation to another? Consider also whether the removals themselves were critical history in Nietzsche's sense -- the past judged and condemned for the sake of the living -- or whether a toppled statue is better described as a new monumental act, the founding gesture of a rival history, since Nietzsche notes that the critical mode requires strength and can itself become destructive when it wants to level rather than to judge.

On the case-tests-theory side: say where the typology strains. Nietzsche wrote about historiography, the writing and study of history, not about the built environment; does his analysis transfer to objects that structure public space whether or not anyone attends to them, or is something essential lost? His framework also seems to presuppose a single life, a single people or culture, whose health the modes serve; the American case involves plural publics whose lives the same monument serves and injures at once. Is the typology therefore inapplicable to divided societies, or does the conflict instead show each mode operating simultaneously in competing communities, with the dispute really about whose life gets to set the terms? Finally, take the proposed compromises: is a museum with a plaque antiquarianism of exactly the kind Nietzsche warned about, the past pickled and mummified once it no longer nourishes the present, and would Nietzsche regard that outcome as the healthy solution, a failure of nerve, or an option his threefold scheme simply does not see?

Conclude with a verdict on what the episode reveals about the theory: whether it confirms the claim that every society must manage the dosage of these modes, whether it exposes a missing fourth mode, or whether it shows that Nietzsche's question -- does this history serve life? -- cannot be asked at all where 'life' has no single bearer.
