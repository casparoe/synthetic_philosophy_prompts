Exam question: Descartes presents the certainty of his own existence as the first result of his method of doubt. What exactly does this certainty (the cogito) establish, and what does it not establish? Your answer should address: (a) whether the cogito is best understood as an inference, and if so, from what premises; (b) what sort of thing, if anything, it entitles Descartes to say exists; and (c) how far its certainty extends in time.

Student's response:

The cogito is Descartes's answer to the radical doubt of the First Meditation. Even if an evil demon deceives him about everything, he cannot doubt that he exists, since being deceived is itself a kind of thinking, and he must exist in order to think. This yields one belief that no doubt can touch, and it anchors the rest of the Meditations: the cogito's certainty later becomes the model for his general rule that whatever is perceived clearly and distinctly is true.

Descartes's famous conclusion in the Meditations — I think, therefore I am — is not a syllogism. He does not deduce his existence from the general premise that everything which thinks exists; he insists the truth is grasped by a simple intuition of the mind, and if anything the general premise is learned from the particular case rather than the other way round. What the cogito establishes is that the I exists as a thinking thing: the self whose existence is proved is an immaterial thinking substance, essentially distinct from the body, which is why its existence can be certain even while the existence of the body remains doubtful.

Nor is this certainty confined to the present moment. Because the proposition that he exists is a necessary truth, it holds for as long as Descartes exists as a thinking thing, so the cogito secures the continued existence of the self between individual thoughts as well as during them.
