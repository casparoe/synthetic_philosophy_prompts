I have come to accept the strong version of the claim that famines are political rather than natural events: on this view, mass starvation almost never results from there simply not being enough food to go around, but from the collapse of particular groups' ability to command food -- through lost wages, collapsed markets, hoarding, wartime policy, or deliberate state action -- combined with the absence of political institutions that would force governments to respond. The usual exhibits are Bengal in 1943, where aggregate food availability arguably did not fall enough to explain the death toll, Ireland in the 1840s, where food continued to be exported while people starved, and China after 1958, where an enormous famine coexisted with a closed political system that suppressed information about it. Play it straight: what would count against this view? I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, including: what it would show if careful reconstruction of food supply data in the classic cases found genuine, severe declines in availability; whether the view actually predicts that famines cannot occur under democracies with a free press, and what follows if well-documented counterexamples exist; whether the hoarding and speculation mechanisms invoked to explain price spikes without shortage can themselves generate famine-like outcomes without anyone's political failure; and whether the view has quietly been qualified -- from "shortages never cause famines" to "shortages rarely fully explain them" -- in a way that insulates it from any possible evidence. Please also say which of these defeaters defenders of the entitlement approach have themselves treated as serious, and which they dismiss as missing the point. Keep your answer under 600 words.
