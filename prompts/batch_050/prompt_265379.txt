I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas desk at a magazine, and I'm writing a long feature on the "levels of selection" fight in evolutionary biology -- genes vs. organisms vs. groups. Last month I had dinner with two biologists I've been interviewing for the piece and they got into it for like two hours. Now I'm staring at my notes and I honestly can't tell who won, which is a problem because I have to write this section. Here's the exchange, cleaned up from my recording:

(1) Marta (population geneticist, gene's-eye view) said group selection adds nothing real. For any trait where people claim selection acted "on the group," the Price-equation partitioning can be rewritten in inclusive-fitness terms, and every supposed group adaptation -- sterile worker castes, restrained virulence, whatever -- was already explained by kin selection decades ago. Genes are the only things that replicate with enough fidelity for cumulative adaptation; groups are temporary vehicles, and talk of selection "for the good of the group" is just sloppy bookkeeping.

(2) Dawid (evolutionary ecologist, works on major transitions) replied that translation isn't explanation. Sure, the bookkeeping inter-translates, but that just shows the math is permissive, not that the gene-level view captures the causality. When a group's output depends on its composition -- say a stand of plants where yield depends on how much leaf area each individual invests in -- contextual analysis shows the between-group term doing real causal work, and an individual-level fitness function actively misleads you. And the major transitions (protists swallowing bacteria, multicellularity, eusocial colonies) only make sense as the higher level evolving ways to police the lower one. A selfish-gene description of cancer suppression, he said, doesn't explain the phenomenon, it relocates it untill it dissapears.

(3) Marta answered that policing mechanisms evolve because they pay off for the individuals (i.e. the genes) doing the policing -- worker policing in honeybees is textbook inclusive fitness, no colony-level fitness function needed. And the deeper problem is empirical: within-group selection almost always swamps between-group selection, because groups are short-lived and migration homogenizes them. The one clean case everyone cites, myxoma virus evolving intermediate virulence in Australia, actually proves her point -- the transmission structure required is so unusual that its basically the exception that tells you the rule.

(4) Dawid said this was a rigged game: every time a group-selection case works, the gene's-eye people relabel it as kin selection, so "groups never win" is unfalsifiable bookkeeping, not an empirical finding. And the equivalence cuts both ways -- if the two descriptions always inter-translate, then "genes are the true units of selection" stops being an empirical claim and becomes a convention, and conventions don't get to call other conventions sloppy. He also said the strength asymmetry Marta leaned on is outdated, pointing to group-selection experiments that worked fine (the flour beetle ones) and to cultural group selection in humans, where conformity makes migration-homogenization a feature rather than a bug.

So: please go move by move, (1) through (4), rate each one from clearly right to clearly mistaken with a short justification, then tell me who's more right overall -- and, most useful for my article, what both of them are missing or talking past each other on. I want the section to be fair to both but not both-sides mush.
