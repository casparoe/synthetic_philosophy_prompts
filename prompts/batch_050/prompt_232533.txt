So my reading group spent the last two months on the Dennett–Caruso debate over free will and punishment — compatibilist Dennett who wants to save some notion of people getting what they deserve, versus free-will-skeptic Caruso who thinks desert is dead and criminal justice should run on something like his public health / quarantine model instead. And here's the thing: both of them reject hardcore retributivism, both think the actual prison system is a moral disaster, and yet they somehow managed to argue for a whole book. Half our sessions dissolved into fights over whether "desert" even means the same thing to the two of them. So I want you to write the document I wish they'd produced: the joint statement that a committed Dennett-style compatibilist and a committed Caruso-style skeptic could both actually sign at the end of a serious adversarial collaboration.

Here's the structure I want. First, a numbered list of agreed claims — and don't pad it with mush like "punishment requires justification." I want substantive agreements, including the ones that would surprise outsiders, e.g. where Dennett's side concedes something about basic desert, and where Caruso's side concedes something about accountability, blame practices, or the possible costs of public disillusionment about free will. Then a disagreements table where each position is phrased so that its own side would accept the wording — no strawmanning Caruso as wanting to coddle offenders, and no painting Dennett as a blood-for-blood retributivist. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions where settling them would genuinely move at least one party. Some candidates I suspect belong there, though feel free to dispute my list: whether the quarantine-style model can ground proportionality limits (why only this much detention, for this long?) without smuggling desert back in through the side door; whether what Dennett calls desert is really just forward-looking consequentialism wearing a retributivist hat — and if that particular fight is merely verbal, say so explicitly; whether ordinary moral anger and blame survive free will skepticism or have to be replaced with something else; and whether it actually matters, practically, what the general public comes to believe about free will. For each crux, give a concrete case, argument, or piece of empirical evidence that BOTH sides would agree bears on it, and write down in advance what each side predicts or expects from it — that's the whole point of the exercise, no moving goalposts afterward.

Two more things. (1) Give each side a short "what would change my mind" paragraph, and if one side's falsification conditions turn out to be suspiciously unfalsifiable, call that out rather than politely ignoring it. (2) End with an honest scorecard: which parts of the dispute are real and which are squabbles over words like "desert" and "responsibility"? If you think the whole debate bottomes out in one deep disagreement, name it and explain why the rest is downstream.
