I sit on the advisory board of a national environmental regulator. Our impact assessments for new development currently treat habitat destruction as an unambiguous harm to wild animals: any project that removes woodland, wetland, or grassland is scored negatively in proportion to the area and richness of the habitat lost, and that score feeds directly into the approval decision. A junior analyst has now challenged the assumption underneath this. Her argument is that the score presupposes the displaced animals would otherwise have lived lives worth living, whereas the actual condition of wild animals — chronic hunger, parasites, disease, predation, exposure, and early death for the great majority of offspring produced by r-selected species — may mean that most wild lives contain more suffering than wellbeing. If that is right, she argues, then habitat destruction, insofar as it prevents vast numbers of future animals from being born into such conditions, might reduce total suffering rather than increase it, and our framework could be scoring a benefit as a cost.

Please describe the most important implications of this line of thought for how our assessment framework should be revised. I am not asking whether you think wild lives are in fact net negative — the empirical question is unsettled — but what follows for a regulator who must act under that uncertainty. In particular, address: whether the framework should distinguish between killing existing animals (which plausibly causes suffering regardless of the net value of their lives) and merely preventing future births; whether the conclusion is sensitive to the choice between total and average conceptions of population welfare, and if so, which conception a public body should adopt; whether the uncertainty is genuinely symmetrical, or whether there are asymmetries (in the magnitude of the possible error, in reversibility, or in whose interests are at stake) that justify continuing to score habitat loss as negative even without resolving the net-welfare question; and whether adopting the analyst's logic would commit us to positions we would find unacceptable elsewhere, such as welcoming droughts, reintroducing predators to increase mortality, or opposing habitat restoration.

Please reason from the underlying considerations rather than citing authorities, since I want an argument I can defend in a board meeting, not a reading list. Be explicit about how confident you are in each implication you draw, distinguishing those that follow fairly directly from the premises from those that depend on contested philosophical or empirical commitments. Finally, state clearly what would change your mind: which empirical finding or philosophical argument would lead you to a different verdict on the framework, and identify the point in your reasoning where you think error is most likely. Please write in British English.
