For several years now I have been following the philosophical literature on gauge theories -- specifically the dispute over whether gauge symmetry is mere descriptive redundancy in our mathematical formalism or something with genuine physical significance. On one side there is the eliminativist or deflationary camp: gauge transformations connect representations of one and the same physical state, the gauge-dependent degrees of freedom are "surplus structure," and any appearance of physical content in them is an artifact of writing physics in a redundant formalism -- the proper response being to work with gauge-invariant quantities only, or to quotient the state space. On the other side there are those who defend some form of gauge realism or at least gauge significance, typically leaning on the Aharonov-Bohm effect (where the electromagnetic potential seems to do explanatory work no local gauge-invariant field strength captures) and on arguments from subsystems: when you consider a region of the universe as a subsystem and ask how its state transforms under Galilean or gauge boosts of its environment, the symmetry transformations seem to have directly observable consequences, which supposedly shows the "redundant" degrees of freedom are doing real work after all. There is also a middle or "sophisticated" position, modeled on treatments of the hole argument in general relativity, which keeps the gauge-dependent formalism but reads it non-literally, insisting that isomorphism under gauge transformation is the standard of sameness.

What strikes me, as an outsider with physics training, is that everyone involved seems to agree on all the physics: the same Lagrangians, the same quantization procedures, the same experimental predictions, the same formal facts about which quantities are gauge-invariant. And yet the dispute has run for decades without visible convergence, and each side routinely accuses the other of missing the point. My working hypothesis is that this is not a disagreement about physics at all but a disagreement about what the criteria for "physical reality" are supposed to be -- that the eliminativists are implicitly applying something like an Occamist or verificationist principle (no real structure without invariant empirical signature), while the realists are applying something like a modal or explanatory principle (whatever is needed to state the laws, compose subsystems into wholes, or explain counterfactual dependencies is real) -- and that because these principles are rarely stated, let alone defended, the two sides talk past each other with the formalism sitting innocently between them.

I would like you to evaluate this hypothesis and offer your own diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is: Is it, as I suspect, a clash of unstated metaphysical-cum-methodological first principles, such that neither side can be moved by any further formal or empirical result? Is there a concealed substantive question doing the work -- for instance about determinism, about what composition of subsystems requires, or about locality -- that could in principle be settled and would thereby settle the rest? Or are the parties perhaps answering different questions under the single banner of "is gauge structure real?" Please support the diagnosis with what the positions actually commit their proponents to, and then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction, a theorem, a new physical theory (say, quantum gravity) changing the evidential situation, or an explicit argument about the meta-principles themselves. If you think nothing could count as progress, argue for that rather than assuming it. Be candid about how confident you are in the diagnosis, distinguish what is well established in the literature from what is your own reconstruction, and if my framing of the dispute -- or my characterization of either camp -- is confused or unfair, say so plainly rather than working around it.
