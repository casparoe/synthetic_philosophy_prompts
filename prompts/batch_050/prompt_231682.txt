I am preparing a statement of my working commitments for a graduate seminar in moral psychology, and I would like a consistency audit before I circulate it to the group. I hold the following five views:

1. Moral judgments driven primarily by disgust — condemnations of incest between consenting adults, or of unconventional foods, dress, or bodily practices — deserve substantially less evidential weight than other moral judgments, because disgust is a pathogen-avoidance system that cultures recruit for purposes having nothing to do with anyone's welfare.

2. Moral judgments driven by empathy and compassion — opposition to cruelty, concern for suffering — deserve substantially more weight than average and should be deliberately cultivated, because they are keyed to genuine harms rather than to cultural contingency.

3. Adaptive preferences are not authoritative: when people have adjusted their desires to deprivation or subordination — wanting only what they can get, or endorsing a diminished role they were never permitted to imagine leaving — we should not treat their expressed preferences as settling what is good for them, even when they report satisfaction with their lot.

4. On the question of whether a practice demeans or harms a group, we should give significant weight to the testimony of members of that group; people with lived experience of a practice are better placed than outsiders to judge its effects on them.

5. Moral progress is real, and it consists in the correction of distortions in moral judgment — the paradigm case being the gradual extension of full moral consideration to previously excluded groups — rather than in mere changes of collective taste.

I am fairly confident of each of these individually, but I have a nagging sense that they do not sit well together, and I have been unable to locate the friction myself. Please do the following:

- Identify which pairs (or larger subsets) of these commitments are in genuine tension, in the sense that the considerations standardly used to support one of them undermine the other. For each, explain the tension concretely and rate its severity.
- Identify any pairs that look inconsistent at first glance but are not, and explain why the apparent tension dissolves on inspection.
- For each genuine tension, state the least costly available revision and what it costs, and then tell me which single revision would resolve the most.
- Rank the tensions rather than treating them as equally serious, and flag any place where my formulations are doing unacknowledged work — for instance, where a distinction I rely on may not be as stable as I need it to be.

I do not want a survey of the literature or a list of names to read; I want an audit of the position set as stated. Finally, please indicate what would change your verdict on whichever tension you take to be most severe — what consideration, if established, would lead you to conclude that the relevant commitments are jointly tenable after all — and where your own analysis is most likely to be going wrong.
