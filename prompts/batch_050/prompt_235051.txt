I'm in a philosophy elective at my school, and for our unit on irrationality our teacher asked each of us to bring in a set of cases showing where we think genuine self-deception, as opposed to ordinary bias or wishful thinking, is actually occurring. Here are my verdicts, and I'd like to know whether there's a principle behind them. Case one: a mother whose son was caught cheating, shown the evidence, and who remains sincerely convinced of his innocence and gets angry at anyone who raises it -- I say that's real self-deception. Case two: a smoker who deliberately avoids reading anything about health risks and so never forms a view either way -- I say that's not self-deception, because there's no false belief there, just avoidance. Case three: a student who every Sunday sincerely believes he'll start studying early this time, despite years of evidence, and is genuinely surprised every Friday when he hasn't -- I say that's also self-deception, even though he never avoids anything. Case four: a fan who "just knows in her heart" her team will win the championship -- I say that's wishful thinking, not self-deception, because I don't think she actually believes it the way she believes the bus comes at 8:00. Case five: a con artist who, after years of telling the same pitch, comes to fully believe it himself -- I say that's self-deception, though I couldn't tell you when it happened. Case six: someone who honestly has no memory of an embarrassing thing they did -- I say not self-deception, just forgetting. And the case I keep going back and forth on: a friend who insists, very convincingly and at length, that she is completely over her ex, while checking his social media every night.

Is there one principle that delivers all of these verdicts, or close? I have a tentative one: self-deception requires that the person actually holds the false belief (that's supposed to rule out the fan and the forgetter), and that they themselves did something to bring it about against their own evidence (that's supposed to rule in the smoker-avoider... except I said he's out, so maybe it doesn't). The problem I keep running into is that my principle seems to want self-deception to be something you do on purpose, but if you did it on purpose you'd have to know the truth while getting yourself to believe the opposite, and then it's not clear you've ever really deceived yourself at all -- and yet the mother and the reformed con artist seem like paradigm cases anyway. So: what principle, stated precisely, best captures my pattern? Which clause of it does each case motivate? What does the resulting account say about my borderline friend, and about a case I haven't listed, say a job candidate who walks out of a rejection genuinely convinced the interview went great? And if no single principle saves all six verdicts, tell me honestly which one I should give up and why.
