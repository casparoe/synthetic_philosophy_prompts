I'm a software engineer, no formal philosophy training, and last week I got dragged into a fight in my company's #random-thoughts Slack channel about whether LLMs could be conscious. Someone dropped what they presented as a total truism: you can't have a conscious experience you're not aware of. They used it to argue LLMs obviously can't be conscious, since nothing inside the model is "aware of" the rest of it. Then a coworker flipped the same sentence around: LLMs constantly model their own previous outputs, attention is a kind of awareness, so by the truism they're conscious already. Both of them treated the identical sentence as settling the debate, in opposite directions, which made me suspect the sentence is doing zero actual work and just sounds deep.

My old roommate did a philosophy minor and once mentioned something called "higher-order thought theory" -- I think the rough idea was that a mental state becomes conscious when you have some further thought or representation about it, so like pain only consciously hurts if there's some additional state representing you as being in pain. I'm guessing that's one reading of the truism, maybe the reading the first guy had in mind, but it can't be the only one, and I have no idea which readings are considered respectable versus crank.

So here's what I want: lay out the different precise claims that sentence could be making, as a menu I can paste back into the channel. Start from the trivial readings (consciousness just IS experience, so saying you're "aware of" your conscious states adds nothing) and work up to the substantive ones: that consciousness requires a *distinct* higher-order state representing the first-order one; that this higher-order state has to be noninferential or unmediated; that the subject needs actual concepts of mental states to do the representing; that the awareness has to itself be conscious (or explicitly doesn't, which I gather is the standard move); and maybe a reading where the awareness is built into the experience itself rather than being a separate state. For each reading, tell me: is it a platitude, is it a live and defensible substantive theory, or is it just false? And the payoff question -- which reading, if any, would actually support either side of the Slack argument? Does "awareness of one's own states" mean something like metacognition in the engineering sense, or something stronger that no amount of self-modeling would satisfy? Don't give me a verdict on whether LLMs are conscious; give me the disambiguation with verdicts on the readings.
