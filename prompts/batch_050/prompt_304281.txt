Since my father's stroke, I've been sitting in on a lot of case conferences, and I keep running into a pattern I don't have a word for. The neurologist explains why his hand moved the way it did entirely in terms of misfiring neurons and damaged tissue, and the explanation is complete -- nothing is left over. Then the occupational therapist explains the same movement in terms of his frustration and his habit of gesturing when he can't find words, and that explanation also seems complete. Nobody on the team thinks one explanation is false, but they also clearly don't treat them as two independent causes teaming up, the way two people might push a stuck car together. It's more as though the second explanation is somehow crowded out by the first, or floating on top of it, and the staff slide between the two without ever saying how both can be true of the same event at the same time. I've since noticed the same shape elsewhere: a software bug explained fully by the machine code and also by the programmer's intentions, a traffic jam explained by the cars and also by the drivers' impatience. Is there an established name for this situation, where a lower-level explanation that seems to leave nothing out nonetheless competes with, rather than cooperates with, a higher-level one? I'd want to know how it differs from ordinary cases of two sufficient causes, like two assassins whose bullets arrive simultaneously, and whether philosophers think the higher-level cause genuinely does any work or is merely a convenient summary. If there is no established concept for exactly this, what would you call it, and where would you draw its boundaries?
