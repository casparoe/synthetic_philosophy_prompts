Is there an established name for this? I keep noticing a pattern and I don't think it's just the sunk cost fallacy, though it's clearly in the neighborhood.

Two examples of what I mean. First: I was watching an online auction for a used camera lens, maybe worth $150. Two bidders got into it and ran it up past $200. Neither of them was confused about the lens's value -- the thing is, at any given moment, dropping out means the other guy gets the lens for a price you were just willing to beat, and bidding once more only costs you a few dollars. Each individual bid made sense. The total didn't. Second: two people at my office were both up for the same promotion and got into this quiet war of staying late, taking on extra projects, answering emails at midnight. Both of them would have preferred a world where neither did any of that, but standing down first means the other person coasts to the win on the back of effort you already put in.

So the structure seems to be: (1) there's a prize only one side can get, (2) both sides keep paying costs to stay in, (3) quitting is unattractive not because of what you've already spent, exactly, but because quitting hands the prize to the rival at a discount built partly out of your own past spending, and (4) the combined costs end up exceeding what the prize was ever worth.

When I bring this up people say "sunk cost fallacy," but that doesn't feel right. The sunk cost fallacy is about irrationally weighting past investments, and I can grant the past spending is gone and *still* feel the pull, because the trap works through the competitor, not through my accounting. I also know there's a game theory thing involving auctioning off a dollar, but as far as I understand it that's more of a paradox about how the trap gets started, whereas I'm asking about the ongoing dynamic once you're in it, in messy real life cases where there's no auctioneer.

So: does this specific configuration have a name of its own, distinct from the sunk cost fallacy and from the dollar auction setup? And whatever the answer is, I want the carving, not just a label. Does it count if there's no rival -- one person alone pouring money into a failing restaurant? Does it count if the costs are effort and reputation rather than money? What if quitting doesn't benefit the rival at all, they just also stop? Where exactly does this stop being the phenomenon I described and turn into plain sunk cost reasoning? If there genuinely isn't an established term for the competitive version, propose one, define it precisely, and give me a boundary case on each side.
