A colleague and I have been arguing about the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, and he has cornered me with a chain of reasoning whose every step looks defensible and whose conclusion I refuse to accept. (1) On the many-worlds interpretation, a quantum process with several possible outcomes produces branches realizing each of them, so in any apparatus that kills the subject on all but one quantum outcome, there is always some branch in which the subject survives, no matter how many trials are run and no matter how unfavorable the odds. (2) A subject's stream of experience continues only into branches where the subject remains alive; branches in which the subject dies contribute nothing to what the subject goes on to experience. (3) Therefore, conditional on having any future experience at all, the subject's future experience will be an experience of having survived: from the first-person point of view, survival through the apparatus is certain. (4) What it is rational for an agent to do is fixed by what that agent should expect to experience. (5) So, if many-worlds is true, it is rational to accept the following deal: a device pays you ten million dollars in every branch where you survive a quantum coin flip and kills you instantly otherwise, and it remains rational to accept the deal however lopsided the flip is made. (6) By the same reasoning, any risk whose only bad outcome is death is no risk at all, so on many-worlds it is rational to treat mortal danger as costless. (7) That conclusion is absurd; a worldview that advises betting one's life at a million to one is defective even if many-worlds is true, so the chain must break somewhere. My colleague insists the only honest exits are to abandon many-worlds or to bite the bullet at (6). I would like to keep both many-worlds and ordinary aversion to mortal risk. Tell me which single step I should reject, why that step looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs compared with its neighbors — in particular, what rejecting (2) commits me to about how much weight branches of differing sizes deserve, and what rejecting (4) does to decision theory in ordinary, non-quantum cases. Please answer in continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
