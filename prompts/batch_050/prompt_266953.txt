An analogy I have encountered repeatedly -- first in an introductory economics course, and since then in newspaper columns -- runs as follows. A statutory minimum wage is simply a price floor, and a price floor on labor works like a price floor on anything else. If the government set a minimum legal price of $20,000 on used cars, the argument goes, then cars worth less than that would not become more valuable; they would become unsellable. Their owners would be excluded from the market just as surely as if the cars themselves had been banned. By parity of reasoning, a minimum wage of fifteen dollars an hour does not raise the wage of a worker whose labor is worth less than that; it renders that worker unemployable, forbidding a transaction both parties would otherwise willingly make. The analogy is usually deployed as a conversation-stopper: raising the minimum wage harms precisely the low-skilled workers it purports to help, with the same logical necessity with which the car floor would strand cheap cars.

I would like a careful audit of this analogy, in three parts. First, lay out the correspondence explicitly: what in the labor market plays the role of the car, the seller, the buyer, the car's market value, and the unsold inventory, and note any places where the mapping is already strained at the outset (for instance, the fact that a worker sells hours of effort rather than a durable object, and remains alive and consuming while "unsold"). Second, identify the disanalogies and sort them by whether they matter for the conclusion the analogy is asked to carry: consider, at minimum, monopsony power and search frictions on the employer side, the possibility that higher wages elicit greater effort or lower turnover, the elasticity of demand for low-skilled labor compared with demand for used cars, and any macroeconomic feedback from low-wage workers spending their income. Third, state whether the strong necessity claim survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the honest verdict is something weaker -- for example, that the analogy establishes a presumption rather than a proof, or that it holds in one kind of labor market and fails in another. If a better analogy than the car market exists for conveying what a price floor genuinely guarantees and what it leaves open, propose one and explain its own limits.
