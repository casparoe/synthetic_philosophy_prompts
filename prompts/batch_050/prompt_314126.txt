I am a retired molecular biologist, fairly new to philosophy, and I host a small reading group that has just worked through Dennett's distinction between cranes and skyhooks, together with his charge of greedy reductionism. For our next meeting I want a one-page decision tree that sorts a person into a position on reduction and emergence by what they say about cases, not by which label they find congenial. Please act as a professional philosopher of science and think carefully: before drafting the tree, briefly map the logical space — the possible combinations of answers to your diagnostic questions — and only then locate the actual positions within it.

Requirements:

- Four to six branching questions, each phrased as a concrete case or plain claim that a non-philosopher can answer. For example: whether the gliders in Conway's Game of Life are really there or merely a convenient fiction; whether anything is left unexplained once a thunderstorm is fully described at the level of particle physics; whether an explanation of why the antelope flees is ever improved by descending from natural selection to molecular mechanisms. Each question must genuinely divide the positions from one another.
- The leaves must include: greedy or eliminative reductionism; Dennett's own view that cranes suffice but higher-level patterns are real; non-reductive physicalism that denies downward causation; weak, merely epistemic emergence; and strong emergence with genuine downward causation — the honest skyhook. Any unoccupied combination of answers should be noted, with a brief diagnosis of whether the slot is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.
- If two positions cannot be separated by the tree, say so explicitly rather than contriving a spurious distinction.
- Close each leaf with one sentence stating the position's most awkward commitment.
