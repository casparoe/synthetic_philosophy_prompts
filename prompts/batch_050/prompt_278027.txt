I have no formal training in philosophy or engineering, but over the past year I've been reading public debates about AI safety, and I keep watching two analogies get thrown at each other, and I can't tell whether either side is actually entitled to its conclusion.

The first side wants worst-case guarantees before advanced AI systems are deployed, and they reach for civil engineering: we don't certify a bridge by showing it survives average traffic, we certify it for the heaviest plausible load plus a safety margin. An AI system that is aligned on average but catastrophically misaligned under rare conditions, they say, is like a bridge that's fine 364 days a year and collapses on the 365th. Nobody would accept that bridge, so nobody should accept that AI.

The second side says worst-case guarantees are impossible in principle and would strangle a beneficial technology, and they reach for drug approval: no medication is certified for the worst case, since essentially every approved drug harms somebody somewhere, and we approve drugs on average benefit across a population. Demanding a worst-case proof for AI, they say, is like refusing to approve any drug until it's shown that no patient could ever have a bad reaction, which would mean no medicine at all.

Here's my own attempt to cut between them, which I'd like stress-tested. It seems to me the two analogies quietly disagree about what the word "case" ranges over. For the bridge, the worst case is the tail of a known and roughly stationary distribution of loads, and the bridge itself doesn't change after it's certified. For the drug, the average is taken over many independent patients, so one bad outcome is one patient. But an AI system keeps learning or getting updated after deployment, operates in an environment that includes people actively trying to find its weak points, and its bad tail event might not be one "patient" but everyone at once. So when I try to say what the "case" is for AI, I can't figure out whether it's a point in a distribution, a strategy of an adversary, or a state of a system that didn't exist at certification time, and I suspect the whole argument hinges on which one it is.

Could you audit this properly? For each analogy, give me the explicit correspondence table — what plays the role of the bridge, the load, the safety margin, the patient, the side effect, the approval agency — and then mark where the mapping breaks. Separate the disanalogies that actually bear on the conclusions from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Specifically: does the bridge analogy secretly assume that nature doesn't adapt to your design, and does that assumption fail for AI? Does the drug analogy secretly assume that harms are independent and bounded, and does that assumption fail for AI? Then tell me whether either side's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, whether my wrinkle about what "case" means is a genuine insight or a confusion, and if neither analogy is up to the job, what a better one would be.
