I am writing a long-form piece on disputes that outlive their institutional settings, and I want to use the middle knowledge controversy as my central case. Here is the situation as I understand it. Since the late sixteenth century, when Luis de Molina's Concordia proposed that God knows what any free creature would freely do in any circumstance in which it might be placed, critics in the Dominican tradition of Domingo Báñez have pressed the grounding objection: prior to the divine decree, nothing exists to make such counterfactuals of creaturely freedom true, so there is nothing for God to know. Molinists reply that the truths need no further ground than their own modal status. A papal commission spent nearly a decade on this and dissolved in 1607 without a verdict, and my reading of the contemporary literature suggests nothing has changed in four hundred years: the same objection, the same reply, with open theists and Calvinists now sniping from the sidelines.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really a theological dispute at all but a dispute about truthmaking and the metaphysics of modality conducted in providential costume -- that is, about whether there can be contingent truths with no ground, a question that arises for counterfactuals quite generally and has nothing essentially to do with God. I want to know whether that diagnosis is right, or whether something irreducibly theological remains once you strip the modality out -- say, a disagreement about what divine sovereignty requires, or about what kind of freedom is worth having.

Please give me a diagnosis, not a verdict and not a survey. What kind of disagreement is this: substantive, verbal, or two traditions answering different questions under the single heading of how God knows future free acts? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress -- a formal result about counterfactuals, a conceptual distinction, an independent argument about grounding -- or should we expect no resolution at all, and why?
