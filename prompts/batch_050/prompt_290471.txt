I sit on the allocation committee of a mid-sized health charity (we fund treatments, equipment, home adaptations, that kind of thing, roughly 40 applications a meeting, decisions are final and I'm one of seven votes, we have about 20 minutes per case). I recently read up on Scanlon's contractualism because the "what could nobody reasonably reject" framing seemed way more usable than the utilitarian spreadsheet approach some of my colleagues default to. But I've hit the wall everyone hits: the aggregation problem. Scanlon apparently says you compare complaints one at a time, but then also seems to allow that when two complaints are equally strong, the number of people on each side can break the tie -- and I genuinely can't tell what his view implies for the messy middle cases we see constantly, like one applicant needing an expensive treatment for a severe condition versus a pool of forty applicants each needing something modest but still life-improving.

What I want is an actual decision procedure we could pilot: a sequence of tests to run on any multi-claimant case, in order. Something like -- first screen for which claims are even comparable, then pairwise complaint comparison, then a rule for when (if ever) headcounts get to count, plus what to do with claims of very different magnitude where neither is trivial. For each step, tell me what information the committee needs to gather, what a "pass" looks like, and where the procedure has to just stop and say "this is a judgment call, vote on it" because no procedure can do that part for you. I'd also like you to flag which parts of the procedure are faithful to Scanlon and which parts are pragmatic patches that a contractualist purist would object to, because I'd rather know that going in.

Then run the procedure on three of our actual recurring case types: (1) one severe high-cost claim vs. forty modest low-cost claims, same total budget; (2) two medically identical claimants where funding one means the other waits a year; (3) a wheelchair ramp for a community hall used by hundreds vs. a prosthetic for one child. I'm especially interested in a case where the procedure spits out an answer most of my committee would hate, because I suspect the forty-modest-claims case is exactly that, and I want to see whether the reasoning holds up or whether the procedure breaks somewhere. Also tell me honestly what the procedure can't handle at all -- like claims from people who don't exist yet, or cases where the "complaint strength" depends on what people deserve rather than what they'd lose.
