At a fundraiser casino night last month they had this weird game at one table, and two friends of mine got into a serious argument about it that I got dragged into as referee. I'm still stuck, so I want an actual adjudication.

The game: pay $25 to play. The dealer flips a fair coin until it lands heads. If the first heads comes on flip n, your prize is (-1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n dollars. So heads on flip 1 you win $2, flip 2 you lose $2, flip 3 you win about $2.67, flip 4 you lose $4, flip 5 you win $6.40, flip 6 you lose about $10.67, and the swings keep growing. Negative prize means you pay them.

Marta (stats PhD) said the game has no expected value at all. The expectation is the series 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which only converges because of cancellation — the absolute values give the harmonic series, which diverges. By the rearrangement theorem you can reorder the terms to sum to literally anything, so there's no such thing as "the" expectation. And since decision theory evaluates gambles by expected value, it simply has no verdict here. Paying $25, refusing, paying $500 — all equally rational. Rationality is silent.

Theo (philosophy PhD) said silence is a cop-out. First move: run the game but stop after N flips (prize 0 if no heads by then) — that truncated game has a perfectly good expected value, and as N grows those values converge to about 69 cents. The real game is the limit of the truncated ones, so its value is 69 cents and $25 is a ripoff. Marta said the truncation is one approximation among infinitely many, and the limit you get depends on how you embed the game — the 69 cents is a property of her story about the game, not the game. She added that if the casino generated the whole sequence by computer in advance and just handed you an envelope, there'd be no "flip order" to privilege anyway.

Theo's second move: fine, drop numbers. Even without an expectation, some verdicts are obviously right or wrong. Nobody rational would trade their house for one play. So "no expected value, therefore everything is equally rational" can't be true — the correct position is that rational evaluation here is partial: it rules some things out without ranking everything. Marta said "trading your house is nuts" only because the disutility of losing a house dwarfs any plausible gain — i.e., because real utility over money is bounded. But if utility is bounded, the expectation of the game converges absolutly and exists after all, so the puzzle never gets started. Either stakes are effectively bounded (no puzzle, and the theory survives by being about small stuff) or they're not (puzzle back). And note bounded utility has its own embarassing consequence: it makes you refuse some bets with astronomically positive money-value, which seems just as bad.

Theo's last stand: then denominate the prizes in something else — days of happiness, whatever — and the puzzle returns even for bounded money-utility people, so boundedness is a psychological fact, not a principle of rationality, and can't dissolve a problem about what rationality requires. Marta said that's fine, it just confirms her original point: where expectations don't exist, rationality has run out of things to say.

So: who's right about each piece — the rearrangement point, the truncation proposal, the bounded-utility exchange, and the "anything goes" inference? Who's more right overall? And is there something both of them are missing?
