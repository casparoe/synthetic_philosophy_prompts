I am a third-year philosophy major currently enrolled in an upper-division seminar on personal identity, and our department runs a student colloquium series in which an undergraduate moderates the question-and-answer portion of each guest lecture. I have been assigned next week's session. The speaker is a visiting philosopher who defends a neo-Lockean account of personal identity: roughly, the view that a person at one time is identical to a person at an earlier time just in case the two are connected by an appropriately continuous chain of memories, where direct memory connections between adjacent stages suffice to hold the whole chain together. Our seminar has already worked through Locke's original memory criterion and the classic objections to it, so the audience will know the basic shape of the position, and I do not want to spend the session on questions that merely invite her to restate it or on soft general questions about her career and influences.

Please draft eight to ten questions that press where the literature actually presses, each paired with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. At minimum, I would like the following pressure points covered. First, Reid's brave officer objection: the aged general remembers the young officer, the officer remembered the flogged schoolboy, but the general no longer remembers the boy, so a simple memory criterion appears to make the general both identical and not identical to the boy. Her likeliest reply is that the criterion requires only an overlapping chain of direct memory connections rather than direct remembering between the endpoints; I want a follow-up probing whether identity can plausibly be grounded in a chain that the person at one end can no longer survey from the inside, and whether that sits awkwardly with the thought that one's identity over time ought to matter to oneself. Second, the circularity worry that memory presupposes personal identity rather than constituting it, since we distinguish genuine memories from merely apparent ones precisely by whether the rememberer is the person who had the experience; the standard reply invokes a notion of apparent or quasi-memory defined without reference to identity, and the follow-up should test whether that notion does genuine explanatory work or only relabels the difficulty. Third, fission: if a person's memories could be duplicated into two successors, a one-one criterion seems to deliver the unwelcome result that both continuers are identical to the original and hence to each other; I want the follow-up to address the escape route of declaring that survival, rather than strict identity, is what matters, and to ask what then becomes of the original theory. Fourth, I would like one question built around a thought experiment of my own devising, which I call the amnesiac diarist: a woman loses all firsthand memories from before age thirty in an accident, but keeps a meticulous diary throughout her twenties, and years later, by studying the diary, she comes to have detailed apparent memories of events she no longer recalls directly. I want to ask whether identity runs through such externally mediated, diary-dependent connections, and if so, whether the theory has quietly stopped being a memory criterion at all. Finally, please end the set with one question asking her to state plainly what result or case would lead her to abandon the view.

Please present the questions and their follow-ups as continuous prose, the way one would write them out in a letter or preparatory notes: no headings, numbered or bulleted lists, bold text, or tables. Since the colloquium is being held at our partner university in Britain and my notes will be circulated to faculty there, please write the whole response in British English.
