I'm giving a colloquium talk next month to a joint math/philosophy audience arguing that the Church-Turing thesis is not, and could never be, a theorem: it's a hypothesis about what human calculators can do, and Turing himself basically says so. My exhibit A is this passage from On Computable Numbers (1936, §1): "No real attempt will be made to justify the definitions given until we reach §9. For the present I shall only say that the justification lies in the fact that the human memory is necessarily limited." Later in the same section he calls it "my contention that these operations include all those which are used in the computation of a number." My reading: the whole edifice rests on an empirical premise about human memory, and "contention" is doing a lot of work -- a contention with a defense isn't a derivation.

The room: mostly logicians and theoretical CS people who absorbed Church-Turing as practically a definition, two philosophers who've read there Gandy and Sieg and think Turing's §9 analysis of the human computer is about as close to a proof as conceptual analysis gets, and one physicist who keeps muttering about hypercomputation and quantum gravity. Prep my Q&A sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this crowd (not from a generic audience), roughly ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that specific person would put it -- the logician's version will not be the philosopher's version -- with a two-minute reply for each and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Also: what should I concede up front to buy credibility, and what's the question I'm hoping nobody asks? Answer in American English.
