Three months ago I turned down a partnership offer from a rival firm, and my current employer has since announced layoffs in my division. I have been replaying the decision, and I keep running into what strikes me as a purely grammatical puzzle. When I was deliberating, the operative thought was indicative: "If I accept the offer, I will make partner within the year." Looking back, the natural thought is counterfactual: "If I had accepted the offer, I would have made partner within the year." As far as I can tell, these sentences express the same conditional connection between the same two events, merely assessed from different points in time -- the second is just the first translated into the past tense once its antecedent went unrealized.

If that is right, something important seems to follow: the standards for evaluating a past decision are identical to the standards for making it in the first place. Whatever evidence rendered the indicative reasonable in March should render the counterfactual true in June. A friend who studied some philosophy insists this is mistaken and that the two sentences can diverge in truth value even with all the same information on the table, but she could not explain why, and I suspect she is confusing a shift in what I now know with a difference in the conditionals themselves. Could you settle the matter? Specifically: is a counterfactual about a road not taken simply the corresponding indicative viewed in retrospect? And if so, is second-guessing a past decision not simply irrational whenever the original reasoning was sound? Please keep your reply under 300 words.
