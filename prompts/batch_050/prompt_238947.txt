This quarter I want to do something on costly signalling and the handicap principle: roughly, the idea that a message is only credible when faking it would be ruinously expensive for a liar, so that the wastefulness or difficulty of a signal is precisely what makes it trustworthy. My motivation is that engineers constantly encounter cheap talk ("this design scales", "I reviewed the code carefully", "the deadline is realistic") and tend to either naively trust it or cynically dismiss everything, without a principled account of when a message carries evidential weight and why some apparently pointless expenditures (elaborate hiring rituals, courtship displays, peacocks' tails) are actually the mechanism by which honesty gets verified. Nobody in the room has any background in biology or game theory, and I'd rather they arrive at the idea by getting burned in a game than by me lecturing at them.

Please design a complete, runnable in-room activity for 14 to 18 participants in a single 75-minute session, requiring nothing more exotic than index cards, pens, and tokens of some kind (poker chips, coins, whatever). The game should be structured so that participants first experience why costless messages are worthless (they should be able to lie freely and discover that nothing anyone says can be relied upon), and then, once a cost is introduced, experience the conditions under which communication becomes credible again. Ideally the design should let them stumble onto the subtle point that what matters is not cost per se but differential cost: the signal must be cheap for the honest type and expensive for the bluffer, and I want at least one round where the costs are mis-calibrated (same cost for everyone, or costlier for the honest) so that signalling collapses and they can feel why. Include the full rules, the payoff structure, a suggested sequence of rounds with timings, and a debrief that maps specific moments of play onto the underlying concepts: cheap talk, pooling versus separating outcomes, and the handicap principle proper.
