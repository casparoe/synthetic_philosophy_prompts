A move I keep encountering in political argument is to say that life without government is a prisoner's dilemma: each person does better by seizing what they can whatever others do, everyone thereby ends up worse off than if all had restrained themselves, and so a coercive state is needed to make cooperation stick. I first heard it from a lecturer, who used it to close down sympathy for anything short of strong central authority — the thought being that once you grant the dilemma, the state's power is not one arrangement among many but a logical necessity. I would like to know how far the analogy actually holds. Set out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the players, the two available moves, the payoffs, and the absent referee. Then audit the mapping. Is the state of nature really one-shot, as the dilemma is usually set up, or repeated — and does that change what rational parties would do? Can they talk, bargain and build reputations in ways the dilemma forbids? Are their situations as symmetric as the analogy assumes? And does it matter that the anxiety driving the original picture seems to be less temptation than fear — that even those inclined to keep faith must strike first because they cannot be sure of others? I am also puzzled by a structural point: the dilemma's players cannot alter their own game, yet the social contract is precisely an attempt to alter it, and the agreement establishing an enforcer is itself struck where there is no enforcer. Which of these disanalogies actually matter for the intended conclusion, and which are idle? If the predicament is better modelled as one where mutual restraint is everyone's preferred outcome provided the rest restrain themselves too, would the remedy still be an overwhelming sovereign — or has the prisoner's-dilemma framing quietly smuggled in the strong conclusion it was meant to establish? If a different comparison does the work better, say which, and why.
