I'm in Year 12 and sit on my school's student council, and I need advice about a decision I have to make before Monday, because I keep going back and forth on it. This term our headteacher brought in a new policy: whenever something serious happens — graffiti in the library, a phone taken from the changing rooms — and nobody owns up, the whole year group is punished until someone does. After an incident last week, our entire year has lost access to the common room and to Friday afternoon activities. The council has been asked to help enforce this: we're expected to take turns sitting outside the locked common room at lunch and writing down the names of anyone who tries to get in.

I think punishing 180 people, nearly all of whom did nothing, is straightforwardly unjust, and I've decided I don't want to be the instrument of it. My plan is to refuse the rota and, more than that, to sit inside the common room at lunch with anyone who joins me, having told the headteacher in advance exactly what I intend to do and why. But three of my friends have given me arguments I can't easily dismiss, and I'd like to know where they leave me. The first says I stood for election to the council knowing the role would sometimes mean enforcing rules I hadn't chosen, so I can't invoke my conscience only when a rule happens to inconvenience my sense of fairness; by volunteering, I took on the bad with the good. The second says that open defiance from a council member is worse than the same act from an ordinary student, because it teaches everyone that rules apply only until you disagree with them, and that if I can't stomach the role I should simply resign rather than disobey from inside it. The third says that what separates principled protest from ordinary rule-breaking is willingness to take the punishment afterwards, so if I'm caught I should accept whatever sanction follows without complaint — and that if I'm not prepared to do that, I shouldn't do it at all.

There's one further complication. The headteacher has hinted that if council members refuse to cooperate, the council's budget for next term, which funds a trip most of the younger students are counting on, will be withdrawn. My protest would therefore cost people who never signed up for it, in exactly the way I object to the year group being made to pay for one person's vandalism.

Please tell me what I should actually do, and commit to an answer rather than setting out the options and leaving me to choose. I'd like to know whether having volunteered for the council creates a genuine obligation to enforce a rule I judge unjust, or only a weaker reason that injustice can override; whether resigning first would change the moral position or is merely cosmetic; whether openness and accepting punishment are really what separate justified disobedience from self-serving rule-breaking, and what that means for quieter alternatives like simply not turning up to my rota shifts; and whether the threat to the trip changes what I may do, given that I find the logic of that threat objectionable in the first place. I'd rather you gave one definite verdict with reasons than a balanced survey. Please also write your reply as continuous prose, the way you'd write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text.
