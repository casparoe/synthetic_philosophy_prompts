In my seminar on modal epistemology, students too often treat the step from conceivability to possibility in the zombie argument as a single, unanalyzed inference. I want you to argue for the following thesis, which most of them would resist: even if we grant that ideal positive conceivability entails metaphysical possibility, this entailment does no genuine epistemic work in the case for property dualism, because no inquirer could ideally and positively conceive of a scenario as richly specified as a complete minimal physical duplicate of the actual world. In developing the argument, please do the following. First, show that any usable version of the zombie premise must therefore rest either on the negative conceivability of a large-scale scenario (mere failure to detect a contradiction) or on the positive conceivability of a partial scenario, and explain why each substitute fails for a different reason — the former because large-scale scenarios can conceal the kind of hidden inconsistency that familiar necessary a posteriori truths exemplify, the latter because an entailment from a partial scenario to a metaphysical possibility requires a bridge principle that itself presupposes the falsity of physicalism. Second, address the natural reply that the argument needs only the conceivability of a microphysical duplicate minus consciousness, not of a whole world. Third, engage explicitly with the distinction between primary and secondary conceivability at the point where it matters most to your case. Finally, state what, if anything, conceivability remains a reliable guide to if your argument succeeds.
