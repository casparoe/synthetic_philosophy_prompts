My flatmate thinks this is proof that sad music makes you feel sad in a controlled way so you can kind of drain the sadness out, like lancing something. My own instinct is the opposite — the sadness felt like it was in the music, out there, and I was just witnessing it, not actually feeling sad in the way I feel sad about the cat. We got into a proper argument about it and realized neither of us actually knows what the philosophical theories here even are.

So here's what I want: construct a thought experiment designed to discriminate between two theories of how music can be sad — on one side, the arousal theory, roughly the view that sad music is sad because it makes listeners sad; on the other side, a recognition or contour-style theory, roughly the view that sad music is sad because we perceive a sadness-like profile or character in it, whether or not it makes us feel anything. The scenario should be one where the two theories give clearly different predictions about what the same listener will report or how they'll behave, such that the actual outcome would count as evidence for one theory over the other. Constraints: the music must be purely instrumental (no lyrics, no suggestive titles, since those smuggle in semantic content), the listener can't have personal associations with the piece (no this was our song stuff), they should be an ordinary person rather than a trained musician or philosopher, and the setup has to be something you could plausibly run as a psychology study — no brain scanners that read qualia, no stipulating the listener's inner states, just observable or reportable outcomes.

Keep it under maybe 500 words of setup for the scenario itself; the analysis can be longer.
