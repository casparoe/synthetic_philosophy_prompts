I am preparing a short methods paper for a Peircean pragmatism reading group and want a consistency audit before I circulate it. My commitments are these. First, I accept the pragmatic maxim quite generally: the content of a concept is fixed by the conceivable practical difference its correct application would make to expectation, conduct, or inquiry. Second, I make an exception for normative concepts such as rationality, evidence, and truth, which I treat as having authority over inquiry that cannot itself be cashed out merely in further expectations or habits of action. Third, I endorse Peirce’s demand that inquiry begin only from genuine doubt, not from Cartesian paper doubt; a belief should not be disturbed unless some recalcitrant experience, conflict, or stake actually irritates it. Fourth, I also favor institutionalized red-teaming: laboratories, journals, and forecasting groups should periodically attack even well-settled background assumptions as a standing discipline. Fifth, I am a thoroughgoing fallibilist and extend this to logic and probability: no inferential rule is metaphysically exempt from revision. Sixth, I defend inference to the best explanation by saying its authority comes from the long-run self-corrective character of inquiry, which presupposes that some canons of correction remain stable enough to distinguish improvement from drift. Seventh, I hold that truth is neither usefulness nor consensus, but the would-be terminus of inquiry carried far enough under better evidence and broader criticism.

Please treat two diagnostic devices as part of the exercise rather than as established jargon. Use the cash-value test for whether a purported conceptual distinction would alter any possible prediction, decision, or method if it were true rather than false. Use the scar test for whether a doubt bears the marks of having been produced by an actual anomaly or cost, as opposed to having been generated to satisfy a procedural demand for rigor. Audit the set above for coherence: identify which commitments genuinely pull against one another, which pairs only appear to conflict, and which tensions are fatal unless revised. Rank the tensions by severity, explain for each what exactly would have to give, and propose the least costly revision that preserves the broadly Peircean spirit of the view. Do not turn the answer into a general lecture on pragmatism; I want the audit of this particular package, including whether the exception for normative concepts and the standing red-team requirement can be stated in a form that survives both tests.
