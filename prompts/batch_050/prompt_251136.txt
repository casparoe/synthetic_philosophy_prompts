Hey so here's a challenge for you about cognitive penetration of perception (the debate over whether what you believe or want can literally change what you see, not just how you judge it).

Construct a realistic, everyday scenario -- no lab equipment, no neuroscience gadgets -- in which it genuinely looks like someone's high-level belief or desire changes their raw perceptual experience, but that is engineered to survive the standard debunking moves. Specifically, your case has to hold up against all three of these:

1. The attention confound: the skeptic says the person just looked at the thing differently (shifted gaze, focused on different features), so perception itself was untouched.
2. The judgment confound: the skeptic says perception stayed the same and only the person's report/interpretation downstream changed.
3. The memory confound: the skeptic says the belief contaminated the recollection of the experience, not the experience itself.

So you need to build in realistic reasons each of those explanations fails -- e.g. how the task or setup pins attention down, or produces a behavioral signature that wouldn't show up if only judgment changed. It can involve experts vs novices, expectations, emotion, whatever you like, but keep it to stuff that could plausibly happen in a normal week.

After the scenario, walk through how it blocks each of the three confounds one by one, then give the strongest counterattack a defender of perceptual impenetrability (Pylyshyn-types) would still make, and say whether you think your case actually survives it or just makes it more expensive to maintain. Be honest if one of the confounds can only be weakened, not fully ruled out.

Keep the whole thing under 600 words.
