I co-host a small philosophy podcast and our next guest is a philosopher who defends karma as a literal theory of moral desert -- not karma as a metaphor for habits or character, but the claim that suffering and flourishing really do track the moral quality of one's deeds, across rebirths, and that this is what makes the universe just. She's sharp and has thought about the obvious objections, so I dont want questions that let her just restate the view. Draft eight to ten interview questions, with at least one on each of the following, and for each question a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give:

- what the view implies about people born into misery (disease, famine, abuse) -- does she bite the bullet that they deserve it, and how does she keep that from licensing indifference toward them
- the mechanism question: with no cosmic judge, what is supposed to connect a deed done decades or lifetimes ago to a later fate, and is that even a respectable kind of causation
- the falsifiability worry: when the wicked prosper, she can always say unknown past merit; when the good suffer, unknown past vice. Is there any imaginable distribution of fortune that would count against the theory?
- whether karma makes our own moral practices redundant or conflicts with them: if the ledger settles itself, is there still reason to punish wrongdoers, and more awkardly, is helping the suffering actually interfering with justice being done?

For that last one, I'd especially like a follow-up for the move where she says our compassionate impulses are themselves part of how karma works.
