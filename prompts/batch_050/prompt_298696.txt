I am a PhD student in decision theory and formal epistemology, and I keep encountering a pattern of reasoning I cannot place. In each case an agent treats their own one-shot choice as evidence about what causally inaccessible agents are doing, and cooperates on that basis alone. A voter in an electorate of hundreds of millions reasons that her staying home would be evidence that like-minded citizens stayed home too, so she votes. A player in a single anonymous Prisoner's Dilemma against a stranger selected for psychological similarity cooperates because whatever he does is evidence about the other's choice. A consumer reduces emissions not because it causes anything measurable but because her choice is a sample of what billions of similar consumers do. In none of these cases is there causal influence, communication, repetition, or any belief that the counterparts are simulating the agent. Is there an established name for this pattern, or is it merely ordinary evidential decision theory applied to large populations? Please identify the nearest concepts — I suspect it sits somewhere between evidential decision theory proper, superrationality, and acausal coordination — explain how the described pattern differs from each, say which fits best, and mark its edges: must the counterparts actually exist, must the correlation run through a shared decision procedure, and does it still count when the agent's ends are self-interested? Answer in at most 300 words; the carving matters more to me than the label.
