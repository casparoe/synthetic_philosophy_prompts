I'm in an undergrad historiography seminar and last week we were discussing whether historians actually *explain* events or just redescribe them in more detail. I asked whether something like the outbreak of WWI could ever be explained the way, say, an eclipse gets explained -- by showing it had to happen given certain conditions and general regularities. The TA basically waved the question off by saying 'history is just one damn thing after another,' like that settled it, and the professor nodded along. A couple of us stayed after and realized none of us could actually say what that line is supposed to *mean*, beyond a general vibe of 'it's complicated and contingent, don't ask.'

So here's what I'd like: lay out the different precise theses that slogan could be expressing, from the most trivial to the most radical. For instance, is it just the banal point that historical events are temporally ordered and numerous? Is it the stronger claim that there are no exceptionless laws in history of the kind covering-law models of explanation would need -- and if so, does the absence of strict laws actually block explanation, or just one specific model of it? Is it a claim about causation -- that events aren't really connected at all and any narrative thread we draw between them is projection? Or a claim about narrative specifically -- that historians impose plots (tragedy, progress, decline) on a sequence that doesn't contain them, so that 'explanation' in history is always partly fictional? Or maybe it's about unpredictability, or about the irrelevance of the distant past to the present, or something else entirely I'm not seeing.

For each reading, say whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or just false, and what the best argument for and against it would be. And crucially: which of these readings, if any, would actually justify the TA's move of shutting down my question about whether events can be genuinely explained? I have a suspicion the line only works rhetorically if you slide between a weak reading that's true and a strong reading that would do the work -- but I'm not sure, so tell me if I'm wrong about that.
