So I'm prepping for a political philosophy reading group where we each have to write down our "position profile" on collective choice and rights, and then everyone else tries to break it. I'd like a stress test before I hand mine in, because I've had the nagging feeling something in it doesn't cohere but I can't locate it. Here's where I've landed. First, I take the weak Pareto principle to be non-negotiable: if literally everyone prefers one social outcome to another, going with the dispreferred one is just irrational, full stop, and I mean everyone's actual preferences as they are, not some idealized or filtered version of them. Second, I'm a minimal liberal in Sen's sense: there are purely personal matters -- what you read, how you dress, what you do in your own bedroom -- where the individual's preference has to be decisive over the social outcome, no matter how strongly the rest of us feel about it. Third, I think the impossibility result connecting those two is real but ultimately not deep: all it shows is that nosy, meddlesome preferences about other people's private lives shouldn't get counted in social evaluation at all, and once you've screened those out, Pareto and liberty slot together fine. Fourth, I think of rights as side-constraints rather than goals: a rights violation isn't a cost to be weighed against other costs in some social calculus, it's just off the table. Fifth, people should obviously be free to waive or trade the exercise of their own rights if they'd rather -- nobody has to read the book or refrain from reading it, freedom includes contracting around your own defaults when it suits you.
