You are grading a graduate seminar response in population ethics. Assign a numerical grade out of 100 and provide point-by-point comments explaining what is correct, what is mistaken, and what is incomplete. Identify any inference that does not follow from the stipulated view, even if it initially sounds plausible. Conclude by briefly describing what an excellent answer would have included.

Exam question:

For purposes of this question, take tranquilism to be the view that suffering and unfulfilled craving are the only things that are intrinsically bad. Pleasure, happiness, and satisfied desire have no intrinsic positive value, although they may have instrumental value—for example, by preventing suffering or making cooperation possible.

A policymaker must choose among three uses of the same resources:

1. Eliminate an existing person’s severe suffering.
2. Prevent the existence of a future person who would otherwise undergo the same severe suffering.
3. Create several additional people whose lives would contain abundant pleasure and satisfied desire, but no suffering or unfulfilled craving.

Explain what tranquilism implies about these choices. Your answer should address: whether preventing suffering in an existing person and preventing it by avoiding a person’s creation have the same moral significance; whether adding happy people can make an outcome intrinsically better; whether the nonidentity problem arises if the alternative is creating a person whose life would contain serious but endurable suffering; and whether tranquilism entails that one case of severe suffering always takes lexical priority over any number of mild unfulfilled cravings.

Student answer:

Tranquilism introduces a fundamental asymmetry between relieving bad states and creating good ones. Because pleasure and satisfied desire have no intrinsic positive value, option 3 cannot make the outcome intrinsically better, even if the added people would be very happy. It could make the outcome worse if their lives contained any suffering or unfulfilled craving. This does not mean that happiness is causally irrelevant, since it may still be instrumentally useful in reducing suffering.

Options 1 and 2 prevent the same amount of intrinsic disvalue. Under a purely impersonal version of tranquilism, they therefore have the same moral significance. A person-affecting version might treat existing people differently from merely possible people, but that distinction is an additional commitment rather than something required by tranquilism itself.

The nonidentity problem does not arise here. If the policymaker refrains from creating the person who would suffer, there is no created individual who has been harmed by that decision. Therefore, the fact that no particular victim can be identified removes the supposed difficulty.

One case of severe suffering also takes lexical priority over any number of mild unfulfilled cravings. Since tranquilism recognizes only intrinsic disvalue and no positive value that could compensate for it, preventing the most intense suffering must always come first, regardless of how many mild cravings are involved.

Thus tranquilism gives policymakers a strong reason to prevent suffering, whether in existing or merely possible people, but no corresponding intrinsic reason to create happy lives.
