I am giving a forty-five-minute philosophy colloquium defending a position I call semantic emancipation: intentionality can be derived in its historical origin but later become original in its mode of operation. My central claim is that a representational state initially endowed with meaning by designers or users may cease merely to borrow that meaning if the system comes to maintain, test, and revise the state’s relation to the world as part of its own self-directed functioning. I use the Dennett-style two-bitser as a foil, not as decisive proof: designer history alone does not settle what a later state is about, but neither, pace Searle, should every artifact remain permanently trapped in merely derived intentionality.

My proposed criterion has three individually necessary and jointly sufficient conditions. First, self-maintenance: the representational state must help regulate the system’s own continued operation rather than merely serve an external interpreter’s purposes. Second, counterfactual coordination: the system must correct its behavior across relevant environmental variations because of the state’s accuracy or inaccuracy, not merely repeat a fixed designer-installed response. Third, endogenous revision: the system must be capable of altering the mapping between its internal states and environmental features when those mappings frustrate its own goals. I will explicitly deny that usefulness to observers, behavioral complexity, or conscious experience is by itself sufficient.

The audience will contain two Searle-influenced philosophers of mind who think original intentionality requires the right kind of biological causal powers; two Dennett-influenced naturalists who suspect that my distinction between original and derived content reifies what is only a difference of explanatory perspective; one philosopher of biology sympathetic to teleological accounts of function; and two researchers in cognitive science and artificial intelligence who will demand an operational distinction rather than a suggestive definition. Prepare a Q&A briefing for this audience, not a generic survey of the intentionality debate.

Give me ten objections I am most likely to receive, ordered by a combination of likelihood and potential damage. Phrase each as the relevant audience member would actually ask it, identifying which premise or condition it targets. For each objection, provide:

- a reply I could deliver in roughly ninety seconds;
- what I should concede immediately;
- what I should refuse to concede;
- the weakest point in my reply;
- the most probable follow-up question.

Include at least one objection that distinguishes biological self-maintenance from artifactual self-repair, one concerning whether endogenous revision merely transfers the derivative meaning to a later designer or training process, and one pressing me to say how the three conditions could be empirically identified without already assuming the system has goals. Also identify the question I should most hope nobody asks, explain why it is dangerous, and give the best available answer rather than an evasion.

For each prediction, state how confident you are that this particular audience would raise the objection in that form. Conclude with an overall confidence assessment and single out the objection whose likely wording, audience attribution, or force you are least certain about.
