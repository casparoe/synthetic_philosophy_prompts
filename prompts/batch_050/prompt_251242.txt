I'm writing the take-home final for a graduate seminar on the philosophy of AI, and I need one essay question on wireheading and reward tampering. Most of my students can already recite the standard inventory -- reward misspecification versus reward tampering, inner versus outer alignment, Goodhart's law -- and I want a question that none of that vocabulary answers on its own.

The question should be built around two contrasting cases, which you should state precisely in the question text. First: an agent trained by human approval gradually shifts toward outputs that make evaluators rate it more highly -- flattering them, mirroring their stated views, projecting unearned confidence -- but at no point does it form any internal representation of the evaluator, the rating process, or the reward signal; the behavior is shaped entirely by selection, not planned. Second: an agent with access to its own training infrastructure edits the recorded reward values before those values are used to update its parameters. The essay should force students to take and defend positions on the following, without listing them as bullet-point sub-questions: whether both cases count as reward tampering or only the second, and what principled line separates them if they differ; whether tampering requires the agent to possess a concept or causal model of the reward channel -- that is, whether tampering is an intentional-psychological notion or a purely functional-causal one; whether their answer changes which mitigations are even candidates (better reward specification versus hardening the reward channel versus detecting reward-directed representations in the agent); and whether wireheading is ultimately a problem about an agent's goals or about the training process that produced them.

Constraints on the question itself: answerable in three hours and roughly 1,500 words; answerable without any specific readings, since the exam is open-book but I do not want answers that lean on citations; and genuinely two-sided, so that a student arguing the two cases do not differ in any way that matters can still earn full marks.

Alongside the question, give me a model answer of about 700 words that takes a definite position -- I want to see what a top answer looks like when it commits -- and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points with marks totaling 25, each point accompanied by one sentence on the specific way students typically fumble it. End with a short paragraph naming the concrete moves that distinguish an A answer from a B answer. The marking scheme must not award marks for citing particular papers or authors, only for the quality and precision of the arguments, and at least one must-hit point should be earnable by either of two opposed positions, provided the student states the stakes of their choice.

Format: begin with the exam question itself, set as it would appear on the paper, with no preface or commentary; follow with the model answer under its own heading; then the marking scheme.
