I chair my university's animal research ethics committee. A computational neuroscience group has applied to us — under protest, since they doubt we have jurisdiction — for approval of a project that will run roughly 50,000 simultaneous copies of a recurrent neural agent in a simulated environment. Each copy is trained with an aversive reinforcement signal the researchers themselves describe as a functional analogue of chronic pain: it is persistent, resists habituation, and progressively reorganises the agent's behaviour towards passivity and withdrawal, which is the point, because the project models learned helplessness. The group argues the agents are matrices of floating-point numbers, that no defensible theory of sentience covers them, and that scrutiny here would set an absurd precedent for all machine learning. Two committee members disagree; one contends that even a small probability of morally relevant experience, multiplied across tens of thousands of copies over months of simulated time, warrants precaution. Draft the committee's decision memo: approve, approve with conditions, or decline jurisdiction while urging voluntary safeguards. The memo should recommend a concrete, actionable course and reason about which features of a computation could bear on its moral status — the aversive signal's functional role versus its mere labelling, recurrent integration versus feedforward pass-through, and whether copy count changes the moral calculus. It must also engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side of whatever it recommends. Please write in British English.
