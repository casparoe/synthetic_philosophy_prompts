Some backstory: I'm in a reading group on evidence law and the philosophy of proof (mostly law students, I'm the token philosophy person), and last week a friend presented an argument that's been nagging at me because the conclusion seems crazy but I can't locate the bad step. The setup is a gatecrasher-style case: a stadium holds 1,000 people, the organizers' records show only 400 bought tickets, and they sue one attendee picked at random for the admission price, offering no evidence about him specifically beyond the base rate. Preponderance of the evidence is just more-likely-than-not, so 60% clears it, yet everyone in the room felt a verdict for the organizers would be an outrage. Fine so far. But here's my friend's argument, as close to his version as I can reconstruct: (1) A verdict is justified whenever the evidence makes the claim more probable than the standard of proof requires. (2) All admissible evidence works the same single way — it shifts the probability of the claim. Eyewitnesses misremember at known rates, fingerprint examiners have measurable error rates, labs have contamination rates, so testimony is really just statistics we refuse to call statistics. (3) If that's right, the source of a probability can't matter to whether it justifies a verdict; only the number can. (4) Base rates like the 60% are frequently more reliable than a single eyewitness. (5) Therefore verdicts resting on naked statistics alone are justified whenever the number clears the threshold, and our revulsion at the stadium verdict is a cognitive bias to be trained away, not a datum to be respected.

Then came the twist that really broke the room. My friend argued that even people who insist evidence must be individualized can't hold that line, because naked statistics are always one short step away from becoming individualized: add second-order evidence — testimony from the stadium's data analyst that the turnstile counters are 99% accurate, maintenance logs, accreditation records — and now the base rate is embedded in ordinary, person-specific testimony about a measuring process. (6) If individualized evidence is just naked statistics plus enough second-order scaffolding, then the distinction everyone reached for in the stadium case does no real work, and any threshold of individualization you set is arbitrary. His conclusion: either accept statistical verdicts, or admit that much of forensic practice (which rests on exactly this scaffolding) is equally suspect. This feels like it proves too much — surely the scaffolding can't bootstrap a merely statistical case into an individualized one, or every naked statistic would come pre-laundered — but when I pressed him, I couldn't say where the bootstrapping goes wrong without sounding like I was just restating the intuition he attacked.

So: where does this go wrong, if it does? If forced to choose, I'd rather weaken (1) or (3) than accept verdicts on base rates alone, but I want to know what I'm paying for that — e.g., whether rejecting (3) commits me to saying a less reliable eyewitness can properly convict where a more reliable base rate can't, which also sounds bad. Please identify the specific premise or step you'd reject, explain why it looked innocent, and price the alternatives honestly. And whatever diagnosis you land on, say what would change your mind: what argument or case would convince you the revulsion really is just bias, and where is your own reasoning most likely to fail?
