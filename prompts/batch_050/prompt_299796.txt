So I just got off a three-hour argument with my friend about Frankfurt cases and moral responsibility, and honestly I'm more confused than when we started. Her position is basically that Frankfurt's cases settle the matter: you can be responsible without being able to do otherwise, the principle of alternative possibilities is dead, end of story. My pushback was the flicker-of-freedom thing — in every such case there's some prior sign the intervener is watching for, and the agent *could* have produced or withheld that sign, so an alternative possibility survives, just a tiny one; and if you redescribe the case finely enough to kill even that, the case stops being a fair test of responsibility. We also argued about a case she invented herself: someone who years ago, fully voluntarily and with clear foresight, had a chip implanted that will force the choice if they ever waver. She says responsibility is obviously preserved precisely because the mechanism is self-imposed; I said self-imposition changes the case so much it can't be evidence about responsibility in general, only about that weird agent.

Anyway, I realised we're both arguing about verdicts without any shared standard. So here's what I actually want, and answer as a philosopher who works on free will and responsibility, please, not a survey generator: before any verdict on Frankfurt cases, write the specification — the desiderata an adequate theory of moral responsibility must meet once Frankfurt-style cases are on the table. I mean the stuff like: which cases it has to get right (ordinary coercion, addiction, historical manipulation, my friend's chip case), which distinctions it cannot collapse (couldn't have done otherwise vs. wouldn't have done otherwise anyway; responsibility for the act vs. for its upshots; being excused on an occasion vs. being exempt as a person), which commitments it must stay consistent with or openly reject (ought implies can, the transfer-style arguments about consequences, determinism itself), and which intuitions it's permitted to sacrifice and at what price. For each desideratum, give the case or argument that motivates it, not just the label. Think carefully and brainstorm a generous candidate list first, then prune — I want to see what got cut and why.

A few specific demands. Flag any pairs of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied — my suspicion is that "takes Frankfurt cases at face value" and "preserves ought-implies-can" is such a pair, but tell me if I'm wrong. Say explicitly whether my friend's chip case adds a genuinely new desideratum (something like: a theory must handle responsibility where the constraint is self-installed) or whether it's a distraction that reduces to ordinary historical-manipulation worries. Same question for the flicker of freedom: does it force its own requirement, or is it answerable within a decent theory? And fix the purpose: the theory needs to be good enough to ground everyday blame and praise and, ideally, criminal responsibility — if some desiderata only matter for one of those, say which. Most importantly: be committed. Don't hand me a menu. Mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just the baggage of one camp (libertarians demanding sourcehood, say), and take a definite position on which single desideratum knocks out the most existing theories. If you think my flicker objection fails, say so plainly rather than hedging.
