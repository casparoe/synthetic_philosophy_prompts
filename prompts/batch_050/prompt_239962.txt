Earlier this year I was diagnosed with a progressive neurological condition. The proposed treatment is staged replacement: over several years, failing cortical tissue will be substituted, region by region, with synthetic tissue engineered to assume the exact functional role of what it replaces. Reading the consent materials for the first stage has made certain thought experiments considerably less abstract for me, and I have noticed that my gut verdicts form a pattern I cannot articulate. I would like the principle behind them, if there is one.

My verdicts: if my neurons are replaced one at a time over five years, each successor seamlessly taking over its predecessor's causal role, the person at the end is me. If my brain is instead scanned in an instant and destroyed, and a functionally identical emulation is then run on a computer, that emulation is not me, whatever it believes. If the scan is non-destructive and I go on living while the emulation runs, the emulation is plainly not me. If each hemisphere of my brain were transplanted into a separate body, I cannot say either resulting person is me, yet it does not feel like plain death either; my relation to both seems to matter in something like the way my own future matters. Ordinary deep anaesthesia, during which my brain does almost nothing, threatens none of this.

The case I keep circling: the first procedure compressed into a single afternoon — same replacements, same endpoint, no time for gradual integration. My verdict wavers, which makes me suspect my first two verdicts are in tension.

Is there a coherent principle here, and what does it say does the work — continuity, causal dependence, branching, speed? Please state it precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and report what it predicts for the compressed case and for one further case I have not raised.
