I've been a longtime reader of the rationalist blogs, and one position I absorbed early on -- through Dennett's writings and the various posts discussing his work -- is the view that the self is a center of narrative gravity: not a thing residing somewhere in the brain, but a useful abstraction, the "author of record" spun out of the stories an organism tells about itself, much the way a novel's protagonist is constituted by the text rather than standing behind it. On this view, asking where the self really is, or whether it survives this or that transformation, is a confusion on par with asking where the center of gravity of a novel is located. I still find this broadly convincing, but I've started to worry that I find it convincing partly because it's elastic -- it seems to absorb every apparent counterexample (split-brain patients, dissociation, the reported dissolution of self in deep meditation, the phenomenologists' claims about a pre-reflective minimal self that precedes all storytelling) by redescribing them as either more narrative or as cases where the narrative machinery is doing something unusual. Meanwhile, rival pictures -- self-models in the predictive processing tradition, or the "minimal phenomenal selfhood" people -- at least purport to make commitments about implementation that could turn out wrong.

So: what would count against the center-of-narrative-gravity view? Please map its exposure rather than just argue against it. I'd like the candidate defeaters sorted by how decisive they'd be -- distinguishing findings that would genuinely refute or badly damage the view from findings that would merely be awkward for it and invite a (perhaps legitimate) qualification. For each candidate defeater, say what it would have to look like to actually land: for instance, what would split-brain or meditation data have to show to be more than the view's defenders can accommodate, and what would a successful predictive-processing self-model have to explain that a narrative account cannot, rather than merely re-describe? Where possible, indicate what the view's own defenders would concede counts against it, not just what its critics say.

Finally, I want an honest verdict on the falsifiability question. If it turns out that every putative counterexample can be absorbed by loosening what "narrative" means, is that a sign that the view is a flexible and correct framework, or that it has been insulated into vacuity? And is unfalsifiability actually a fatal flaw for a view of this kind -- a piece of metaphilosophical bookkeeping about what the concept of self is for -- or does that category of view get judged by different standards than, say, a theory of selfhood meant to guide neuroscience? I'd find it most persuasive if the answer engaged seriously with what the narrative view predicts that its rivals don't, since I suspect the honest answer is "very little," and I'd like to know whether that should move me.
