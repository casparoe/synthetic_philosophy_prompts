I recently ended up in a weird rabbit hole about organ donation after a friend of mine registered as a donor and we got talking about how they actually decide someone is dead enough to take organs. Here's what I gathered, correct me if I've got any of it wrong: there are basically two routes. One is brain death, where the whole brain has irreversibly stopped functioning even if a ventilator keeps the heart going. The other is donation after circulatory death, where they withdraw life support, wait for the heart to stop, wait some extra minutes (like 2 to 5 depending on the hospital) with no circulation, and then declare death and start procurement. And there's this newer thing where, after declaring death by the circulatory standard, they can restart circulation inside the body with a machine to keep the organs fresh, but they clamp off the vessels to the brain so blood never reaches it.

The more I think about it the less I understand what concept of death this whole setup is actually running on. In the brain death case the standard seems to be that the function couldn't be restarted by any means, but in the circulatory case, two minutes after the heart stops, circulation absolutely could be restarted -- we just don't intend to. So "dead" there seems to mean something like "we've decided not to try" rather than "couldn't be brought back." And the perfusion thing makes it weirder: if death was the permanent cessation of circulation, and you then deliberately restore circulation to most of the body, in what sense is the person still dead? Apparently the answer is supposed to be that the brain is excluded, but then isn't that just admitting that brain function is the thing that actually mattered all along, in which case why did we wait for the heart at all?

So my question: what does the practice of organ procurement, taken as a whole (both standards plus the perfusion technique plus the rule that donors must be dead before you cut), presuppose about what death is? Is there any single coherent concept of death that licenses all three pieces, or does the practice quietly switch between a biological notion, a moral one (roughly: it's now permissible to treat this body as a source of organs), and a legal one depending on which case you're in? And if you think the circulatory standard secretly rests on the intent not to resuscitate rather than on the patient's state, does that mean death can depend on the decisions of the people standing around the bed -- and if so, is that actually a problem, or just a fact we've been politely ignoring?
