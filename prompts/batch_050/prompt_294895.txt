Consider a set of practices standard in large online games, which my studio is about to adopt as a package: (1) we permanently ban players who deceive others into handing over rare in-game items, treating the victim's loss as a genuine harm worth punishing; (2) our terms of service state that all in-game items are and remain our property, which we may delete, revalue, or wipe entirely at our sole discretion — including at server shutdown — with no compensation owed to players; and (3) we prohibit real-money trading of items and ban accounts that engage in it, on the grounds that it corrupts the game's economy. What conception of virtual objects and virtual harm does this package presuppose? In particular, does (1) presuppose that virtual possessions can be really stolen from someone, and if so, is that consistent with (2)'s insistence that nothing in a player's inventory is really theirs? Does (3) presuppose that virtual value is real enough to be corrupted but not real enough to be owned by the player who holds it? Reconstruct the implicit theory under which all three practices would be coherent, distinguish what the package strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and if it turns out inconsistent, identify the single revision a defender of the practice would find least costly. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether the package is consistent, and under what theory — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
