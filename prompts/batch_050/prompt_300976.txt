I work in data infrastructure and spend most of my time on event-sourced systems: append-only logs, snapshots, materialized views, replay, logical clocks, and the whole question of what a system should treat as the ground truth of what happened. I want the philosophical debate about the ontology of time translated into that vocabulary, because I suspect large parts of it already have counterparts in design decisions I make every week, and I want to find out whether I'm right about that or flattering myself.

Please take the main positions -- presentism (only the present exists), eternalism (past, present, and future all equally exist), and the growing-block view (past and present exist, the future does not) -- and restate each one as precisely as you can in terms I actually use: whether the system retains a complete event log or only the current snapshot; whether "now" is a cursor position over the log or a fact about which events have been committed; what replay and rollforward correspond to; what it would mean to query a future event. Where a position seems to correspond to a real architectural distinction, say so; where it seems to correspond to nothing -- where two "different" positions would produce byte-for-byte identical systems -- say that too.

Two specific translation problems I want addressed. First, McTaggart's argument that an event's being past, present, or future requires a second time-series in which those properties change, and so on to a regress: does this have a counterpart in the fact that a "current offset" pointer needs its own clock to be updated, and is the regress real or an artifact of confusing the log with the index? Second, the relativity of simultaneity: in distributed systems I know there's no global clock and that "happened-before" is a partial order, and I gather physics says something similar. Does that map cleanly onto the argument that presentism requires a privileged global "now" that physics doesn't supply, or is the analogy thinner than it looks?

Then the residue report. Which parts of the debate translate cleanly into storage and indexing decisions? Which parts translate only by being distorted -- by quietly turning a claim about what exists into a claim about what is retained, committed, or queryable? And is there anything left over that has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? I'm especially interested in the verdict: if the entire dispute between the eternalist and the growing-block theorist comes out as a difference in retention policy with no observable consequence for any query, should I conclude that my framework is missing the concept needed to state the disagreement, or that the disagreement was never about anything my concept of existence was too coarse to catch? Don't flatter the engineering vocabulary, and don't flatter the metaphysicians either.
