I have no formal training in philosophy, but for the past year I have been reading Spinoza's Ethics, in R. H. M. Elwes's translation, with a small reading group, a few pages a week. We are still in Part I, and the passage that has split the group is Definition VII:

"VII. That thing is called free, which exists solely by the necessity of its own nature, and of which the action is determined by itself alone. On the other hand, that thing is necessary, or rather constrained, which is determined by something external to itself to a fixed and definite method of existence or action."

We keep circling the same disagreements. Some of us read this as saying that freedom has nothing to do with open possibilities: everything that happens is fixed, and a thing is called free simply when its behavior flows from its own nature, which strictly speaking only God manages. Others insist the definition must leave room for human beings to become genuinely free later in the book, by coming to act from their own nature instead of being pushed from outside. A third camp says Spinoza is quietly distinguishing two sources of determination, from a thing's own nature or imposed on it, and that this distinction is not the same as the claim that everything is necessary in the strongest possible sense. And one member holds that the definition cannot be squared with what Spinoza says elsewhere about necessity, and that we should stop trying to reconcile them.

I do not want the dispute settled; I want it sorted. Please design a "which reading of this definition do you actually hold?" instrument for our group: a decision tree, or equivalently a quiz with a scoring key, built from eight to ten diagnostic questions, each with two or three answer options. Every question should be a concrete case or plain claim on which the readings genuinely come apart, phrased so that a member can answer without knowing any of the positions and without any modal vocabulary. The questions must include, at minimum: a die roll and what it would mean to say the result could have been different; the respondent's own decision that morning; whether the laws of nature themselves could have been otherwise; and an alternative that was never realized, such as a trip the respondent considered but never took. The required leaves are the four readings described above, but if the logical space supports additional coherent positions, add them as leaves too.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from my list: first set out the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the readings in that space, and for every unoccupied combination say briefly whether no one holds it because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. If two of the readings cannot be separated by any set of questions of this kind, say so plainly rather than papering over it. Finally, for each leaf, include a short paragraph showing how that reading construes the definition itself, and in particular what it makes of Spinoza's self-correction from "necessary" to "or rather constrained": why does he amend his own wording, and what work does that amendment do on this reading? The whole instrument, including the scoring key and the per-leaf readings of the passage, should run to no more than about twelve hundred words, since I plan to print it for the group.
