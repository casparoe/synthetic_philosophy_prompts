So I'm a novelist — two books out, decent reviews, small sales — and I've started the one I've been circling for years. It's set in a regional hospital in the week after some mass-casualty event (haven't settled what exactly), and the spine of it is an intensivist and a hospital ethics guy making allocation calls while the supply situation keeps degrading. Ventilators, ECMO beds, ICU space, eventually staff attention itself.

Here's the thing. I know the pop version of this material cold — the last ventilator, the young mom versus the old guy, the doctor who goes rogue. TV has strip-mined that seam. What I want from you is help figuring out which open questions in triage ethics — QALYs, life-years vs. lives, the value of a statistical life, age weighting, worst-off priority, the rule of rescue pulling against all of it, whatever else belongs on the list — are actually still live and unresolved, and which two or three of those would carry a novel best. And I want the ranking defended, not just listed.

My criteria, in rough order: (1) it has to be genuinely unsettled, so my characters can be smart and decent and still land in different places — if a philosopher would say "oh we basically know the answer there, it's just politically radioactive," I want to know that; (2) it has to generate conflict between people rather than just inside one person's head at 3am, because interiority alone won't hold 300 pages; (3) bonus points if it's a question readers *think* they have settled intuitions about until the specifics pile up — that's where fiction does its thing. Feel free to tell me my criteria are wrong or in the wrong order, but tell me why.

Also: which of the famous dilemmas should I avoid because they're either philosophically dead or dramatically exhausted? I have my suspicions about the single-ventilator-two-patients setup but I'd rather hear yours.

One constraint — this is a novel, not a dissertation, so no reading lists or "as So-and-so argued in 1994." Just the reasoning, in plain language I can hand to a character.
