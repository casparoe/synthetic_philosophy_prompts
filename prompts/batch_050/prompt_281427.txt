I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a fertility clinic, and we've got a case that's spliting us down the middle. You're a philospher who consults on practical ethics -- please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before our next meeting, recommending a concrete decision. Think this through carefully, and brainstorm the strongest argument on each side before you settle on a bottom line; I don't want either position strawmanned.

Here's the case. A patient is about to start an IVF cycle. She's finishing a course of medication that, while it's still in her system, reliably causes a congenital condition in any embryo conceived then -- not a catastrophic one: the child would have lifelong but managable chronic pain, and everyone involved agrees the resulting life would be well worth living. The drug clears the body completely in about four months, and embryos created after that carry no elevated risk. She wants to proceed now rather than wait: her insurance covers only this cycle, her age makes delay costly, and she says she's waited long enough already. Her partner supports proceeding.

The committee's fight is about a strange asymmetry. Half of us say: proceed, because refusal can't be justified by appeal to the child's welfare -- any child born from this cycle couldn't have existed without the condition, since waiting would produce a genetically different child. Nobody is made worse off than they otherwise would have been, so the only real value left on the table is the patient's reproductive autonomy, which counsels proceeding. The other half has a stubborn intuition that knowingly bringing about a worse-off child when a different, healthier child was available at roughly the same cost is simply worse, full stop, and that the clinic shouldn't be complicit in it even if no particular person is harmed. Some of them also worry what it does to our standards if we knowingly go along.

The opinion should: (1) recommend proceed / refuse / proceed with conditions, specific enough that the clinic could actually act on it; (2) say whether the "no one is harmed" argument really settles the matter, or whether there's a coherent way to defend the refusal side without pretending this particular child has been wronged; (3) take the autonomy argument seriously rather than waving at it; and (4) state what would change the recommendation -- e.g., roughly how bad the condition would have to get before the analysis flips, since that tells us which principle is actually doing the work. Keep it under 800 words.
