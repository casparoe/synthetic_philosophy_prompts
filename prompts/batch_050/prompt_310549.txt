I teach an upper-level undergrad seminar on decision theory and I'm writing the final exam (2 hours, essay section worth 40 marks total, this question worth 25). Every year half the class writes some version of "intransitive preferences are irrational because you'd get money pumped" and stops there, which shows me they've memorized a slogan. I want one essay question that makes that answer insufficient.

Here's the setup I'd like baked into the question itself, so students dont need to have memorized any particular paper: an agent strictly prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A; she's offered B for free, then offered the chance to pay a penny to swap B for C, then a penny to swap C for A, then a penny to swap A for B, and so on round and round. The twist I want students to wrestle with: suppose she knows the full sequence of offers in advance before any of them are made. I want the question to force them to explain (a) what hidden assumptions the standard money pump argument actually needs beyond cyclic preferences — something about how she chooses at each node and whether her preferences stay fixed over time, (b) whether an agent who can foresee the whole sequence and plan accordingly is genuinely pumpable, or whether the argument only works against a certain kind of chooser, and (c) whether "you can be turned into a money pump" even establishes irrationality, as opposed to just showing you're exploitable or imprudent — i.e. what kind of normativity does a sure-loss argument actually deliver.

Please give me:

1. The question text as it would appear on the exam, including the scenario, worded so that an answer that just recites the textbook money pump clearly doesn't answer it.
2. A model answer of around 600-700 words that a top student could plausibly write in the time available (so not requiring anything we didn't cover — it should lean on the scenario itself plus general reasoning, not on citing literature).
3. A marking scheme out of 25 that breaks the marks down by specific moves, and explicitly names what separates an A answer from a B answer — e.g., the A answer notices the argument assumes the agent chooses myopically node-by-node rather than planning over the whole sequence, while a B answer describes the pump correctly but never questions the setup. Also list 3-4 common mistakes that should cost marks, like conflating being exploitable with being irrational, or claiming the pump proves transitivity is a requirement of rationality without saying what would go wrong for the foresightful agent.
