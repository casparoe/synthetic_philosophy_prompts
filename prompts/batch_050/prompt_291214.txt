Write a parody of a chapter from a self-help book — the kind organized as numbered principles, each with a bolded imperative heading followed by an earnest paragraph — titled something like "The Possible You: A Counterpart-Theoretic Path to Peace with Your Regrets." The author is a sincere, uncritical convert to David Lewis's modal realism with counterpart theory, and the chapter offers therapy to readers tormented by counterfactuals: the job not taken, the lottery almost won, the person not married. The core therapeutic move is that these are not the reader's successes and failures at all. The lottery winner in the nearby world is somebody else — someone who merely resembles the reader — so envying him is a category error, on a par with envying a stranger who happens to share your haircut. The author never doubts any of this for a moment.

The comedy must come from getting the metaphysics exactly right rather than wrong, so that a metaphysician laughs instead of winces. Among the moves available to you: the fact that nothing literally exists in more than one world, so there is no single person who both almost-won and won; the fact that the counterpart relation is a matter of resemblance and shifts with context and the subject's interests, so that which possible person "you might have been" is partly up to how the question is asked — a point the author should present, deadpan, as a feature of the therapy (essence is cheap, and a good therapist helps you choose the resemblance standard that heals); the fact that the other worlds are concrete and spatiotemporally and causally isolated from ours, so no reassurance from them can ever actually arrive; and the standard complaint that a person who narrowly lost an election or a lottery is not consoled by being told that some lookalike elsewhere prospered — he cares about whether he himself might have won — which the author should reproduce in the voice of a skeptical reader and then rebut with complete technical accuracy and total therapeutic uselessness.

Keep the irony deadpan and legible: the reader should be able to tell that the satire's target is the way counterpart theory answers modal questions by redescribing them, and the charge that this changes the subject — but the author of the chapter must never wink, hedge, or break character.

Format: exactly five numbered principles, each consisting of a bolded imperative heading and one paragraph of roughly 60–90 words, with no introduction, conclusion, or framing material; about 450 words in total.
