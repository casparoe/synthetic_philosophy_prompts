I am preparing a short position statement for a graduate reading group on decision theory, and I would like an internal consistency audit before I circulate it. The topic is expected value fanaticism: roughly, the thesis that the rationality of maximizing expected value is insensitive to how small the relevant probabilities are, so that a minuscule probability of an enormous payoff can rationally dominate a decision just as a large probability of a modest one can.

Here is where I currently stand:

(a) Fanaticism is true. There is no probability threshold below which stakes cease to matter; an agent who disregards a prospect merely because its probability is sufficiently small is making a mistake of rationality.

(b) In Pascal's mugging, the pedestrian should refuse to hand over the wallet. The mugger's promise of astronomically large payoffs does not rationally compel compliance, and any decision theory that says otherwise is thereby refuted.

(c) Fanaticism is not a pathology of expected value theory but its chief virtue: it is precisely what lets the theory explain why minuscule probabilities of existential catastrophe can legitimately dominate institutional priorities, in a way that intuition on reflection respects.

(d) Utility is not bounded, or at any rate we should not impose a bound merely to make decision theory behave. If unbounded utility generates strange results, we should locate and fix the problem elsewhere rather than legislate the strangeness away.

(e) A correct normative decision theory must be approximately implementable by bounded agents. It cannot demand that agents survey arbitrarily improbable payoff-relevant hypotheses before acting, since no finite mind can do that, and a theory that cannot in principle be followed is not a norm for anyone.

(f) In ordinary life it is often perfectly rational to simply ignore low-probability risks (meteor strikes, bridge collapses) without performing any expected value calculation whatsoever.

My question: does this set hang together? For each pair of commitments that is genuinely in tension, state the tension precisely, say how severe it is, and identify the cheapest revision that would resolve it. For pairs that only appear to be in tension, explain why the appearance is misleading. Rank the genuine tensions from most to least severe, and conclude by telling me which single revision would clean up the most and what it would cost me. I have no special attachment to any one commitment, so please do not spare any of them.
