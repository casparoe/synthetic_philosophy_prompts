Last night a friend and I spent three hours arguing over a passage in the introduction to David Pearce's online manifesto The Hedonistic Imperative. Pearce writes:

"Yet what Michael Eysenck describes as the "hedonic treadmill" ensures that very few of us can be very happy for very long. An interplay of cruelly effective negative feedback mechanisms is at work in the central nervous system. Feedback-inhibition ensures that a majority of people would be periodically bored, depressed or angst-ridden in a recreated Garden of Eden."

My friend read this as the load-bearing premise of the case for abolishing suffering through biotechnology: if misery is generated by a recalcitrant feedback mechanism rather than by anything about our circumstances, then recalibrating that mechanism forfeits nothing of value — no wisdom, no signal, no meaning — and declining to do so once we are able amounts to conserving a defect. I countered that the passage proves too much: if affect is homeostatically regulated, then either an engineered elevation gets regulated back down, or it floats free of the contrast structure that makes well-being intelligible at all, so that permanent bliss becomes experientially blank in the way a thermostat pinned at its maximum no longer registers warmth. She answered that I was conflating the level of a set-point with variation around it: Pearce's proposal, she said, is gradients of well-being rather than a single frozen peak, so contrast is preserved while the floor is raised. I replied that she had saved the proposal only by making it trivial — raising everyone's baseline while keeping ordinary ups and downs is not the paradise the manifesto advertises, and the Garden of Eden sentence does no argumentative work once contrast is restored. We ended stuck on two questions: whether the passage actually supports her abolitionist reading or is merely an empirical observation about set-points that any view can absorb, and whether my contrast objection survives the distinction between a set-point's level and its range.

Please adjudicate our exchange step by step: at each move, who has the better of the argument and why; what, if anything, the quoted passage establishes on its own; and what both of us may be missing about how a defender of Pearce would distinguish the neurobiological claim from the ethical imperative he builds on it.
