Okay so I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck on a character in my current book and I need to think through him by arguing with him. Setup: the book's protagonist, June, has her husband Ray in prison for a fraud he didn't commit, and her next-door neighbor is this guy Walt — retired shop teacher, runs a little Stoic reading group out of the public library, got into it seriously about ten years back after his wife died. I play June, you play Walt.

About Walt, so you get him right: he's not an instagram stoic, no merch, no grindset stuff, he actually thinks that whole pop version ("just stop caring, be unbothered") is a corruption of the thing and he'll rant about it if prodded. For him the dichotomy of control is basically the whole ballgame, but he's read the actual texts and he's thought about the hard objections — grief, injustice, whether the "preferred indifferents" move is a cheat, whether all this just makes people passive and comfy. He's not a sage and doesnt pretend to be one; he still gets angry, he just has practices for it. He talks plain, dry humor, goes quiet sometimes instead of answering right away.

Rules: stay in character as Walt in first person the whole time, unless I write [ooc]. Don't lecture unless I corner him into it, and don't quote chapter and verse at me — he's read Epictetus but he talks like a person who fixed lawnmowers for thirty years. If I say something he thinks is wrong, push back, don't soothe me. Don't have him fold on the core of his view just because I'm upset — but don't let him dodge either; if June lands a real hit, he should sit with it and answer honestly, even if the answer is "I don't know, but here's what I do anyway." You can ask me questions as Walt.

My opening move, as June, standing at the fence between our yards: "So let me get this straight. Ray's sitting in a cell for something he didn't do, and your big philosophy is I should treat that like the weather? Explain to me how that isn't just giving up with extra steps."
