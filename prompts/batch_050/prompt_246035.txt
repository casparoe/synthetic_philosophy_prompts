I'm in a normative ethics seminar, and last week our discussion of constraints got stuck on what the professor called the paradox of deontology: if it's wrong to violate a constraint, why isn't it right -- or even required -- to violate it once when doing so would prevent several violations of that same constraint by others? I've been stress-testing my own intuitions on cases, and I'd like to know whether there's a coherent principle underneath them, or whether I have to give some up. Here are my verdicts. Lying to the murderer at the door is the one case where I depart from the constraint: lying to him seems clearly fine, even obligatory. But lying to a grand jury in a way that would discredit three other witnesses' perjured testimony and thereby reduce the total number of successful lies in the proceedings -- that I think is not permitted, even though it minimizes lying. Torturing one suspect to stop a plot in which five other people will be tortured: not permitted. Breaking my promise to a friend in order to prevent three strangers from breaking their promises (say, by withholding information that would tempt them): not permitted, and honestly the suggestion feels faintly absurd. Yet here's the one I can't square with the rest: shoving a passerby hard enough to bruise his arm, in order to knock him clear of falling scaffolding that would have broken the arms of two people beside him, seems plainly permitted -- and that's also a case of inflicting a harm to minimize that very same kind of harm.

So my pattern is: constraints against lying, torturing, and promise-breaking hold even against minimizing those very violations, but the constraint against injuring apparently doesn't. I've considered that the difference might be the size of the gap between harm inflicted and harm prevented, or whether the victim is used as a means versus merely affected, or whether the wrong involves treating someone's agency as a thing to be manipulated rather than their body as an object in the causal path -- but each of these seems to either over- or under-generate when I test it. Is there a single precisely stateable principle, or a small family of them, that captures all of my verdicts, with the motivating case attached to each clause? If not, tell me honestly which verdict has to go and why it deserves to. And whichever way you go, tell me what the resulting account says about two cases I keep circling: briefly imprisoning one innocent person when a mob will otherwise lynch five innocents, and censoring one dissident newspaper when refusing to do so will result, through a predictable chain of events, in a regime that censors every newspaper in the country.
