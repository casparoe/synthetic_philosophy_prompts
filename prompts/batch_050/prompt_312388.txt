I am a novelist at work on a book about a man in a small town who is publicly shamed for a betrayal and who, over the course of years, becomes genuinely better. Before I commit to the book's architecture, I want to know whether the moral psychology I have been assuming is coherent. My working commitments: (1) guilt is the morally superior emotion — it attaches to what one did and motivates confession and repair, whereas shame attaches to how one is seen and motivates concealment and the management of appearances; (2) in my story it is the town's shaming, not any private pang of conscience, that sets the reform in motion, and I want that to be psychologically credible rather than a novelist's shortcut; (3) the reform must be genuine on the page — by the end the character acts for the right reasons, not merely handles his reputation more skillfully; (4) a culture cannot simply decide whether to be a guilt culture or a shame culture, since that balance is a deep inheritance rather than a policy choice; (5) the novel is set in a community that, after a collective trauma one generation back, deliberately re-educated itself away from its old honor practices. Are these five jointly tenable? Tell me which tensions are real and which merely apparent, ordered by severity, and for each real one, which commitment I would have to surrender and what that would cost the book.
