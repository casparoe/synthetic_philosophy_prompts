Last week, over the course of a very long evening, a friend and I reread W. E. B. Du Bois's essay "Criteria of Negro Art" (delivered at the 1926 NAACP celebration honoring Carter G. Woodson and published in The Crisis that October) for a graduate seminar we both sit in on, and we ended up arguing past each other for hours. My friend reads the essay the way it is usually anthologized: as a blunt declaration that art should serve the cause of racial justice, full stop, and she files Du Bois under whatever the aesthetics textbooks call the position that moral and political merit is artistic merit. I kept objecting that this reading flattens the essay, and I pointed her to a passage she had skimmed, where Du Bois has just listed four beautiful things he remembers — Cologne Cathedral, a West African village, the Venus de Milo, a snatch of song in the American South — and then writes:

"Such is Beauty. Its variety is infinite, its possibility is endless. In normal life all may have it and have it yet again. The world is full of it; and yet today the mass of human beings are choked away from it, and their lives distorted and made ugly. This is not only wrong, it is silly. Who shall right this well-nigh universal failing? Who shall let this world be beautiful? Who shall restore to men the glory of sunsets and the peace of quiet sleep?"

Whatever else this is, I argued, it is not a claim about the content of artworks. It is a claim about the distribution of beauty: that access to beauty and to the making of it is a good that can be justly or unjustly distributed, and that a society can be condemned for choking people off from it even if every artwork in it is, by formalist lights, impeccable. My friend countered that I was inventing a position the history of aesthetics has no drawer for, and that the passage is just rhetorical throat-clearing before the propaganda declaration later in the essay. We agreed to settle it, or at least to clarify it, by mapping the actual space of positions.

So: please build me a decision tree, suitable as a seminar handout, that sorts the positions on the question this passage presses — what, if anything, does beauty have to do with justice? — into named views. Start the tree from the most permissive commitment the passage suggests (roughly: beauty matters enough that its absence from people's lives can be a wrong, not merely a pity) and let each branch be a genuine diagnostic question a student could answer without knowing which label it leads to. The tree must contain at least one branch separating positions by whether the political bearing of art runs through the content and message of works (what artworks say and do) or through the social arrangements around them (who gets to make beauty, who gets to see it, whose lives are made ugly). Seven to ten leaves is about right. The leaves must include: art-for-art's-sake aestheticism of the Wilde and Whistler sort; Clive Bell-style formalism; Tolstoy's view that art is justified by the moral and religious communion it effects; a Marxist or social-production view of art; the propaganda position Du Bois himself declares later in this same essay; and at least one leaf for the distribution-of-beauty reading I am defending, whether or not it turns out to coincide with his propaganda position. For each leaf, give the name, one sentence stating its central claim, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where the space of possible answers contains combinations nobody seems to occupy, note them and say briefly whether that is because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored; and where two named positions cannot actually be separated by the questions on this tree (I suspect aestheticism and formalism may collapse into one another here, since neither cares about distribution), flag that explicitly rather than forcing them apart. Finally, use the tree to adjudicate our argument: does Du Bois, in the passage quoted, occupy the same leaf as the author of the essay's famous propaganda declaration, or has he walked to a different one — and which reading better explains why he calls the choking-off of beauty not only wrong but silly?
