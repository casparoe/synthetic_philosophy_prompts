For an advanced undergraduate epistemology seminar, design a decision-tree classifier for the debate over pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism. The tree must sort a participant by what they believe rather than by labels they already know, and it must be built from the logical space first: diagnostic questions, then named positions placed at the leaves, then any empty or unstable regions noted. Do not provide citations, readings, or bibliographic gestures; define every case inside the prompt and rely only on widely known positions.

Use these starting materials. Case A, Low Deposit: Maya has the same ordinary evidence that the bank will be open Saturday as she has had many weeks, nothing is riding on it, and she says she knows it will be open. Case B, Ruinous Deposit: the evidence is unchanged, but if the bank is closed Maya misses a payment that triggers bankruptcy; she says she had better check even though she still thinks it probably will be open. Case C, Surgical Consent: a nurse has excellent but not infallible evidence that a vial contains the approved drug; a mistaken belief would kill the patient, while a needless delay would merely be costly. Case D, Attributor Shift: two speakers describe the same agent with the same evidence and stakes; one speaker is in a casual conversation and calls the belief knowledge, while the other is in a courtroom review and refuses the word knowledge. Case E, Reversed Stakes: the subject is personally indifferent to the outcome, but the audience evaluating the claim faces catastrophic loss if the subject is wrong.

Build the tree around at least these fault lines, adding others only if they earn their keep: whether practical facts about the subject can make the very same belief cease to be knowledge; whether variation is located in the truth of the subject’s knowledge state, in the semantics of knowledge attribution, or only in norms of assertion and action; whether high stakes merely raise a threshold or can change the epistemic relation itself; whether stakes can ever help constitute knowledge rather than only defeat it; whether merely salient error possibilities matter even when no one would act on them; whether the evaluator’s practical situation can affect the truth of an attribution about a practically indifferent subject; and whether apparently conflicting verdicts in Cases A through E should be reconciled as both true, one true and one loose talk, or true only relative to different standards. Each node must be phrased as a concrete question answerable before encountering names such as contextualism, invariantism, subject-sensitive invariantism, relativism, encroachment, or norms of assertion. Limit the instrument to at most twelve nodes and six to eight leaves.

The required leaves are: strict intellectualist invariantism; stakes-sensitive invariantism in which practical factors affect knowledge itself; a stronger constitutive encroachment view in which knowledge is partly fixed by what the subject may rationally do; epistemic contextualism centered on the attributor’s conversational context; assessment-sensitive relativism; a deflationary view that keeps knowledge invariant and explains the data through assertion or action norms; and at least one skeptical or high-standards leaf if your logic permits it. Flag explicitly any leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, especially contextualism versus relativism using only subject-side cases, and deflationary assertion-norm views versus contextualism in single-speaker cases. For every logically available combination with no occupant, say whether it is empty because incoherent, costly but coherent, or simply underexplored.

Format the answer with these sections in this order: Design Constraints, about 120 words; Diagnostic Questions, a numbered list of at most twelve items; Decision Tree, an indented flowchart no longer than 500 words; Leaf Key, one short paragraph per occupied position; Empty Regions and Indistinguishable Pairs, about 250 words; Confidence and Revision Triggers, about 180 words. In the final section, state your confidence in the tree’s main cuts, identify the single distinction most likely to be doing too much work, and say exactly what result or argument would change your verdict: for example, a stable intuitive verdict in Case E, a linguistic pattern your tree predicts but does not find, or a case where stakes appear to create rather than defeat knowledge.
