I'm prepping a seminar on the ethics of voting and I keep going around in circles with my own intuitions, so let me just dump them on you and see if there's a principle in there. Verdict one: I think there's something morally fine, even admirable, about staying home in a safe-seat election where your candidate will win or lose by tens of thousands regardless -- I really don't buy that you've done anything wrong. Verdict two: I think voting third-party in a genuinely close swing-state race between a bad candidate and a catastrophic one is blameworthy, even if the third-party candidate is the one whose platform you actually endorse. Verdict three: I think it's fine to vote *against* a candidate you despise rather than *for* the opponent -- negative votes don't bother me at all, and I don't think you need to be able to sincerely say anything good about the person you're electing. Verdict four (and here's where it gets sticky): I think someone who refuses to vote at all in that same close race, on the grounds that both candidates are below some moral floor, is doing something defensible and maybe even honorable -- but I can't for the life of me square that with verdict two, since the third-party voter arguably does *more* to stop the catastrophe than the abstainer. Verdict five: I think a celebrity using their platform to tell millions of people to vote third-party in that same election is much worse than the individual third-party voter, even though the celebrity's own single vote does nothing special.

So here's what I want, in exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no intro or outro: (1) the best single principle you can construct that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to new cases; (2) which verdict the principle has to sacrifice, and why that's the right sacrafice rather than a different one; (3) what the principle predicts for a case I haven't mentioned -- say, voting in a primary rather than a general election; (4) whether the difference between verdicts two and four can be saved by appeal to expression (the vote as a statement of one's values rather than a causal contribution), or whether that move collapses under its own weight; (5) the strongest objection to your own principle, stated the way a sharp grad student would put it in seminar.
