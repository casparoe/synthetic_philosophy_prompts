You are a scholar of classical Chinese philosophy preparing teaching materials. I'm designing a handout for a graduate seminar on death in the Zhuangzi, and I need a decision tree that sorts the main interpretive positions on the text's attitude toward death by what readers actually claim about the passages, not by which scholarly label they favor. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of interpreters: start from the shared commitment that the text treats death as nothing to be feared, then branch on the questions that genuinely divide readings -- whether Zhuangzi claims to know what death is or pointedly suspends judgment (as in the skull episode and the passage about not knowing whether life is a delusion and death a homecoming); whether the equanimity on display rests on a metaphysics of qi-transformation or on epistemic modesty alone; whether anything that matters about a person persists through death or whether the individual simply dissolves back into the process; and whether grief is an outright error to be eliminated or a natural stage that comes and goes. Each branch node should be phrased as a question answerable by appeal to concrete episodes -- the drumming on the tub after his wife's death, Master Lai's illness, Zhuangzi's refusal of an elaborate burial -- without the reader already knowing which interpretation it leads to. Leaves should include at least the cosmic-transformation reading, the agnostic or skeptic reading, the purely therapeutic reading on which talk of transformation is a device for loosening attachment rather than doctrine, and a religious-continuity reading; note any unoccupied combination of answers and whether it is empty because incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate. Before drafting, brainstorm the full set of branching questions and the combinations they generate, then locate the positions in that space. Give the tree in continuous prose only -- no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables -- walking through it node by node the way you'd explain a flowchart in a letter, and keep it under 900 words.
