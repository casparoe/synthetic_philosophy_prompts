I run a small research program — three researchers, roughly three years of funding — on the problem of specifying "low impact" for increasingly autonomous AI systems without presupposing a complete specification of human values. Our target is concrete: methods a capable lab could plausibly adopt within five years that would bound the unwanted side effects of goal-directed agents acting in open-ended environments. We are deciding how to divide our effort among the live lines of work, and I would like a defended allocation, not a survey.

The lines of work we are weighing:

1. Baseline design. Impact is always measured relative to a counterfactual — do-nothing baselines, stepwise inaction baselines, initial-state baselines, or baselines derived from an imitation of a human policy. Open problems include interference incentives (an agent that manipulates what the baseline would have been) and the fact that "do nothing" is not itself a neutral action in a changing world.

2. Penalty-based impact measures. Attainable-utility-style preservation, relative reachability, empowerment-related measures, and their known failure modes: offsetting (reversing one's own side effects in ways that score as low impact but are themselves costly), perverse incentives toward freezing the environment, and the objection that any such measure smuggles in an implicit value function, so "low impact" may just be misspecified value with extra steps.

3. Learned side-effect judgment. Training models on human assessments of whether an outcome constituted an unwanted side effect, and the generalization problem: judgments calibrated on household-robot episodes may not transfer to agents making large, novel interventions.

4. Whitelisting and safe-region methods. Rather than penalizing deviation in general, characterize in advance the kinds of change the agent is permitted to make, and refuse everything else. Obvious cost: brittleness and capability loss; open question: whether the whitelist itself can be maintained and extended safely as the agent's competence grows.

5. Structural approaches. Constraining the agent's causal channels of influence — limiting which variables it can affect, how much it can change per unit time, or requiring reversibility — rather than measuring impact after the fact.

Please do the following. Rank these five lines of work for a group of our size and our stated goal, and defend the ordering explicitly: state the criteria you are using (importance for the goal, tractability for three people, neglectedness relative to the wider field, and how many other open questions depend on progress here), note where the criteria conflict and how you resolve the conflict, and identify the single crux — a claim about how the world or the problem is — on which your top ranking most depends, such that if it were false your ordering would change substantially. Then, for your top two lines only, name the one open question within each that you consider most load-bearing, and say what a competent skeptic's strongest objection to that line is.

Keep the table terse; the prose is where the argument should live.
