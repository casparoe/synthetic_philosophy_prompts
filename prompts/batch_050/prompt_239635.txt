I work on the safety case for autonomous warehouse robots (the kind that share aisles with human pickers), and I keep running into trolley-problem talk from the compliance people, so I finally went and read some of the actual philosophy. The doctrine of double effect keeps coming up, and apparently it goes back to Aquinas. In the Summa Theologica (II-II, q. 64, a. 7) he writes: "Nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is beside the intention. … Accordingly, the act of self-defense may have two effects: one, the saving of one's life; the other, the slaying of the aggressor." And then he adds the proportionality bit: "And yet, though proceeding from a good intention, an act may be rendered unlawful if it be out of proportion to the end."

Here's my ask. I'm fluent in a very different vocabulary: policies, reward terms, constrained RL with Lagrangian penalties, causal DAGs, do-operators, counterfactuals, the "avoiding negative side effects" literature in AI safety. Restate what Aquinas is saying in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can. My suspicion is that some of it translates cleanly -- the proportionality clause looks like it's just a constraint or a cost term that has to stay below some weight, and the means/side-effect distinction looks like it might be recoverable as something structural in the graph, like whether the good outcome still happens under a do() that blocks the harm, i.e. whether the harm is a causal intermediary for the good or just a downstream sibling. But "intended" vs "beside the intention" is where I get stuck, because in my world everything the policy reliably produces is just... predicted outcomes of the action, and whether something is "intended" seems to be either trivially read off the reward function (in which case the doctrine collapses into "just don't put harm in the objective," which can't be right, since that would license anything you didn't bother to penalize) or it's about the agent's internal goal representation in a way that has no analogue for a learned policy at all.

So give me the translation, and then the residue report: which parts of the passage survive into causal-model-and-policy vocabulary, which parts you had to fudge or quietly replace with something else, and which parts have no expression there at all. And then the verdict I actually care about: where there's residue, is that because my engineering vocabulary is missing something a moral theory needs (and if so, what would we have to add to it?), or because the intended/foreseen distinction is doing less real work than the philosophers think? Bonus points if you apply the whole thing to one concrete case -- say, a robot that can only stop a falling rack from crushing one worker by shoving it so it injures a different worker, vs. a robot that accepts a foreseen collision risk to an bystander as a side effect of a faster route.
