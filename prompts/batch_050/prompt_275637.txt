I'm a high school junior, and for my English class's satire unit I'm writing a parody piece after we discussed the Daoist idea of wu-wei, effortless action, and the puzzle of whether you can deliberately try to stop trying. My inspiration is an episode from the Zhuangzi, in the chapter usually called The Great and Most Venerable Teacher (chapter 6 of the Inner Chapters). In that episode, Yan Hui keeps reporting back to Confucius that he is making progress: first he has forgotten benevolence and righteousness, then he has forgotten rites and music, and finally he announces that he can simply sit and forget — his body and limbs sloughed off, his hearing and sight abandoned, parted from knowledge and merged with the great thoroughfare. Confucius, startled, ends up asking to become Yan Hui's follower. (I'm paraphrasing rather than quoting the translation, since I want to leave you free to structure the piece yourself.)

Here is my assignment for you: write the landing page and FAQ, roughly 650 words, for a fictional premium online self-improvement program called The Sitting-and-Forgetting Method (trademark pending), which has turned Yan Hui's three reports into a three-tier subscription curriculum. The piece should read like a real Silicon Valley wellness startup page, deadpan corporate copy throughout, aimed at overworked professionals who want to achieve effortlessness on a schedule.

A few requirements, because I want the satire to be smart and not just silly:

1. Structure the pricing tiers around the actual stages from the passage: a Basic plan for forgetting benevolence and righteousness, a Pro plan for forgetting rites and music, and an Executive tier for full sitting-and-forgetting, with a waitlist. The sales copy should treat each successive stage as a measurable deliverable with milestones, progress tracking, and a certificate.

2. Every feature you invent should contradict the product's own promise in a way that is visible to the reader but invisible to the copywriter. The humor must come from the real paradox of trying not to try — gamified streaks for non-striving, KPIs for letting go, a certified instructor lineage claiming direct descent from Yan Hui — not from random absurdity. Don't exaggerate wu-wei into something it isn't; the point is that the marketing logic collapses under the concept it sells.

3. Include a short FAQ section where the support team handles customer complaints, and let at least two answers grapple with genuine philosophical trouble: for example, a customer who worked very hard at the course and got worse at spontaneity, or a customer who asks how the company can certify that he has forgotten something he is still filling out forms about. The answers should stay in corporate voice and must never resolve the paradox — they should route around it with policy language, which is where the irony lives.

4. End with a testimonial. Given how the Zhuangzi passage ends, I would like the final testimonial to be from a distinguished customer, an eminent teacher with thousands of followers, who asks to enroll as a student — and the copy should present this as the company's proudest endorsement without noticing what it implies about the product.

5. Stay deadpan the whole way through: no winking at the reader, no asides, no jokes flagged as jokes. A reader who knows the Zhuangzi passage should be able to tell exactly what is being satirized (the commodification of wu-wei, and the attempt to schedule spontaneity) purely from how faithfully the copy follows the passage's logic to conclusions it cannot survive.

Please don't explain the satire before or after the piece — just deliver the landing page as if it were real.
