For an article I am writing on Locke's memory criterion of personal identity, I have been cataloguing the objections to it, together with the objections that survive the standard repair. Some terminology, so we mean the same thing: by the memory criterion I mean the view, standardly attributed to Locke, that a person existing at one time is identical with a person existing at an earlier time just in case the later person can remember an experience had by the earlier one. By the standard repair I mean the familiar move of replacing direct memory connections with overlapping chains of such connections — psychological continuity rather than psychological connectedness — motivated precisely by Reid's brave officer case: the old general remembers his exploits as a young officer, the young officer remembered being flogged as a schoolboy, but the general no longer remembers the flogging, so on the unrepaired criterion the general is the officer and the officer is the boy while the general is not the boy, violating the transitivity of identity.

Here are the objections I already have on my list, which you should not repeat:

1. Butler's circularity objection: memory presupposes personal identity rather than constituting it, since to remember an experience is, at least on the face of it, to remember having had it oneself — so the criterion analyses identity in terms of something that already contains it.

2. The problem of apparent memory: people sincerely but falsely seem to remember things that never happened or happened to others, so the criterion must be restricted to veridical memory, which raises the question of whether that restriction reintroduces circularity.

3. The forgetting objection: there are vast stretches of every life that no one can recall at all, and it seems wrong that the person simply fails to exist in the gaps between what is remembered.

4. The fragility objection: the criterion makes a person's continued existence hostage to contingent facts about recall, so that total amnesia would amount, strictly, to the annihilation of the person, which seems to get the metaphysics backwards.

5. The duplication objection: since nothing in the notion of memory rules out two later persons each remembering the experiences of one earlier person, the criterion can deliver two claimants to one identity, forcing an ad hoc non-branching clause — and this problem, unlike Reid's, is not dissolved by the repair, since branching chains of overlapping memories are perfectly coherent.

What I want is one further objection that is not on this list and not a rewording, special case, or conjunction of anything on it. It should meet two constraints. First, it must apply specifically to the repaired criterion — the version cast in terms of overlapping chains — rather than merely rehearsing difficulties for psychological-continuity theories of personal identity in general. Second, it must target the choice of memory as the connecting relation, rather than raising difficulties (such as those about what persons are fundamentally) that would press equally against any criterion built from psychological states.

After stating the objection, assess it honestly: does it force a further patch to the criterion, does it show that memory is the wrong connecting relation even within a broadly psychological approach, or does it collapse under scrutiny into one of the five objections already listed?
