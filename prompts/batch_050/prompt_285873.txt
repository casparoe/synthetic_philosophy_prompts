You are a decision theorist with a specialty in the foundations of expected utility theory and games with divergent or undefined expectations. I want you to adjudicate a dispute between two friends of mine. Think carefully, check the mathematics where it matters, and begin with a short brainstorm of the distinct issues in play before delivering your verdicts.

Some background both friends accept. A fair coin is flipped until the first heads appears; if that happens on flip n (probability 2^{-n}), the player receives (−1)^{n+1} · 2^n/n dollars. So the prize is +1 with probability 1/2, −1 with probability 1/4, +8/3 with probability 1/8, and so on. The candidate expectation is the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2; but the absolute values form the harmonic series, which diverges, and by rearranging the order of summation the series can be made to converge to any real number at all. So the game has no expectation in the standard sense. Call this game P. My friends, Maya and Daniel, disagreed about what follows. Here is their exchange as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Maya: Since the positive and negative parts of the prize series each diverge, P has no expected value, full stop. Orthodox expected utility theory is simply silent about it, which means there is no rational price for playing it. Declining to rank it against any sum of cash isn't a failure of rationality — it's the correct verdict of the theory. Anyone who names a price is projecting precision onto a case where none exists.

(2) Daniel: That can't be the whole story. Consider a modified game, M: identical to P except that every prize is raised by exactly one dollar. M pays strictly more than P in every possible state of the world, so M is strictly preferable to P by state-wise dominance — and dominance reasoning needs no expectations. So the games aren't incomparable after all. If rational comparison is possible between them, Maya's blanket claim that the theory is "silent" about this family of games is false.

(3) Maya: Dominance gives you a thin partial order among these shifted twins and nothing more. Every one of these games has possible prizes above and below any number you name, so dominance never compares any of them to a sure cash amount — which is what "price" means. And note what happens if Daniel tries to justify a price c via expectation-style reasoning: for any c, the prize series can be rearranged to converge below c, and rearranged again to converge above c. Every justification he offers is defeated by an equally legitimate reordering. That's exactly what having no expectation means.

(4) Daniel: The reordering isn't "equally legitimate." The game comes with a canonical enumeration: prize n is generated by the event that the first heads lands on flip n. Summing in that natural order — which amounts to valuing truncated versions of the game and taking the limit — yields ln 2 for P and ln 2 + 1 for M, a coherent pair of values that respects dominance and prices P at about 69 cents. And Maya's own position is unstable: if she strictly prefers M to P but would pay nothing to switch from P to M, her strict preference is toothless; if she would pay, she's already attaching a numerical value to the difference, which is just P-plus-one-dollar minus P.

(5) Maya: The "natural order" is an artifact of the story we tell about how the prizes are generated, not a fact about the lottery itself. A gamble is a probability distribution over prizes, and P's distribution genuinely has no mean — its positive and negative parts are separately infinite, which no choice of summation order can undo. Daniel's 69 cents changes if the same distribution is presented with a different generating story, and a value that depends on the narrative rather than the distribution isn't a value of the gamble. As for the switch: she'd pay up to a dollar to move from P to M because the difference is a sure dollar, which is an ordinary cash comparison requiring no valuation of P itself — so she can honor dominance while still refusing to price either game.

I stayed out of it, but I'd like your assessment. Please go through moves (1) through (5) one at a time: for each, give a rating on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification that engages the actual argument made, not a caricature. Then tell me which of the two is more right overall, and identify anything important that both of them missed — for instance, whether there are principled ways to extend decision theory to games like this without full expectations, or whether the right moral is instead a constraint on which gambles a rational theory must refuse to evaluate.
