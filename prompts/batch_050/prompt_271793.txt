People at work and on the blogs I read love saying that institutions are basically legacy code: qwerty keyboards, the electoral college, file formats from the 80s -- some decision made sense at the time, everything since got built on top of it, and now ripping it out breaks a million downstream dependencies. So serious reform is naive and all you can do is patch around the edges. I've maintained actual legacy systems and I suspect the analogy is doing more work than it should. Audit it for me. First the mapping: what plays the role of the original dev, the deprecated API, the downstream callers, the rewrite that takes down prod? Then where it breaks -- off the top of my head, code doesn't get angry when you refactor it, nobody builds an identity around a function signature, and in a codebase you can actually estimate switching costs. Also tell me what the software picture quietly assumes that institutions don't give you, like a single commit where the bad decision got made, or a designer who intended something. And a bigger one: in software the lock-in moment is right there in git blame, but with the keyboard we're trusting a story about the 1870s that for all I know is contested, and we can never rerun the 20th century with dvorak to see whether the better layout wins anyway. Does it matter for the argument that historical lock-in is untestable like that? Finally, does the cautious concluson even follow, given that engineers do sometimes rewrite the legacy system and it works? If there's a better analogy for whatever's really going on here, give me that too.
