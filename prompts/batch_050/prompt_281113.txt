In my philosophy seminar we've been working through David Lewis's account of convention: a regularity in behavior within a population facing a recurring coordination problem, sustained because nearly everyone conforms, expects others to conform, and prefers to conform given that others do, with all of this a matter of common knowledge and at least one alternative regularity that could have taken its place. Meanwhile, I serve as an officer of a 400-person student housing co-op, and our board keeps arguing about the same underlying question: is some entrenched pattern a genuine convention we can safely codify, a habit that will dissolve if we touch it, or a rule that only persists because we enforce it? Please turn Lewis's theory into a diagnostic procedure we could actually run in a short meeting: the tests to apply in order, what would count as evidence for each beyond the bare observation that everyone does it, and what each test rules out. In particular, build in the failure modes I suspect we keep missing: a regularity sustained by sanctions rather than coordination expectations; one sustained by pluralistic ignorance, where most members privately dislike it but each assumes everyone else endorses it; and one where no viable alternative equilibrium exists, so the arbitrariness condition fails. Then apply the procedure to two of our live disputes: an unwritten Friday-evening cleanup that nearly everyone joins voluntarily and values, and a bylaw requiring dishes to be washed within twenty-four hours, backed by a fine. Finally, mark the point where the diagnostic ends and a value judgment begins -- the theory can tell us what kind of practice we have, but not whether it deserves to be written down -- and be explicit about where my board will have to decide something the procedure cannot decide for us.
