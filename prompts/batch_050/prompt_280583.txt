I work as an industrial electrician, and some months ago I became curious about what people thought electricity actually was before the modern picture settled in, which led me to Henri Poincaré's Science and Hypothesis in the old English translation that is freely available online. In chapter ten, where he discusses how Fresnel's wave theory of light was absorbed into Maxwell's electromagnetism, he points out that Fresnel's equations still work as well as they ever did, and then writes: "It cannot be said that this is reducing physical theories to simple practical recipes; these equations express relations, and if the equations remain true, it is because the relations preserve their reality. They teach us now, as they did then, that there is such and such a relation between this thing and that; only, the something which we then called motion, we now call electric current. But these are merely names of the images we substituted for the real objects which Nature will hide for ever from our eyes. The true relations between these real objects are the only reality we can attain, and the sole condition is that the same relations shall exist between these objects as between the images we are forced to put in their place."

I have been turning this passage over, and I have ended up with an argument that follows it where it seems to lead and arrives somewhere I refuse to go. (1) Fresnel attributed light to motions of an elastic ether; Maxwell discarded the ether-motions but kept the predictive power, because the same equations continued to hold. (2) So what survives the replacement of one successful theory by another is the network of relations expressed in the mathematics, not the account of what the things related actually are. (3) This pattern is general: Newton's gravitational law survives inside Einstein's as an approximation, and I am told the same story repeats wherever a theory is superseded -- the relations are retained, the posited natures are dropped. (4) Therefore our best science gives us knowledge only of relations between things, never of the things related. (5) But a purely relational claim -- there exist items standing in such-and-such relations -- is satisfied by any collection of items whatever, provided they stand in those relations; as far as such a claim goes, electrons, angels, numbers, or a bare pattern with no occupants at all are equally good. (6) So science cannot tell us that there are electrons rather than unknown somethings, or nothing but structure itself, realizing the electron-pattern. (7) Since the same goes for every theoretical term, science tells us nothing about what exists -- only about patterns. (8) Yet that is absurd: a cloud chamber does not register a pattern, it registers a particle, and when I measure a current I am measuring a flow of something, not a relation floating free of everything.

I would like to know exactly which step breaks, why it looked solid, and what it costs to give it up, including what the main alternative repairs would cost instead. If I have to sacrifice something, I would rather it be one of the later steps, since (1) and (3) strike me as plain history; but I suspect the rot is earlier and cannot locate it. Two further things. First, is (4) even a fair reading of Poincaré? His "sole condition" clause seems to assume the real objects are there standing in the relations, which already contradicts my (5), so perhaps my mistake is in how I read him at (2). If so, tell me what he is actually claiming in this passage and whether it is defensible. Second, if the argument can be repaired by restricting what counts as a relation or a pattern, I want to know whether that restriction is principled or just an ad hoc wall built to keep out the conclusion I dislike.
