I am preparing a session of my upper-division seminar on rational choice and its critics (about fifteen students, one eighty-minute meeting), and the topic is what Hofstadter calls superrationality: the idea that in a perfectly symmetric situation, rational agents who know that the others are rational will reason in the same way, reach the same conclusion, and should therefore choose on the assumption that whatever they do, everyone does. Every previous time I have taught this, the class has split immediately into students who find the argument trivially right and students who find it an obvious fallacy, and neither group has actually been moved by it. I want a game that makes the argument do some work on them before anyone names it: a sequence of played rounds with genuine (small) stakes in which the students' own behavior first follows ordinary game-theoretic reasoning, then becomes unstable in a way they cannot fully explain, so that the symmetry argument arrives as a resolution of something they have actually experienced rather than as a curiosity on a slide.

Concretely, I want you to design the full activity: the rules and payoff structure for each round (points convertible to something modest, with a fixed budget I can fund myself), precise enough that a teaching assistant could run them from your instructions alone. The design should proceed by progressively removing the mundane reasons a student might cooperate in a prisoner's-dilemma-like game — repeated interaction with a known partner, the possibility of communication, then identifiability of any kind — so that by the final round nothing remains but the symmetry of the situation, and the class can observe how cooperation decays or survives at each removal. I also want two specific controls built in. First, at least one round with an asymmetric payoff table, so that students discover for themselves exactly where the symmetry argument stops applying rather than being told. Second, and this matters most to me, a manipulation that separates two conditions students reliably conflate: a round framed so that each player's choice will be mechanically copied by everyone else (causal dependence, a transparent-box Newcomb flavor) against a round framed so that the other players merely face the same problem with the same information (purely logical dependence). Superrationality proper claims the second is enough; I want the activity to reveal whether my students' willingness to cooperate actually tracks that difference or only the first. Please specify the exact wording of the instructions for these two rounds, since I suspect small wording choices will drive the result.

Then write the debrief I will lead: a mapping of each round's predictable behavioral shift onto the components of the theory — dominance reasoning and the Nash equilibrium, the symmetry premise, the role of common knowledge of rationality — along with guidance for handling the standard objection that superrational cooperation rests on a subtle confusion between influencing others and merely predicting them, using what actually happened in the room as the evidence. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (students conferring between rounds, treating nominally one-shot rounds as iterated because they will see each other again next week, a defector announcing their defection precisely in order to shatter the mirror, students mistaking the argument for a fairness norm), and how to handle each. Finally, be frank about which feature of superrationality the activity cannot reproduce in a classroom and that I will have to supply in lecture — I have my own suspicions here, involving the impossibility of genuine anonymity among people with continuing relationships, and the theory's need to assume that all parties really do reason identically, and I want to see whether your account of the activity's limits matches them.
