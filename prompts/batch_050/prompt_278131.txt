I host a philosophy podcast and next week my guest is a philosopher whose new book defends a strong version of Dennett's idea of free-floating rationales — the claim that nature is full of reasons (why the cuckoo's egg mimics the host's, why the antibody fits the antigen) that no mind ever formulated or entertained, because natural selection discovers them without any reasoner being involved. Her twist is that she extends the idea past biology: markets, cultural evolution, and machine learning systems all harbor free-floating rationales on her view, and she argues this dissolves the question of whether LLMs "really understand" anything, since rationale-tracking and understanding are matters of degree on one continuum.

You're an expert in philosophy of biology and philosophy of mind, especially Dennett and the teleosemantics literature. Think carefully before drafting — I'd like you to first do a short brainstorm (just for me, a few bullet points) of where this position is genuinely vulnerable vs. where it only looks vulnerable, and then write the questions based on that.

I want eight to ten interview questions with, for each one, a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. Requirements:

- One question on whether "rationale" is doing real explanatory work or just redescribing selective history — why isn't this whole thing just the intentional stance applied to evolution and then forgotten to be marked as a stance?
- One on the reasons-vs-causes worry: what makes something a reason at all if no one holds it? Anticipate she'll answer that the optimization structure is enough; the follow-up should ask whether a guided missile then has free-floating rationales, and if she bites that, whether the notion becomes so permissive that every artifact's function is free-floating right up until its designer writes the rationale down.
- One pressing her on what's new relative to teleosemantics (Millikan-style accounts also ground function and content in selection history). If a rationale doesn't require any representation anywhere, is 'free-floating rationale' just 'biological function' rebranded? She'll probably say function-talk is too narrow — pin down what extra thing a rationale is supposed to be.
- One on her extension to machine learning: gradient descent involves no reproduction and no lineage, so does her account of what generates a rationale smuggle in something only actual selection has? Build the follow-up around whatever criterion she offers — test it against a counterexample (a river sorting sediment by weight has an optimization-flavored structure too).
- One on normativity: can a rationale nobody represents still ground evaluation? Can selection get things *wrong* — and if malfunctions are just failures, not mistakes, does she lose the right to call these things reasons at all?
- One on whether her dissolution of the AI-understanding question overgenerates: by her lights does the immune system understand antigens, and if yes, what's left of the distinction she presumably still wants between digestion and cognition.
- End with a question about what would have to be true for her to conclude that free-floating rationale is a useful metaphor rather than a literal category — she deflects with "it's a research program," so design the follow-up to force something falsifiable-ish.

For any question where you predict she'll retreat to some version of "that's just how the intentional stance works," flag it and build the counter-move into the follow-up. Phrase the questions like actual spoken interview questions, not seminar-paper abstracts, and keep the whole thing to a length I could realistically get through in an hour.
