A friend and I have been running a small reading group on the classics of the rationalist blogosphere -- the usual material on calibration, base rates, the inside view, and epistemic modesty -- and at our last meeting I presented an argument I had written up beforehand, expecting it to be knocked down within minutes. It was not, and it has been bothering me since, because its conclusion is one I cannot actually live with. The argument runs as follows. (1) Where my considered first-person judgement conflicts with the relevant outside view -- an expert consensus, a base rate, or the observed track record of people like me attempting the same thing -- I should defer to the outside view unless I have strong, independent evidence that my case is exceptional. Call this the modesty principle; it is, near enough, what the forecasting literature and the better sort of rationalist writing recommend. (2) Whether I ought to follow the modesty principle is itself a question, and on this question the relevant reference class -- thoughtful people who have examined the evidence -- overwhelmingly declines to live by it: in practice almost everyone, including the writers who preach modesty most loudly, trusts their own judgement in precisely the domains where they have thought hardest. (3) Applying (1) one level up, I should therefore defer to that near-consensus of practice and trust my own judgement on the question of whether to be modest -- that is, I should disregard the modesty principle. (4) A principle which, when applied to itself, instructs you to disregard it is self-effacing. (5) A self-effacing principle is either false or practically empty, since a rule whose consistent application cancels itself can never direct you to do anything you would not have done anyway. (6) Hence the modesty principle is either false or empty, and in either case I am licensed simply to reason from the inside view and back my own judgement, even against consensus.

The reason this proves too much is that (6) does not merely permit me to take my own side on abstruse meta-questions; it appears to license ignoring base rates when I am founding a company, dismissing the medical consensus whenever I have a contrary hunch, and treating every expert disagreement as an occasion for my private reasoning to win by default. Yet I plainly do not behave this way -- I defer on vaccine safety, on climate, on the efficient-market question -- and I think I am right to, so somewhere between (1) and (6) something has gone wrong. My friend suspected the empirical load-bearing in (2); I keep circling back to (5), which has the smell of a step that proves too much in its own right, but I cannot say cleanly why. If forced, I would rather keep (1) intact than preserve any of the later steps, since (1) is the only part I actually use.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey. Tell me which single step you would reject, explain why that step looked plausible enough to survive a room full of attentive readers, and say what each of the obvious alternatives -- rejecting (2) as empirically false, or rejecting the move from (3) to (4) -- would cost me. Please commit to one answer and defend it, rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose. I would also like the reply written as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. British English throughout, please.
