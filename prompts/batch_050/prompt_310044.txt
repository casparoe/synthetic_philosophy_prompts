A colleague has convinced himself that Scanlon-style contractualism collapses into moral paralysis whenever people's interests conflict, and his argument has been bothering me for weeks. The background: contractualism holds, roughly, that an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by any principle that no one, suitably motivated to find principles for the general regulation of behavior, could reasonably reject. Its signature feature is the individualist restriction: whether a principle can be reasonably rejected is assessed person by person, from each individual's own standpoint, so the complaints of different people may not be summed into a single collective grievance. Now consider a standard rescue case. I can divert a runaway vehicle so that one trapped worker dies rather than five, all six equally placed in every morally relevant respect, and death is the outcome on whichever side I do not save. His argument runs as follows:

(1) If a non-rejectable principle required me to save the five, then the rival principle requiring me to save the one would have to be reasonably rejectable by someone.
(2) Under the individualist restriction, the five cannot pool their claims: each speaks only for herself, and each individual complaint — the loss of one's life — is exactly as strong as the single worker's complaint.
(3) No complaint on one side of a conflict can be defeated by a complaint on the other side unless it is strictly weaker in kind.
(4) Every complaint in play here is equal in strength, so no one's complaint defeats anyone else's; hence neither "save the one" nor "save the five" is rejectable in a way the other is not.
(5) Where neither competing principle can be rejected, the outcome must be settled by something weaker than either — a coin toss, or the rescuer's whim — and a principle that lets a person lose her life for no morally sufficient reason is one she could reasonably reject.
(6) Therefore no non-rejectable principle governs the case at all: contractualism yields no verdict here, or worse, yields the verdict that every available choice is wrong, even though one option saves four additional lives at no greater cost to anyone.

He takes this to show that contractualism proves too much: a moral theory that cannot even require saving five rather than one has broken down at exactly the point where any adequate theory must speak clearly. I refuse to accept (6). But I want to keep the individualist restriction, since it is precisely what distinguishes contractualism from aggregation and blocks the conclusion that millions of people's mild pleasures could outweigh one person's death — and I also want to keep the thought that death is an equally serious loss for each of the six. Which step should I reject? For the step you name, explain why it looked sound on first inspection, what the most defensible repair of it looks like, and what contractualism must give up — or must carefully manage to retain — in order both to condemn aggregating trivial benefits against a catastrophe and to deliver the verdict that I ought to save the greater number. Please lead with your bottom line — the step to reject — in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the argument.
