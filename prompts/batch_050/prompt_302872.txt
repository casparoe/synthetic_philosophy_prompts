I help run a small annotation project that labels generic English sentences — bare plurals like "ducks lay eggs" — as acceptable or unacceptable in ordinary usage, with the labels feeding into training data. One of our annotators is markedly more consistent than the rest, and I would like to pre-label her next batch by predicting her judgments and, ideally, to write down the policy she is implicitly following so it can go into the guidelines. Her labels so far, with the relevant facts as she understands them:

- "Ducks lay eggs." — acceptable. (She knows only females lay eggs, and not even all of them.)
- "Ducks are female." — unacceptable. (She knows the prevalence is roughly the same as for egg-laying.)
- "Lions have manes." — acceptable. (Essentially only adult males have them.)
- "Mosquitoes carry malaria." — acceptable. (She knows only a small minority of mosquitoes carry the parasite.)
- "Ticks carry Lyme disease." — acceptable. (Same situation.)
- "Sharks attack swimmers." — acceptable. (She knows such attacks are exceedingly rare.)
- "Books are paperbacks." — unacceptable. (Most new books sold in her country are paperbacks, which she knows.)
- "Canadians are right-handed." — unacceptable. (True of a large majority, which she knows.)

The next batch contains two items I cannot call: "Vending machines kill people." (The relevant facts: a few people each year are killed when a machine tips over, usually because someone rocked it to dislodge a stuck purchase; she knows this.) And: "Pandas are carnivores." (Giant pandas sit in the order Carnivora and have the short gut of a meat-eater, but eat almost nothing but bamboo; she knows this too.)

Please do the following. First, state at least three distinct policies consistent with all eight labels — genuinely different rules, not rewordings of one another — and note any obvious candidate policy that her record already rules out. Second, say what each surviving policy predicts for the two new items, and point to whichever of her existing labels does the most work in constraining each prediction. Third, give your best guess for each new item and your reasons; if you think her record genuinely underdetermines either item, say so plainly and state what we would be assuming about her policy in labeling it each way. Fourth, propose one further test sentence, not from either batch, such that learning her label for it would most efficiently discriminate among the policies still standing after your prediction, and explain what each possible label would show. Finally, give your confidence in each of the two predictions, and single out the point in your analysis you are least sure of.
