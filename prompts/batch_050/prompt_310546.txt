So my friend Mara (we met in the comments section of a rationalist blog like ten years ago, she's the kind of person who says "carve reality at its joints" unironically at parties) and I keep getting into it about what counts as a single organism. She's read a bunch of philosophy of bio but she insists she's just going off intuition. I've been quietly logging her verdicts because I'm convinced she's running some hidden classifier and I want to reverse-engineer it.

Her calls so far:

- Humans, dogs, oak trees: organisms, obviously.
- A coral colony: organism. "It's basically one animal that got weird about it."
- Pando, that giant clonal aspen stand: one organism, she says, since it's all the same genome on one root system.
- A Portuguese man o' war: organism. I pointed out it's a colony of specialised zooids and she said "yeah but they're stuck together and can't leave, so it's one thing."
- A plasmodial slime mold: organism, even the multinucleate blob stage.
- A lichen: NOT an organism. "That's two critters in a trenchcoat." This one surprised me.
- An ant colony: also not an organism. I pushed on this because the colony is nearly genetically homogeneous, but she said the ants are the organisms and the colony is a group of them. When I said "superorganism" she made a face.
- A biofilm: not an organism.
- Your gut microbiome plus you, the "holobiont": not an organism. You're the organism, the bacteria are passengers.
- Viruses: not organisms at all, can't reproduce on their own.

Fine. Now two new cases she hasn't ruled on:

1. A lichen that reproduces through soredia -- little propagules that contain both the fungus and the alga, so the partnership disperses and "reproduces" as a unit, same way a dandelion seed does. The partners can theoretically be separated in a lab, but in the wild the thing only ever travels and reproduces as a package.

2. A tetraparental mouse: lab-made by fusing two early embryos, so one perfectly healthy mouse whose body is a mosaic of two genomes, two sets of parents, functions totally normally, mates normally.

My question is threefold: (a) predict her verdict on each of these, (b) tell me what rule she's actually running, because I can think of at least three that fit her track record so far -- something like "one genotype = one organism", something like "irreversible physical integration is what matters", and something like "it's an organism if it's a unit of selection that goes through a single-package reproductive bottleneck" -- and these seem to give different answers on the two cases, and (c) if her past calls genuinely underdetermine the new ones, tell me which hypothetical case I should put to her next that would maximally distinguish the rules. I want to win this argument but I also genuinely want to know which principle she's got.
