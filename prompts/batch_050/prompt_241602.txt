I help run a weekend mathematics circle for advanced high-school students, and our most recent session — on Hilbert's hotel and the diagonal argument — ended in a dispute I could not settle. After I showed the students that the real numbers cannot be exhaustively listed the way the natural numbers can, one student announced, with the air of someone reciting a fact absorbed from a novel, that some infinities are bigger than others. A second student pushed back: infinity, she insisted, is just the failure to come to an end, and two things that both never end cannot differ in how much they go on. Over the next hour the slogan was made to do strikingly different jobs. It was used to explain why the hotel can absorb a full bus of new arrivals but allegedly not every possible crowd; to argue that the even numbers are not "really" fewer than the natural numbers despite being a proper part of them; to claim that there is no largest infinity and hence no set of absolutely everything; and — this is where I intervened — to support the claim that the physical universe cannot actually be infinite, since then there would have to be infinities of different sizes out there in reality, which the first student found absurd and the second found incoherent.

I would like a careful disambiguation of the slogan, suitable for distribution at the next session. Please lay out, as an explicit menu, the distinct precise theses that "some infinities are bigger than others" could be expressing: for instance, a claim about the non-existence of bijections between certain infinite sets, a claim about ordinal length as opposed to cardinal size, a part–whole claim about proper subsets, the stronger claim that for every infinite set there is a strictly larger one, a claim about which size comparisons are fully determinate, and a claim about physical magnitudes rather than mathematical structures. For each reading, give a verdict: is it a near-triviality, a theorem of standard set theory, a substantive but defensible philosophical position, a genuinely contested claim (and if so, contested by whom — finitists, potentialists about infinity, those who think some set-theoretic questions lack determinate answers), or simply false? Where a reading is false, say what nearby truth its proponents are probably reaching for.

Finally, for each of the four uses the slogan was put to during my session, tell me which readings on the menu would actually license that use, and which would not. My suspicion is that the afternoon's arguments equivocated — that the interpretation needed to make the slogan come out true is not the interpretation needed to make the conclusion about the physical universe, or the conclusion about the nonexistence of a universal set, follow — and I would like the diagnosis stated precisely enough that two determined sixteen-year-olds cannot wriggle out of it.
