I recently took over my late uncle's small business preparing estates for sale -- mostly furniture, prints, and paintings of modest value, with the occasional piece that might be something. Twice in my first six months I have been badly wrong about how much authenticity matters, in opposite directions: once I spent a client's money authenticating a drawing whose value barely changed when it turned out to be genuine, and once I described a competent nineteenth-century copy of a minor Old Master as 'after' the artist and consigned it cheaply, only to learn that collectors of copies would have paid ten times that if I had understood what I had. I clearly have no working theory of when the difference between an original, a workshop piece, a later copy, a restored original, and an outright fake actually matters, and to whom.

Philosophers have thought about exactly this -- why an original should be worth more than an indistinguishable copy, what kind of value a history of production confers, whether the premium on authenticity is rational or a species of superstition, and how the answer changes across media (a cast bronze, a photographic print, and a painting are not the same case). I would like you to turn whatever is sound in that thinking into a procedure I can actually run at an intake appointment.

Concretely, I want:

1. A decision procedure, in the form of questions to ask in order, for any object whose authorship or origin is in doubt. The output at each node should be an action, not a verdict: whether it is worth paying for authentication at all; how to describe the object honestly in a listing given each level of uncertainty; and how to think about pricing when the market itself may be responding to something (provenance, the artist's hand, mere celebrity of the name) that my procedure cannot certify.

2. An explicit account of which distinctions the procedure insists on preserving and which it deliberately collapses. For instance, I suspect 'indistinguishable to the eye' and 'indistinguishable in value' must be kept apart, and that 'the artist made it' and 'the artist authorized it' must be kept apart, but I am not sure, and I want the reasoning, not just the checklist.

3. Honest marking of the points where no procedure can substitute for judgment or for a specialist, and of the points where the honest answer is that the market's premium for originals may be irrational but is a fact I must price anyway.

Then run the procedure on three cases I have actually faced: a signed print from a large edition where the signature is the only thing distinguishing it from a poster; a painting probably from an artist's workshop rather than the artist's hand; and an object that is almost certainly an outright fake of something that was never very valuable in the first place, where the client is sentimentally attached to believing it real. At least one of these should come out somewhere I will not like.

Constraints: I have about forty minutes per intake, no formal art-historical training, access to specialists only by paid referral, and everything I write in a listing has legal exposure. The procedure should assume the reader does not know the philosophical vocabulary, so define whatever you use.
