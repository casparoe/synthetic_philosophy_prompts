I’m a retired plant physiologist who has come late and somewhat suspiciously to philosophy. Our little reading group is doing Nietzsche’s Ecce Homo, and one member keeps saying that his obsession with diet, climate, digestion, walking, sleep, coffee, and alcohol is basically an altitude-training manual for thinkers: the body is the instrument, food and surroundings are training variables, illness is poor calibration, and philosophy is the high-performance output. He pointed to Nietzsche’s remark that “Philosophy, as I have understood it hitherto, is a voluntary retirement into regions of ice and mountain-peaks—the seeking-out of everything strange and questionable in existence, everything upon which, hitherto, morality has set its ban.” I can see the attraction of the analogy, but my old laboratory instincts make me suspect it is doing rather too much work.

Could you audit the analogy carefully? Give me the correspondence table—what plays the role of altitude, athlete, coach, training load, acclimatization, diet, and measurable performance—and then show where it breaks. In particular, does Nietzsche treat health merely as a means to intellectual productivity, or as a standard by which philosophies themselves are to be judged? How should his own recurring sickness and praise of convalescence complicate the “peak performance” reading? I’m also interested in the possibility that he is making the stronger claim that bodily conditions help determine which ideals a person can even find believable, not just how efficiently that person reasons afterward. Please separate the disanalogies that matter from the merely obvious ones, say whether the altitude-training comparison survives, and offer a better analogy if one is definately needed.
