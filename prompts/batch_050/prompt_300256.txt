I am grading final exams for my upper-level undergraduate course on nineteenth-century social thought. Students were given the following passage from Karl Marx's Preface to *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859), which was printed on the exam paper, and answered the question beneath it in 30 minutes. The question is worth 20 marks.

Passage:

In studying such transformations it is always necessary to distinguish between the material transformation of the economic conditions of production, which can be determined with the precision of natural science, and the legal, political, religious, artistic or philosophic – in short, ideological forms in which men become conscious of this conflict and fight it out. Just as one does not judge an individual by what he thinks about himself, so one cannot judge such a period of transformation by its consciousness, but, on the contrary, this consciousness must be explained from the contradictions of material life, from the conflict existing between the social forces of production and the relations of production.

Question: (a) On what basis does Marx distinguish the material transformation of the conditions of production from the ideological forms, and what work is the analogy with judging an individual doing in his argument? (b) Read strictly on its own terms, does this passage commit Marx to the view that law, politics, religion, and philosophy play no role in social transformation? Defend your reading by close reference to the text.

Here is the student's answer:

The distinction Marx draws rests on the different standards of precision available in each domain. The material transformation of the economic conditions of production "can be determined with the precision of natural science," whereas the ideological forms cannot; we can in principle measure changes in productive forces and relations of production, but legal, religious, and philosophical change resists any such determination. The analogy with the individual reinforces this: just as a person's self-description is not evidence about his character but is itself something to be explained, a period's self-understanding — its laws, religions, and philosophies — is not evidence about what is really changing in that period, but something whose explanation must be sought in the contradictions of material life. The analogy thus transfers an epistemic point about individuals to an epistemic point about whole epochs.

On the second question, Marx does commit himself to the view that ideological forms are causally inert. This follows from the asymmetry built into the distinction: economic transformations admit of scientific determination because they are the real motor of history, while ideological forms merely register, in distorted form, a conflict whose actual content is economic. When Marx says that men "become conscious of this conflict and fight it out" in ideological forms, he means that these forms are the theatre in which the conflict is symbolically staged and resolved — an arena in which the outcome has in effect already been decided elsewhere. His maxim that a period cannot be judged by its own consciousness implies that the legal and political struggles of a revolutionary epoch are a mask the analyst must lift in order to find the real conflict underneath. So although Marx grants that people experience social transformation through ideology, the passage rules out attributing any independent causal role to law, politics, religion, or philosophy. As he says earlier in the Preface, forms of social consciousness correspond to the economic structure, and correspondence is a one-way relation.
