You are a philosopher specializing in normative ethics and the philosophy of law, with particular expertise in the theory of consent. Before writing anything, spend some time brainstorming: list the main axes along which theories of valid consent diverge (what consent is, what defeats it, and how each kind of defeater works), and then think about which concrete cases make those axes visible. Only after that, complete the task below.

I am building a "Which theory of valid consent do you actually hold?" module for a graduate seminar website in moral and legal philosophy. I need a sorting quiz that assigns each taker a position on the basis of their intuitions about cases, not their familiarity with the literature. Please produce the following, in this order.

1. Eight to ten diagnostic questions, each built around a concrete scenario, with two or three answer options apiece. The scenarios should be varied in domain: some sexual, some medical, some commercial, and at least one involving consent given to one person while a different person acts (an impersonation or substitution case). The cases must collectively exercise all of the following fault lines:

- whether consent is fundamentally a mental state of acquiescence or a public, performative act of permission-granting (include at least one case where the two come apart, e.g., wholehearted inner acquiescence never communicated, or a signature on a form the signer did not read);
- how deception invalidates: whether any operative lie vitiates consent, whether only lies about what one is consenting to (the nature of the act) do so while lies about one's reasons or the surrounding circumstances do not, and whether the answer changes when the deceived party would have refused had they known the truth (include cases of a lie about a deal-constituting feature, such as which surgical procedure will be performed or which person one is in bed with, and a lie about a mere motivating fact, such as a partner's marital status or wealth);
- how pressure invalidates: whether the threat/offer distinction does the work, and if so whether the baseline separating threats from offers is fixed by what the pressured party was morally entitled to or by something non-moralized (include one case of a genuinely generous offer with heavy strings attached and one case where the line between threat and offer depends entirely on how the proposal is described);
- how impaired capacity invalidates: whether competence is a single threshold or varies with the stakes of the decision, and how to treat a person who is sincerely enthusiastic in the moment but predictably will repudiate the choice when sober or untreated.

2. A scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions. The leaves should include at least: an attitudinal account of consent paired with a restrictive (deal-focused) account of deception; a performative account paired with a restrictive account of deception; a performative account on which any operative deception vitiates consent; a moralized-baseline account of coercion on which offers can never coerce regardless of the pressure they exert; a view on which sufficiently weighty offers can be coercive in effect; and a sliding-scale competence view. Where a standard name or a well-known defender exists for a leaf, use it; otherwise mark the leaf "unnamed."

3. A short section on the quiz's limits. Identify at least one coherent combination of answers that no prominent position occupies, and say whether it is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your scoring key cannot reliably separate, and say what further case would be needed to split them.

Every scenario must be answerable by an intelligent reader who has never encountered the labels, so no question may simply ask, for example, whether the taker thinks consent is a mental state. The tone should be neutral throughout: the answer options must not telegraph which one the quiz's author considers correct, and the scoring key should present each leaf's most awkward commitment in one sentence, so that no position comes out looking like the obviously right answer.
