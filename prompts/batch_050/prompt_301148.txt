The two-envelope paradox seems to show that expected value reasoning is self-defeating: whatever amount I find in my envelope, the other envelope has an expected value of 1.25 times that amount, so I should always switch — but since the envelopes are symmetric, this reasoning must be wrong somewhere. The standard fix I've seen is to point out that the "always switch" calculation treats the probability that the other envelope holds double as 1/2 regardless of what I observe, which no proper probability distribution can sustain. My question: if that's the resolution, then the paradox never actually threatened expected value maximization — it just exploited a disguised inconsistency in the priors. But then why do philosophers still treat it as a serious problem for decision theory rather than a settled puzzle with a bookkeeping error at its core? Is there a version of the puzzle that survives the fix, and if not, what is the literature on it actually about?
