At a going-away lunch, two of my colleagues got into a serious argument after one of them, Daniel, mentioned that he had declined a free and highly accurate genetic test that would have told him whether he carries a marker for an untreatable late-onset condition. The other, Sofia, said his refusal was straightforwardly irrational. The exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Sofia argued that there is a theorem in decision theory — Good's theorem on the value of information — establishing that for an agent who maximizes expected utility and updates by Bayes' rule, the expected value of acquiring free information is never negative, and is strictly positive whenever the information could change at least one decision. A free, accurate test is the theorem's paradigm case. Refusing it is therefore either pointless (if the result could never alter anything Daniel does, refusal gains him nothing) or strictly costly (if it could, he is discarding positive expected value). Either way, declining is at best a matter of indifference and at worst plainly irrational.

(2) Daniel replied that the theorem's notion of "free" is doing all the work. Free must mean costless in every relevant dimension, including having no direct effect on the agent's wellbeing. But for him, knowing would itself be costly: a positive result would mean years of anticipatory dread, and dread is a genuine cost, not a processing fee. Once the observation enters the payoffs directly rather than merely informing a decision, the information is no longer free in the theorem's sense, and the theorem simply does not apply to his case.

(3) Sofia answered that the dread rationale is incoherent on its own terms. A Bayesian's beliefs form a martingale: one's expected posterior equals one's prior, which means Daniel cannot rationally expect to learn bad news. Whatever he would dread after the test, he should already be dreading now, in exact proportion. One cannot be made worse off in expectation by evidence not yet seen; anticipating that the test will go badly is simply miscalibrated pessimism.

(4) Daniel responded that the martingale property constrains expected beliefs, not the experience of holding them. Before the test he lives at the prior, which he finds tolerable; afterward he is certain of one branch or the other, and there is no reason his wellbeing must be linear in his own degree of belief. If the bad branch hurts more than the good branch relieves — that is, if his wellbeing is a concave function of his own belief about being a carrier — then the expected wellbeing of knowing falls below the wellbeing of remaining at the prior, and declining is maximizing rather than irrational. He added that the theorem also presupposes things he knows are false of himself: that his future self will update cleanly and choose optimally given the information. If knowing would predictably distort his later decisions — over-reading every minor symptom, for instance — the guarantee lapses, and the same holds for agents whose attitudes toward risk and ambiguity do not fit expected utility theory in the first place.

I was asked to referee and could not settle it. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating, and then say who is more right overall. Commit to definite verdicts rather than treating both sides as equally defensible — where a move conflates distinct issues, misstates a result, or proves too much, say so plainly. End your answer with a final line of the form "Verdict: [name] is more right overall", followed by a single clause stating the most important reason.
