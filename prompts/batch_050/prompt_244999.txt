My officemates Maya and Dan (both third-year grad students) got into a long argument last week about Frankfurt cases, and I've been going back and forth on who actually won. Here's the setup they were using, which Maya invented: a neurosurgeon, Dr. Reyes, had a safety implant installed years ago after a near-miss in the OR. The implant monitors her motor-planning regions, and if she ever begins to form the decision to make the reckless cut rather than the careful one, it fires and makes her choose the careful cut instead. In five years it has never fired, because every time she just decides to do the careful thing on her own, for her own reasons. Last Tuesday she did a tricky operation, chose the careful cut on her own, patient did great. Question: is she morally responsible for operating carefully, given that she couldn't have chosen otherwise?

Their exchange, roughly as I remember it:

(1) Maya said yes, obviously she's responsible, and that's the whole point of Frankfurt-style cases: the implant never fired, it did nothing at all on Tuesday, so everything about her actual action flowed from her own deliberation and character. If you can't do otherwise but the blocker never activates, your responsibility is intact, so the principle that responsibility requires alternative possibilities is false.

(2) Dan said this version is actually worse for her than the standard Black-and-Jones case, because Reyes consented to the implant. Whatever responsibility she has on Tuesday just traces back to her earlier free choice to get the implant, when she did have alternatives. So the case never even gets started: it's a self-imposed constraint, like Ulysses and the mast, and nobody thinks those cases threaten the alternative-possibilities principle. They just relocate the relevant choice earlier.

(3) Maya said that can't be right as a general defense, because you can just redescribe the case so the constraint isn't self-imposed -- say the hospital installed it as a condition of employment when she was a resident, or she was born with it. And the original Frankfurt point doesn't depend on who installed the blocker, it depends on the blocker being idle. If tracing were doing all the work, then the version where she never consented should make her not responsible, but Maya says she'd still be responsible there too, so tracing is a red herring.

(4) Dan then shifted ground and said fine, drop the tracing point, the real problem is that Reyes still has alternatives. She could start to form the reckless decision -- the implant only fires once she begins forming it, so that beginning is a flicker of freedom, and her responsibility on Tuesday is grounded in her freely not exercising that flicker. Every Frankfurt case smuggles in some tiny alternative like this and the tiny alternative is doing all the work.

(5) Maya said this is the standard flicker move and it fails, because the flicker isn't "robust" -- it can't be the source of her responsibility, since merely being able to twitch toward a decision that would be instantly overridden adds nothing of value that could explain why she's praiseworthy for a careful operation she'd have performed regardless. She said Dan's principle collapses into the claim that responsibility requires any metaphysical alternative at all, however pointless, which is no longer the intuitive principle Frankfurt was attacking.

(6) Dan's last move: he said then the case is question-beging, because for the implant to guarantee she can't choose otherwise, the connection between her neural states and her decision has to be deterministic, and the whole reason anyone believed the alternative-possibilities principle in the first place is libertarian intuitions about choices not being determined. So the case only works by assuming the very thing its target denies, and a libertarian can just deny that the implant guarantees anything.

Maya didn't have a great comeback to (6) beyond muttering about buffer cases, which she didn't explain well.

Can you adjudicate this? Go through each move and say who's right about what -- including whether the tracing objection in (2) is as weak as Maya claims, whether the flicker objection in (4) survives her robustness reply, and whether (6) is actually a good point or just a change of subject. Then tell me who's more right overall about whether this case shows you can be responsible without alternatives, and whether either of them is missing something important.
