I'm a novelist, mostly literary fiction, and my current project is written partly from the perspective of a hooded crow -- not a talking crow, not a fable animal, just a crow doing crow things, scavenging, caching, watching people. My writing group is split down the middle about whether I've committed some terrible sin of anthropomorphism or whether I've actually been too timid and made the bird a furry little robot that only ever does the simplest thing that could explain its behavior. Someone in the group threw around the phrase Morgan's canon, and when I looked into it I fell down a rabbit hole about how scientists and philosophers decide when it's legitimate to credit an animal with something like planning, or grief, or a sense of fairness, versus when we should chalk it up to simpler mechanisms.

Here's what I actually want to know. If I had, say, six months to really get my head around this debate before my next draft, which open questions in it should I spend that time on, and in what order? I don't mean a survey of the whole field -- I mean a defended agenda. Which questions are upstream of the others, in the sense that until you settle them the rest is just noise? For instance, does everything hinge on what "simpler" even means when we're comparing explanations of behavior, or on whether the canon itself was ever meant as a law rather than a rule of thumb, or on the flip-side worry that refusing to attribute minds is its own bias with its own bodycount, historically speaking? Which of these are things an interested outsider could genuinely get traction on versus which are basically hostage to empirical work that hasn't been done yet? And which disputed points in the area are actually load-bearing for someone in my position -- i.e., would change what ends up on the page -- versus which are philosophers arguing past each other in ways that wash out by the time you're writing scene-level prose? Please rank them and tell me what would make you re-rank them, because I'm going to make decisions off of this.

One more ask: write it as plain continuous prose, like an email from a friend who happens to know this stuff. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables -- I find that stuff breaks my ability to actually absorb an argument, I want to have to follow the thread.
