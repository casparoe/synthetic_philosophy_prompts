I'm a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and my dissertation rests on a position I would like stress-tested rather than defended. Let me first stipulate the setup I work with, since the details matter and I don't want to rely on your familiarity with any particular text. Imagine a vast sealed tray containing enormous numbers of small ball-bearings that collide, rebound and roll purely according to low-level mechanics. The tray is engineered so that certain large-scale configurations of the balls are stable and re-identifiable: they persist, combine, trigger one another and transform according to a higher-level rulebook that can be stated without mentioning any individual ball. Call these configurations symbols, and suppose the whole system does something interpretable -- say, it plays a board game against an opponent outside the tray, and wins. Every trajectory of every ball is fixed by the mechanics; yet there is also a complete story of the game told entirely in terms of symbols and their rules.

The position I have come to hold is that, in such a system, the symbol level is genuinely in charge. The correct causal explanation of why the tray makes the move it makes runs through the symbols and their rule-governed interactions, and one can say, without irony, that the symbols push the substrate around: the balls go where they go because of what the configurations mean and what the symbol-level rules dictate. I do not intend this as a façon de parler. I take symbol-level causal claims to be true in roughly the way that ordinary special-science causal claims -- an embargo causing a recession, a famine causing a revolution -- are true, and I take a symbol-level cognitive science to be autonomous from the neuroscience of the substrate in a strong sense: it risks nothing from whatever we might discover about the balls.

What would count against this? I would like a map of my exposure, not a balanced survey. Specifically: which arguments or formal results, if established, would show that symbol-level causal claims are at best a useful shorthand for substrate-level goings-on -- for instance, some version of the causal exclusion problem, or an appeal to the causal closure of the physical, or a demonstration that my view commits me to systematic overdetermination of every ball's trajectory? Second, what would an actual case have to look like to prise my position apart from its deflationist rival, on which all the causing happens among the balls and the symbol level is merely a compact predictive gloss? Both views predict that the tray wins the game, so I need cases -- perhaps ones involving breakdowns, interventions, or failures of the rulebook -- on which they genuinely diverge. Third, on difference-making or interventionist accounts of causation, which manipulations of the tray would actually bear on the question, and would my view survive the verdict those accounts deliver? Fourth, does my position stand or fall with theses elsewhere in metaphysics -- about the causal status of multiply realisable kinds, or about special-science laws generally -- such that I inherit defeaters I haven't noticed? Please rank the defeaters you identify from fatal to merely awkward, say which of them a committed defender of higher-level causation would herself concede as costs rather than dismiss as misunderstandings, and close with an honest verdict on whether my 'true in the same sense as the embargo' manoeuvre insulates the position from falsification altogether -- and if so, whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or simply what it is to be a thesis about levels. Please answer in British English.
