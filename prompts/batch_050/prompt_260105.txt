I'm a novelist and I'm stuck on the moral architecture of my current book, so I've gone back to Crime and Punishment to steal some ideas. What strikes me is how much of Raskolnikov's guilt is shaped by pure chance: Lizaveta just happens to walk in, so he becomes a double murderer instead of a single one, and later Marmeladov gets run over by a carriage and dies in Sonya's room, which is luck all the way down. Read the novel through the literature on moral luck (Nagel's categories and all that). Does Dostoevsky treat Raskolnikov as worse because of how things turned out, or does the novel push against that? And where does constitutive luck come in -- Raskolnikov being the kind of person who could even entertain the theory in the first place? I'm less interested in a tidy verdict than in where the novel dramatizes something the philosophical debate handles clumsily, since I want my own book to sit in that uncomfortable spot. Also tell me honestly where the moral-luck reading strains against what the novel is actually doing, because I don't want to kid myself.
