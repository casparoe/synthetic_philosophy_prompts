I am a retired experimental physicist who has come late to political philosophy, and I am reading Henry George’s Progress and Poverty with a mixture of sympathy and suspicion. In the preface George writes:

"The facts upon which I have relied are not facts which can be verified only by a search through libraries. They are facts of common observation and common knowledge, which every reader can verify for himself, just as he can decide whether the reasoning from them is or is not valid."

Please help me understand what methodological claim is being made here, and how well it survives translation into a present-day housing dispute. I am not asking for a summary of George’s land-value-tax argument, nor for sources or further reading. I want to know how to read the sentence itself.

In particular, distinguish at least these possible interpretations, and add any important one I have missed: (a) an anti-esoteric claim, that political economy should not depend on archive-bound expertise; (b) an empiricist claim, that the relevant premises are publicly checkable rather than merely intuitive; (c) a rhetorical claim, inviting the reader to treat familiarity as proof; and (d) a democratic-epistemic claim, that affected people must be able to contest the facts used to justify coercion. For each, say what would count as evidence for or against it, and whether “common knowledge” means merely widely believed, individually observable, or something closer to mutual knowledge in the technical sense.

Then test the passage against a concrete zoning fight: a wealthy neighborhood association opposes a mid-rise apartment building near a transit stop, citing traffic, shadows, school crowding, and “neighborhood character,” while younger renters cite rents, displacement, and exclusion. Which facts in that dispute could plausibly be “of common observation and common knowledge” in George’s sense, and which cannot be? When residents’ lived experience conflicts with municipal data, does George’s standard favor the residents, the data, neither, or different answers for different kinds of claims? Finally, say whether the passage gives any support to NIMBY procedural vetoes, to a “right to the city” for newcomers and outsiders, or whether it cuts against both by denying that either side may treat its own familiarity as self-certifying.
