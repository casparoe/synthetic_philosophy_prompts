A recurring proposal in the alignment literature is to supervise the process rather than the outcome: reward a model for producing reasoning a human can follow step by step and endorse, rather than rewarding final answers that merely appear correct. Write an essay weighing process-based supervision against outcome-based supervision for training advanced AI systems, and defend a verdict about which regime a frontier lab should prefer, under what conditions. In doing so, take seriously two objections that I think are underexplored. First, the collapse objection: process supervision requires a human evaluator who can actually verify each step, but in the domains where supervision matters most -- novel mathematics, long-horizon planning, code the human cannot execute mentally -- the only checkable steps are themselves mini-outcomes, so process supervision may just be outcome supervision at finer granularity, inheriting the same Goodhart dynamics it was meant to escape. Second, the legibility-laundering objection: outcome-based training at scale may actively select against faithful reasoning, producing models whose visible chain-of-thought is optimized to make evaluators believe the answer rather than to reflect the computation that produced it, so that the observable transcript becomes adversarially untrustworthy in a way process supervision could have prevented. Assess whether these two objections can both be true, whether one dissolves the other, and what the strongest hybrid regime would look like. End by naming the single most plausible failure mode of whichever regime you recommend, and explain why it is nonetheless the right bet.
