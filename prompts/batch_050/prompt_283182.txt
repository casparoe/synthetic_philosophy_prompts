Something I keep noticing in arguments: people get away with stuff by not quite saying it. If I ask "have you stopped reposting from that account?", I've accused you of reposting without ever asserting it, and a yes or a no both grant it. Or a coworker says "anyway, back to the actual topic" and suddenly whatever you were saying is officially not the actual topic, even though nobody voted on that. Most of the time the listener just absorbs the extra claim and the the conversation moves on as if it had been on the table all along. I know linguists have names for this machinery (presupposition, accommodation, treating a conversation as a kind of scoreboard that updates as we go), but I'm not asking for the names.

What I want is the implicit theory. What does this whole arrangement presuppose about what a conversation even is? Does it only make sense if there's a shared ledger of what-we're-taking-for-granted that both sides are stuck maintaining? Does it presuppose a real distinction between asserting something and letting it slip in sideways -- and is that distinction even coherent, if both end up in the ledger? Is the practice basically running a speak-now-or-forever-hold-your-peace rule, where the hearer consents to being updated unless they object fast enough? Reconstruct the theory of conversation the practice needs, and then poke at it: is the object-now-or-lose-your-chance norm fair, given that catching a smuggled claim costs attention and social capital, and that objecting makes you the difficult one? Which parts of the practice strictly require the implicit theory and which would survive under several rival pictures of what's going on?

And then the application that made me ask: advertising and political messaging lean on this hard. "Our tablets don't cause headaches" -- wait, whose do? "Unlike some people, I never sold out" -- okay. Does the implicit theory explain *why* that works, and does it predict the trick keeps working once an audience knows the mechanism, or does it predict a kind of arms race where hearers get guarded and speakers get sneakier? Finally: what would actually have to change in everyday talk if we ran conversation on the strict model where nothing enters the ledger unless it was explicitly asserted and explicitly accepted -- and would we even be able to talk at all?
