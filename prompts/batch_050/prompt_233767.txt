I have spent some time defending a version of the language of thought hypothesis, and I would now like a disciplined audit of its vulnerabilities rather than further advocacy.

The position, stated as precisely as I can manage: human cognition requires a system of mental representations with genuinely combinatorial syntax — that is, a stock of atomic symbols whose permissible combinations are governed by structure-sensitive formation rules, such that complex thoughts literally contain simpler ones as constituents, and such that the causal processes of inference are sensitive to that syntax. I hold that the classic arguments from productivity and systematicity remain the strongest case for this: an unbounded capacity to entertain novel thoughts, together with the fact that anyone who can think that the chemist distrusts the journalist can also think that the journalist distrusts the chemist, is best explained by constituent structure and structure-sensitive operations, and not merely by behavioral regularities or by vector-space proximity in a learned representation. I do not treat the hypothesis as committed to any particular claims about natural language, consciousness, or the neural implementation of the medium; I take it to be an empirical thesis about the architecture of cognition, not a stipulation about how we must talk.

Here is what I want from you. First, identify what would actually count against this position, and separate the categories carefully. Which empirical results would be genuinely decisive — for instance, would it be fatal if artificial neural networks reliably exhibited robust systematic generalization across demanding compositional benchmarks while demonstrably lacking constituent structure in their internal representations, or would a defender be entitled to reply that such systems have merely implemented a classical architecture by other means, so that nothing follows? Conversely, does the apparent failure of those same networks on some systematicity tests count for the position, or only against a particular rival? Second, identify the retreat paths and assess whether they constitute falsification-avoidance. A defender under pressure can weaken the claim in several ways: from classical constituent structure to merely functional compositionality, from a thesis about all cognition to one about some central, modular, or human-specific stratum of it, from an empirical conjecture to a methodological presumption about how cognitive science ought to proceed. For each weakening, tell me whether what remains still deserves the name, and whether the original arguments from productivity and systematicity survive the retreat or were always arguments only for the strong version. Third, distinguish the defeaters that defenders themselves would concede as bearing on the position from those they would dismiss as changing the subject, and say who is right in each case. I am especially interested in whether the hypothesis makes any prediction that its rivals do not — that is, whether there is an observation on which language-of-thought architecture and a sufficiently sophisticated connectionist alternative genuinely diverge — or whether the two have become notational variants at the level where evidence could discriminate. Finally, rank the threats from fatal to merely awkward, and give me a verdict on the prior question: is this a position with real exposure, and if it is well insulated, is that insulation a mark of its strength or a symptom that it has quietly ceased to be an empirical thesis at all? I would rather be told the view is unfalsifiable in its current form than be handed a comfortable list of objections it can absorb.
