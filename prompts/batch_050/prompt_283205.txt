I'm a third-year undergrad in a seminar on longtermism and global priorities, and our unit on the hinge of history hypothesis has turned into the most frustrating discussion of the semester. Roughly: the hinge camp (taking its cue from writers like Will MacAskill and Toby Ord) holds that we may be living at the most influential period in all of history, because of the conjunction of unprecedented extinction-level risks and technologies like advanced AI that could lock in the long-run trajectory of civilization. The skeptics in my seminar push three lines: first, a base-rate worry that essentially every era has believed itself to be at a turning point, so our sense of specialness is unreliable; second, a conceptual worry that "influence" is being measured in a question-begging way, since counterfactual difference-making per person was arguably far greater in small early populations or at historical bottlenecks where one decision echoed for millennia; third, an objection that the whole claim secretly rests on contested empirical forecasts about AI timelines, which are not philosophy's business to adjudicate. The hinge camp replies that earlier eras lacked the one-way valve of extinction risk, that transformative AI could compress centuries of change into decades, and that the claim only needs influence to be concentrated in expectation, not certain.
