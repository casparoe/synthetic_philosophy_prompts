Think carefully and step by step.

Here's the situation. One member of the group keeps leaning on the classic "inner sense" picture of introspection (the Locke-through-Armstrong lineage): introspection is basically perception turned inward. Just as vision gives you non-inferential, mostly reliable access to colored surfaces out there, introspection gives you non-inferential, mostly reliable access to your own current mental states -- pains, itches, occurrent thoughts. And she uses the analogy to do real argumentative work: (1) to argue that introspective errors must be rare and local, the way perceptual illusions are, since a faculty that misfired systematically would have been selected against; (2) to argue against "constitutive" or "deliberative" accounts of self-knowledge (the Shoemaker/Moran-ish family, roughly: knowing what you believe is bound up with deciding what to believe, not with observing yourself), because on her view those accounts are just confused responses to the fact that the inner "organ" is unusual in lacking a medium -- you don't need a medium for everything.

I'm suspicious of the analogy, but I can't fully articulate why. So please:

1. Lay out the mapping explicitly (a table of correspondences is great): what plays the role of the sensory organ, the medium, the distal object, the appearance/reality gap, the possibility of illusion, the evolutionary-cost argument, etc.
2. Identify where it breaks. Some candidate disanalogies I've half-noticed: there's no distance or perspective on the "object" (you can't get closer to your pain for a better look); the "object" may be partly constituted by the "sensing" of it in a way a red wall isn't; attention works totally differently (attending to your grief seems to change it, attending to a wall doesn't); there's no intersubjective triangulation. Tell me which of these actually matter for the conclusions it's being used to support (1) and (2), and which are just superficial mismatches every analogy has. Feel free to add disanalogies I haven't thought of.
3. What's the analogy quietly importing? E.g., does framing self-knowledge as detection smuggle in the idea that mental states are fully formed prior to being known, and does that stack the deck against the deliberative accounts before the argument even starts?
4. Does conclusion (1) -- errors are rare and local, like illusions -- survive once the disanalogies are on the table? Note that this matters given how badly people seem to do at, say, identifying why they made a choice or even whether they're in some emotional states. Does the perceptual analogy predict the *pattern* of introspective failure we actually see, or does it predict a different pattern (occasional vivid illusions against a backdrop of accuracy)?
5. If the analogy mostly fails, what's a better one for the same phenomena -- or is the failure itself evidence for the constitutive side? I.e., does the collapse of the inner-sense picture lend support to Moran-style views, or could introspection be reliable for reasons that have nothing to do with perception *or* constitution?

I don't want a neutral overview of the literature, I want the audit itself, with an actual verdict on whether my reading-group friend's two arguments survive. Also flag anywhere you think the analogy is actually stronger than I'm giving it credit for -- I'd rather be talked out of my suspicion than have it flattered.
