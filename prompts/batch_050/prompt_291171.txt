Last night a friend and I got into a long and surprisingly heated argument about evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics — the line of thought, associated with philosophers like Sharon Street and Richard Joyce, that natural selection shaped our moral intuitions to promote survival and cooperation rather than to track any mind-independent moral truth. My position was that once you grant that Darwinian story, moral realism is straightforwardly refuted: if we would hold exactly the same moral beliefs whether or not any moral facts existed, then there simply are no such facts, and realism is false. My friend kept insisting that the argument shows, at most, that our moral beliefs are unjustified or unreliably formed — which she said is perfectly compatible with realism being true, since our beliefs might happen to coincide with the moral facts anyway, even if that coincidence would be sheer luck. I told her this was pedantic hair-splitting: a belief produced by a process that would deliver it regardless of the facts is not knowledge, so there is no meaningful difference between "realism is false" and "we could never be warranted in believing realism." We eventually agreed to consult someone more systematic before meeting again.

So, setting aside who should win the argument overall, I want the logical structure sorted out. First, does an evolutionary debunking argument actually purport to show that moral realism is false, or only that our moral beliefs are epistemically defeated? Second, if it is the latter, is my friend's distinction a substantive one rather than a verbal quibble — does the argument genuinely leave room for a realist who concedes that our intuitions merely happen to align with the moral facts, and what moves (for example, so-called third-factor responses) are available to that realist? Third, if the argument's official conclusion is only epistemic, why do so many people — apparently including me — slide so naturally from "our moral beliefs are undermined" to "there are no moral facts"? Is that slide ever defensible, or is it precisely where the argument's critics should dig in?
