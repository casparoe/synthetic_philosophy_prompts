I've been part of a reading group this term working through Darwin's Descent of Man alongside a selection of his correspondence, and a tension in his thinking keeps surfacing that we'd like pressed further than our sessions have managed. On the one hand, Darwin grounds the moral sense in the social instincts, treats sympathy as its cornerstone, and seems to regard the historical widening of sympathy -- from kin, to tribe, to nation, to all humanity, and ultimately toward the lower animals -- as the natural trajectory of moral progress rather than a betrayal of our evolved nature. On the other hand, he worried in print that civilized societies check the action of natural selection by sustaining the weak and sickly, and in his letters he treated the sheer volume of suffering built into the natural economy -- parasites, predators, starvation, the destruction of most offspring -- as a genuine obstacle to belief in a benevolent designer, while apparently never concluding that this suffering called for human remedy.

Here is the anachronistic question we want to explore. A small but serious contemporary movement, influenced by utilitarian and effective-altruist thinking, argues that wild-animal suffering is among the most neglected moral problems there is: wild animals vastly outnumber humans and domesticated animals, most of them die young and painfully, and so we ought to develop interventions -- vaccinating wild populations against disease, managing herbivore numbers by contraception rather than culling, redesigning ecosystems to reduce starvation, and, in the very long run, perhaps even phasing out predation. Assume Darwin were presented with this program today, with full knowledge of modern population genetics and ecology. What would he make of it? In answering, please do several things. First, distinguish what Darwin the Victorian country gentleman -- with his actual documented sensibilities, his hatred of slavery, his support for regulating vivisection, his fondness for animals -- would probably have felt, from what his own theoretical commitments in the Descent and elsewhere would push him toward, since these need not coincide. Second, address the specific fault lines: does his account of sympathy's expansion commit him to welcoming the project, or did he mean the expansion to stop somewhere short of reorganizing nature itself? Would he regard large-scale intervention as another instance of the civilized 'checking' of selection he already found uneasy-making -- and was that unease a moral objection, a prudential one, or merely an observation? Third, take seriously his view that natural selection is the very engine that produced the instincts and affections we prize, and consider whether he could coherently hold both that nature's cruelty is an argument against benevolent design and that deliberately softening nature is a mistake.

Finally, I'd like an honest verdict on where the exercise breaks down: which parts of this question admit a reasonably grounded answer from Darwin's writings, and which parts require so much projection -- onto ecology, genetics, and a moral landscape he never inhabited -- that any confident attribution becomes fiction rather than inference. Where his views were genuinely in tension, say so rather than resolving the tension on his behalf.
