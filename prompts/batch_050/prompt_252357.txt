Build me a thought experiment that stress-tests Marx's claim that all profit ultimately traces back to surplus value extracted from wage labor. The setup: some corner of a capitalist economy gets fully automated — engineer the scenario so that whether profit can survive without exploited workers is the crux. Constraints: keep it economically realistic-ish (no post-scarcity magic, no government handouts quietly propping anyone up, markets stay competitive), the scenario itself should be a paragraph or two at most, and don't just stipulate that the firm turns a profit — the profit has to fall out of the setup, or conspicuously fail to.

Then walk through what Marx's framework predicts at two stages: (a) a single fully automated firm operating inside an economy that still mostly uses human labor, and (b) the whole economy going automated. For (a), explain where the theory says the firm's profit comes from given that it employs nobody. For (b), explain why the mechanism is supposed to break down. After that, name the exact structural feature doing the work in your case — I suspect it's the redistribution of total surplus value across firms via the equalization of profit rates, but tell me if it's something else — and say what happens to the scenario if you remove that feature. Finally, play defense for Marx: how would a committed defender stop your case from counting as a refutation? And going the other way, what outcome in the scenario would genuinely count against the labor theory of value — i.e., what result would change your mind about whether the case actually works?
