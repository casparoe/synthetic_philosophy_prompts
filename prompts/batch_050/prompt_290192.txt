Write an etiquette-advice column of roughly 600–750 words, titled "Ask the Scorekeeper," in the voice of a genteel, old-fashioned advice columnist (Miss Manners, not Dear Abby) whose entire worldview is that conversation is a rule-governed game played against a shared, continuously updated record of mutually accepted information. The column should answer three or four invented reader letters, and the humor must come from the columnist taking conversational pragmatics with complete seriousness: every piece of advice should be sound as theory and deranged as social guidance.

The columnist's doctrine should include, at minimum:

- Presupposition as the polite person's weapon. Advise letter-writers that a contested claim is best smuggled into the common ground by presupposing it rather than asserting it, since an assertion invites open disagreement while a presupposition forces any objector to do the conspicuously rude work of halting the proceedings to protest what was merely taken for granted. Include at least one letter in the mold of the classic question that is damning whether answered yes or no (the "stopped doing it?" trap), with the columnist calmly admiring its workmanship and explaining that both answers concede the smuggled content.

- Accommodation as exploitable reflex. The columnist should explain, approvingly, that hearers silently repair the conversational record so as to make sense of what was said — treating the needed assumption as having been on the books all along — and that this reflex can be farmed, but with craft: some presuppositions are too blatant or too central to be absorbed without protest, and timing and offhandedness matter, since backgrounded content slides in where foregrounded content would be challenged.

- Scorekeeping as adjudication. The columnist should treat conversational moves as governed by rules under which whatever is required to make a move count as correct play gets entered onto the score, and should issue rulings on which party played properly in a given dinner-party dispute, the way a bridge columnist judges bids.

- At least one letter about presupposition failure or projection: for instance, a reader who announced that they regret not yet having begun to insult the host and wants to know what exactly got communicated, or a reader anxious about what becomes of a presupposed assumption when the sentence carrying it is negated, put into a question, or tucked inside a conditional.

Constraints: the columnist stays deadpan and courteous throughout, and the satirical target is the gap between what the theory says conversation is and how humans actually experience it — the jokes land on that gap, not on a strawman. A philosopher of language should be able to nod along with every theoretical claim the columnist makes; the comedy is that no sane person would give this advice to a bride-to-be. Name no actual philosophers and cite no literature: the columnist speaks with the unearned, serene authority proper to the etiquette genre.
