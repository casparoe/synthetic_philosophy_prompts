I help run a community workshop (mostly woodworking, a bit of electronics) where about 15 of us have keys and access to the shared bank card for supplies and repairs. Any keyholder can buy equipment, book the space out to outside groups, rearrange the layout, basically whatever. We've had a couple of blowups lately — someone signed us up to host a weekend market that lost money, someone else "fixed" the drainage on their own initiative and flooded the neighbour's yard — and it's got me thinking about when one person should just be able to go ahead and do something that lands on everybody.

I never studied philosophy or economics or anything, but I went down a rabbit hole online and found this idea called the unilateralist's curse: if anyone in a group can individually green-light something, stuff gets green-lit too often, because the person who goes ahead is always the keenest one, and the keenest one is usually the one overestimating how good the idea is. Nobody has to be stupid or selfish, it just falls out of the setup. That really clicked for me. So here's roughly where I've landed — tell me if this is actually a coherent set of views or if I'm contradicting myself somewhere:

1. The curse is structural, not a character flaw. When a solo move goes wrong, it's not that the person messed up — given what they knew at the time, going ahead could of been perfectly reasonable. The setup is the problem, not them.

2. More keyholders makes it worse, not better. Every new member is one more chance that someone's over-optimistic about any given idea. So as we grow we need tighter rules on acting alone, not looser ones.

3. The fix sits with the individual: before you do something that commits everyone, mentally knock your own confidence down towards what the average member would think, and only go ahead if it still seems worth doing after that.

4. All that said — when someone does just go and do a thing, I think the rest of us should mostly back them. They cared enough to act, they probably saw something the rest of us missed, and nothing kills a volunteer group faster than punishing initiative.

5. Vetoes are the same disease in reverse. If any one person can kill a proposal, the most pessimistic member is basically running the place. So I'm against both: no unilateral action, no unilateral blocking — anything that binds the group gets talked through first.

6. Whoever takes a job on gets the final say in how it's done. If I volunteer to rebuild the workbench, I pick the design. Design by committee is how you end up with a bench nobody can use.

7. When someone's solo move costs us, they should wear it: apologise properly, and if money's involved, cover at least some of it out of their own pocket. Otherwise there's no accountability at all and people will just keep freewheeling.

So: which of these genuinely conflict, and which only look like they do? For the real ones, rank them by how serious they are, and for each one spell out the smallest change that would fix it and what I'd be giving up by making it. And if I only get to change one commitment, which single change does the most to clean this up? Don't just tell me to "find a balance" — I want to know what actually follows from what.
