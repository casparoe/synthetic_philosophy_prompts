I'm on a small ML safety team where two colleagues have been arguing for months and finally agreed to try an adversarial collaboration instead of another shouting match at the whiteboard. Mira is a realist about interpretability: she thinks language models genuinely contain features and circuits, that sparse autoencoders and activation patching are discovering structure that's really in there, roughly the way neuroscience discovers that some brain region does face recognition. Dan thinks she's projecting. His line is that a network is a blob of linear algebra that admits endlessly many equally good descriptions, that any probe or dictionary we train mostly tells us what *we* can compute from the activations, and that "features" are our concepts pasted onto directions that only look clean because we searched for clean-looking directions. Their concrete flashpoints: whether the linear representation hypothesis is a discovery or an assumption baked into the methods; whether superposition and polysemantic neurons show the network lacks neat representations or just that it compresses them; whether causal interventions (patching, ablations, scrubbing) settle anything Dan should care about; and whether the fact that you can rotate a basis and get a totally different but mathematically equivalent story means there's no fact of the matter, or just that you need extra structure (like what the model actually uses downstream) to privilege one decomposition.

Write the joint statement Mira and Dan could both sign. I want: (1) a numbered list of claims they can both endorse -- about what the math guarantees, what probes and SAEs do and don't show, what interventions establish, and what the neuroscience analogy does and doesn't license; (2) the remaining disagreements, each phrased in words the person holding that view would actually accept for thier own position (no strawmanning Dan as a nihilist who thinks the models are magic, no strawmanning Mira as someone who thinks SAE dictionaries are carved at the joints by definition); (3) the cruxes -- the sub-questions where settling one would actually move at least one of them; and (4) for each crux, some experiment, analysis, or result they'd both agree bears on it, with each of their predictions written down in advance. One thing I specifically want them to hash out: how much of the fight is about the word "representation" itself. Dan sometimes sounds like he has an extremely demanding bar (the model must *use* the direction in something like the way we use concepts) and Mira sometimes sounds like any direction that predicts anything counts. If part of their disagreement evaporates once that's disambiguated, the statement should say which part. And if there's a version of realism weak enough that Dan could accept it and a version strong enough that Mira actually needs it for safety-relevant claims (like being able to audit a model's internals before deployment), the statement should draw that line explicitly instead of leaving "realism" vague.
