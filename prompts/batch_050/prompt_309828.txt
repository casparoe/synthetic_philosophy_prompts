Here are the diagnostics I know for telling English indicative conditionals apart from subjunctive (counterfactual) ones:

1. Adams pairs — keeping the antecedent fixed and changing only the mood flips the verdict: on the standard evidence, the indicative claim that if Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did is assertible, while the subjunctive claim that if Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have is at best doubtful.
2. Morphology — indicatives take "will" or plain present in the consequent ("if he leaves, she'll be glad"), whereas subjunctives take "would" with a backshifted antecedent ("if he had left, she would have been glad").
3. Contraposition — subjunctives fail it (from "if I had struck the match, it would have lit" we cannot infer "if the match hadn't lit, I wouldn't have struck it"), while indicatives at least appear to contrapose more readily.
4. Antecedent openness — indicatives are typically felicitous only when the speaker treats the antecedent as epistemically open; subjunctives are the natural choice when the antecedent is taken to be false.
