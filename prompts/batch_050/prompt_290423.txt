I've been reading about hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy and I'm trying to sort out my own gut verdicts on when a historical explanation is legit and when it's just storytelling after the fact. Here are my reactions, tell me what principle (if any) is behind them:

- A historian says WWI was very likely to break out sooner or later even without the assassination at Sarajevo, because of the alliance system, mobilization timetables, and arms race dynamics. This feels like fine causal reasoning to me.
- The same historian says the war was inevitable. Somehow that feels like narrative overreach, even though it seems like almost the same claim as the first one.
- A pundit in 2009 publishes a book explaining that the financial crisis was bound to happen given leverage and incentives, though as far as anyone knows they never said anything of the sort before 2008. Feels like textbook hindsight bias.
- An economist who also never predicted the crash writes a careful post-hoc analysis of what caused it, and I think that's totally legitimate, even valuable. Same lack of prior prediction as the pundit, opposite verdict. I can't fully explain this one.
- A doctor says after a treatment fails "I knew all along this wouldn't work." Obvious hindsight bias, red flag.
- A coach says after the game "we lost because we couldn't control the midfield" — that feels like a genuine explanation even though it's also post hoc and fits the observed ending.
- Someone says the Soviet Union collapsed because planned economies simply can't work, full stop. Feels way too tidy, like the story is doing the work the evidence should be doing.

Is there one principle that gets all of these, or do I have to give something up? My hunch is it's about whether the explanation would have generated predictions ex ante, or maybe whether it depends on having already seen the outcome, but neither of those quite handles the pundit vs economist pair, where my verdict seems to turn on the mode of the claim rather than its content, which feels suspicious. Also: what does your principle say about a case I keep flip-flopping on — a historian who argues the Roman Empire fell largely because of lead posioning? It's specific, checkable, at least somewhat testable against evidence, but it was clearly assembled after the fact to fit a known ending.
