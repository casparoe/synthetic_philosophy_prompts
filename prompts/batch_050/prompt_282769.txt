I serve on the National Security Council of a mid-sized democracy — think something like Poland or South Korea in terms of military capacity — and we have been asked to contribute to a proposed coalition intervention in the Republic of Vessaria, a fictional but I hope plausible case I've constructed for a tabletop policy exercise I'm running with some colleagues (we're all longtime readers of rationalist blogs and take the exercise embarrassingly seriously). The scenario: Vessaria's government has begun systematically expelling an ethnic minority, roughly 800,000 people, from a border province; there is credible evidence of massacres in the low thousands so far, refugee flows are destabilizing two neighboring states, and the UN Security Council is deadlocked because one permanent member is Vessaria's patron. The coalition would go in without explicit UNSC authorization, relying instead on a Responsibility-to-Protect-style justification. Our military's contribution would be air power and logistics, not ground troops; the coalition's stated goal is a safe corridor and protected enclaves, not regime change — though everyone in the room knows that protecting enclaves from a government that regards them as internal enemies tends to slide toward something more.

Here is where I'd like your help, and I'd like you to approach this as a senior policy analyst with genuine expertise in both international-relations theory and the ethics of war — think carefully, and before drafting anything, spend some time brainstorming the strongest considerations on each side. Draft the memo I should circulate to the Council: a recommendation (support participation, decline, or support with conditions) with an actionable bottom line. The memo should grapple with the things that actually divide us. Some members argue from the deontological side: deliberate killing by state agents outside a UN mandate sets a precedent that strong states will exploit, and the "humanitarian" framing of Kosovo, Iraq, and Libya is exactly what makes them nervous. Others argue from expected consequences: the base rate of interventions producing stable postwar outcomes is poor, and air-power-only campaigns historically protect civilians imperfectly while removing our leverage over the aftermath. I want the memo to take the losing side seriously — whichever side that turns out to be — rather than strawmanning it.

Two further constraints, reflecting habits from my corner of the internet that I'd like the memo to embody. First, please include an explicit attempt at reference-class forecasting: pick what you think is the right reference class for this intervention (is it Kosovo, Libya, the no-fly zones, Rwanda as the non-intervention case, or something else?), defend that choice briefly, and state what it implies — but flag honestly how sensitive the recommendation is to that choice. Second, distinguish clearly between the decision-theoretic question (what should we do given our uncertainty?) and the norm question (what rule do we want states in general to follow?), and say whether your recommendation is the same under both framings. Aim for something a real council could vote on: under 800 words for the memo itself, with your brainstorming kept separate.
