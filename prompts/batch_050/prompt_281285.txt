So I sit on the capital projects committee for a mid-sized coastal city -- about 12 of us, we meet monthly, and roughly once a quarter something lands on the table with a 50-to-100-year footprint: a storm barrier, a reservoir expansion, a decision about whether to fund the full decommissioning reserve for an old industrial site or just "monitor it." Nobody on the committee is an economist or a philosopher, I'm the closest thing and I read this stuff as a hobby. Decisions over about $5M need a written rationale that the city auditor and, awkwardly, a citizens' oversight panel can later pick apart.

I want a written procedure -- a checklist or flowchart-in-words, runnable in the 20-ish minutes of discussion we actually get per item -- that operationalizes what the philosophy/economics of intergenerational justice would actually say about how to weigh these decisions. Specifically it needs to handle three things without requiring anyone to have heard of them:

1. Discounting the future. I know the standard move is a discount rate, and I know there's a fight between "people later count for less just because they're later" and "people later will probably be richer so a dollar helps them less." Turn that fight into actual questions the committee answers in order, including what number range is defensible to write down and what to do when we genuinely can't estimate future wealth. No "consult the literature" steps -- it has to terminate.

2. The people-who-might-not-exist problem. A lot of the people our barrier protects in 2085 only exist because of decisions being made now (where housing gets zoned, whether the neighborhood is still inhabited at all). I want the procedure to force us to classify the affected parties into, roughly, "people who'll exist either way" vs "people whose existence depends partly on choices like this one," and then say how the weighting should differ -- or make the case that it shouldn't -- rather than letting us silently count hypothetical future residents the same as current ones, which is what our cost-benefit consultant does by default.

3. Side constraints. Some options have small-probability catastrophic tails (reservoir failure) that pure expected-value math swallows. The procedure needs a step where a catastrophic or rights-violating outcome can veto an option that wins on the numbers, with a stated threshold for when the veto triggers so it isn't just "anyone can yell catastrophe."

Also: worked applications to two realistic cases for us -- (a) the storm barrier, benefits mostly 2060+, and (b) whether to fully fund the decommissioning reserve now vs. a cheaper monitoring regime -- including at least one where the procedure spits out an answer the committee politically won't want, so we can see that coming. Then be honest about which steps are judgment calls in procedural clothing, and what the procedure flat-out can't settle. Keep the whole thing to something that fits on two printed pages, we actualy have to use it.
