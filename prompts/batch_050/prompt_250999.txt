My mate Dave and I have been having the same arguement on and off for about a year now. He's a programmer, and his position is that the whole debate about whether an economy can be run from the centre is basically settled by technology: supermarkets already predict what millions of us will buy next week, Amazon shifts an ungodly number of parcels with barely a human deciding anything, so give a well-built system enough data and computing power and it could organise production and distribution for a whole country, minus the waste and chaos of everyone just doing their own thing. I keep saying it wouldn't work, he asks why, and my reasons turn to mush. What gets me is that people were apparently having this exact row long before computers existed, so the answer can't just be "the machines aren't fast enough yet". Something else has to be doing the work, and I want to know what it is.

So before the next round at the pub, I want the checklist first, not a verdict. What would any proposed way of organising a country's economy — planned by a committee of experts, planned by Dave's data-hoovering system, ordinary markets, or some mix — have to be able to do before you'd even entertain living under it? I want each requirement with the everyday case behind it, the way a decent exam question comes with the situation that motivates it. To seed it, here's what I suspect belongs on the list, though I've only got running a café with my sister for nine years to go on: it has to function using knowledge that's scattered across millions of people, half of which can't even be written down (I can tell the bread order's wrong by the smell of the place before I can tell you why); it has to cope with genuinely new things nobody's seen before, not just shuffle a fixed pile of existing goods around; it has to get people to report honestly what they need and what they can actually produce when fibbing might pay; and when a plan flops, someone specific has to absorb the loss rather than it dissolving into the ether. Tell me which of those are genuine requirements and which are just me projecting my job onto the whole country.

Then the finer points, which are the bits I actually care about. Which requirements are non-negotiable and which you could trade away? Is there any pair on the final list that can't be satisfied at the same time, because if so that's a result in itself and I want it before I waste another evening? Which single requirement disqualifies the most proposals people seriously put forward? And which demands do each side make that are really just their politics wearing a lab coat? Afterwards, briefly, score the three obvious contenders — expert committee, Dave's algorithm, markets with a safety net — against the list (a table's fine for that part, keep it short). The standard for "good enough" throughout: a system I'd personally have to live under for the next forty years, in a country of sixty-odd million strangers, not a commune of fifty mates. Could you answer in British English, please?
