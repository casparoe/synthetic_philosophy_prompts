I'm a novelist -- three books published, one quietly remaindered -- and this year I've made the mistake of chairing the jury for the Halloran Prize, a mid-sized fiction award run by a charitable trust in Edinburgh. We owe the trustees a written opinion in ten days recommending a winner, and as chair I've been landed with drafting it. I need your help, because we are deadlocked and the deadlock is philosophically interesting, which is making it worse.

It's come down to two novels. Book A is technically dazzling: narrated by a property developer who gently ruins a string of elderly investors, and the prose does absolute backflips while never once stepping outside his voice to tell you he's monstrous. Half the jury think it's the most accomplished novel submitted in the prize's history. The other half think its brilliance is precisely the problem -- that it's cold fireworks, a performance of mastery rather than the thing itself, and that rewarding it means rewarding a book that has made cruelty charming. Book B is quiet, morally serious, beautifully built; everyone admires it, nobody's pulse races, and it would be the safe choice nobody celebrates.

Here's what sent me back to Hume. At our last meeting I realised both camps use exactly the same words. Everyone praises "elegance" and "spirit" and "accomplishment". Everyone insists the other side's favourite lacks them. Nobody is arguing about vocabulary, and yet we're split 3-3 and the shared language is doing nothing at all. Then I reread David Hume's essay "Of the Standard of Taste" (in his Four Dissertations, 1757), and this passage has been rattling round my head because it describes my jury far too well:

"There are certain terms in every language, which import blame, and others praise; and all men, who use the same tongue, must agree in their application of them. Every voice is united in applauding elegance, propriety, simplicity, spirit in writing; and in blaming fustian, affectation, coldness, and a false brilliant: But when critics come to particulars, this seeming unanimity vanishes; and it is found, that they had affixed a very different meaning to their expressions."

So, the draft. Write the jury's written opinion to the trustees recommending a winner. Take an actual position -- no split award, the deed of gift forbids it (I checked, twice). But the reasoning has to genuinely work through Hume's passage rather than nodding at it: is our deadlock a disguised disagreement about what "accomplished" means, which we might talk our way out of, or a real disagreement about the book, which we can't? Is Book A what Hume would call "a false brilliant", and could a jury ever establish that rather than just assert it? And if the seeming unanimity really does vanish the moment you reach particulars, what is a prize verdict even claiming to be -- something more than a headcount of six people's tastes, or should the opinion be honest that it isn't? Whichever way you reccomend, give the strongest possible version of the losing camp's case, because three very good novelists have to sign this document and live with each other afterwards.

Practical bits: around 700-800 words, the Hume passage not counting towards that; British English please, the trustees definately notice; and make it readable enough that I won't die of boredom reading it aloud at the prize dinner.
