In my philosophy of religion seminar we ended up talking about Mother Teresa, not as a saint-or-fraud culture-war case but as a possible test case for divine hiddenness. The relevant facts, as I understand them, are these: she reported feeling called by God to begin her work among the poor, but private writings published after her death indicate that for decades she experienced little or no felt divine presence, often describing spiritual darkness while continuing her religious practices and public ministry. So this isn’t the textbook example of someone investigating God from the outside and finding no evidence. It’s a devout believer whose life was organized around God yet who apparently lacked the experiential assurance many theists think a loving God would provide.

Analyze this episode through contemporary arguments from divine hiddenness, especially Schellenberg’s idea that a perfectly loving God would ensure that anyone capable of a conscious relationship with God, and not resistant to one, has evidence sufficient for belief. First map the case carefully: does Mother Teresa count as “nonresistant,” given that she believed and wanted communion with God? Does the argument concern propositional belief, trust, felt presence, or some stronger relational state? Then consider whether her case strengthens hiddenness arguments by showing that even exceptionally receptive believers can experience prolonged divine absence, or weakens them because her perseverance suggests that a relationship with God can survive without subjective assurance. Please take seriously the main theistic responses--the value of free choice, soul-making, the possibility that hiddenness is temporary or spiritually purposeful, and the danger of confusing divine silence with divine absence--but don’t let any of them become a blank check that could explain every possible case.

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: what does the episode reveal about hiddenness theories themselves? For example, does it show that “nonresistance” is too coarse-grained, because someone can be receptive at one time, resistant in some respect, or uncertain what response God wants? Does it expose a tension between treating religious experience as evidence and treating obedient perseverance as evidence? And can a theory specify in advance what kind or duration of hiddenness would count against perfect love without becoming unfalsifiable? I’m less interested in whether Mother Teresa was admirable than in whether her inner life gives us a genuinely hard case for both sides.
