I'm on the senate of a mid-sized public university in a fictional German-speaking country where university has been tuition-free since the 70s, funded out of general taxation. The finance ministry, facing a deficit, has proposed a reform: cut the block grant to universities by about 20%, and let universities replace the lost money with means-tested tuition fees, capped around 3,000 euros a year, payable only by students whose family income is above the national median, with an income-contingent loan so nobody pays upfront. Graduates below a certain salary after finishing never repay anything.

The senate votes in six weeks on whether to endorse the plan, oppose it outright, or issue a neutral statement. Things have gotten heated. One camp says free higher education is a social right and any fee, however means-tested, is the thin end of the wedge -- the cap will creep up, the threshold will creep down, and in twenty years we'll have the Anglo-American mess. The other camp points out that under the current system, the baker's daughter who goes straight into an apprenticeship pays taxes her whole life that subsidize the law-firm partner's son's six years of study, and calls that a regressive transfer dressed up as equality.

Draft the memo I'll circulate to the senate before the vote. It should recommend one of the three options (endorse / oppose / neutral) as our official position, with a clear bottom line up front. It needs to engage seriously with the strongest argument on whichever side loses -- not a strawman. Two things it specifically has to address: first, the debt-aversion worry, i.e. that even no-upfront-cost, income-contingent loans deter first-generation and low-income students from applying at all, and whether that's a knockdown objection or a manageable design problem; second, whether neutrality is actually a defensible stance here or just the senate ducking responsibility while the decision gets made for us. Around 600-800 words, written so senators who aren't economists can follow it.
