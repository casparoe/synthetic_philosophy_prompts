I teach a grad seminar on value theory and every year the wrong kind of reasons problem wrecks my students in the same way: they can recite the demon case (demon threatens to punish everyone unless you admire him, so you have a reason to admire him, but he's not admirable) and then can't tell, when a new case shows up in a paper, whether it's a genuine counterexample to a fitting-attitude analysis or just a normal reason about the costs and benefits of having an attitude. I want a procedure I can hand them -- a checklist or ordered set of tests, runnable in a few minutes -- for classifying a proposed "reason to feel F toward X" as right-kind or wrong-kind. It should at minimum force them to: state exactly which attitude and which object the reason attaches to, check whether the reason points at features of the object that bear on whether the attitude fits it or at features of having the attitude, run some version of the counterfactual test (would the reason survive if holding the attitude had no consequences?), and handle the sneaky middle cases like reasons to *want* to feel something, or reasons that cite your relationship to the object rather than the object's own merits. Be explicit about which steps are mechanical and which are judgment calls the checklist can't settle for you. Then show it working on three cases: the classic demon, someone who has a reason to feel grateful to a benefactor purely because gratitude is good for their own mental health, and a case where the procedure gives a verdict my students will find counterintuitive (your pick which). End by saying what the procedure can't do -- e.g., whether it assumes object-given/state-given is a real distinction rather than a stipulation we're just helping ourselves to.
