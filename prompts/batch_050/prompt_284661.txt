A colleague and I have been informally logging the moral verdicts of a mutual friend, Dana, whenever rescue-type cases come up in conversation, and we've noticed she seems to be running some kind of rule we'd like to reverse-engineer. Three data points so far. First, the classic: walking alone through a park, she sees a toddler face-down in an ornamental pond; wading in means ruining a pair of suede boots she loves. She says rescue is obviously mandatory and that anyone who hesitated would be a moral monster. Second: she knows that roughly $300 given to a well-vetted global health charity would, in expectation, prevent a child's death from malaria somewhere she will never visit. She gives occasionally but insists that declining to give is not wrong -- merely less admirable than giving. Third: at her office, colleagues raised a fund for a coworker's daughter's surgery; after everyone else had contributed what they were going to, the fund was $300 short, and the organizer came to Dana directly as the last person to ask. She says she was obligated to close the gap and that it would have been wrong to refuse.

Now the new case, which is where we want your help. Dana attends a town-hall meeting of about two hundred residents. A specific local family -- introduced by name, standing at the front with their children -- has lost its home to a fire, and the organizers pass a collection, asking each attendee to chip in; the suggested share works out to about $300, which Dana can afford without real sacrifice, and no single attendee is the linchpin, since the target can be reached by many combinations of givers. We'd like to know what Dana will say about someone who pockets the suggested envelope and gives nothing. In working this out, please keep in mind a sentence from Peter Singer's "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972) that I think cuts against one natural reading of Dana's track record: "The principle makes no distinction between cases in which I am the only person who could possibly do anything and cases in which I am just one among millions in the same position."

Concretely, I'd like the following. First, lay out two or three distinct rules, each fully consistent with all three of Dana's recorded verdicts, stated precisely enough that they actually generate predictions (I'm not interested in rules so vague they fit anything). Second, show what each rule predicts about the town-hall case, and confirm that at least two of them genuinely diverge there. Third, give your single best prediction of Dana's verdict, with your reasons -- for instance, which rule best explains why she splits cases one and two the way she does, and how her likely verdict bears on the Singer sentence quoted above: does accepting that sentence force a revision of one of her earlier verdicts, or is her pattern an implicit rejection of it? Fourth, describe one further hypothetical case that would best discriminate between the two leading candidate rules if the town-hall case leaves them tied. Please end your reply with a final line in exactly this form: \answer{required} or \answer{not required}, followed by a colon and the name of the rule you take Dana to be running -- e.g. \answer{required: <rule name>}.
