I'm grading first-year philosophy essays and would like a second opinion on one. The essay question, which students were told to answer in about 300 words with reference to the Second Meditation of Descartes's Meditations on First Philosophy, was: "After the cogito passage, Descartes takes himself to have found something that survives even the most radical doubt. What exactly does he take himself to have established at this point in the Meditations, what entitles him to it, and what does it not yet establish?"

Here is the student's answer:

Descartes begins by supposing that some supremely powerful deceiver is constantly misleading him about everything, including arithmetic and the external world. He then notices that the deceiver cannot deceive him about his own existence, since he could not be deceived unless he existed to be deceived. So "cogito ergo sum" survives the doubt: whenever Descartes thinks it, it is necessarily true. Importantly, this is not a syllogism requiring the extra premise that whatever thinks exists; Descartes insists it is grasped directly, by a simple intuition of the mind, which is why no deceptive god can pry the conclusion apart from the experience of thinking it. What the passage establishes, then, is that the self is a thinking, immaterial substance whose nature is wholly distinct from the body — this is the "first principle" of his philosophy, the indubitable foundation on which all later knowledge, including the proofs of God, will be built. Because the cogito holds at every moment of doubt, it secures a stable, persisting self whose continued existence throughout the Meditations is guaranteed from this point on. Descartes can therefore proceed to rebuild knowledge without worrying that the thinker himself might vanish or turn out to be bodily.
