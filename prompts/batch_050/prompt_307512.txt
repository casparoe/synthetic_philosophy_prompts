I help run a small family business, and over the years I've become quietly obsessed with a pattern in how people talk about decisions, and I'm hoping you can tell me whether it's a known phenomenon with a proper name. Here's the pattern, in a few real instances. First: a manager approves a scheduling change purely to cut costs, fully aware that it will probably hurt staff morale. Morale tanks. At the meeting afterward, everyone says some version of "he knew exactly what he was doing — he chose this." Second: the same manager approves a different change, also for cost reasons, and it happens to rescue a struggling employee's career as a side effect. Nobody says he chose that outcome; they say it just worked out, or that he got lucky. Third: I've noticed it in politics — a policy has a foreseeable bad side effect and people say the politician "did it deliberately," but a foreseeable good side effect and suddenly she "can't take credit for that." And here's the part that really puzzles me: I do it to myself. When I stayed late on a project that failed, I told myself I should have known, that in some sense I brought it on; when a similar gamble paid off, I honestly felt I'd been fortunate rather than that I'd made the success happen. So my first question: does this asymmetry — where whether we describe an outcome as chosen, intended, or brought-about seems to track whether we disapprove of it — have an established name? And is the self-directed version the same phenomenon or a different one?

Two complications I'd like addressed rather than glossed over. One: I don't think this is just hypocrisy or an ordinary double standard about praise and blame, because it seems to change what people think actually happened, not merely how they judge it — the same person will flatly say "he caused this" about the bad outcome and "it was one factor among many" about the good one, even when the causal story is identical. So if there's a separate name for the way this bleeds into judgments of what caused what, I want to know that too, and how the two patterns relate. Two: I keep going back and forth on whether this is a flaw in our thinking — a bias to be corrected — or whether it's a deep and maybe legitimate feature of how concepts like "intended" and "caused" work in the first place. I'd like your verdict on that, but only after you've laid out the strongest case for each side.

Please answer as a philosopher with real expertise in both the philosophical and the empirical study of how ordinary people deploy concepts like intention, choice, and causation. Before committing to an answer, briefly brainstorm the candidate concepts or literatures this pattern might fall under, note which ones you've set aside and why, and then identify the best fit — and if nothing established fits exactly, say so plainly and coin a term with a definition and some boundary cases. I won't be satisfied with a bare label: I want to know precisely what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are, including whether it really does extend to self-judgment and to causal talk. Throughout, flag how confident you are in each claim, and end by telling me what would change your mind — which argument or piece of evidence would lead you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong. Please write your answer in American English.
