I'm teaching a philosophy of religion seminar next term and I'm tired of students treating "middle knowledge" as a magic word that solves providence. Before we read anything, I want them to have a checklist: what does any adequate theory of how God knows what free creatures *would* do actually have to deliver? So write the desiderata. For each one give the case or consideration that motivates it -- e.g. it has to make sense of prophecy that turns on someone's free choice (Peter's denial type cases), it has to explain what petitionary prayer is even doing if the future is already settled, it has to say what makes counterfactuals of creaturely freedom true if the creature never exists and nothing causes the choice (the grounding worry), it has to deal with the fact that God's own free choices seem to be the same kind of thing as ours so if middle knowledge works for creatures why doesn't God have middle knowledge of what *he* would freely do, and it has to still function if creaturely choices are genuinely chancy rather than determined-by-character. Also say which desiderata you think are real requirements and which are just Molinist (or Thomist) prejudices dressed up as neutral criteria, and flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied -- that's the interesting part, if the list itself is inconsistent that's a result. Then, briefly and I mean briefly, score the main contenders against the list: Molinism, simple-foreknowledge views, Thomist concurrence, and open theism. Keep the weight on the criteria, the scoring is just to show the list has teeth.
