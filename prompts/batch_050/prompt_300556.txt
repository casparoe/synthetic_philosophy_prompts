I work as a software engineer, with no formal training in philosophy or genetics, and for the past year I have been a participant in a long-running internal debate at my company about hiring and professional development. Roughly, one camp holds that engineering ability is mostly innate and that we should pour our resources into selection, while the other holds that structured training and mentorship account for most of the differences we observe among developers. To think the matter through I read a fair amount of popular and semi-technical material on behavioral genetics, and I have drafted an internal post defending a position. Before I publish it, I want a consistency check on the view itself, because I have a nagging sense that I may be straddling two incompatible ways of appealing to the science. Here is where I have landed. (1) Heritability is a population-level statistic: it measures the share of variance in a trait associated with genetic variance within a particular population and range of environments, and it is a category error to say of an individual that her intelligence is, say, sixty percent genetic. (2) Heritability estimated within one group is uninformative about the causes of differences between groups: genetically variable seed planted in uniform rich soil can show highly heritable height within that field, while the average difference from a field planted in poor soil is entirely environmental in origin. (3) High heritability does not imply low malleability: height is highly heritable, yet average heights rose markedly within a few generations as nutrition improved, so a trait can be strongly genetically driven at a given moment and still shift considerably when environments change. (4) Because cognitive ability and educational attainment remain substantially heritable even in wealthy, comparatively egalitarian countries, I expect most training programs and early interventions to have small and fading effects; in a sufficiently equal society the remaining differences in outcomes are mostly genetic in origin, and organizations (and governments) do better to invest in selection and matching than in remediation. (5) Twin and adoption studies, whatever their imperfections, yield roughly trustworthy heritability estimates for the populations actually studied. (6) Polygenic scores, though built from the same population-level associations, can support useful probabilistic predictions about individuals — good enough, for instance, to inform embryo screening for disease risk.

What I am asking for is an audit of this set of commitments, not a verdict on my policy leanings or a reading list. Which pairs are in genuine tension, and which only appear to be? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating every possible friction as equally serious, and for each one, say exactly which inference creates the problem, what would have to give, and what that revision would cost the overall position. If some apparent tension dissolves on closer inspection, explain why, since I want the post to preempt that objection rather than stumble into it. Finally, if a single revision would restore the most coherence to the set as a whole, name it and be specific about which commitment has to move.
