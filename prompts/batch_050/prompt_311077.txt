But now redescribe the same weather using different vocabulary: keep "hot", but replace "rainy" with "rainy-if-and-only-if-hot" and "windy" with "windy-if-and-only-if-hot" (call them rainy* and windy*). The actual truth in the new vocabulary is (YES, NO, NO)... wait let me redo this: hot=YES so rainy* = (rainy iff hot) = (NO iff YES) = NO, windy* = (YES iff YES) = YES. Truth: (YES, NO, YES). Model A translated: (YES, YES, YES). Model B translated: (NO, YES, NO)... hold on, B says hot=NO, rainy=NO so rainy* = (NO iff NO) = YES, windy* = (NO iff NO) = YES. So B becomes (NO, YES, YES): wrong on hot AND rainy*, right only on windy*. Hmm, in my head A came out ahead by two bits in one vocabulary and B caught up or passed in the other, my coworker had it written down properly on the whiteboard and the ranking definately flipped. You get the idea though: the "count the matching bits" measure depends on how you choose to describe the world, and the choice of vocabulary seems totally arbitrary, so "closer to the truth" seems to not be a fact about the models at all but a fact about the language.
