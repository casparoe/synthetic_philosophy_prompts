I'm applying for a one-semester senior thesis grant in philosophy of mathematics and need a two-page proposal. Here's my half-formed idea. Benacerraf's multiple-reductions argument is usually taken to show that numbers cannot be sets: since the von Neumann ordinals make 2 ∈ 4 true and Zermelo's sequence makes it false, and both reductions do all the mathematical work required, there is no fact of the matter about which sets the numbers are. Structuralists conclude that numbers are nothing over and above positions in a structure. My suspicion is that the argument secretly relies on a stronger premise than anyone defends: that if numbers were genuine objects, every question about them would have a determinate answer. I want to test that premise against non-mathematical cases where we tolerate multiple realization without anxiety — a chess position instantiated on different boards and in a computer's memory, a melody transposed into different keys, a bank account realized in different ledgers — and ask whether these are genuine objects with indeterminate identities, or cases where we have simply learned not to ask certain questions, and then whether arithmetic is relevantly like them.

The proposal should state the question precisely (does the identity problem refute object-platonism, or only the stronger thesis that real objects admit no indeterminate cross-realization facts?); explain why the standard structuralist responses — ante rem, in re, and eliminative — leave this comparative angle unaddressed; give a week-by-week plan for the semester, including which analogy cases to develop first and how to extract a general criterion from them; describe what a positive result would look like (a principled test for when cross-realization identity questions are ill-posed, plus a demonstration of whether 2 ∈ 4 passes it); and state honestly what would count as failure — for instance, if every analogy case turns out to involve merely epistemic indeterminacy, the project would end up strengthening the structuralist rather than rescuing objects.
