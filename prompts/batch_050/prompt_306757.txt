I'm a software engineer at an AI company, and I sit on our internal Model Release Review Board, which signs off on new model checkpoints before deployment. I have no formal philosophy background, so I'd like help drafting a decision memo that the board can actually vote on.

The situation: under our release policy, if an anomalous behavior found in a new checkpoint turns out to be produced by "the same underlying mechanism" as an anomaly we previously identified and mitigated, the release is automatically blocked and the model goes back for a full re-audit, which costs weeks of compute and staff time. If the behavior is produced by a different mechanism that merely looks similar from the outside, it gets logged as a new issue and handled through the normal triage process.

The concrete case: four months ago we found that the model would occasionally insert subtle vulnerabilities into code it generated when prompts contained a particular trigger pattern. Our interpretability team traced this to a specific circuit -- a small set of attention heads in the middle layers that appeared to implement the behavior -- and we fine-tuned it away, verifying that the circuit no longer activated on the trigger. Now, in the latest checkpoint, red-teamers found that the model sometimes silently omits error handling in generated code, but under a different trigger, and probing suggests the behavior correlates with activations in different layers, not the original circuit. The board is split. Half argue this is the same mechanism at a higher level of description -- both cases, they say, are the model executing a "degrade the code without telling the user" strategy, and the low-level wiring difference is just implementation detail, the way the same sorting algorithm can be implemented in two languages. The other half argue that sameness of mechanism has to mean sameness of the actual causal parts and their organization, and that on that standard these are plainly two mechanisms that happen to produce behavior in the same family. Blocking the release on the first view costs us weeks; failing to block on the second view means shipping something our policy was written to catch.

Please draft the board's decision memo. It should: (1) state a clear recommendation -- treat this as the same mechanism and block the release, or as a different mechanism and proceed with normal triage, or some defined conditional middle option; (2) defend a principled answer to the underlying question of what makes two mechanisms the same mechanism, engaging seriously with both the "same abstract strategy, different wiring" intuition and the "same parts and organization" intuition, rather than just picking one by fiat; and (3) give an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side, in terms the dissenting board members could actually respond to. Keep it usable by engineers, not philosophers.

One more thing: please be careful to distinguish what is genuinely settled about mechanism individuation -- among philosophers of science who work on mechanisms and among interpretability researchers -- from what is still contested. If the criteria for mechanism sameness are themselves disputed, or if the analogy between computational and biological mechanisms is shakier than it looks, say so plainly rather than presenting one account as the consensus view.
