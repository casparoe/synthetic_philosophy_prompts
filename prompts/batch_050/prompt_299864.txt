I'm a junior at an American high school and I help run our school's ethics and philosophy club, which has about sixteen regular members and meets for one hour after school. For an upcoming meeting I want to run a structured activity on the question of what actually counts as corruption. The standard classroom treatment of this topic tends to present bribery as obvious and then gesture at cultural differences, and students nod along without their intuitions ever being tested. I'd like something that makes them genuinely unsure where the line is, ideally before anyone tells them there is a debate about it.

The core idea I want them to feel is this: whether a transfer of money, gifts, or favors is corrupt seems to depend on things like whether it happens before or after the favor, whether it is expected by everyone involved, whether it is openly known in the community, and whether refusing it would itself be a kind of insult -- and these features vary enormously across societies. In some places, giving a gift to an official is a basic norm of respect, and a system of ongoing reciprocal favors between patrons and clients is how people without connections get anything done at all. I want my club members to catch themselves applying a rule in one scenario and then violating it in the next, and to notice that the rule they thought they had was something like "what my culture happens to find normal."

Please design the activity itself: the scenario or scenarios, the roles students play, the sequence of decisions or judgments they have to make, and how the situations should escalate so that each new case breaks the rule they formed from the previous one. I'd prefer they vote or commit to judgments publicly in some way, so that they can't quietly revise their earlier positions once things get uncomfortable. No lecture from me beforehand and no vocabulary like clientelism or quid pro quo until the debrief.

Then include the debrief: how to map specific moments from the activity onto the real philosophical question of whether there are universal anti-corruption standards or whether Western norms are just one local custom dressed up as principle. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with teenagers (for example, I suspect some students will treat it as a game of spotting the "right answer" rather than reporting their actual judgment), how to handle that, and one feature of real-world corruption that a classroom simulation simply cannot reproduce, so I know what I'll have to supply through discussion afterward.
