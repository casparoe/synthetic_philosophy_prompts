Hey, can I get a consistency check on something? I write historical fiction and I'm three drafts into a novel set around the transatlantic trade, and its only just occured to me that I've been working off a set of moral ground rules I never actually wrote down. Here they are, as best I can reconstruct them:

a) The "man of his time" defence is legitimate. You can't blame people for failing to see what nobody in their world had the conceptual tools to see. Blame requires could-have-known-better, and could-have is relative to the moral horizon of the era.

b) But the defence dies the moment there were audible contemporary critics. Which is why I think Columbus gets no pass: plenty of his own contemporaries were appalled by what he did in the Indies, the Spanish crown literally had him arrested and shipped home in chains over it. If a Las Casas could see it, the capacity existed, full stop.

c) My protagonist is a 1760s Liverpool merchant with a silent partnership in two slave ships. Devoted father, churchwarden, gives generously to the local infirmary, has never personally been to Africa or the Caribbean, has never raised a hand to anyone. The novel needs him to stay basically sympathetic — the reader should grieve for him at the end, not convict him.

d) I have zero patience for the idea that judging the dead is just "presentism." Judgement of historical figures is legitimate precisely because it isn't about them — they can't be harmed — it's about us and what we're prepared to endorse now.

e) Symmetry: whatever excuse the era gives the bad ones, it also gives the good ones. If Columbus's contemporaries couldn't help their moral horizon, Wilberforce couldn't help his either — he's not a hero, just lucky in when and where he landed. I'm at peace with that.

f) My narrator is modern-voiced but must never be anachronistic: nobody in 1765 gets to sound like a 21st century anti-racist.

My writing group keeps saying this is all fine, and I'm not so sure. I'm not asking which of these are true — I'm asking which ones are pulling against each other. Tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which ones only look like they are, and for each real tension what the cheapest fix is — and I'm counting changes to the book as fixes (his biography, the decade, whatever), not just changes to the principles. Rank them by severity, and if there's one single revision that buys the most consistency, name it.
