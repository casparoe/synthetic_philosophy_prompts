I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a long-form feature about the moral and metaphysical puzzles of standing by — cases where a bad outcome results not from anything anyone did, but from what nobody did. To give readers an intuitive entry point before I get into the theory, I want to open the piece with a short work of fiction. Please write me that story, of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, built around the following scenario and constraints.

The setup: a preventable death (or comparable serious harm — your choice) occurs in a setting where two different people each had a duty or opportunity to intervene, and each failed to act. Crucially, either intervention alone would have been sufficient to prevent the harm, and each person refrained partly because they assumed — or hoped — the other would step in. So neither omission was individually necessary for the outcome, yet the outcome happened because of the two omissions taken together.

Within that frame, the story must do the following:

1. Stage a genuine argument between two characters about whether an omission can be a cause at all. One character defends the view that causes are events, and that failing to act is literally nothing — no energy transferred, no mechanism, hence no causation, only blame. The other character presses a counterfactual line: if you had acted, the harm would not have occurred, and that dependence is enough to make the omission part of the causal story. Neither character should be a straw man; give both the strongest version of their position, and let the dispute have real emotional stakes rather than reading like a seminar transcript.

2. Include a late revelation that complicates the counterfactual analysis: evidence surfaces suggesting that at least one of the interventions, had it been attempted, would probably have failed anyway (some hidden factor — a broken piece of equipment, a physiological detail, an earlier unnoticed event). A character should then have to reckon with the question: if my acting wouldn't have changed anything, was my not acting still a cause of what happened — and does the answer change how guilty I should feel?

3. Distinguish, somewhere in the narrative texture rather than in explicit exposition, between causation and moral responsibility: let the reader feel that these come apart, and that settling who caused the death does not by itself settle who deserves blame.

End the story on a note of genuine resolution for at least one character — not necessarily forgiveness or absolution, but some earned movement — rather than trailing off into open questions. Keep the prose literary and concrete: specific setting, specific bodies and objects, no characters who exist only as mouthpieces.
