I'm a novelist at work on a book whose chapters branch into alternate versions of its protagonist's life, and after my editor told me the conceit risked reading as a lesser Everything Everywhere All at Once, I rewatched the film and found myself stuck less on craft than on the metaphysics. The film seems to commit to something close to David Lewis's modal realism: every choice Evelyn Wang might have made is lived out in a world no less real than hers, and the drama comes from her gaining access to those other lives. But the more I think about it, the more I suspect the film is philosophically incoherent in instructive ways, and I'd like a careful reading before I decide what my own book is committed to.

Read the film through modal realism, and be precise about where the fit works and where it breaks. On Lewis's view, possible worlds are causally isolated from one another, which the film's verse-jumping flatly violates, and a person existing in more than one world is, for Lewis, a matter of counterpart relations rather than strict identity. Does that distinction matter to the story the film tells? Evelyn's grief and guilt concern Joy, her daughter, but the Jobu Tupaki she confronts is presumably a counterpart of Joy rather than Joy herself — and the film's emotional logic seems to require that this difference not matter, or even that Evelyn's ability to feel her counterparts' lives collapses it. I'd like to know whether that's a confusion the film is merely exploiting or a thesis it is actually defending.

The harder question, for my purposes, is the moral one. If every possible outcome of every choice is equally actual, then nothing Evelyn does changes the total sum of suffering and joy across the worlds, and the film gestures at exactly this despair through Jobu Tupaki's everything-bagel nihilism before answering it with an ethic of kindness toward the people in this life. Lewis's own response to the analogous objection to modal realism is roughly indexical: moral concern is properly directed at what happens in one's own world, since that is all one's actions can affect. Is the film's answer just Lewis's answer with sentiment attached, or does verse-jumping — the experience of one's counterparts' pain as one's own — give the film a different and possibly stronger reply, one grounded in empathy rather than indexical restriction? Where does the film resist the modal-realist reading altogether, and what, if anything, does it contribute to this debate that the philosophical arguments alone do not? I want to steal its insights honestly rather than its plot.
