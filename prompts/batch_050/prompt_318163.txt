I'm a novelist, and my current book has backed me into a philosophy corner. Almost everything the reader learns in it arrives secondhand: a small town's gossip chain about what happened at the lake, a dying woman's account of something she swears she saw forty years ago, a kid repeating what his uncle told him. So I went down a rabbit hole on the epistemology of testimony and found this very old, apparently unresolved fight. On one side, the reductionists (Hume's camp, roughly): testimony only gives you knowledge if you have independent, non-testimonial reasons to trust the speaker — your track record with them, their incentives, corroboration. On the other side, the anti-reductionists (Reid's camp): testimony is a basic source of justification, like perception or memory, and it's innocent until proven guilty — you don't need positive reasons, just the absence of defeaters. This argument has been running since the 1700s and as far as I can tell nobody has landed a punch.

I was complaining about this over drinks with two writer friends and we ended up reenacting the whole debate about my own plot, which is what made me suspicious. My hunch, as someone who builds unreliable narrators for a living: the two sides aren't really disagreeing about testimony at all, they're disagreeing about what a justification has to *be*. The reductionist wants justification to be the sort of thing you could in principle produce and check; the anti-reductionist thinks that demand is itself the mistake, since you can't check perception against non-perception either. In other words it's a fight about where the burden of proof sits, wearing a hearsay costume. But I might be wrong — maybe there IS a real disagreement underneath, about how children actually calibrate trust, or about what goes wrong in pure-gossip chains like the one in my book, where everyone is honest but the original source is nobody anyone can name.

So: diagnose this debate for me, don't adjudicate it. Is the disagreement verbal, substantive, or a substantive one hiding inside a verbal one? What would each side have to concede to lose, and is there anything — a clean distinction, some developmental psychology, a formal result about chains of testimony — that could actually end it, or are these just two internally consistent starting points and the smart move is to stop trying? Use my gossip-chain scenario as a test case if it helps. Lead with your bottom-line diagnosis in a single sentence, then defend it. Hard limit: 600 words.
