I work on the personalization team at a mid-sized streaming company, and last week two of my coworkers got into a long argument at lunch that I haven't been able to stop thinking about. I want an outside verdict. Quick background: we're building a system that tries to learn what users want from their behavior -- what they click, how long they watch, when they bail on something halfway -- and then recommends stuff accordingly. Here's roughly how the argument went, as best I remember it.

Ana (ML engineer) kicked it off by saying the whole project rests on a solid foundation: behavior is the best evidence of preference there is. If a user picks documentary A over documentary B fifty times when both are sitting right there on the homepage, they prefer A, end of story. Talk is cheap -- people constantly misreport what they like, in surveys and to themselves, whereas choices actually cost them something (time, at least).

Luis (backend guy, reads a lot of econ blogs) pushed back: a choice shows what someone did, not what they wanted. People pick things they regret all the time -- the textbook case being the smoker who buys cigarettes while genuinely wishing they didn't. And watch time especially confounds wanting with habit, autoplay, whatever got placed at the top of the screen, and plain inability to stop scrolling.

Ana said that objection proves way too much: by that standard you could never infer anyone's preferences from anything they do, which would make all of economics and honestly most of ordinary social life impossible. When your friend chooses to come to your party, you take it as evidence they like you, and you're right to. The sensible move is to treat choices as noisy evidence, not as zero evidence.

Luis said the noise isn't random though, it's systematic, and worse, it's partly self-inflicted: our recommendations shape what users watch, which shapes the data we learn from, which shapes the next round of recommendations. So the preferences we "discover" are partly ones we manufactured ourselves. He compared it to a pollster who only ever asks about candidates they put on the ballot and then announces the public loves those candidates.

Ana's last move was basically: even granting that, it doesn't matter for our purposes. The system doesn't need the user's deep true preferences, whatever those are, it needs to predict what will keep them watching and stop them cancelling. Predictive accuracy is measurable; "true preference" is philosopher's vaporware, and if the predictions are good then the job is done.

Luis said that's exactly the problem -- you can be great at predicting clicks while being terrible at giving people what they'd actually endorse, and the difference only shows up slowly, as churn and regret and people feeling vaguely gross about the product without knowing why.

So: who's right at each step? Please go through the moves in order and say who's winning each exchange and why, then tell me who is more right overall, and if both of them are missing something I want to know what it is. I definately have my own leanings but I'll keep them to myself so I don't bias you. No philosophy training here so keep the jargon light, but don't dumb it down either. One format request: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables in your reply -- just continuous prose, the way you'd write an email or a letter.
