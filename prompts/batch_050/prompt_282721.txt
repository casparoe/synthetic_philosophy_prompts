So lately I've been chewing on the problem of verisimilitude — the question of what it could possibly mean for one false theory to be closer to the truth than another — and instead of asking for a survey of the literature, I want to do it inductively: here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and you tell me what principle, if any, captures them, and which of my intuitions has to go if the principle is going to stay simple.

First: Ptolemaic astronomy is false, but it seems obviously closer to the truth than the claim that the planets move in perfect squares and that the sun is a cold lamp. Second: "all swans are white" is closer to the truth than "all swans are green," because the first at least gets most actual swans right while the second gets none of them. Third: Newtonian mechanics, though strictly false, is closer to the truth than Aristotelian physics, and this has something to do with how its numbers come out almost right within a huge range of everyday cases. Fourth: a maximally cautious claim like "there exist some planets" is entirely true but feels *less* truthlike than Newton's full system, so mere truth can't be the measure — content or informativeness has to count for something. Fifth: how close a theory is to the truth shouldn't depend on the vocabulary it's stated in — if you reword a theory using some gruesome gerrymandered predicates, it's the same theory, same distance from the truth. And sixth, the one I suspect breaks everything: compare a geocentric theory with centuries of tinkered epicycles that predicts planetary positions to within an arcminute, against a crude heliocentric model with circular orbits whose numbers are way off everywhere. My gut says the heliocentric one is closer to the truth becuase it has the basic structure right, even though the geocentric one makes massively more true predictions. But that seems to be in tension with my second verdict, where what did the work was basically "gets more particular facts right."

Is there a single principle — or a small handful of principles — that captures most of these, stated precisely enough that I can see which verdict motivates which clause? Where the verdicts conflict, tell me honestly which one the best principle forces me to abandon, rather than twisting the principle into knots to save all six. And then apply the finished account to two cases I haven't listed: a very bold theory that's almost exactly right versus a timid theory that says little and is entirely true (which is closer?), and two theories about completely different subject matters, like a theory of planetary motion and a theory of heredity (can they even be compared for closeness to the truth, or is verisimilitude only ever relative to a question?).
