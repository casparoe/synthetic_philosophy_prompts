Our town is turning 250 next year and I've somehow ended up chairing the committee planning the anniversary exhibit for the local historical society. We have a decent budget and full access to the town archives, and there's a fight brewing over what the exhibit should even be. The older members want the classic setup: the founding families, the mayors, the mill owners, the big fire, the senator who was born here and whose desk we have. A grad student we hired to catalog the collection has a completely different idea. She found a tavern keeper's ledger from the 1790s -- literally just lists of who bought what, on credit or cash -- plus some court filings and a bundle of letters, and she wants to build the whole central room around reconstructing the lives of maybe a dozen ordinary people: the tavern keeper, a washerwoman who sued a customer for unpaid bills, an itinerant shoemaker who drifted through. Her pitch is basically that the founders were a handful of people while the town was actually built and lived in by thousands of people like these, so the exhibit should show what most lives were like and how they changed across the centuries.

The older members counter that this is just trivia dressed up as history. We happen to know this tavern keeper existed because his ledger survived, but nothing he did changed anything, whereas the mill owner actually *made* the town -- jobs, the rail line, the church. And here's the part where I keep flip flopping: the grad student's people do seem chosen almost at random, by which papers happened not to get thrown out, and it feels arbitrary to put them at the center. But then I walk through the old-fashioned exhibits in other towns and I genuinely wonder why I'm supposed to care more about one rich guy's career than about how the other nine thousand people ate, worked, fought, and died.

I have to run the meeting where we settle the floor plan, so I want to get my own thinking straight first rather than just counting votes. Is there a real sense in which the washerwoman is as historically important as the mayor, or is "historical significance" just a matter of causal impact, in which case the mill owners win going away? If nothing you did had effects beyond your own street, does your life matter to *history* at all, or only to you? And on the practical side -- is there some principled way to ask what a town history exhibit is even *for* that could actually arbitrate this, instead of it coming down to whoever has the loudest committee members?
