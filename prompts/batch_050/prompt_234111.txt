Last night I had a long argument with a friend about the Judy Benjamin problem, specifically about whether learning a conditional licenses changes to one’s unconditional probabilities. Please adjudicate the following stripped-down case by explaining the conceptual issue rather than merely naming standard updating rules.

A drone is in exactly one of four cells—dry-north, dry-south, wet-north, or wet-south—and my prior assigns probability 1/4 to each. An infallible monitor says: “If the drone is in the wet region, it is in the northern half of that region.” My friend updates by preserving P(wet) = 1/2 while setting P(north | wet) = 1, obtaining posterior probabilities of 1/4, 1/4, 1/2, and 0 for dry-north, dry-south, wet-north, and wet-south respectively. I initially treated the assertion as a material conditional—true in dry-north, dry-south, and wet-north, but false in wet-south—which instead yields probability 1/3 for each of the first three cells.

We then realized that the monitor’s utterance policy might matter. In one variant, it sends this exact sentence from wet-north, sends “If the drone is in the wet region, it is in the southern half” from wet-south, and remains silent in both dry cells.

Please give an intuitive explanation of which probabilities are doing the work in each case. Calculate the posterior under my friend’s stipulated constraint, under the material-conditional reading, and under the explicit utterance-policy variant. Then take a definite position on the general lesson: should rational updating use the indicative conditional’s semantic content, the event that this particular utterance was produced, or a separately supplied conditional-probability constraint? Do not merely say that it depends; state which item should be entered into Bayes’ rule and why. Finally, give the strongest reason my friend’s marginal-preserving update might nevertheless be correct, identifying precisely what further assumption it requires.
