I'm a PhD student in logic, and I keep running into a one-liner that I suspect is doing more work than it should. I first met it in a discussion of Escher's Drawing Hands, where someone remarked that however tangled the hierarchy of hands drawing each other may be, there is always an inviolate level — the hand of the artist — that does the drawing without being drawn. Since then I've heard essentially the same sentence — "there's always an inviolate level" — deployed in at least three other settings: to argue that a self-modifying AI can never rewrite its own utility function, since something has to do the rewriting; to gloss Gödel-style results as showing that no sufficiently expressive system can get fully outside itself; and, more loosely, to insist that any engineering stack bottoms out in a physical substrate the system cannot touch. Please disambiguate the precise claims this slogan could be making, from the near-trivial (the act of revision cannot revise itself while it is occurring) to the strong (no system can alter any of its own governing principles, even across many steps). For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, substantive but defensible, or simply false; note which readings are undermined by the possibility of tangled hierarchies, where levels mutually modify one another over time even if no single act is self-applying; and, most importantly, tell me which readings would actually license the conclusion about utility functions, since that is the use I most want to evaluate. A list I can annotate in the margins would be ideal.
