I help run a small philosophy reading group and last week we got into a fight about the Libet experiments that I want to settle properly, or at least understand properly. The basic setup, as I understand it: in the early 1980s Libet had subjects flex their wrist whenever they felt like it while watching a fast clock, and asked them to note where the clock hand was when they first felt the urge to move. The scalp recordings showed a readiness potential building up several hundred milliseconds before the movement, while subjects only reported the urge about 200ms before the movement -- so the brain seemed to get going well before "they" decided. Libet himself pushed back on the anti-free-will reading and argued consciousness could still veto the initiated act in the window before movement, a "free won't". And then decades later a group using fMRI claimed they could predict which of two buttons someone would press, above chance though nowhere near perfectly, several seconds before the person reported choosing. That whole arc, from the wrist flexes to the fMRI, is the episode I want analyzed.

Here's what I want: run the episode through the three big positions in the free will debate -- libertarianism, compatibilism, and hard skepticism -- and tell me what each one says the experiments actually show. I suspect (but want pressure-tested) that the experiments are close to evidentially inert between the positions: the compatibilist says spontaneous urges were never the seat of free will anyway, since freedom lives in reasons-responsive deliberation, not in arbitrary flicks; the libertarian says the readiness potential is just preparation or bias, not a decision; the skeptic says both are moving the goalposts. Tell me whether that's right, or whether some position takes real damage from these results. Then do the reverse direction too: what does the episode show about the theories? Does the fact that all three positions could absorb the findings within a week of publication suggest the positions were never really hostage to neuroscience at all, and if so is that a virtue (conceptual robustness) or a vice (unfalsifiability)?

Some specifics I want the analysis to engage rather than wave at: Libet's own veto escape hatch, and whether it actually saves anything or just kicks the same problem one step back (does the veto itself need a readiness potential?); the methodology of asking people to time their own urges against a clock, and what systematic error there does or doesn't do to the argument; and the fact that both experiments use trivial, reasonless actions -- button presses and wrist flicks where nothing is at stake -- versus the weighty deliberated choices (career moves, confessions, refusals) where the free will debate supposedly has its stakes. If the gap between those kinds of action does all the work, say so explicitly and defend it; if it doesn't, explain why not. Also tell me honestly where each theory just has nothing to say, instead of forcing it to have a view.
