I retired three years ago after four decades in condensed matter physics, and since then I've been reading philosophy the way other retirees take up golf. Lately I've gotten stuck on the "hinge of history" idea — the claim that we might be living at an unusually influential moment, where what people do now (mostly about AI, but also things like engineered pandemics or locking in political values for a long time) could matter vastly more than what people do at ordinary times.

What puzzles me isn't whether the claim is true, it's the state of the arguement about it. Smart people have been going back and forth for years and nothing seems to settle. The enthusiasts say: do the expected value math, even a modest probability that we're at the hinge swamps every other consideration, so act accordingly. The skeptics reply that the probabilities are pulled out of thin air, you can't run statistics on a sample of one history, and this is just the age-old conviction that one's own era is special, dressed up in equations. And then it goes in circles.

Here's my hypothesis, based on watching physics controversies from the inside: the disagreement isn't really about any single claim, it's about what standards of evidence apply to claims that can't be tested the normal way. We had this with string theory — nobody was disputing the calculations, they were disputing whether elegance, coherence, and other non-empirical considerations count as evidence at all. Am I right that something similar is going on here? Or is there actually a clean empirical crux (AI timelines, say) that would settle the thing if it got resolved?

So my question: what kind of disagreement is the hinge-of-history debate, actually? Is it (a) a factual dispute about how likely various futures are, (b) a methodological dispute about what you're allowed to reason from when you have no usable data, (c) a moral dispute in disguise — about whether future people count the same as present ones, or whether expected-value reasoning is even legitimate under that much uncertainty — or (d) some mixture, and if a mixture, which ingredient does the real work? I want the diagnosis argued, not just asserted: engage with what each side actually says and show where they talk past each other.

Then the practical follow-up: given your diagnosis, what would progress even look like? A distinction that dissolves part of the dispute? A class of evidence both sides would be forced to accept? Or should we expect this to simply never resolve, the way I suspect some physics disputes never will? If the answer is "never resolves," say so plainly — and then tell me what a reasonable person does in the meantime. Does rationality push you toward acting as if the hypothesis is true anyway, and what does that depend on?

One constraint: no reading lists, please. I've read the basic arguments on both sides already. What I want is the structure of the disagreement.
