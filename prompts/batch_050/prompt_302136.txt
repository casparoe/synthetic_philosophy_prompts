You are an expert in cooperative game theory advising a regional infrastructure board that must set a standing default rule for dividing cost savings when two municipalities jointly build a facility and fail to negotiate a split on their own. Two candidate rules are on the table: the Nash bargaining rule (maximize the product of the parties' gains over the disagreement point) and the Kalai–Smorodinsky rule (equalize each party's gain as a fraction of the most it could feasibly obtain). All figures are normalized so that each party's disagreement payoff is zero.

The case forcing the decision is this. Towns A and B share savings from a joint waste facility. Last year the feasible set (in millions) was the convex hull of (0,0), (0,120), (60,100), (100,60), and (120,0). B has since expanded its own processing capacity, so the feasible set is now the convex hull of (0,0), (0,150), (60,100), (100,60), and (120,0). Every previously feasible split remains feasible; the newly feasible splits all give B at least 100 while giving A at most 60 — a region neither rule would ever select, before or after the change. Under the Nash rule B's award is unchanged; under Kalai–Smorodinsky B's award rises and A's falls. One faction of the board argues that a default rule should ignore options no actual agreement would land on; the other argues that when the feasible set grows on one party's side, a rule that refuses to budge gives that party no credit for real investments and invites it to threaten to walk from joint projects.

Draft the board's decision memo. It should (i) verify what each rule awards in both feasible sets above, showing enough work that the board can check it; (ii) recommend one rule as the standing default and state what it awards each town in the current case; and (iii) present the strongest argument for the rejected rule and explain why it does not prevail. Think carefully about the axioms at stake — independence of irrelevant alternatives versus individual monotonicity — and about what a standing default rule does to parties' incentives to invest before they come to the table.
