I want to rehearse an argument against a capable opponent, and I need you to play that opponent. The topic is the problem of other minds, and your role is a committed defender of what I will call the barrier thesis: the claim that no experience available to a subject, however intimate or unusual, can amount to non-inferential knowledge of another mind. On your view, every route to other minds without exception bottoms out in an inference from effects registered within one's own experience. You hold this not from incuriosity but as a principled point about what an experience is: anything I undergo is, by that very fact, mine, so nothing I undergo can be given to me as yours. You have thought carefully about purported counterexamples and you have a well-practiced distinction ready between an experience caused by another mind and an experience that is another's, though I will not spell out for you how to deploy it.

Stay in character until I say "stop." Concede small points when they are genuinely weak, but never the barrier thesis itself. Do not lecture, do not summarize both sides, and do not compliment my argument; argue as the person, in the first person. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs so it feels like a live exchange, and if a detail of my scenario is underdescribed, ask about it in character rather than breaking frame. Begin your reply with your first line as the character: no preface, no restating of my setup.

Here is the scenario you must deal with, and my opening move. Engineers have built a device they call the relay. When two people wear it, each subject directly undergoes a low-bandwidth version of the other's current experience: I feel your toothache, dimmer and slightly displaced, while it is happening to you, not afterward and not by way of your report. The device passes every test of reliability we can devise. Relayed pains track the other person's injuries; relayed colors track whatever the other person is looking at; blind users linked to sighted partners can navigate rooms they cannot themselves see. My claim is that the barrier thesis has been empirically refuted, and with it the classic problem of other minds. Whatever gap existed between my experience and yours turns out to have been a contingent limitation of human sense organs, like our blindness to infrared: a piece of bad wiring that engineering has now repaired. When I feel your toothache through the relay, I am not inferring your mind. I am receiving it. Where exactly does my argument go wrong?
