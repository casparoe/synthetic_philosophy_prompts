I teach a second-year undergrad course on rational choice theory, about 25 students, and I've got one 75-minute session. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the pull of Quinn's self-torturer puzzle from the inside -- the way a long chain of individually negligible tradeoffs (each step slightly worse on one dimension, slightly better on another, no single step worth refusing) can march you somewhere you'd never agree to go directly, and how that seems to force either intransitive preferences or the rejection of an apparently trivial step. The catch: they should make the actual choices themselves under a payoff structure (points, not money), ideally not being able to see the full chain of options in advance, so the drift only becomes visible when someone reconstructs their own sequence of choices at the end. Give me the rules and setup, the payoff scheme, how to keep them from gaming it once they smell the trap, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the puzzle (including the point about whether 'no noticeable difference' is transitive and what it costs to deny it at each link). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- e.g. students who just refuse all trades, or who treat tiny differences as strictly zero -- and how to handle it, plus an honest note on which feature of Quinn's original case (genuine pain, say, or the temporal dimension) the game can't capture.
