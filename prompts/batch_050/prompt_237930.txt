So I'm a novelist -- literary fiction, nothing fancy -- and I run a Tuesday night writing workshop at our local libary, eight adults, mostly retired folks and two grad students, we meet for two hours. Next month I've got them for a special session and I want to smuggle in some philosophy without calling it that. Specifically the Jain idea of anekāntavāda, the many-sidedness of truth: reality is too rich for any one standpoint to capture, every account gets something right and something wrong, and the honest way to assert anything is with a built-in "from this perspective, yes." Like the blind men and the elephant, except I want my people to *be* the blind men, not hear a parable about them.

Design me a game/activity for this. Since they're fiction people, I figure the raw material should be stories: same event, multiple characters, each holding a piece of the truth and sincerely getting the rest wrong. I want them to feel, in their own hands, how a group that starts out arguing about what "really happened" ends up unable to write one clean master version -- and how switching to standpoint-flagged speech ("from where Mara stood, he was lying") suddenly makes everybody's claims fit together without making them all equal. The failure has to happen in the room before anyone names the idea; I don't want to lecture them into it.

Tell me what to prepare (I'd love actual short snippets of the partial accounts -- six or so -- that I can adapt, with each one engineered to be locally true and globally misleading), the rules, and how to stop the session collapsing into lazy relativism ("everyone's right in their own way!") when the whole point is that some standpoints miss more than others.

Structure your answer in these sections, in this order: The Setup (~100 words, what I tell them the evening is about -- it can't be "Jain philosophy"), The Six Accounts (~300 words, the snippets), Rules of Play (~150 words), Timeline (~100 words, minute by minute across two hours), The Debrief (~150 words, mapping specific moments onto anekāntavāda and the "in some respect" way of speaking), What Will Go Wrong (~100 words), and Where the Analogy Breaks (~75 words, be honest about what part of the actual doctrine a workshop game can't carry).
