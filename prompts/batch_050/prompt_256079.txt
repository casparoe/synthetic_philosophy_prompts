I run public consultation meetings for a municipal planning department — typically sixty to ninety minutes, a dozen to forty attendees, on contested proposals like rezoning, transit routing, and siting of shelters. My team is required to certify, in writing to council, whether each consultation produced a recommendation that residents reached through genuine deliberation or merely acquiesced to. We have no shared standard for making that call, and we currently make it by feel.

I want you to turn Habermas's account of communicative action and the ideal speech situation into an audit procedure a facilitator can actually run. By that I mean the conditions Habermas takes to be implicitly presupposed whenever people try to reach understanding rather than merely influence each other: that everyone affected may participate, that anyone may question any assertion and introduce any assertion or express any need, that no one is prevented from doing so by pressures internal or external to the discussion, and that claims are raised under the three validity dimensions (truth, normative rightness, sincerity) and remain open to challenge on each. I know the idealized conditions are never fully met; the point is not to certify a meeting as ideal but to detect, in an orderly way, the specific failures that turn would-be communicative action into strategic action wearing deliberation's clothes.

Structure your response in the following sections, in this order:

**Summary** (about 100 words): the procedure's logic in brief, so a manager can grasp it before reading the details.

**The Procedure** (about 600 words): a sequence of numbered checks, ordered from pre-meeting setup (agenda control, distribution of speaking time, who speaks first on technical material) through in-meeting tests to post-meeting certification. For each check, state what observation counts as a pass, what counts as a fail, and what the facilitator should do on a fail (pause the item, defer it, require a written response, schedule a second session). Include at least one test aimed at each validity dimension — how a facilitator notices that a factual claim has been placed beyond challenge, that a norm has been assumed rather than argued, or that a participant's stated position is a strategic performance.

**Points of Irreducible Judgment** (about 200 words): where the audit cannot be mechanical and a facilitator must decide — for instance, distinguishing a resident's legitimate deference to expertise from manufactured deference, or distinguishing healthy compromise from coerced concession.

**Worked Applications** (about 450 words): run the procedure on these three cases. (1) A developer's consultant presents a traffic-impact study in technical language; no resident questions it and the room moves on. (2) A neighborhood association president claims throughout to speak for "everyone on our street," and no one contradicts him. (3) With twelve minutes left, the chair says "I think we're all agreed," the room nods, and the item passes unanimously — including two attendees who earlier raised objections and went quiet. I expect the procedure to fail case (3) and require a second session even though the unanimity looks ideal and a second session delays the project; if your procedure does not yield that unwelcome result, say why.

**Limits of the Translation** (about 150 words): what this procedure cannot do — what gets lost when a theory of communicative rationality is compressed into a checklist, and which Habermasian concerns (for example, the broader colonization of the lifeworld by administrative systems) simply cannot be audited meeting by meeting.
