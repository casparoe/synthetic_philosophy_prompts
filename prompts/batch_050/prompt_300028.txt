I've sort of settled on a set of views and before I use them in another argument I want to know if they even fit together. Here they are:

1. Telling a flat-out lie is morally worse than saying something literally true that you KNOW will give the person the wrong idea. Like if my wife asks where I was and I say "I stopped by the office" (true, I did, at noon) knowing she'll assume that's where I was all evening when really I was at the casino -- that's bad, but not as bad as just saying "I was at the office all evening."

2. The basic reason deception is wrong is the outcome: the other person walks away believing something false. That's the harm.

3. If someone believes something wrong all on their own and I had nothing to do with it, I don't owe them a correction. Staying silent is fine.

4. Intent matters a lot. Accidentally giving someone the wrong idea isn't the same as doing it on purpose, even if the result is identical.

5. If the stakes get high enough -- like someone could genuinely get hurt -- you should speak up even if you never lied and nobody ever asked you.

A couple of these feel like they might be fighting each other but I can't pin down which ones or how badly. Like, I want (1) to be true but I'm not sure my reasons for (2) and (4) actually let me have it? And I'm not sure if (3) and (5) are a problem or just look like one.
