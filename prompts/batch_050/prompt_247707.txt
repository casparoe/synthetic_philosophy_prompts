For my graduate seminar on historical materialism, I have set my students a passage from Engels's letter to Joseph Bloch of 21 September 1890, in which Engels defends the materialist conception of history against the charge of crude economic reductionism:

"Thus there are innumerable intersecting forces, an infinite series of parallelograms of forces which give rise to one resultant — the historical event. This may again itself be viewed as the product of a power which works as a whole unconsciously and without volition. For what each individual wills is obstructed by everyone else, and what emerges is something that no one willed."

Elsewhere in the same letter Engels insists that among the conditions under which we make our history, "the economic ones are ultimately decisive." I want a paper that presses hard on the tension between these two claims. Please argue that the parallelogram metaphor cannot be reconciled with the doctrine of ultimate economic determination: in a genuine composition of forces, no component is privileged as the determinant of the resultant — each vector contributes only in concert with the rest, and altering any one of them alters the outcome — so Engels must either abandon the metaphor as a serious model of historical causation or abandon the asymmetry between the economic base and everything else. In developing the argument, give Engels his strongest reply (for instance, that economic conditions fix the magnitudes and directions of the individual vectors rather than constituting one vector among many, or that "ultimately" marks a claim about long-run tendencies rather than particular events), and show why each such reply either fails or quietly concedes the point.
