Consider the following auction. An auctioneer offers a $20 bill to a group of bidders. The rules are standard except for one: the highest bidder wins the bill and pays their bid, but the second-highest bidder must also pay their bid and receives nothing. Bidding starts low and proceeds in small increments, with any bidder free to top the current high bid. In practice, this game reliably ends with the winning bid well above $20, sometimes far above, even when the players are intelligent, understand the rules in advance, and are bidding with their own money.

Please give a conceptual analysis of why this happens. I am not looking for the observation that people get carried away or dislike losing; I want to know precisely where, if anywhere, the reasoning of the players goes wrong. In particular, please address the following three questions.

First, is the trap a matter of individually rational steps aggregating into a collectively ruinous outcome, or is some individual step already irrational? Once two bidders are committed, each faces a choice between a certain loss (their current bid, paid for nothing) and a further small outlay for a chance of winning the bill. Topping the bid can look like the better of those two options at every single round. Explain whether a sequence can be composed entirely of locally best moves and yet be one no reasonable person would choose to begin, and what that shows, if anything, about the limits of evaluating choices one at a time.

Second, what does the trap presuppose about how a player treats money already committed? Does the game bite only for a player who reasons from the standpoint of their sunk bid -- treating the recovery of past losses as a live stake -- rather than from their current position, in which the earlier bids are gone regardless? Work out what the game looks like to a player who strictly ignores sunk costs at each decision point, and determine whether such a player escapes the trap or merely falls into it at a different point.

Third, how should a player with perfect foresight of the game's dynamics evaluate the very first bid? If entering at all is predictably ruinous, then declining to bid appears to be the only rational move. But explain whether this conclusion depends on the players failing to foresee the escalation, or whether it holds even when everyone sees the entire future clearly -- and, in the latter case, whether the game's danger lies in mistaken reasoning at all, or rather in something else, such as the absence of any way to credibly commit to stopping.

Finally, say what your analysis implies about real-world escalation: continuing a failing war to justify what has already been spent on it, or pouring further funds into a failing project. Is this auction a good model of those cases, or does it smuggle in structural features -- most obviously the rule that the runner-up pays -- that real escalation lacks? Where real escalation differs from the game, identify which features of the game do the explanatory work and which are incidental.
