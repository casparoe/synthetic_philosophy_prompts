Design a finite, non-anthropic thought experiment in which two explicitly defined decision rules diverge over whether an agent should cooperate. The first, narrow evidential decision theory, considers only how the agent’s act bears on outcomes within that act’s causal future. The second, evidential cooperation in a large world, also treats the act as evidence about the choices of relevantly similar agents in causally isolated situations and counts those agents’ outcomes in the deliberation.

The case must satisfy the following constraints:

- Use an ordinary institutional setting, such as hospitals, schools, firms or local governments; do not use a prisoner’s dilemma in so many words.
- There must be no communication, causal interaction, common beneficiary, shared reputation mechanism or enforceable agreement between the agents.
- Do not invoke a perfect predictor, identical copies, time travel, exotic physics, infinite populations or an anthropic selection effect.
- The relevant similarity between the agents must have a plausible finite explanation, such as common training, the same public evidence or an independently developed but structurally similar decision procedure; do not merely stipulate a numerical correlation.
- Narrow evidential decision theory must recommend defection even after all ordinary evidence available to the agent has been taken into account.
- Large-world evidential cooperation must recommend cooperation because the agent’s choice is evidence concerning a sufficiently large class of similarly situated choices, not because the agent mistakenly believes that she can control the others.
- Include numerical payoffs or probabilities sufficient to show both recommendations, while keeping the arithmetic elementary.

After presenting the scenario, calculate or otherwise demonstrate the verdict of each decision rule. Identify the single structural feature that makes the large-world rule depart from the narrow rule, and explain what happens if that feature is removed. Finally, give the strongest objection that the case merely converts individual choice into an illegitimate form of group agency, and assess whether the large-world rule can answer that objection without becoming a disguised version of causal or rule consequentialism.
