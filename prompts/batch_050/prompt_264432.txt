You are a mathematical philosopher specializing in algorithmic fairness, equally at home with the impossibility results on calibration and error-rate parity and with their normative interpretation. Think carefully and reason step by step. Begin with a brief brainstorm of two or three candidate setups, note what each would make easy or hard, and then commit to one and carry it through.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications.

Setting. A consumer-lending scenario — not criminal justice, not hiring. A bank assigns each loan applicant a default-risk score and approves everyone whose score falls below a threshold. Two demographic groups apply. Keep the story realistic and non-malicious: no oracle, no poisoned training data, no biased or undertrained model on the bank's part.

Numerical constraints. (i) Use a finite applicant pool small enough that every relevant quantity can be computed by hand — a few dozen applicants in total, with all counts as integers. (ii) The two groups must have genuinely different base rates of default, and the score the bank uses must be the Bayes-optimal one given the available features. That is, the impossibility must not arise because the model is badly built; it must persist for the statistically perfect predictor. (iii) Present the full joint distribution explicitly as a small table (score value × actual outcome × group).

The deliverable. From the table you construct, derive each of the following from the arithmetic — do not stipulate any of it:

1. Show that the score is calibrated within each group: among applicants assigned a given score, the observed default frequency matches the score, for both groups separately.
2. Fix a single approval threshold, apply it to both groups, and show that the resulting classifier violates error-rate parity: the false-positive rate (or false-negative rate) differs across groups. Give the actual rates as fractions from your table.
3. Show that inside your toy example, no alternative choice of threshold equalizes both error rates across groups while the score remains calibrated — exhibit the impossibility concretely, rather than appealing to the general theorem.
4. Identify which feature of the example is doing the work, and demonstrate the counterfactual: rebuild the table with equal base rates across the groups, keep calibration, and exhibit a threshold at which error-rate parity now holds. If you find that equalizing base rates does not suffice in your construction, explain what additional condition (e.g., the quality of the predictor) is implicated.
5. Stakeholder test: returning to the original unequal-base-rate table, state which fairness criterion the bank's compliance officer would invoke and which a rejected applicant's advocate would invoke, and determine whether either party is making an arithmetic mistake or whether the disagreement is genuinely normative once the arithmetic is agreed.

Honesty requirements. The conflict must fall out of your numbers; a scenario that merely asserts that the criteria clash fails. If any of the specifications above turn out to be jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly rather than quietly relaxing one, and identify exactly which two specifications collide and why.
