I chair the incident review board for a regional hospital network. Every month we review a handful of serious adverse events, and our reports feed regulators and occasionally courts. Our recurring failure mode is retrospective: once we know how a case ended, reviewers write narratives in which the bad outcome was obviously coming and the clinician obviously should have seen it -- hindsight bias, outcome bias, and the narrative fallacy all tangled together in one document.

Write a reusable procedure my board can run on every case, in order: how to reconstruct what information was actually available to the clinician at each decision point (and how to keep what we now know from contaminating that reconstruction); how to keep "the decision was bad" and "the outcome was bad" from collapsing into each other; and a test for whether our causal story explains too much -- i.e., whether it would also "explain" the same outcome had things gone differently. Mark explicitly the steps where judgment cannot be eliminated and the board chair has to make a call.

Then run the procedure on two cases: (1) a patient dies after a triage nurse, facing ambiguous symptoms and a packed waiting room, assigns a non-urgent priority -- show me a case where the procedure clears the decision despite the death; and (2) a surgeon skips a standard verification step, the patient turns out fine, and the event surfaces only in a routine audit -- show me how the procedure faults the decision even though nothing went wrong. Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do: which kinds of retrospective distortion it will fail to catch, and what I should tell regulators it does and does not establish.
