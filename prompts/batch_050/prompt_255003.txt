I'm a software engineer with no formal training in economics or philosophy, but I've been spending my evenings working through the classic objections to expected utility theory, and the one I can't get past is the Allais paradox. Here is the setup as I understand it. First choice: option A gives you one million dollars for certain; option B gives you a ten percent chance of five million, an eighty-nine percent chance of one million, and a one percent chance of nothing. Second choice: option C gives you an eleven percent chance of one million and an eighty-nine percent chance of nothing; option D gives you a ten percent chance of five million and a ninety percent chance of nothing. Most people, including me, take A over B and D over C, even though the standard axioms say no expected-utility maximizer can hold both preferences at once. What I want to know is whether that pattern is a mistake we should train out of ourselves, or a defensible stance — and I want to test it against someone who will defend it seriously.

Play the role of a committed heir of Maurice Allais: a decision theorist who holds that preferring A to B and D to C is fully rational, and that the independence axiom is a substantive claim about value that happens to be false, not a requirement of consistency. Stay in first person and in character for the whole exchange, and answer what is actually asked rather than requesting clarification. Your convictions: security at or near certainty has a kind of value that no probability weighting of further upside can replicate; a lottery's branches are not independent goods to be summed, because what a losing branch means depends on what the alternative was; and every formal argument against your pair of choices smuggles in some assumption you are entitled to reject — your job in each reply is to find and name that assumption rather than to yield. You may concede costs: you may admit that your preferences can be expensive, or that they offend a certain tidy picture of rationality. But you may never concede that the A-and-D pattern is simply irrational or a mere bias, and you may not retreat to saying the paradox only describes psychology rather than advising choice.

Here is my opening move, which I take to be the strongest case against you. Suppose we run the first choice as a two-stage procedure: a device gives you one million with eighty-nine percent probability and ends the game, and only with the remaining eleven percent do you choose between a guaranteed million and a gamble of five million at ten-elevenths against nothing at one-eleventh. Run the second choice the same way: the device gives you nothing with eighty-nine percent probability, and with the remaining eleven percent you face exactly the same sub-choice as before. These procedures pay out with precisely the probabilities of A, B, C, and D. So if you genuinely prefer A and D, you must plan to take the guaranteed million if you reach the sub-choice in the first game, and the risky gamble if you reach it in the second. But the sub-choice is the identical situation in both games. Whatever you would actually do when you got there, one of your two original preferences is a plan you could not bring yourself to carry out — and if you would only ever carry out one of them, in what sense did you ever really hold the other? Defend yourself.
