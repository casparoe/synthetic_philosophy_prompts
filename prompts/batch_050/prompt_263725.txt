Having retired from a forty-year career in molecular biology, I have spent the past two years reading moral philosophy with the diligence of a newcomer and, I confess, the impatience of someone accustomed to disputes that eventually submit to data. The debate over moral bioenhancement -- roughly, whether biomedical means (pharmaceutical, genetic, or otherwise) may or must be used to improve moral motivation itself, rather than leaving moral education to upbringing, institutions, and exhortation -- strikes me as a dispute in which the two sides have never agreed on what they are actually disagreeing about. I would like you to repair that.

Consider two camps. The first, which I shall call the enhancement advocates, holds roughly the position associated with Ingmar Persson and Julian Savulescu: that human moral psychology evolved for small-group life and is poorly matched to an era of weapons of mass destruction and global-scale environmental harms; that traditional means of moral improvement have demonstrably failed to keep pace; and that, given the stakes, moral bioenhancement is not merely permissible but potentially obligatory, perhaps even compulsory for some populations. The second camp, which I shall call the freedom-first critics, holds that whatever is gained by pharmacologically or genetically blunting aggression or amplifying empathy is purchased at the price of moral agency itself: that conduct produced by an engineered disposition is not genuinely moral conduct, that the freedom to do wrong is a precondition of moral worth, and that state-administered moral enhancement is a uniquely dangerous form of paternalism.

Write the joint statement these two camps should be able to co-author after a good-faith adversarial collaboration -- not a debate, and not a verdict, but the document both would sign. Specifically, I want:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. These should include empirical or conceptual points both camps can accept (about the limits of conventional moral education, about what bioenhancement can and cannot plausibly change, about the difference between improving capacities and installing outcomes, and about historical abuses of biological interventions dressed in moral language). Take care that none of these is vacuous: each should be a claim that some reasonable person has in fact denied.

2. A statement of the residual disagreements, each formulated in language that the side holding it would accept as accurate -- no caricature, no straw. Where a disagreement turns out on inspection to be terminological (for instance, disputes about the word moral rather than about any policy or fact), say so and quarantine it from the genuine ones.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. I suspect, but do not insist, that these include whether moral worth requires the live possibility of doing otherwise; whether there is a principled line between enhancing moral motivation and enhancing other capacities such as intelligence; and how one should weigh a small probability of civilizational catastrophe against a near-certain cost to liberty. Test my suspicions; discard or add as the logic of the positions requires.

4. For each crux, a thought experiment, empirical finding, or hypothetical policy case that both camps agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that case recorded in advance -- so that one could in principle tell, later, which camp the outcome embarrassed.

Two constraints from my years in the laboratory: where the dispute depends on empirical premises (the heritability of relevant dispositions, the efficacy of existing interventions, the track record of moral education), state them as testable premises rather than asserting them; and do not pad the document with citations or a bibliography -- I want the argument, not the apparatus.
