In debates over the European Union's future, one image is reached for again and again: the Union is a bicycle that must keep moving forward — that is, must keep integrating — because if it stops pedalling it falls over. I have seen it deployed from both sides of the argument. Integrationists use it to warn that any prolonged pause in deepening or widening (after a large enlargement round, say, or a failed treaty ratification) risks unravelling the whole project, and to dismiss proposals for a permanent standstill or a settled two-tier arrangement as incoherent. Eurosceptics invoke the same image to argue that the Union is structurally incapable of stabilising anywhere short of a federal state, so that countries unhappy with that destination should leave rather than seek a halfway house.

Please audit this analogy rather than simply endorsing or rejecting it. First, make the correspondence explicit: what plays the role of the rider, the pedalling, the speed, the gyroscopic stability, the fall — and who or what is the road? Second, examine the load-bearing premise on its own terms. A moving bicycle really is dynamically stabilised in ways a stationary one is not, so identify what, if anything, in the economics or politics of a partial union is supposed to play the role of that physics: is there an actual mechanism — functional spillovers between policy fields, expectations priced into markets, the ratchet effect of accumulated supranational law — that makes an intermediate settlement genuinely unstable, rather than merely unfashionable? Third, lay out the disanalogies and say which of them matter for the conclusions the metaphor is used to reach: bicycles have a single rider with a known destination, whereas the Union's direction and endpoint are precisely what is contested; bicycles can be fitted with a kickstand, ridden in circles, or walked alongside, so do opt-outs, multi-speed arrangements, and long-frozen policy areas show that the Union already stands still in places without falling; and a rider who pedals on regardless of terrain sometimes rides off a cliff. Finally, say what argumentative work the image is doing that a literal argument would have to do instead — in particular, whether the claim that standing still is fatal survives restatement without the metaphor — and, if you think a better analogy exists for the Union's actual stability conditions, propose one and explain why it is better.
