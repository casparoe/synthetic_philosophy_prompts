I serve on the joint worker-management safety committee at a mid-sized freight company, and next month we have to rule on a proposal that has split us right down the middle. The operations department wants long-haul drivers to wear a headband device, made by an outside vendor, that reads electrical activity at the scalp and sounds an alarm when its software judges the wearer to be drifting toward sleep. Everyone agrees fatigue-related crashes are a real problem -- we had two serious incidents last year -- and the vendor insists the raw signal never leaves the device, that only an alertness score is stored, and that drivers could in principle opt out. Our company's monitoring policy, written years ago with cameras and GPS in mind, permits "reasonable safety monitoring proportionate to risk," a phrase nobody defined. Half the committee says this is just a seatbelt for the mind and refusing it borders on negligence; the other half says a line is being crossed that no workplace should cross, though they struggle to say exactly where the line is.

I have no training in philosophy or law, but this proposal has set me thinking, and I want the committee's decision to rest on something sturdier than gut feeling. It seems to me that information about my body and information about my mind might deserve different treatment: a breathalyzer measures what I did, a heart monitor measures how my body is doing, but a device reading my brain seems to be measuring -- or at least approaching -- what is going on inside my thinking itself, even if today it only reports drowsiness. I keep wondering whether "alertness scores" are really as innocent as the vendor says, whether consent is meaningful when refusing the device might cost a driver the best routes, and whether the worry is really about what the device does now or about the precedent it sets once the hardware is already on every driver's head. I have seen the term "cognitive liberty" used for the idea that people should control access to their own mental states, and I'd like the memo to take that idea seriously without treating it as an automatic trump card.

Please draft the decision memo I will bring to the committee: a clear recommendation -- approve, reject, or approve with conditions -- that we could actually vote on, including concrete conditions (data retention, who may see scores, opt-out terms, what happens if the vendor changes its software) if you think conditional approval is right. The memo should explain where the relevant line lies between legitimate safety monitoring and intrusion into mental life, and it must give the strongest honest statement of the losing side, whether that is the safety argument or the privacy argument, so that whichever way we vote, the minority feels their position was understood rather than dismissed.
