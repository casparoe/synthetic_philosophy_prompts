I'm a junior in high school and I help run our school's philosophy and AI reading group, where we recently spent a few weeks on the idea of "factored cognition" -- roughly, the proposal that any hard deliberative question can be answered without anyone ever doing deep thinking, so long as the question is recursively decomposed into subquestions small enough that a person can answer each one in a few minutes, with each sub-answerer given only a short memo of context and no knowledge of the larger project, and a coordinator at the top who assembles the results. The hope is that a vast tree of shallow, context-free micro-thoughts can approximate (or exceed) the output of one expert thinking carefully for a year. When I tried explaining this to my friend Maya, she said it sounded exactly like every group project we've ever done, which made me realize the idea is ripe for parody. For the satire issue of our school magazine, I'd like you to write the official employee onboarding handbook of a fictional strategy consultancy -- call it something like "Meridian & Meridian LLP" -- that operates entirely on this principle. Every analyst is contractually forbidden from spending more than ten minutes on any single question, from asking why they are being asked it, and from receiving more than a paragraph of context; the founding partners at the root of the tree have not personally thought about a client problem in decades, but remain, on paper, the authors of every recommendation.

Please act as a humor writer who also has genuine training in philosophy of mind and cognitive science and who has, regrettably, written real corporate training materials in a past life. Before drafting, briefly list (in a short preamble) five or six specific features of the factored-cognition idealization that are most ripe for exaggeration, then write the handbook itself as about 800 words of deadpan, numbered sections with plausible HR section titles. The comedy must come from the actual structure of the idea, not from invented absurdities: the context-loss-by-design policy, the faith that decomposition is lossless, quality control performed by reviewers who also don't know the original question, the exit interview conducted by someone who hasn't been told anyone is leaving, and the complaint box whose complaints are recursively decomposed until none of the pieces is a complaint anymore. Include at least one section where the handbook straight-facedly answers the objection that "no one in this building understands what the firm is doing" by declaring understanding to be a property of the org chart rather than of any employee, since I want a reader to feel the real philosophical question underneath -- whether a structure made entirely of people who don't understand something can itself understand it. Keep the irony sustained and legible without winking at the reader; a specialist in this literature should recognize that the satire is fair, while a classmate who has never heard of any of this should still laugh at it as pure bureaucratic nightmare.

Two consistency constraints, which are part of the joke: the handbook must genuinely function according to its own rules (for instance, its authorship page might note that the handbook itself was assembled from subsections written by people who had not read the other sections), and no one in the document may ever display knowledge they could not have obtained within the ten-minute limit. Please don't name any real researchers or use any technical jargon from the literature inside the handbook itself -- within the fiction, the firm simply invented this way of working and considers it obviously correct. Think carefully about tone: the best corporate satire sounds helpful, warm, and slightly menacing. Aim for something I could plausibly paste into the magazine between the parody course catalog and the fake club announcements.
