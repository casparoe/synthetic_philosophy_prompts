A colleague in the law school sent me this argument and I can't find the exit, though the conclusion can't be right. Background: in civil cases the standard is preponderance of the evidence -- liable if more likely than not. His argument:

(1) A defendant is liable iff the plaintiff's evidence makes it more likely than not that the defendant did the thing.
(2) Suppose 1000 people attend a rodeo and the gate records show only 499 paid. The organizer picks an attendee at random and sues. For that attendee, the evidence (attendance count plus gate records) makes it 50.1% likely they snuck in.
(3) So by (1), that attendee is liable.
(4) But nothing in the reasoning depends on which attendee got picked, so the organizer can sue all 1000 and win every suit -- collecting the ticket price 1000 times for 501 gatecrashers, including full payment squeezed out of 499 people who already paid.
(5) That's absurd. My colleague concludes that (1) is false: clearing the 50% bar is never by itself sufficient for liability; you always need some further, non-statistical, 'individualized' evidence.

And here's the part that got me. He cheerfully accepts the consequences: in the Blue Bus case (plaintiff run off the road at night, 80% of the buses on that route belong to the one company, no eyewitness, no camera) the plaintiff loses, fine. But he also has to accept that nobody could ever be convicted on DNA evidence alone, since a DNA match is, at bottom, just very very good statistics -- and that seems like a lot to swallow.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I'd most like to keep (1) -- I like the idea that the civil standard is just a probability threshold -- and I'd also like the rodeo organizer to lose. Can I have both? And if the fix is some distinction between naked statistics and genuine evidence, state the distinction precisely enough that DNA comes through the gate but the gate count doesn't, because every version I try either lets the rodeo suit in or locks DNA out.
