Three months ago my father was diagnosed with a condition for which the treatment evidence is genuinely thin: two small studies pointing in different directions, expert opinion divided, and no defensible way to pin down a single probability that the more aggressive treatment helps. I have a statistics background and am fully fluent in orthodox Bayesian decision theory -- priors, likelihoods, posteriors, hierarchical models, expected utility maximization -- and my instinct was to handle this by putting a hyperprior over the unknown parameters and integrating. But in reading around the problem I keep encountering the literature on imprecise probability: credal sets, lower and upper previsions, the claim that rationality sometimes requires refusing to commit to any single probability measure, with the Ellsberg-style urn cases offered as motivation.

Here is what I want. Restate the case for imprecise probability entirely in my vocabulary -- single priors, hyperpriors, model averaging, sensitivity analysis, the likelihood principle -- as faithfully as you can, and then give me a residue report. Which of the imprecise-probabilist's claims translate cleanly into hierarchical Bayes, which translate only with distortion (and say exactly what gets distorted), and which have no expression in single-measure Bayesianism at all? For the residue, take a position: is the untranslatable remainder a genuine phenomenon my framework cannot capture -- something about the difference between ignorance and risk that no hyperprior encodes -- or is it an artifact of the imprecise framework's own notation, evaporating once you are careful about what a hyperprior actually says? And do not smuggle Bayesian assumptions into the translation and call it fidelity; I want to know whether my instinct that this is all just hierarchical modeling is right or is missing something that matters for my father's decision.
