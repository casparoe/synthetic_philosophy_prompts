My brother in law Dave and his friend Mark have been having basically the same argument for over a year now, and I want to settle it by making them do one of those adversarial collaborations where two sides have to write a joint statement together. I read the Haidt book (The Righteous Mind) a couple years ago so I can mostly follow along, but I never studied philosophy or psycology, so bear with me.

Here's the fight. Dave's takeaway from the book is what he calls the taste bud view: liberals build morality mostly on care/harm and fairness, conservatives use those plus loyalty, authority, and sanctity, and no palette is more "right" than another, same as you can't argue someone out of liking cilantro. So politics isn't about who's right, it's translation between moral languages, and being smug about your side is like an Italian guy being smug that his taste in pasta is objectively best.

Mark thinks this is a total cop out. His line is that taste buds can't be wrong but moralities can -- slavery felt perfectly moral to plenty of people, so some palettes are just better than others. He says the harm-and-fairness cluster has a track record: basically every reform we're proud of now (abolition, women's suffrage, civil rights) was argued in harm/fairness terms, while loyalty/authority/sanctity mostly propped up whatever hierarchy happened to be sitting there. And he doubts the extra three foundations are even moral foundations at all. He bets that when you press people on a "harmless" violation (like someone eating their dog after it died, or cleaning a toilet with an old flag) they eventually mumble something about harm to society or harm to kids watching, so loyalty/authority/sanctity might just be harm-detection heuristics dressed up with disgust.

Dave fires back that the history point is cherry picked, since abolitionists talked constantly about souls and the sanctity of every human being and the civil rights movement was led by ministers quoting scripture. He says the "everything collapses into harm-talk" result is just what you get when you press WEIRD college-educated people. And he claims (he says he read this somewhere) that liberals are worse at predicting what conservatives believe than vice versa, so Mark's side doesn't even understand the view it's declaring bankrupt.

So: write the joint statement these two should produce. I want (1) a numbered list of claims both would actually sign, including the basic findings of the theory and whatever both accept about the difference between describing morals and judging them; (2) the exact remaining disagreements, each phrased so the guy holding that position would accept the wording of his own side; (3) the cruxes; and (4) for each crux, something concrete both would accept as bearing on it -- a study you could run, a historical check, a thought experiment -- with each guy's prediction written down in advance. For example, what would each predict about whether devout traditionalists, pressed hard enough, keep saying the harmless violations are wrong or eventually reach for harm? If some of their dispute turns out to be about words rather than substance, say which part and why.

My own guesses for cruxes are: (a) whether the binding foundations are genuinely moral or just disgust plus heuristics, (b) whether track-record arguments even work given that everyone in history used mixed rhetoric, and (c) whether who-understands-whom stats have anything to do with who's right or only with who's persuasive. Tell me at the end whether those are the real cruxes or if I'm missing one.

Format: numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table, then cruxes with predictions. Start your response with the statement itself, no intro, no "here's the document I drafted", nothing -- and put anything you want to say to me personally (like about my crux guesses) after the document, clearly marked.
