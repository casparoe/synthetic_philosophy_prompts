For a graduate seminar I am designing on the foundations of probability, I want the specification before the survey. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of randomness for finite sequences must satisfy to count as an answer to the question "can this bit string be random?" — what it must deliver, with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement. At minimum, I expect you to address: whether the theory must classify the all-zeros string of some length as non-random while classifying a typical record of fair coin tosses as random, and whether that classification may be a matter of degree; whether the verdicts may legitimately depend on a choice of universal machine, encoding, or background measure, and what a theory has to say for itself if they do; whether a finite theory must fall out as the limiting case of a satisfactory account of randomness for infinite sequences, or whether the finite problem is autonomous; and whether the theory must earn its keep by underwriting something practice-adjacent — compression, successful gambling, prediction failure — or whether a purely structural characterization suffices. For each desideratum, give the case for treating it as genuine rather than as the prejudice of one research tradition, and mark it non-negotiable or negotiable. I want you to commit: if some subset of your desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — I have my suspicions about machine-independence combined with sharp finite verdicts — say so explicitly and say which requirement you would sacrifice and why; likewise, identify the single requirement that excludes the most existing contenders. Brief closing scorecard of the complexity-theoretic, frequency-based, and betting-theoretic approaches against your list is welcome, but keep it brief. End with a last line beginning "Verdict:" naming the one desideratum you would refuse to give up.
