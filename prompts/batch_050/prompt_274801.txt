I am a professor of philosophy at a mid-sized university, and I have been appointed chair of our Committee on Commemoration and Naming, which reviews disputes over building names, statues, plaques, and official ceremonies. The disputes that reach us almost always take the same shape: a community's living memory of a person or event conflicts with what the documentary record shows, and each side claims the other is falsifying the past. Recent cases include a dormitory named for a benefactor whom local memory credits with sheltering student protesters in 1970, though the surviving correspondence suggests he was out of the country at the time; a memorial honoring mill workers whose descendants insist the 1911 fire killed twice as many as company records and newspapers reported; and an annual founders' ceremony built around a story that no historian on our faculty will endorse but that alumni regard as the moral heart of the institution.

I want to approach these cases systematically rather than ad hoc, and I would like your help doing so. Please respond as a professional philosopher with expertise in social epistemology and the philosophy of history. Think carefully; before drafting the procedure itself, spend a short section brainstorming the relevant distinctions (for example: memory as evidence versus memory as identity-conferring practice; errors of fact versus contestable interpretations; cases where the record is genuinely silent versus cases where it positively contradicts memory). Then give me the deliverable I actually need: a written decision procedure, usable by a committee of non-philosophers, that operationalizes an answer to the question of when collective memory should give way to the historical record and when, if ever, the reverse.

The procedure should be a sequence of ordered tests or questions a committee member can work through in a single ninety-minute meeting, with instructions for what to do when the answer to a step is "we don't know and cannot find out." It must specify: which features of a case are decisive and which are merely relevant; how the stakes of the decision (renaming a building versus correcting a plaque versus canceling a ceremony) interact with the epistemic verdict; and at which steps judgment cannot be eliminated, flagged explicitly as such rather than disguised as mechanical application. It should also state plainly what the procedure cannot settle and what kinds of cases will break it.

Finally, apply the procedure to the three cases I described above. In at least one of them, show me the procedure yielding a recommendation that I, as a philosopher, will find uncomfortable — and tell me honestly whether that discomfort is a reason to distrust the procedure or a reason to trust it. The committee's recommendations go to the provost, who signs off, so the procedure must produce conclusions I can defend in two written pages to an intelligent administrator with no philosophical training.
