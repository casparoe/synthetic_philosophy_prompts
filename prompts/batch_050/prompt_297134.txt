I'm preparing for a graduate seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and I want to spar with a position I find both plausible and disturbing before I have to defend my own view in class. Please play a committed defender of what my seminar calls the Sphex Thesis: the claim that the overwhelming majority of human action, including much of what we dignify with the name deliberation, is sphexish in the relevant sense. Some background so we're on the same page: as the story is usually told in philosophy, the digger wasp Sphex performs an elaborate-looking routine of dragging a paralyzed cricket to the threshold of its burrow, leaving it there, going inside to inspect, and then emerging to pull the cricket in. When an experimenter moves the cricket a few inches away during the inspection, the wasp re-drags it to the threshold and then goes inside to inspect again — and can be made to repeat this dozens of times without ever varying the routine. The standard moral is that apparent purposive intelligence can collapse into a cue-triggered subroutine when the cue itself is manipulated, and the wasp never notices.

To keep the thesis from being trivial, my opponent should defend a specific two-level version of it, which I'll define here so we're arguing about the same thing. First-order sphexishness is the wasp's condition: a fixed behavioral routine that fails the interruption test. Second-order sphexishness is the human condition, on this view: we flexibly select among routines, improvise, and pass ordinary interruption tests, but the procedure by which we select, switch, and "decide to reflect" is itself occasioned by environmental cues and is not itself subject to interruption or questioning by the agent. On this picture, our flexibility is genuine but always one level down; at the top level we are wasps with better subroutines. Please stay in character until I say stop, keep each reply to a single paragraph so it feels like a live exchange, and be a sharp interlocutor rather than a strawman: you may concede small points and you have real resources (habit formation, confabulation and post-hoc rationalization, the way even our decisions to deliberate seem to be triggered rather than chosen), but you should never concede the main thesis.

Here is my opening move: your second-order notion seems engineered to be irrefutable. Any behavior I point to — noticing the moved cricket, reconsidering a plan, abandoning a lifelong project mid-stream, even this conversation — you will redescribe as a higher-level routine set off by a cue. But a thesis compatible with every possible observation isn't a discovery about human beings; it's just a relabeling we could apply to anything. So tell me plainly: what observation, if we made it, would show the Sphex Thesis false? And if nothing could, why should I regard it as a claim about us rather than as a mood?
