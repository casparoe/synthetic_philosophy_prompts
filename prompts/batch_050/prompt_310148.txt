As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun reading ethics, I keep encountering the claim associated with John Taurek that in a forced rescue choice we should not simply save the greater number. My scientific instincts rebel: if the choice is between saving one stranger and saving five, each facing the same otherwise fatal threat, it seems obvious that five deaths are worse than one. Yet Taurek’s equal-chance proposal also seems to capture something important—namely, that the one person can complain that his life was treated as less worthy of protection merely because fewer people happened to be endangered alongside him. I find myself unable to state exactly where the apparent conflict lies: is Taurek denying that five deaths constitute a worse outcome, denying that impersonal betterness should govern rescue, or making a subtler claim about what each person is individually owed?

Please explain the strongest version of Taurek’s position in a way that does not caricature it as indifference to numbers. In particular, distinguish among three possible principles: giving every endangered person an equal chance of rescue; weighting each person’s chance by the size of the group in which he or she appears; and simply saving whichever group has the greater total. Explain why these can diverge even when everyone’s life is assumed to have equal value. Please also address a complication that interests me: if a rescuer has a policy of weighted lotteries, repeated over many emergencies, doesn’t that policy protect each person ex ante better than an unweighted lottery, even if in any particular case it favors the larger group? Why might a Taurek-style argument nevertheless reject that probabilistic aggregation, and would that rejection depend on denying expected-value reasoning in general?

Finally, please clarify whether Taurek’s view must imply that numbers never matter. Consider a sequence in which the rescuer first cannot save anyone, then can save only one of two groups, and finally can save both if some additional resource becomes available. Does the position license different comparisons at different stages, or does it prohibit treating the extra people in the larger group as reasons to save that group even in an otherwise tied choice? I would like an intuitive but conceptually exact account of where “each counts for one” ceases to support “count the ones,” and whether any coherent middle position preserves both equal concern and a meaningful sense in which saving five is better than saving one.
