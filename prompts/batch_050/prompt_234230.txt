Some background: I am a retired physicist who has taken up philosophy in the last few years, and I have been reading about the problem of logical omniscience. For a discussion group, I was asked to write down my settled views so the others can attack them. Here is what I currently hold:

(1) Probabilistic coherence is a genuine rational requirement, and the Dutch book theorem is what justifies it: an agent whose credences expose them to a guaranteed loss is thereby shown to be epistemically defective.

(2) It can be fully rational to assign an intermediate credence to a mathematical conjecture whose truth value is already fixed by logic alone, before any proof is known; and discovering a valid proof is then a paradigm case of rational belief revision — one's credence in the conjecture ought to rise toward certainty.

(3) Conditionalization on one's total evidence is the correct rule for rational credence revision.

(4) Epistemic norms obey an ought-implies-can constraint: no agent can be rationally faulted for failing to perform a computation that no physically realizable system could carry out. As a physicist I take seriously that the computational resources of any actual agent, human or artificial, are finite and bounded.

(5) Accordingly, a bookmaker who posts odds on logical matters and gets Dutch-booked by a supercomputer has not thereby been shown irrational: the exploit was not one that any feasible agent could have found.

(6) The point of an ideal of rationality is to guide and evaluate actual, bounded agents. An ideal that is in principle unreachable and that offers no way of grading better and worse approximations to it is philosophically idle.
