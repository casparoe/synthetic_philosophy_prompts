You are a professional speechwriter with graduate training in political philosophy, and you take unusual care to get the philosophy right even when the audience would never notice. Think through the brief carefully before drafting; you may want to sketch the argumentative structure first, then write the speech itself.

Here is the assignment. I chair the board of a mutual insurance cooperative founded sixty years ago by a man who completed a philosophy PhD, published one well-regarded paper on distributive justice, and then left academia to build this company. He is ninety-three now, too frail to attend our annual members' meeting, and he has asked me -- through a characteristically bossy letter -- to use my ten-minute opening remarks to explain, plainly, the moral idea on which he founded the cooperative, because he thinks our recent marketing has turned us into just another insurer and the members have forgotten why we exist. The idea is luck egalitarianism, and in particular the distinction that runs through his old paper: some misfortunes arrive as brute luck, simply befalling people through no choice of theirs, and a just arrangement pools and compensates those; other losses are the foreseeable price of gambles people freely chose, and asking others to absorb those is a kind of unfairness. He built the cooperative's underwriting rules on exactly that line -- we cover what befalls you, we have always refused to underwrite certain speculative ventures, and members whose premiums subsidize strangers are, on his view, receiving something back morally rather than merely paying out.

The difficulty, and the reason I'm hiring you rather than writing this myself: the speech has to make that line feel both demanding and humane to an audience of about two hundred ordinary policyholders, none of whom has read a word of political philosophy, without collapsing into either of the two failures my founder dreads. The first failure is the everything-is-luck failure -- the speech must not imply that effort, prudence, and responsibility are illusions, because he explicitly rejects that and because half our members prudently maintain their homes and deserve to hear that this matters. The second failure is the you-earned-your-fortune failure -- the speech must not flatter the room that everyone present simply deserves whatever they have, which he considers the one thought his company was founded to refute. There will also be four or five people in the audience -- a retired professor, a journalist who covers philosophy, the founder's granddaughter -- who will catch any mischaracterization of the view, so the underlying ideas have to be accurate even when simplified.

The speech should run about 900 words, open with one concrete, non-cliched image or story that carries the distinction (do not open with a dictionary definition, a hypothetical drowning stranger, or an anecdote about my founder's childhood), address directly and warmly the objection a skeptical member would raise -- if we're pooling bad luck anyway, why should I bother being careful? -- and close by making the members feel that their premiums are an achievement rather than an expense. Include no quotations from philosophers and no names of philosophers or books; my founder wants the idea to stand on its own two feet in plain language, and he says namedropping is how insurance men sound when they're bluffing. Give me the brainstorming first -- three or four possible framings with a sentence on each -- then the full text of the speech.
