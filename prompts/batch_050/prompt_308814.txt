I teach a second-year undergraduate module on rational choice, and we've just finished the unit on bounded rationality. My students all came away with the same picture: satisficing is what you do when search is costly, and the only reason anyone ever stops at a good-enough option rather than finding the best one is that looking further takes time, effort or money. A student put this to me after the lecture and I realised that every case in my notes — and every one I could think of — really does lean on search costs in exactly this way. So I want a thought experiment that prises the two apart, and I'd like you to build it for me.

Construct a single realistic scenario — flat-hunting, hiring, choosing a school, something of that sort, and please nothing involving demons, perfect predictors, or stipulated utilities — in which a satisficer ends up strictly better off than a maximiser, even though the scenario stipulates that search is costless and instantaneous, that both agents face precisely the same menu of options, and that there is no deadline or other time pressure. The satisficer's advantage must therefore flow from the structure of the decision rule itself rather than from economising on search. You may introduce frictions of other kinds (imperfect discrimination between options, for instance, or evaluations that shift with the comparison set), but you may not make the maximiser careless, irrational, or ignorant of anything the satisficer knows, and the satisficer's aspiration level must be set sensibly rather than rigged to win.

After the scenario, do the checking properly. State each agent's decision rule precisely, including how the satisficer's threshold is set; walk through the choice each rule produces and why; and identify the single feature of the case that does the work, telling me what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed. Then give the maximiser's best counter-attack — I expect it will be that a properly specified maximiser, one who maximises expected value given whatever frictions you've built in, would simply mimic or beat the satisficer, so the case only shows a badly specified maximiser rather than a genuine advantage for satisficing — and respond to it honestly on the satisficer's behalf, conceding whatever should be conceded.
