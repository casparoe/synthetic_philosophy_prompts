I'm a software engineer with a long-standing side interest in philosophy of mind and action, and I'm trying to get clear on when a computational system counts as an agent rather than a tool. My rough, private vocabulary has been to call some systems "wrenches" -- they do work only in a user's grip, and nothing about them points anywhere once the user lets go -- and others "compasses" -- they steer toward something, and would keep steering even if no one watched. I'd like to know whether this gut distinction corresponds to any principled line, so here are my verdicts on cases.

A thermostat: plainly a wrench. Stockfish, the chess engine: a wrench, even though it searches a game tree and in some sense tries to force mate -- it does this only when invoked, within a closed board. A robot vacuum that maps my house and schedules its own cleanings: a wrench. A deep reinforcement-learning system trained to maximize its score in a video game: a compass -- it has an objective and pursues it. A self-driving car: a compass, though I say this reluctantly. A GPS navigator that silently re-routes me around traffic without being asked: a wrench, but this is the case I go back and forth on, because it takes initiative. A large language model answering a single question in a chat window: a wrench. The very same model placed in a loop with access to my email and calendar and instructed to keep my inbox under control: a compass. A recommendation system optimizing total watch-time across millions of users: a wrench in any single invocation, yet something about its aggregate behavior feels compass-like, and I can't articulate why.

The tension I can't resolve myself is between the game-playing RL system and Stockfish. Both optimize an objective inside a closed, fully specified environment; both "pursue" something in exactly the same structural sense. Yet I call one a compass and the other a wrench, and the only difference I can point to is that the RL system was trained rather than programmed, which seems like a fact about its origin, not about what it is.

My tentative principle: a system is an agent when its outputs make a difference to the world directly rather than only through a user who must first interpret and act on them. But I can already see that this principle convicts the RL game-agent of being a wrench, since nothing it does leaves the game, and it may acquit the recommendation system of being one, since its outputs change what millions of people watch without any single user mediating. I'm not sure I'm willing to pay either price.

What I'd like from you: test my tentative principle against these cases and repair it, or replace it. The deliverable is a principle (or two serious candidates) stated precisely, with an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why those should be the ones to go. Please also apply your preferred principle to three cases I haven't listed: a high-frequency trading algorithm, a spam filter, and a predictive-text model.

After your analysis, include a summary table with exactly these columns: Case; My verdict; Verdict under your preferred principle; Verdict kept or overturned; One-sentence rationale. Rows should cover all nine of my cases plus the three new ones. Please write in American English.
