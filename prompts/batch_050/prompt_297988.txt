I am a retired biochemist who has spent the past two years working through the philosophy of economics, and I now help run a small evening seminar for similarly waylaid natural scientists. Next month we are hosting a philosopher of economics who defends full interpersonal comparability of utility: on her view, statements of the form "the benefit to this patient exceeds the cost to that taxpayer" are genuinely true or false, and cost-benefit analysis and optimal-tax theory are right to rely on them. I would like eight to ten interview questions that do more than invite her to restate the position. At minimum, I want the exchange to reach four pressure points: first, the ordinalist objection that utility functions are unique only up to monotone transformation, so that cross-person comparisons are not invariant under re-scaling and hence not meaningful — including the reply I expect she will give about cardinalization via choice under risk, and a rejoinder pressing whether von Neumann–Morgenstern cardinality captures welfare or merely attitudes toward gambles; second, Robbins's charge that such comparisons are disguised ethical judgments rather than scientific claims; third, the problem of other minds as it bears on verifying that one person's pleasure outweighs another's pain, including whether clinical pain scales or eventual neuroscientific measures could settle the matter or only shift it; and fourth, whether her defense, if successful, actually vindicates the use of QALYs and distributional weights in policy, or merely licenses them as useful conventions. For each question, supply the answer you think she is most likely to give and a follow-up keyed to that answer. Finally, be candid about the security of your own draft: tell me which anticipated reply you are least confident she would actually give, where your reconstruction of the strongest objections is most likely to have failed, and what consideration, if you encountered it, would lead you to reorder or rewrite the questions.
