I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting a long essay on how twentieth-century fiction absorbed the philosophy of time. The section I'm struggling with pairs Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five with Borges's writings on time. Read the novel through that lens. The Tralfamadorians teach Billy Pilgrim that all moments exist at once and that death is merely one moment among many others that continue to be; this is a version of the eternalist or block-universe picture. Borges, by contrast, argues in his refutations of time that succession itself is unreal: there is no series of moments, only the present instant, and an experience that recurs in every detail is not a second occurrence but the same one. On the surface these are opposite metaphysics -- one insists every moment exists, the other that only one does -- yet both seem engineered to soften the same grief, and both yield a strange consolation in the face of death. Where does the novel commit itself to exactly the picture of time Borges attacks? Where does it inadvertently dramatize his position instead? And is there anything the novel can do -- through structure, tone, the refrain that follows every death -- that Borges's argumentative prose cannot? I'd like a reading faithful to both: don't flatten Vonnegut into an illustration of Borges, and don't bend Borges to fit the book.
