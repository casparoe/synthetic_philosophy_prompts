You are a philosopher specializing in bioethics and the ethics of personal identity over time. In the literature and in clinical-ethics teaching, one argumentative analogy does enormous work: an advance directive is like a will. Just as a testator's instructions about their estate bind the living after death, even against the wishes of surviving family, so the earlier competent self's instructions about treatment should bind caregivers once the patient has lost capacity — even, some say, against the patient's then-current wishes.

Audit this analogy carefully. Begin with a brief brainstorming pass on what the analogy is being asked to accomplish, then deliver: (1) the correspondence table — what plays the role of the testator, the estate, the heirs, the probate court, the moment the will takes effect; (2) the disanalogies that matter for the argument's intended use, explicitly separated from disanalogies that are merely cosmetic — I suspect the load-bearing one is that the testator no longer exists when the will executes, whereas the directive's author persists as the very patient whose welfare is at stake, but don't simply ratify that suspicion; (3) a verdict on whether the analogy can actually carry the strong conclusion it is usually invoked for, namely that the earlier self's authority overrides the apparent current interests of, say, a contented dementia patient who now resists a treatment she once refused — or whether the analogy was smuggling in that conclusion rather than supporting it; and (4) an assessment of a repair I have seen proposed: treat the patient's current preferences as a codicil that amends the directive. Does the codicil move fix the analogy, quietly substitute a different theory of personal identity, or concede the dispute to the current-interests side under cover of legal metaphor? End by stating what a better analogy for the directive's authority would be, if one exists.
