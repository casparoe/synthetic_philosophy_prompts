You are a professional epistemologist with particular expertise in the value-of-knowledge literature, and I could use your help preparing next week's meeting of a graduate reading group I run. We have been working through the so-called Meno problem — the question why knowledge should be prized above mere true belief — and the group got genuinely stuck on the exchange in Plato's Meno where Socrates observes that, for practical purposes, true opinion seems to do everything knowledge does:

If a man knew the way to Larisa, or any other place you please, and walked there and led others, would he not give right and good guidance? ... Well, and a person who had a right opinion as to which was the way, but had never been there and did not really know, might give right guidance, might he not? ... And so long, I presume, as he has right opinion about that which the other man really knows, he will be just as good a guide—if he thinks the truth instead of knowing it—as the man who has the knowledge.

(Plato, Meno 97a–b, trans. W. R. M. Lamb, Loeb Classical Library, 1924.) Socrates' own answer, a few lines later, is that true opinions are like the statues of Daedalus: they are liable to run away, and only become knowledge once they are tied down by an account of the reason why. My students found this solution oddly satisfying, and I want a case that forces them to say exactly where, if anywhere, it breaks.

Construct a thought experiment that stress-tests the Meno's solution. Build a single, ordinary, present-day scenario — no lotteries, no barn-façade counties, no identical twins or doppelgängers, no testimony chains longer than one link — in which an agent holds a belief meeting all of the following conditions: (1) the belief is true; (2) it is stable, in the sense that the agent would not abandon it under ordinary questioning or everyday friction, so it is not a runaway opinion in Socrates' sense; (3) it is tethered, in the sense that the agent can articulate an account of why the believed proposition is true, and — this is the hard direction, so take it seriously — every statement in that account is itself true, with no false lemmas anywhere; and yet (4) it is intuitively clear that the agent does not know. The point of the case is to test whether stability plus a true tethering account is sufficient for knowledge, so it fails if the verdicts are merely stipulated rather than generated by the scenario's structure. Before committing, brainstorm two or three candidate scenarios and briefly explain why you rejected the runners-up.

After presenting your scenario (one paragraph, please), do the check: state the belief, the method by which it was formed, the tethering account the agent would actually give, and why each of conditions (1)–(4) holds. Then identify which structural feature of the case does the work, say what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed, and give the strongest reply available to a defender of the Meno solution — for instance, that a genuine tether must connect the belief to the very fact that makes it true, rather than to truths in the vicinity. Finally, step back to the value problem my reading group started from: does your case show that the extra value of knowledge over true belief cannot lie in stability-plus-account at all, or only that Socrates' version of the tether needs sharpening? Close with a last line of exactly this form: Verdict: [one sentence stating whether Socrates' tethering solution survives your case intact].
