I'm a philosophy prof (ancients mostly, so I have opinions about the Protagoras but get shakier after about 1970) and I've agreed to cover a guest lecture for our behavioral econ department's undergrad course while their usual lecturer is on sabbatical. The topic they gave me is weakness of will and hyperbolic discounting, one seventy-five minute slot. The students have utility theory and exponential discounting under their belts but zero philosophy, and my fear is they'll file the whole thing under "people are impatient, next slide." Don't give me the lecture; give me the plan for it. What's the right order of ideas? I have a hunch the Socratic puzzle (how is acting against your own better judgment even intelligible?) has to come before hyperbolic discounting is introduced, so the discounting shows up as a candidate explanation of the puzzle rather than as a redefinition of it away -- but maybe you think that's backwards, so argue with me if so, and tell me exactly which concepts depend on which.

Also: what single example should carry the whole lecture? The classics (the snooze button, one more episode, Ulysses tied to the mast) all feel off to me for this crowd -- Ulysses smuggles in precommitment as the solution before the problem has been earned, and the snooze button invites the deflationary "he just changed his mind, nothing to explain" reading that econ students are primed to give. So pick a carrying case, tell me what it must have and must lack, and tell me where the standard cases can appear later once the students can't misuse them. Then the misconception map: at minimum I'm expecting "hyperbolic discounting just means a really high discount rate," "time-inconsistent preferences are automatically irrational," "akrasia requires feeling torn or conflicted," and "revealed preference means there IS no acting against your own good, so the whole topic is confused." For each one, tell me where in the sequence it will predictably sprout and how to head it off at exactly that point rather than after.

Finally: one question I can put on the screen with five minutes left that would show the distinction between a change of heart, akrasia, and time-inconsistent preference has actually landed (not a definitional quiz -- something that forces them to classify a fresh case). And tell me what to deliberately leave out at this level and to SAY I'm leaving out, so the two or three students who've read ahead don't derail things -- I'm guessing naive-vs-sophisticated agents and whether hyperbolic discounting even survives empirically both belong on the cutting-room floor, but you tell me.
