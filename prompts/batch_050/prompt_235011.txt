I co-teach a graduate writing seminar in epistemology and philosophy of mind, and I've noticed that my students have picked up "myth of the given" as a versatile insult: they deploy it against sense-data theories, acquaintance accounts of introspection, phenomenal conservatism, and even plain reliabilism, usually without being able to say what exactly would have to be true of a view for the accusation to stick. Next semester I want to turn Sellars's critique (as developed in Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind and refined by later commentators) into a diagnostic procedure that each student will run on the others' draft papers before workshop. The users will be competent philosophy PhD students but not Sellars specialists; the procedure has to be executable in under an hour on a 9,000-word manuscript, by someone who has read a summary of the Sellarsian material but not the original. So I'm asking you to write that procedure: a sequence of tests, applied in order, each with a statement of what counts as passing, what counts as failing, and where the test simply hands the question back to a human because no checklist can settle it.

The procedure needs to keep the core distinctions intact while remaining usable. In particular it should separate at least the following, since my students routinely conflate them: (i) a view's merely granting some beliefs or states non-inferential justificatory force, which by itself is no sin; (ii) a view's positing a state whose mere occurrence is supposed to confer justification on beliefs independently of the subject's possessing any conceptual capacities whatsoever — the classic epistemic sense of the given, in which something is both foundationally justifying and pre-conceptual; (iii) a view's relying on what Sellars called sheer receptivity, treating the mind's passively receiving impressions as itself a kind of knowing; and (iv) the tension Sellars dramatizes with his triad about sensing particulars versus knowing facts — roughly, that if the ability to have raw sensory episodes is unacquired while the ability to know facts requires acquired conceptual skills, then sensing alone cannot constitute knowing, and any view that quietly moves from one to the other inherits the problem. The procedure should ask, at each manuscript, questions like: does the argument require the justifier to be non-conceptual, or does it merely happen not to mention concepts? Does the author equivocate between a state being unmediated and its being epistemically efficacious on its own? Does the view need the subject's recognition of the state to be infallible, and if so, is that infallibility doing epistemic work the author hasn't acknowledged? I'd also like the procedure to contain an explicit "false positive" stage — a step where the referee asks whether the accused view can be charitably reformulated so that the justifier is conceptually laden after all, since much of the literature's disagreement is about whether this rescue succeeds for any given target.

Please then run the finished procedure on three paper sketches so my students can see it in action: (1) a draft defending phenomenal conservatism, on which its seeming to S that p gives S prima facie justification for believing p, where the author insists seemings are non-doxastic but is silent on whether they are non-conceptual; (2) a draft arguing that one's own occurrent thoughts are self-presenting — that to consciously think that p is thereby to be in a position to know non-inferentially and incorrigibly that one is thinking that p, with no act of conceptual classification required; and (3) a draft drawing on developmental psychology to argue that pre-linguistic infants' perceptual discriminations justify proto-beliefs, offered as evidence that justification outruns conceptual mastery. One of these verdicts should be an answer I will not like: I am personally sympathetic to (1), and I want the procedure to have the spine to flag it if that is where the tests lead, or to exonerate it only for stated, checkable reasons. Finally, end with a candid section on the procedure's limits: which steps are judgment calls wearing procedural clothing, which well-known positions in the vicinity (acquaintance theories, dogmatism, non-conceptual content views) are contested cases that reasonable users of the checklist will score differently, and what a student should do when the procedure's verdict depends on an unsettled question in Sellars exegesis rather than on anything in the manuscript itself.
