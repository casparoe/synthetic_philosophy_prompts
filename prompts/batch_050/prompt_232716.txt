I've spent a fair amount of time on LessWrong and adjacent forums, and I keep running into what looks like an internal tension in the community's position on time preference. The standard line, inherited from Sidgwick and Ramsey, is that pure time preference is irrational: there is no reason to weight a good less heavily merely because it occurs later, so once you've corrected for the probability that the later good never materializes, your discount rate should be zero. But it's also commonly argued that existential and other risks accumulate over time, so the probability that any given future generation exists decays roughly exponentially with the date. A risk-corrected discount function will therefore look exponential — which is precisely the functional form economists use to model pure time preference, and the one usually singled out as the only time-consistent way to implement it.

So the position seems to collapse into: pure time preference is irrational, yet a fully rational agent ends up with exactly the discount function that pure time preference would have produced. This suggests to me that either the condemnation of pure time preference is toothless, since nothing behaviorally distinguishes the two, or there must be some observable difference between a risk-based and a preference-based exponential discounter. What is that difference, and does it have any practical upshot — say, for cause prioritization between near-term and long-term interventions — or is the whole debate just a terminological dispute about how to label the same curve?
