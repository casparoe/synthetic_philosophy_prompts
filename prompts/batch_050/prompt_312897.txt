Consider the following thought experiment, modelled on Warren Quinn's puzzle of the self-torturer. A person has a device implanted with 1,001 settings, numbered 0 to 1,000. At any time they may advance the dial by a single setting, whereupon they receive £10,000. The increase in pain from any one setting to the next is imperceptible to them, yet the cumulative difference between setting 0 and setting 1,000 is the difference between no pain at all and agony so severe that no sum of money could compensate for enduring it. Once advanced, the dial cannot be turned back.

Write an essay defending the following claim: the standard diagnosis of this predicament mislocates its source. The standard diagnosis holds that the predicament arises because the person's preferences are intransitive — each single step is preferred to its predecessor, yet the final outcome is dispreferred to the starting point. Argue instead that the person's preferences may be perfectly transitive throughout, and that the predicament arises because preferences over adjacent options are formed on the basis of what the person can perceptually discriminate, which is an unreliable guide to cumulative value. In the course of your argument, address two objections. First, the objection from commitment: a person who resolves in advance to stop at a particular setting appears to face no puzzle at all, which suggests the original problem was one of willpower rather than of preference structure. Explain why your diagnosis can accommodate this point without collapsing into it. Second, the objection from reversibility: suppose the dial could be turned backwards at will, with money once received never needing to be returned. Consider whether your diagnosis, unlike the intransitivity diagnosis, can explain why this amended case still presents a difficulty for the agent — and what that shows about where the philosophical interest of the original case really lies.
