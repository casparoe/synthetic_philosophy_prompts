My grandfather was a boy in rural Bengal during the 1943 famine, and the handful of stories he told about it -- the price of rice doubling week to week, people who owned nothing saleable arriving in Calcutta to die -- have been on my mind since I joined a reading group that took up the debate over the British Empire's legacy this spring. We read Niall Ferguson's Empire and Shashi Tharoor's Inglorious Empire back to back, and the group split predictably: Ferguson treats the Raj as a chapter in a larger story in which British rule, whatever its crimes, delivered railways, law, and the institutional preconditions of a modern state, while Tharoor treats it as a extractive enterprise whose costs to India were enormous and whose "gifts" were built for extraction and repaid many times over. What struck me is that both men, despite reaching opposite conclusions, argue in the same way: they run a ledger, tallying benefits against harms. When someone in the group raised the famine, the conversation stalled, because nobody could say which ledger it belonged on -- and I came away suspecting the disagreement runs deeper than either book.

Here is the analysis I want. Take the Bengal famine of 1943 as the test case and run it through this dispute carefully. The salient facts, as I understand them: roughly two to three million people died; the famine unfolded under wartime conditions, with Burma fallen and a Japanese threat on the border; and Amartya Sen's famous entitlement analysis in Poverty and Famines argued that Bengal did not suffer a straightforward collapse in food availability -- the catastrophe ran through wartime inflation, procurement and denial policies, hoarding, and a governing structure in which the entitlements of the rural poor were nobody's urgent responsibility. First, map the episode onto each side's case: what, exactly, does the famine count as for Ferguson -- a wartime tragedy that net-benefit accounting across decades should absorb -- and what does it count as for Tharoor, whose case is precisely that the political structure determined whose starvation mattered? Second, do the harder thing: ask whether Sen's analysis, by relocating the explanation from aggregate supply to the distribution of claims and the accountability of rulers, undermines the ledger methodology itself. If famine deaths result from whose claims counted rather than from how much wealth existed, then perhaps tallying an empire's gross outputs against its gross harms is the wrong instrument, whichever side comes out ahead.

I am not looking for a judicious survey of both books -- I have sat through that conversation twice now. I want a committed verdict: either the famine belongs decisively on one side of the ledger (say which, state the other side's strongest reply, and explain why it fails), or the episode shows that the balance-sheet framework is the wrong way to judge empire at all (say what should replace it, and what the best defense of the framework gets right before you set it aside). Whichever position you take, name the single consideration you found hardest to dismiss and show why it did not move you in the end.
