I'm writing a paper for a graduate seminar on rational choice theory, and I keep circling back to Warren Quinn's puzzle of the self-torturer. To fix the setup: a device with settings 0 through 1000 is attached to a person; at each setting he may permanently advance the dial by one notch, receiving a substantial payment (say $10,000) in exchange for an increase in pain so small as to be imperceptible. Yet the thousandth notch leaves him in constant agony, a state he would gladly pay a fortune to escape. The puzzle is that advancing the dial seems rational at every single notch, while the endpoint reached by a thousand such rational steps is transparently worse than where he began.

Write an essay that adjudicates between three competing readings of what the puzzle shows. On the first reading, the puzzle demonstrates that rational preferences can be cyclic — the self-torturer prefers notch n+1 to notch n for every n, yet prefers 0 to 1000 — so transitivity is not a genuine requirement of rationality, and money-pump arguments to the contrary must fail somewhere. On the second reading, the self-torturer's preferences remain transitive throughout, and the puzzle instead exposes a gap between the rationality of each local choice and the rationality of the global pattern they compose, so that the real lesson concerns diachronic structure rather than the axioms of preference. On the third reading, the puzzle is an artifact of vagueness: because adjacent pain levels are indiscriminable, there is no determinate fact of the matter about what is preferred at each notch, so the standard machinery of preference-based rationality simply has no clean application here. Weigh the strongest considerations for each reading — including how each handles the analogy with sorites paradoxes, how each responds to the classic money-pump argument for transitivity, and whether each survives a version of the case in which the pain increments are small but perceptible — but do not end on a non-committal note: defend one reading as correct.

Finally, to keep the analysis honest, apply your verdict to the following variant of the case. Before the dial is ever turned, the self-torturer is offered a binding one-time choice: either commit to remaining at setting 0 forever, or commit to receiving the full sequence of 1000 offers, each of which will be individually revocable at the moment it arrives (so that once committed, he will face exactly the original series of temptations). Explain what rationality requires in this variant given your verdict on the original puzzle, and whether the variant reveals anything — about the relationship between foresight, commitment, and stepwise choice — that the original framing obscures.
