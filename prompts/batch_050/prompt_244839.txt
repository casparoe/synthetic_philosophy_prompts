After reading about Douglas Hofstadter's Luring Lottery — a lottery anyone may enter as many times as they like, where the prize shrinks as the total number of entries grows, so that the "rational" outcome seems to be that almost nobody enters at all — I've come to accept the superrationalist position: in a one-shot, symmetric situation with agents I take to reason much as I do, I should choose as though I were choosing for all of them. Hence I should enter the lottery at most once, and cooperate in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a sufficiently similar agent, without needing any enforcement or iteration. Tell me what would count against this position — not just the flat assertion that defection dominates, but which cases, formal results, or empirical findings would genuinely trouble it. For instance: does the view stand or fall with the assumption that the other entrants reason identically, and does it degrade gracefully when I only know they reason similarly? Would it survive a demonstration that superrationality collapses into ordinary expected-utility reasoning with correlated beliefs, or one showing its recommendations become unstable under small asymmetries? Please distinguish defeaters a committed superrationalist would themselves concede from ones only critics would press, and rank them from fatal to merely awkward. Also note where your assessment is least secure: which single consideration, if it came out differently than you assume, would most change your verdict.
