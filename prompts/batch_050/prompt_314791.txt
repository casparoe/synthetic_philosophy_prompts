Two analogies keep doing all the argumentative work in my local housing fights and I want both of them audited. The anti-development side: a neighborhood is like a club -- the people already there decide who joins, how fast it grows, and what the character is, so opposing a new apartment building is just a club politely declining members. The pro-housing side: a city is like a public road -- being there first gives you no veto over who else shows up, so incumbent homeowners have no more right to block new residents than drivers have to block new cars. For each analogy give me the correspondence table (what plays the role of membership fees, capacity limits, "club atmosphere", congestion), where the mapping breaks, and what each one quietly smuggles in -- e.g. clubs are things nobody owes you membership in and you can always exit, whereas a neighborhood gates access to jobs and schools; roads are publicly funded, but then so is a lot of the suburb's unpriced land value. Then the verdict: does either side's actual conclusion -- that there is or isn't a legitimate local right to say no -- survive the disanalogies, and if neither analogy can carry it, what's a better one for whatever zoning power over future residents really is?
