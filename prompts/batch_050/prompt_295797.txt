Write a poem that enacts the Jain doctrine of conditional predication, syādvāda, rather than merely describing it. The poem should consist of exactly seven stanzas, tracking in order the sevenfold predication (saptabhaṅgī): (1) in some respect, it is; (2) in some respect, it is not; (3) in some respect, it is and is not, said successively, from standpoints taken one at a time; (4) in some respect, it is inexpressible, because the attempt to assert both at once outruns what a single utterance can say; and then (5)–(7) the three compounds of inexpressibility with the first three. Take a single everyday subject throughout — a clay pot, a mango, a tree — and let each stanza assert or deny it from a standpoint that genuinely makes its predication hold: with respect to its own substance, place, time, and mode the thing is; with respect to another substance, place, time, or mode it is not. The refrain or the turn of each stanza should carry the force of syāt — from a certain standpoint, conditionally — so that no stanza flatly contradicts another and the conditionality is felt structurally, not just stated.

Two constraints matter for philosophical accuracy. First, keep the third stanza's joint predication successive and the fourth's simultaneous: that difference, not mere hesitation, is what distinguishes them, and the fourth stanza should convey speech stalling because the two claims arrive together rather than in turn. Second, do not let the poem drift into skepticism or lazy relativism: each conditional predication states a genuine truth about a reality that is many-sided (anekānta), not unknowable, and the final stanza should land somewhere steadier than the thought that nothing can be said at all.

Do not retell the parable of the blind men and the elephant; the standpoint-structure should arise from the logic of the predications themselves. Use a consistent stanza form and meter of your choosing, and make it succeed as a poem first: a reader who has never heard of Jain logic should still feel the subject turning in the light, while a reader who has should find each stanza's standpoint defensible.
