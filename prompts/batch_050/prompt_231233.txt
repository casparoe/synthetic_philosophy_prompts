So I've been chewing on the Lucas/Penrose stuff again (minds, machines, Gödel) after an argument in a comment thread, and I wrote down where I've landed. Before I defend this anywhere I want to know whether the package is actually coherent, because I have a nagging feeling I've got commitments pointing in different directions and I honestly can't tell which ones. Here's the set:

1. The Lucas–Penrose argument doesn't work. The crucial step is "we can see that the Gödel sentence of the system is true," but that only follows if we know the system is consistent, and if we ARE the system, Gödel's second theorem says we can't know that. So there's no asymmetry between the human and the machine — neither can establish its own consistency, end of story.

2. Rationality requires being able to rationally trust your own careful reasoning. Like, if I've checked a proof carefully, I don't just have justification for the theorem, I also get justification for believing my reasoning was sound. A reasoner who in principle could never be justified in believing their own reasoning is consistent couldn't do mathematics with any real confidence at all.

3. Whole brain emulation will work, at least in principle. A faithful computational emulation of my brain would be conscious, would be me, and would be at least as good at math as I am. I'm a functionalist, pretty standard LessWrong-era view I think.

4. Arithmetical truth is determinate and outruns provability in any formal system. The Gödel sentence of PA is just TRUE, it's not undecidable in some spooky truth-valueless sense. I guess this makes me some kind of platonist.

5. Actual human mathematicians are inconsistent sometimes (mistakes, overlooked cases), but an idealized version of a human reasoner — the thing we're approximating when we're careful — is consistent. The idealization is legit for these arguments.

So: does this hang together? Go through and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which ones only look like they're in tension but dissolve when you look closer, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each one, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs me. I'm especially suspicious that something in 1 and 2 is doing more work than I noticed, but I could be wrong about where the problem is — don't just confirm my suspicion, actually check everything.

Also: for whatever verdict you give, tell me what would change your mind — like, what argument or consideration would flip your assessment of the worst tension — and flag where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fall apart.
