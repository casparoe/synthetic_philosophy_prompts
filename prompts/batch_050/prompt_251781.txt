I'm doing a reading group on Hume's essay Of Suicide and I'm supposed to bring an objection to it that the group hasn't already beaten to death, so I figured I'd outsource the brainstorming. The essay, as I read it, tries to show suicide is blameless by running through our duties one at a time: it's no crime against God, since the whole course of nature is governed by general laws and human affairs are no exception -- if it's not presumptuous to divert a river or alter the motion of some ounces of blood in my veins by taking medicine, it's not presumptuous to alter the motion of the blood in the bigger channel; it's no crime against society, since a man who quits life only ceases to do good, which is the smallest imaginable harm, and his obligations to others presuppose that he keeps living and contributing, which is exactly what's in question; and it's no crime against himself, since when life contains more misery than satisfaction, prudence itself seems to counsel ending it, and nobody is obligated to keep playing a losing hand out of sheer fear of death. Fine. Here are the objections I already have in my notes and don't need repeated: (1) the Kantian one -- suicide treats one's rational nature as a mere means to escaping pain, and a person can't be used as a thing even by himself; (2) the sanctity-of-life one -- life is a gift from God, or a post we've been stationed at, and only the one who gave it can release us; (3) the relational one -- Hume's "no harm to society" assumes people are detachable atoms, but suicide devastates spouses, kids, dependents, and those duties don't evaporate when life gets bad; (4) the impaired-judgment one -- most actual suicides happen under depression or temporary despair, the decision is irreversible, and the cost of a false negative (staying alive when you shouldn't have wanted to) is trivial next to a false positive; (5) the rule-level one -- even if some suicides are justified, a strong social prohibition protects people in moments of weakness, and Hume argues at the level of individual acts where he should argue at the level of rules.

Is there an objection I'm missing? It needs to be genuinely different from all five -- not a rewording, not a special case, and not just two of them stapled together. It also has to actually engage Hume's own arguments (the natural-laws move, the "ceasing to do good" move, the prudence move) rather than just asserting a rival framework, because "well Kant disagrees" isn't an objection so much as a change of subject. Once you've given it, tell me honestly whether you think it's any good -- does it hit a real weak point in the essay or is it just novel? And say what would change your assessment: what would Hume's best reply look like, and if that reply works, which of my five listed objections would you fall back on as the strongest of the bunch and why.
