Quick background so we're on the same page: in A Treatise on Probability (1921), Keynes introduces an idea usually called the weight of an argument. Roughly, two probability judgments can assign the same degree of belief to some conclusion while differing in how much relevant evidence stands behind them. Getting more evidence doesn't always push the probability up — sometimes it pushes it down — but either way, Keynes thinks, something else has grown: the judgment now rests on a more substantial basis.

Here's my problem. I keep a running list of the standard ways people try to cash out what this extra dimension of "weight" is, and I want a new one. My list so far:

1. Weight as sheer amount of relevant evidence — the more of it you have, the heavier the argument.
2. Weight as completeness — the proportion of the total knowable relevant evidence you actually possess, i.e., the balance between relevant knowledge and relevant ignorance.
3. Weight as confidence in the estimate itself — a second-order sureness that your probability assignment is the right one (the reading that casts Keynes as a forerunner of later ambiguity and robustness talk).
4. Weight as practical guidance — whatever property of an argument tells you how much it's rational to stake on a probability when you act (whether to insure, diversify, hold off, or go all in).
5. Weight as resilience — how stable the probability would be under new evidence; high weight means incoming evidence is unlikely to move it much.

Note that 1 and 2 genuinely differ: you can hold a mountain of evidence that's a tiny fraction of what exists, or a handful of facts that exhausts nearly everything knowable. Keep that in mind.

Also, the move of saying weight is just vague or interval-valued probability is off the table; I consider that a flavor of 3.
