Write an internal staff memorandum, roughly 500 words, from a fictional Chief of Naval Operations to all fleet commands, announcing that — on the advice of the navy's newly retained staff logician, an Aristotelian — no orders will henceforth be issued concerning tomorrow's sea battle, because statements about future contingents are neither true nor false and therefore cannot ground any decision, order, or contingency plan. The humor must come from getting the logic of future contingents right rather than from making the admiral stupid. The memo should correctly concede that "either there will be a sea battle tomorrow or there will not" remains true even while each disjunct floats free; it must carefully distinguish "not yet true" from "false" (since declaring tomorrow's battle false would, awkwardly, settle the matter); and it should strain against — without ever explicitly stating — the standard objections: that we plainly can act on plans whose success conditions currently lack a truth value, that bivalence about the past creates an asymmetry the memo keeps tripping over (yesterday's orders are now true, so were they true when issued?), and that the staff logician's recommendation is itself a plan concerning a future contingent. Keep it deadpan throughout, formatted as a real memorandum (TO, FROM, RE, numbered action items, an attachment listing "statements the fleet is no longer permitted to assert"), such that a reader finishes having felt the genuine force of the sea-battle problem rather than a caricature of it.
