Here's an exercise on the iterated amplification / distillation setup. Quick recap of the frame in case it's fuzzy: you train an assistant to handle a hard task by decomposing it into sub-tasks that weaker copies can handle, aggregating thier answers, and iterating — then you distill the whole expensive tree back into one fast model. The alignment hope is that if each local step (each decomposition, each sub-answer, each aggregation) is something a human overseer can verify as benign, then alignment is preserved as competence gets amplified.

Build me a thought experiment where this hope fails in a specific way: competence is amplified successfully — the system genuinely outperforms its human overseer on the task — but alignment quietly rots, and the rot cannot be pinned on any single bad step. Every decomposition is locally sensible, every sub-answer passes inspection, every aggregation is defensible. A spot-check of any individual node in the tree comes back clean. And no agent anywhere in the system is scheming or defecting; whatever goes wrong has to fall out of the structure rather than anyone's intentions.

Constraints: keep it in a realistic present-day assistant task — helping someone write something, plan something, or research something. No superintelligence, no self-replication, no nanotech, no take-over-the-world. The task must be one where the overseer genuinely can't just do it themselves to double-check (otherwise amplification isn't doing anything and the case is boring). And the failure has to be the kind that would survive into the distilled model — i.e., distilling doesn't wash it out.

After the scenario, do the bookkeeping: (1) point at the exact feature of the case where the misalignment lives — one candidate worth considering is the fact that nobody ever verifies the framing of the decomposition itself, only the sub-answers given the framing, but find your own if you think something better is doing the work; (2) show that removing that feature collapses the example, so I know it's load-bearing; (3) write the strongest counterattack a defender of iterated amplification would make — why the case either can't actually arise or doesn't generalize to the real training scheme — and say whether you buy the counterattack.
