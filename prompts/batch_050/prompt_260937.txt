Yesterday evening I had a long and rather frustrating argument with a friend about trolley cases, and I came away unsure whether the position I defended is actually coherent. I would like you to act as a professional moral philosopher with particular expertise in normative ethics and the doctrine of double effect, and to audit my commitments for consistency. Please think carefully and slowly about this; begin by briefly brainstorming the pairwise tensions (real or apparent) among the claims below before settling on your final assessment, and then give me the audit itself. Kindly write your answer in British English.

Here is where I currently stand:

(a) In the standard switch case, where a runaway trolley will kill five people unless a bystander diverts it onto a siding where it will kill one, diverting is permissible — indeed, I am inclined to say it is what one ought to do.

(b) In the footbridge case, where the only way to stop the trolley is to push a large stranger off a bridge so that his body halts it, pushing is impermissible, full stop.

(c) The doctrine of double effect gives the correct explanation of why (a) and (b) differ: in the switch case the one's death is a foreseen side effect, whereas in the footbridge case it is a means to saving the five.

(d) In the loop variant — where the siding loops back towards the five, so that the trolley, once diverted, is only stopped from circling round and killing them by the body of the one person on the loop — diverting is still permissible.

(e) An agent's intentions are irrelevant to the permissibility of what they do; permissibility turns on what the agent actually does and on the foreseeable consequences of doing it, not on what was going on in their head.

(f) Numbers count: other things being equal, the fact that more lives are saved is itself a morally weighty reason in favour of acting, and not merely a tie-breaker.

My friend insisted that something in this list doesn't fit, but pressed to say exactly what, she kept gesturing at everything at once, which I found unhelpful. What I want from you is a precise audit, not a lecture on the trolley literature. Specifically:

1. Identify which of these commitments are in genuine tension with one another, and rank the tensions by severity. For each, state exactly what would have to give — which commitment, or which natural motivation for it — and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in my view.
2. Identify any pairings that look tense but dissolve on inspection, and explain briefly why the appearance is misleading.
3. Conclude with the single least costly revision that restores consistency, noting that I am not yet committed to abandoning any particular item on the list.

Please be specific rather than comprehensive: I would rather have two tensions analysed properly than a checklist in which every pair is declared equally problematic.
