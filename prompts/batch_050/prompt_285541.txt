I work in decision theory-adjacent modeling, and over the past couple of years I've come around to risk-weighted expected utility theory as my default account of rational choice under risk. Let me state the view as I hold it, so we're working from the same formulation. An agent has a utility function over outcomes, a probability function over states, and a risk function r that governs how she weighs the probabilities of doing worse than a given outcome. She ranks a gamble by taking the minimum utility it might yield and adding, for each successive improvement, the increment in utility weighted by r applied to the probability of securing at least that increment. When r is convex, the agent is risk-avoidant in a way that captures why an agent can rationally reject a gamble at favorable odds without claiming the stakes are worth less to her; when r is the identity function, the theory collapses back to ordinary expected utility maximization. I accept this view because I think ordinary expected utility theory gets the Allais-style patterns wrong as a matter of rationality, not just of psychology, and because I think aversion to risk is an attitude toward stakes spread, not a fact about the diminishing value of goods, so it deserves its own place in the formalism rather than being folded into the utility function.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure: what would actually count against it. You are an expert decision theorist and philosopher of economics with no stake in defending the view. Before composing your answer, think carefully and brainstorm broadly -- consider objections from within formal decision theory, from the theory's relationship to its rivals, and from what the view commits me to in dynamic and multi-agent settings. Then organize your answer along the following lines.

First, the formal pressure points. What does the theory give up to get what it gets -- which axioms or ordering principles from the standard framework does it reject, and what are the strongest results to the effect that giving those up has costs elsewhere (for example, in sequential choice, in response to new information, or in settings where others can exploit the agent)? For each such cost, say whether it is a proven consequence or a conjectured one, and whether the theory's defenders bite the bullet or have a reply on the merits.

Second, the collapse worries. There are recurring claims to the effect that the theory either reproduces expected utility theory under a redescription of outcomes or utility, or else departs from it only at the price of some pathology. Lay out the strongest version of this dilemma you can construct, and assess whether the theory's distinctive predictions -- the cases where it and expected utility theory genuinely come apart -- are cases where its verdicts are plausible or cases where they are embarrassing.

Third, the expressive-flexibility worry. The risk function is a free parameter, and one might suspect that with enough freedom in r, the theory can rationalize almost any pattern of choices after the fact, which would make it hard for anything to count against it. Assess how serious this is: does the theory place real, checkable constraints on an agent's preferences across different choice problems, and if so, what are they? If some version of the theory is unfalsifiable, say whether that is a defect of the theory or a feature shared by any theory of rational preference with free parameters.

Fourth, what the defenders themselves concede. I am less interested in what hostile critics say than in what committed proponents of the theory already grant as genuine costs or open problems -- which cases they find hardest, which commitments they accept as bullets to bite, and which objections they think remain unanswered. If you think the defenders' own list of concessions is longer or shorter than it should be, say so.

Finally, rank everything. Give me the defeaters and stressors ordered from potentially fatal to merely awkward, and for each one tell me what would have to be true for it to actually sink the theory rather than just raise its price. If your honest assessment is that the view is well insulated against refutation, say that plainly, and then say whether you think that insulation reflects the theory's strength or its slipperiness. I would rather be talked out of a view I hold than reassured about it.
