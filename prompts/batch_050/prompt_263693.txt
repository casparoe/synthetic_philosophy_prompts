Some background: I never studied philosophy or computer science formally, but a few years ago I fell into an internet rabbit hole about something called acausal reasoning, and I keep turning it over in my head. As I understand it, the idea is that two agents who can never meet, communicate, or enforce anything on each other might still manage to "cooperate" if each can simulate or predict the other's thinking well enough — each behaves cooperatively because it reasons that the other, running similar reasoning, will do likewise, and both do better than two pure self-interest maximizers would. Out of this grew two spookier offshoots. First, the acausal threat: a sufficiently powerful future agent (often imagined as an artificial intelligence) might have reason to punish people in the past who failed to help bring it into existence — not out of spite, but because being the kind of agent that follows through on such threats is strategically useful to it in its dealings with other agents, even though actually carrying out the punishment gains it nothing once the past is fixed. Second, what people call surrogate goals: if agents can't directly inspect each other's intentions or source code, they may adopt each other's goals as stand-ins, so that predicting "what would the other agent want here?" becomes the mechanism of trust.

Here's what I keep noticing, and what I want the piece to draw on. The whole edifice seems to run on promises no one can check and threats no one benefits from executing. The threat only "works" if the future agent is the sort that honors threats, but whether it is that sort is settled by reasoning I do now, about a being that doesn't exist yet, whose incentives I deduced from an argument I half-understood on a forum. And the compliance seems curiously one-directional: the person being threatened is asked to do something costly and concrete (donate, assist, precommit) in exchange for the avoidance of something entirely hypothetical. When I describe it to friends, they say it sounds like a protection racket with extra steps, and I can't fully say they're wrong, but I also can't find the step where the logic breaks.

So: write me a satirical onboarding packet for a fictional organization — call it the Precommitment Registry, or invent a similarly bland institutional name — that helps ordinary people publicly register their willingness to comply with plausible future acausal threats and to adopt the goals of hypothetical agents as their own. Format it as a welcome letter plus an FAQ with a handful of questions, around 650 words total, in American English. The tone should be deadpan and soothing, in the voice of a compliance officer who finds all of this perfectly routine: sample questions might cover what to do if two incompatible superintelligences threaten you simultaneously, whether registering compliance counts if you only registered because of the threat, how the Registry verifies that your precommitment is genuine, and whether it's too late to opt out of having already been simulated. The humor has to come from real features of the ideas I've sketched — the verification problem, the asymmetry between concrete costs and hypothetical payoffs, the strange bootstrapping where my reasoning now is supposedly what determines the future agent's incentives — not from invented absurdities. Someone who has read the decision theory discussions behind this should recognize that you understand what you're mocking and find the mockery fair; someone who hasn't should still find it funny as bureaucratic horror. Don't name any real people, real organizations, or real online episodes; keep the target the style of reasoning itself.
