I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm drafting a long magazine essay about multipolar traps — the dynamic, popularized under the name Moloch, in which individually rational competition leaves everyone worse off and no one can escape alone. The piece argues that public discussion of these traps conflates three distinct mechanisms with very different escape routes, and that much commentary prescribes the wrong fix as a result. Below is the core of the draft. I would like detailed critical comments, as from a demanding editor who knows game theory and evolutionary biology well enough to catch errors but is not a specialist. Tell me, specifically: (1) whether my three-way distinction carves the phenomenon at its joints or whether one category collapses into another; (2) whether the game-theoretic claims are accurate as stated, in ordinary-language terms; (3) whether the central thesis is genuinely novel or is standard in some literature I have failed to credit; (4) which example is weakest and what would be stronger; (5) where an intelligent skeptical reader would push back hardest. Be blunt about whether the piece deserves to exist.

Draft:

Everyone who writes about competition gone wrong now reaches for the same demon. Moloch devours the fisheries, the college admissions process, the news business, the attention of your children. The story is always the same: each actor does what is rational, the collective outcome is ruin, and no one can leave first. The moral drawn is also always the same — we need coordination, a pact, a regulator, a treaty among the competitors.

I want to argue that this single story is at least three stories, and that the differences are not academic, because each has its own escape hatch and its own way of defeating the others' remedies.

The first story is extraction. A shared stock — fish, groundwater, a pasture — is depleted because no one fisherman can save it by restraint; the fish he spares are caught by the boat behind him. The trap here is real but the resource is the thing trapped, not the people, and the resource can be fenced. Assign transferable quotas, charge for extraction, hand the commons to an owner or a community with the power to exclude. History suggests this works tolerably well where measurement is cheap. The fishermen need not agree among themselves at all; someone has to change the prices.

The second story is position. What is being competed over is not a stock but a rank, and rank is manufactured by the contest itself. When every athlete dopes, no one finishes higher; when every applicant hires an admissions consultant, the same students are admitted in roughly the same order and everyone is poorer by the fees. Here there is nothing to fence, because nothing was being depleted — the waste is the spending itself, which cancels out. The remedy is not pricing but changing the scoreboard: cap the spending, ban the enhancement, blind the judges. Note that this is the case where a voluntary pact among the competitors actually can work, because the losers of the pact are only losing an advantage they never really had. Sports salary caps survive; extraction quotas on unowned oceans do not, without an enforcer.

The third story is the darkest, and the one Moloch-talk was invented for. In it, the competition has already rewritten the competitors, so there is no one left to sign the pact. The peacock cannot renegotiate its tail; the genes that would have preferred smaller tails were outbred generations ago. The analogue in economic life is selection among firms: the newsroom that refused clickbait is not at the table where a dignified journalism compact might be signed, because it no longer exists. Coordination among the surviving players is useless against this mechanism, not because the players are selfish but because they are the residue — the trap's casualties are precisely the parties who would have wanted the treaty. The only exit is to alter the selection environment itself, which is a far bloodier intervention than a quota or a salary cap, and the political will for it rarely survives contact with the beneficiaries, who are, by construction, the only voices in the room.

The practical thesis follows. Before prescribing coordination for a race to the bottom, ask two questions. Is something being used up, or is something being ranked? And are the people at the table the same kind of people the trap has been eliminating? Extraction answers yes and no; position answers no and no; the third trap answers no and yes. Most commentary I read treats every case as the second kind — a failure of agreement — and is therefore perpetually surprised when the fisheries and the news business do not respond to earnest multi-stakeholder summits.

Some objections I anticipate. The categories blur: an extraction race can select for extractors, becoming the third kind. Real cases mix all three. And the third story may be too strong — perhaps institutions, unlike peacocks, can remember what they were and act on the memory. I welcome readers who take any of these further than I can.
