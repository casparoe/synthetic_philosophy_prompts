I am preparing a seminar handout on computer-assisted proofs and want a decision tree that sorts readers by their actual commitments rather than by familiar labels. Begin with the claim that a result has been checked by a computation no individual mathematician could survey. Use the four-colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture, plus two hypothetical cases: one in which a transparent human argument reduces a theorem to ten million machine-checked cases, and one in which a tiny trusted proof kernel verifies the formal proof but a large team may have encoded the mathematical definitions incorrectly. Build the tree from the underlying questions—whether surveyability is necessary for proof, whether machine output is deductive warrant or empirical evidence, whether checking the reduction suffices, whether a proof must explain as well as establish, and where responsibility for formalization lies. Each node should be a concrete question answerable without knowing a position’s name. End in eight to ten descriptively named leaves, stating each position’s most awkward commitment; mark coherent but unoccupied combinations and flag any leaves the tree cannot distinguish. Open with one sentence identifying the deepest fault line, followed immediately by the tree, with no preamble or restatement of the task. Conclude by identifying the distinction most likely to be unstable and describing the argument or case that would persuade you to redraw the tree.
