Consider two philosophers who have spent years arguing about Dennett's two-bitser: the vending machine whose coin-detecting state reliably goes on in the presence of quarters and which we unhesitatingly describe as registering, misidentifying, or being fooled about coins. The first is a defender of the Searlean distinction between original and derived intentionality: the machine's state is about quarters only because we interpret it that way, as we do with words on a page, whereas some states — ours — are about things intrinsically, and nothing composed of mere physics could be. The second is a Dennettian functionalist who holds that the two-bitser's intentionality is no worse off than ours: if indeterminacy about what the detector "really" detects (quarters, or whatever coin-like objects it encounters after being moved to a new environment) threatens the machine, parallel indeterminacies about the contents of evolved biological states threaten us, and the demand for original intentionality is a demand for something that does not and need not exist.

Draft the joint statement these two could sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. I want:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims — about what the machine in fact does, about what ordinary and scientific ascriptions of content commit us to, about what each side's argument actually presupposes — formulated to be substantive rather than the vacuous common ground that any disputants could mouth.
2. A table of residual disagreements, each stated in wording the relevant side would itself accept, avoiding caricature in both directions.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that resolving them would actually move at least one party. I suspect candidates include whether derived intentionality requires a prior intentional designer (and what natural selection does to that requirement), whether indeterminacy arguments generalize from artifacts to brains, and whether the notion of intrinsic aboutness does explanatory work that functional description cannot. Test these guesses and find the real cruxes.
4. For each crux, a case, argument, or conceivable finding that both parties agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about it recorded in advance.

Finally, be self-critical about the exercise itself: if some part of this dispute resists joint statement — because the two frameworks share too little neutral vocabulary, or because a supposed disagreement dissolves on inspection into different uses of "about" — say so plainly and say which part, rather than manufacturing agreement or disagreement to complete the format.
