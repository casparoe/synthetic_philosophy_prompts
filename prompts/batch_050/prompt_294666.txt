I started a new job recently and the staff handbook has a whole section on what to do when a colleague wrongs or offends you, and honestly it's a weird mix. It says: for minor stuff (a snide comment, being left off an email chain, someone taking credit in a meeting) you should either let it go or raise it quietly with the person one-to-one. For anything serious you're explicitly told not to confront them yourself or gather allies, but to file a complaint with HR and let the process run, and organising any kind of public call-out or group shunning is itself disciplanable. But then, if a complaint is upheld, the wrongdoer can be required to apologise, sometimes in front of the whole team. My flatmate, who did some philosophy at uni, says the handbook basically picks a side in some debate about honour vs dignity vs victimhood cultures, and that it might not even pick one side consistently. What I want to know is: what theory of what an offence actually is does this policy presuppose? Does it assume the wrong lives in my wounded feelings, in the wrongdoer's intent, or in some standing I have that's been damaged regardless of how I feel? Are the different rules even compatible — telling me to shrug off small slights but also requiring formal apologies seems to rest on two different pictures of what an offence is? And which rival views of offence and standing does the policy quietly rule out, and what would it look like if it presupposed those instead?

Give me your answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. And in British English please.
