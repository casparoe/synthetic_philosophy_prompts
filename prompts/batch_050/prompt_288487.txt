Question set: “Using Heidegger’s analysis of equipment in Being and Time, explain the difference between readiness-to-hand and presence-at-hand, and explain what is revealed when equipment breaks, goes missing, or stands in the way. In your answer, discuss whether breakdown merely interrupts activity or whether it tells us something more basic about worldhood and Dasein.”

Student answer:
Heidegger thinks our ordinary way of encountering things is not as detached observers measuring neutral objects. The hammer in hammering is usually ready-to-hand: we use it without thematising it, and its being is given by its place in a practical nexus of references, hammer to nails, nails to wood, wood to shelter, shelter to Dasein’s projects. Presence-at-hand is the more basic mode, where things are just occurrent entities with properties; ready-to-hand is a deficient private modification of that, because once the hammer is put back on the bench it becomes present-at-hand again. The ready-to-hand is therefore subjective utility while the present-at-hand is objective reality.

When the tool breaks, activity stops. For Heidegger this breakdown is only important because it causes a switch from absorbed coping to explicit theoretical reflection, so a broken hammer makes a carpenter into something like a physicist. The malfunction makes the equipment conspicuous, and in that conspicuousness the whole referential context lights up: assignments, in-order-tos, for-the-sake-of-whiches, and the fact that a tool is never isolated. Missing equipment has the same structure as obstinacy: both are cases where the thing becomes too visible and therefore worldhood collapses into anxiety.

This shows that breakdown reveals Dasein as a subject standing over against objects, since only when the flow is interrupted do we notice there was an internal representation of a workshop all along. Worldhood means the sum of presently-at-hand things arranged by human purposes. Breakdown does not disclose anything metaphysical; it is just frustration plus attention. Still, Heidegger’s point is phenomenological rather than practical: the unusable tool shows that understanding is originally circumspective concern, not contemplation, and that Dasein is always already in a world it did not choose. Anxiety is therefore a species of equipment failure, because both wrench us out of the they-self.
