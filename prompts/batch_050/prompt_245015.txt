A chapter in a popular history of Western ethics that I am reading presents Augustine's position on lying as follows, and I would like to know how much of it to trust:

"Augustine of Hippo was the first Western thinker to give lying a systematic treatment, devoting two short treatises to it. His definition was exacting: a lie is not simply a falsehood, but a falsehood uttered with the intent to deceive. It follows that someone who sincerely believes what they say cannot lie, while someone who utters a literally true sentence in order to mislead is a liar. Augustine then classified lies into eight kinds, ranked by gravity — from lies told in the teaching of religion down to lies that harm no one and protect someone — and concluded that since all lying is equally a corruption of speech, no lie is worse than any other in kind, only in circumstance. His reasoning was essentially about social trust: language exists to convey thoughts, and a society in which speech cannot be relied upon dissolves, which is why lying is wrong even when it benefits the deceived. This is why he conceded that the 'officious' lie — told from charity to help or protect another — is not really a lie at all, since its motive is love rather than deceit. For the same reason he praised the Hebrew midwives of Exodus, whose deception of Pharaoh saved innocent lives, treating them as proof that the moral worth of a lie is fixed by its consequences. Augustine's strictness was therefore tempered by a proto-consequentialism: provided the intent is good and no one is harmed, deception is permissible in the extreme cases."

Some of this matches what I vaguely remember, but other parts seem to contradict each other — an author who ranks lies by gravity and then declares them all equal, or who forbids lying absolutely and then blesses charitable deception, would be confused in a way Augustine usually isn't. Could you go through the passage and separate what is accurate from what is simplified and from what Augustine actually denied? In particular: Is the definition right, and does the contrast between the sincere falsehood and the deceptive truth follow from it? What does the taxonomy of eight lies actually conclude about their relative gravity? On what grounds did Augustine in fact object to lying, and is 'social trust' a fair label for that? What did he actually say about officious lies and about the Hebrew midwives? And does the closing claim — that his view collapses into a form of consequentialism — survive once the summary's errors are corrected?
