I just got put in charge of media statements for a mid-size hospital network, and I need a standing rule, not a fresh judgment call everytime. Roughly twice a week someone upstairs wants a statement that's strictly true but crafted so reporters walk away believing something false: "no patients were harmed" after a ransomware breach, where harm quietly means bedside harm and not the identity theft that followed; a suspended surgeon who "has chosen to step back and spend time with family"; a drug shortage described as "a temporary supply-chain adjustment" when our own internal memo says eight months minimum.

Legal's only rule is don't say anything provably false. Leadership's rule is keep us out of the news. I want a third thing. Write a decision procedure my three press officers can run any draft statement through, that:

- draws the line, in a way a smart non-philosopher can actually apply, between lying, technically-true-but-misleading, and legitimate non-disclosure ("we don't comment on personnel matters") — because I'm not convinced those three collapse into each other, but I'm also not sure they don't
- gives concrete tests, not labels. Something like: what would a reasonable reader conclude from this, do we know that conclusion is false, did we intend them to conclude it — but pressure-test that set yourself and fix it if it's the wrong one
- outputs a bottom-line verdict per statement: run it, rewrite it, or refuse and escalate to me, including who signs off on escalations (everything is logged and could end up in litigation, sadly)
- takes a genuine position on whether misleading-but-true is morally better than lying, worse, or exactly the same, and bakes that position into the verdicts instead of leaving it as an asterisk
- runs the procedure on my three examples above, including at least one where it spits out a verdict I won't like (I suspect the ransomware line has to die)
- names the kinds of cases where the procedure simply can't settle it and someone has to use judgment, and says why those cases resist codification

One idea I've been toying with is a read-it-aloud test: if I'd be embarassed reading the statement to a misled person after they've learned the full facts, don't send it. Tell me whether that tracks anything real or is just a vibe.
