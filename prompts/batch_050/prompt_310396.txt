I'm trying to pin down when the 'man of his time' defence genuinely excuses a historical figure, and I'd like to do it by triangulating from my instincts on cases rather than from a theory. Here they are. Jefferson, who proclaimed human equality while owning hundreds of slaves and living off their labor: I judge him harshly, and the hypocrisy seems to make it worse rather than cancel out. A Roman landowner of the second century who kept slaves, treated them decently, and freed several in his will: essentially excused -- nothing in his world made the wrongness of the institution available to him. A Southern senator of the 1850s who sincerely and consistently defended slavery as a positive good, with no hypocrisy at all: I judge him just as harshly as Jefferson, which sits oddly with my first verdict. A medieval physician who bled his patients and hastened some of their deaths: fully excused, because the fault strikes me as epistemic rather than moral. Columbus: condemned, partly because his brutality was denounced even by his own contemporaries, so the criticism was available in his time. Churchill's decisions during the Bengal famine: partially blameworthy -- wartime pressures mitigate but don't erase responsibility for choices that weren't forced. And the case I keep going back and forth on: a nineteenth-century Boston merchant whose comfortable fortune derived in part, through shipping and insurance investments he never examined too closely, from the slave trade he never touched directly. Is there a principle that captures most of this -- some function of the moral knowledge available in the agent's context, the existence of live dissenting voices, the distinction between personal complicity and structural participation, and whether hypocrisy aggravates or is irrelevant? State the best candidate precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up (I suspect the Jefferson/senator pair is the problem), and tell me what it says about the Boston merchant.
