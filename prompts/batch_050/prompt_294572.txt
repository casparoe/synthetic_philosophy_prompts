I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in political philosophy (about 16 students, one 75-minute session) and we are spending two weeks on the Rawls–Harsanyi dispute over choice behind the veil of ignorance. I want an in-class game to run before students read either author, so that the competing decision rules emerge from their own behavior rather than from the text.

Design a structured activity in which small groups must agree on a rule for distributing a fixed pool of points among three ranked roles (say, well-off, middling, worst-off), knowing that after the rule is chosen, each member will be assigned a role by a draw they cannot influence. The activity should have at least two rounds that isolate the philosophical crux: in the first, the probabilities of drawing each role are unknown or unknowable (genuine ignorance, not merely risk), and in the second, the probabilities are announced in advance, so that the pull of maximizing expected utility can be felt against the pull of securing the worst position. Please engineer the payoff tables so that the two rules actually diverge — maximin and expected-utility maximization must recommend different distributions in at least one round — and so that the divergence turns on features students can articulate afterwards (how bad the worst outcome is, how much is being gambled for gains above the minimum, whether probability estimates seem stable enough to trust).

I also want a third element that standard treatments skip: a constructed case where the group is shown, after choosing, the regret table for their decision — the gap, for each possible role they might have drawn, between what they got and the best they could have gotten had they known their role in advance. Design this so that some groups discover they accepted a large avoidable regret in the role they actually landed in, and use it to motivate minimax regret as a third principle distinct from both maximin and expected utility, rather than a footnote to them.

For the deliverable, give me: complete rules and materials (including the actual payoff tables and how to build the regret tables), a minute-by-minute timeline, a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the game onto maximin, expected utility maximization, and minimax regret, and a set of debrief questions that push students toward the real point of contention — whether maximin is a rational rule under ignorance in general, or only when special conditions hold (unstable probabilities, intolerable worst outcomes, indifference to gains above a secured floor) — so they can see that Rawls needs those conditions and that Harsanyi's objection is that they rarely hold. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (for instance, students who negotiate side deals, or who intuit smuggled-in probability estimates in the ignorance round), how to handle each failure, and which features of the actual veil of ignorance the game cannot reproduce — I assume ignorance of one's own conception of the good is chief among them, but tell me what else a facilitator has to supply by hand.
