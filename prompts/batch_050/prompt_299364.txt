I'm preparing a handout for a first-year graduate seminar in cognitive science: a "what do you think a concept is?" decision tree that students work through before any assigned reading, so every split has to be a question answerable from the armchair rather than a test of vocabulary. Build the tree from the logical space, not from a syllabus: six to eight binary questions, each branch labeled by the claim that separates it, and each question anchored in a concrete case on which the standard accounts genuinely come apart — for instance, the pet fish problem for compositional typicality, or studies in which an animal is surgically disguised as a member of another species and subjects still insist it belongs to its original kind. The leaves must include prototype theory, exemplar theory, and the theory theory, plus at least two further positions drawn from this space: an atomism on which primitive concepts have no internal structure, a pluralism on which "concept" fails to name a single unified psychological kind, and a neo-empiricist view on which concepts are partial perceptual simulations. For each leaf, give the position's name (or "unnamed" if the combination of answers is live but undefended) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Then note any pair of leaves your questions cannot separate and what further evidence would pry them apart, and for any unoccupied combination of answers say whether it looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. One constraint: the root question may not be "similarity-based or explanation-based?" — that is the textbook trichotomy restated, and I want the tree to begin from something the trichotomy itself presupposes.
