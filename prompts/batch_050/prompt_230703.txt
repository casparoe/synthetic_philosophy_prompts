For roughly three decades, philosophers and economists have argued about sweatshops, and the debate has not visibly advanced. One side holds that sweatshop employment, however grim the conditions, is voluntarily chosen by workers whose alternatives are worse, that the exchange benefits both parties, and that boycotts, plant closures, and wage mandates therefore harm the very people they are meant to help; on this view, the only morally relevant comparison is between the worker's situation with the job and without it. The other side holds that a transaction can be voluntary and mutually beneficial while still being exploitative, because one party takes advantage of the other's desperation to capture nearly all the surplus the transaction generates; on this view, the relevant comparison is not the worker's next-best option but a fair division of the gains, and defenders of sweatshops are faulted for treating an unjust background situation as morally fixed. Both sides concede the empirical facts about wages, hours, and alternatives, and neither has moved. My working hypothesis is that the dispute is really about which baseline of comparison carries moral weight, and that each side's arguments presuppose its own baseline rather than defend it. Diagnose the disagreement: is it a substantive normative dispute about the nature of exploitation, a disagreement about how moral reasoning should proceed under non-ideal conditions, or two groups answering different questions -- the ethics of individual transactions versus the political economy of development -- under a single heading? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually argues, and given it, say what would count as progress: a principled and non-question-begging account of the fairness baseline, empirical findings about what anti-sweatshop campaigns in fact do to workers, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the standoff, or an argument that no resolution should be expected and why.
