Since retiring from my chemistry job a couple years back I've been reading my way through philosophy of mind, and the idea I've gotten most stuck on lately is the "user illusion" picture of consciousness. The rough version as I understand it: what we call conscious experience is like the desktop on a computer — icons, folders, trash cans, a simplified interface that hides the actual voltages and circuits — and there's no little person sitting at the desktop, the "user" is just the system itself working through its own dumbed-down self-model. On this view the vivid inner movie we all report is real as a report but not quite what it seems, and our certainty that there's "something it's like" to be us is itself part of the interface. I'm sure I'm flattening it, definately correct me if I've got it wrong.

Anyway, last weekend my daughter made me watch Ex Machina, which I'd somehow missed all these years, and I couldn't stop thinking about it in these terms. Nathan's test is explicitly not the classic Turing test, since Caleb knows from the start that Ava is a machine, and the real question becomes whether he can relate to her as a someone anyway. There's the Pollock conversation about acting without deliberation, there's Kyoko who never speaks a word, there's Caleb's awful mirror moment where he cuts his own arm and starts picking at his face to check whether he's flesh, and there's the ending where Ava walks out and leaves Caleb locked in. So: read the film for me through the user-illusion lens. Where does the film line up with the idea that personhood is an interface — something systems present and other systems respond to, with Ava doing to Caleb more or less what our own brains do to us? Where does it quietly presuppose the opposite, i.e. that the horror and the sympathy only work if there really is something it's like to be Ava? And what about Caleb — is his self-model just as much an interface as hers, and is his fate some kind of comment on trusting what the desktop tells you? I'm also curious whether the film adds anything to the illusionism debate that straight argument doesn't — I have a hunch it's something about how we can't help treating a well-made interface as a person, so the metaphysical question gets displaced by an ethical one, but I might be overreading.
