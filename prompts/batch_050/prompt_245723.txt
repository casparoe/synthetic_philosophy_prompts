I am a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist, a girl of seventeen, receives a psychiatric diagnosis midway through the story, and I have been reading about Hacking's looping effect of human kinds as I plan the later chapters. Before I commit to the book's structure, I would like to know whether the convictions I am bringing to it are jointly tenable. They are these.

First, the diagnosis names something real. It is not merely a label the clinic fastens upon her; it picks out a genuine pattern in her life and renders intelligible things about her that were previously opaque. I want the reader to feel that the diagnosis is true of her rather than imposed on her, and I have written the early chapters so that, in retrospect, the pattern was there all along.

Second, I take the looping effect to be real, and I intend it to be the novel's actual subject. Once she is classified, her self-understanding, her conduct, her friendships, and her narration of her own past reorganize around the classification; much of what she does in the second half of the book is only explicable as the behavior of a person who knows what kind of person she is supposed to be.

Third, I regard fidelity as my chief ethical obligation to my readers. What distinguishes the book from a harmful romanticization of mental illness, in my view, is that it gets the condition right — that it portrays the condition as it actually is, rather than as the surrounding culture would prefer it to look.

Fourth, the response I most hope for is a letter from a diagnosed reader telling me I have described her life exactly as she has lived it. Such a letter would, in my mind, confirm that I had discharged the obligation in my third commitment.

Fifth, I hold that my protagonist's private anguish before anyone names it — the felt texture of her suffering — is the same kind of thing that suffering has been in any century. The vocabularies change; the grief beneath them does not, and the novel depends on readers recognizing in her something older than any diagnosis.

So: do these five hang together? I would like an audit rather than a survey of the literature. Identify which commitments genuinely pull against one another and which only appear to, order the genuine tensions by severity, and for each one state what would have to give and what the surrender would cost the book. If a single revision would do most of the repair work, tell me which it is. I am not asking whether each conviction is defensible on its own — I take each to be — but whether one writer can hold all five of them in one novel.
