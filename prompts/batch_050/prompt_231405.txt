I am preparing a position statement for a graduate seminar on the boundary problem in democratic theory — the question of who is entitled to take part in constituting a polity's decisions — and I would like a consistency audit before I circulate it. My commitments are as follows:

(1) The circularity at the heart of the boundary problem cannot be solved procedurally: any vote on who gets to vote presupposes an already-constituted electorate, so the demos's boundaries must be justified by substantive principle rather than by a further democratic decision.

(2) The right substantive principle is coercion-based: being durably subject to a state's coercive legal order is what grounds an entitlement to a say in it. Long-settled residents without citizenship should therefore be enfranchised, while short-term visitors should not be.

(3) Existing immigration restrictions are largely unjust and borders should be far more open; but this is a matter of the state's duties to outsiders and implies nothing about outsiders' participatory claims. Would-be migrants turned away at the border have no entitlement to a vote in the state that excludes them.

(4) I reject the all-affected-interests principle. It is indeterminate in just the way the boundary problem itself is: which interests count as affected depends on which policies are adopted, which depends on who is included, so the principle cannot pick out a demos without circularity — and taken literally it would hand a say over one state's foreign policy to every foreigner whose interests that policy touches.

(5) Emigrants who retain their citizenship should keep the franchise for at least a generation after leaving; expatriate voting rights strike me as obviously justified.

(6) Children are properly excluded from the franchise on competence grounds, and this sits comfortably with (2).

(7) Future generations are profoundly affected by present legislation — climate policy above all — and justice requires that their interests receive institutional representation (an ombudsman, entrenched environmental provisions, or the like), though not, of course, the vote itself.

Please audit the set rather than survey the literature. Which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, and how severely? For each genuine tension, identify the cheapest available revision and state what it costs. Which pairs only appear to conflict, and why does the appearance dissolve on inspection? And if any objection I deploy against one position rebounds onto a principle I rely on elsewhere, flag that as well. Rank the findings by severity rather than treating the commitments as equally fragile, and conclude by naming the single revision that would restore the most coherence to the whole.
