A family foundation has asked me to help design a small funding program in philosophy, aimed not at ethics as such but at the theory of acting well under moral uncertainty — the kind of work that might eventually matter to people who have to make large, irreversible decisions while genuinely torn between moral frameworks: grantmakers allocating money across causes, policymakers trading off values that don't obviously compare, designers of institutions meant to be robust to moral disagreement. We can fund about five postdoc-scale projects, three years each. I've spent the last couple of months reading around the area, and my tentative impression is that the field has concentrated heavily on the normative question — what decision rule, if any, should govern choice when you have credences spread across moral theories — while treating several presuppositions as more settled than they perhaps are: that moral uncertainty is cognitively on a par with ordinary empirical uncertainty, that having graded credences in moral theories is a coherent and psychologically realizable state, that intertheoretic comparisons of choiceworthiness are at least in principle available, and that the regress problem (uncertainty about how to handle moral uncertainty) is a curiosity rather than a structural threat. A colleague whose judgment I trust pushes back hard: in her view the normative question is the only one with real practical payoff, the foundational worries are a swamp where nothing ever gets decided, and a funder who lets itself get pulled into them will produce five dissertations and no usable insight.

So: which open questions in and around moral uncertainty should this program prioritize, and in what order? I'm less interested in the list than in the reasoning. For each question you include, explain why it is upstream of other work or of practical use to the decision-makers described above, how tractable it is for philosophers working within a three-year window (as opposed to questions that need results from psychology, decision theory, or elsewhere first), and roughly how neglected it is relative to its importance. Please rank rather than merely enumerate, state the criteria driving the ranking — importance, tractability, neglectedness, how much downstream work depends on the question — and make the trade-offs between those criteria explicit where they pull apart. I'd also like you to name at least one question you think is underrated relative to the attention it currently gets, and one you think is overrated, where marginal funding buys little either because the question is nearly settled or because it resists the methods philosophers actually have.

Two further constraints. First, for each prioritized question, say what would move it up or down the list over the next few years — a result, an argument, or a failure mode that would make you revise the ranking — since the foundation will want to revisit this. Second, note that the people reading our eventual report are not philosophers: for the top two or three questions, indicate what a decisive or even a partially decisive result would actually look like and what a funder or policymaker could do with it. Don't bother with citations or a bibliography; I'll fill those in myself. What I need is a defended agenda, not a survey of the territory.
