So I TA for an animal behavior course and we just did the unit on Morgan's canon -- the old idea from C. Lloyd Morgan that you shouldn't explain an animal's behavior with a fancy psychological process (reasoning, planning, understanding) if a simpler one (trial-and-error association, instinct, stimulus-response) could account for it. And the students noticed something that I honestly couldn't give them a clean answer to. In the cognition half of the literature, this canon is enforced like crazy: if you want to claim that a scrub jay plans for the future or that a chimp has a theory of mind, reviewers make you run control after control to rule out every associative explanation first, and the default assumption is basically "it's just learning" until proven otherwise. But then in the welfare half of the literature, the same field operates on almost the opposite default -- nobody demands that you rule out "mere" nociception or reflexive withdrawal before you're allowed to say a rat is in pain or a fish is stressed; if anything the precautionary principle gets invoked and we assume suffering. So behavior that could be explained cheaply gets the cheap explanation when the expensive one is "intelligence," but the expensive explanation ("it feels something") gets waved through when welfare is at stake.

What I want is a reconstruction of what philosophical commitments this whole setup presupposes. Like, does the canon-as-actually-practiced presuppose some particular view about what makes one explanation "simpler" than another -- and is that a view about the mind (associations are metaphysically more basic than thoughts?) or just a bet about evolution, or really just a pragmatic norm about which errors are worse? Why would over-attributing cognition be treated as a scandal (Clever Hans gets brought up constantly) while over-attributing pain is treated as the safe mistake? And is the asymmetry between the cognition literature and the welfare literature actually inconsistent, or can one coherent picture of evidence and error-costs justify both defaults at once? I'd also like to know what the practice would have to look like if you dropped Morgan's canon entirely and just used ordinary Bayesian model comparison -- would anything real be lost, or was the canon only ever a sloppy stand-in for that?

Please keep your answer under 500 words. I'd rather have a tight reconstruction that distinguishes what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit with, than a long survey.
