I'm part of a graduate reading group working through Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia, and the convenor has asked each of us to circulate a short statement of our considered position on distributive justice so that the others can probe it for weaknesses. Below is mine as it currently stands. Before I send it around, I want to know whether it is actually coherent — not whether you agree with it, but whether it hangs together on its own terms, since I'd rather find the cracks now than have them pointed out in the seminar.

My commitments, in no particular order:
1. Justice in holdings is a purely historical matter: a distribution is just if it arose from a just starting point through just acquisitions and voluntary transfers, and no end-state pattern — equality, sufficiency, maximizing the position of the worst-off — is part of what makes a distribution just or unjust.
2. The Wilt Chamberlain argument succeeds: any preferred pattern will be upset by voluntary transactions, so a state committed to a pattern must continually interfere with people's liberty to dispose of what is theirs.
3. Taxing income is morally on a par with forced labor: taking a share of someone's earnings amounts to directing some portion of their working hours toward others' purposes.
4. A near-confiscatory inheritance tax is nevertheless perfectly legitimate, since the dead hold no rights and no living person has a claim to wealth they did nothing to earn.
5. The proviso on original appropriation is, in practice, satisfied in modern market economies, so the great majority of current holdings in a country like mine are legitimate.
6. Historical injustice — conquest, slavery, expropriation, systematic exclusion from markets — was pervasive enough that genuine rectification would require large-scale redistribution of present holdings.

What I'd like is an audit, not a lecture on libertarianism. Work through the commitments and tell me: which pairs or larger subsets are in genuine tension, ordered by severity; which apparent tensions dissolve once the theory is stated carefully; and for each real tension, what the cheapest fix would be — which commitment has to be revised or abandoned, and what collateral damage that does to the rest of the set. Then finish by telling me which single revision would restore the most consistency at the lowest theoretical cost. I honestly don't know where the fault lines are, so please don't assume I've already decided which commitment I'm least attached to.
