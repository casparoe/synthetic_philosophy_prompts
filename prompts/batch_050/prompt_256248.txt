I have been thinking about rational choice under prediction, and I want to know what decision-theoretic principle, if any, lies behind my actual judgments. Here are my verdicts on six cases, stated as honestly as I can:

1. Death in Damascus, standard version: Death's appointment book was written before I ever considered traveling, so the location of my appointment is fixed independently of my choice. My verdict: going to Aleppo is rational if that is where I would rather be, and the sinking feeling that I "chose wrongly" if I meet Death there is a confusion, since nothing I did affected the appointment.

2. Death in Damascus, coin version: Death flipped a fair coin to decide where to wait, and no prediction of any kind was made. My verdict: this is trivially a coin flip for me too; there is no puzzle here at all.

3. Death in Damascus, forecaster version: Death assigns appointments by running an extremely accurate psychological model of each person, so my choice of city is excellent evidence of where Death is waiting. My verdict: neither staying nor fleeing seems more rational than the other; the situation strikes me as genuinely unwinnable, and any sense that I "should have done the opposite" is unavoidable and therefore carries no information about what I should do.

4. Newcomb's problem with a 99% reliable predictor: one-box. The million is there for people with my dispositions, and I want to be the kind of agent who walks away rich.

5. The smoking lesion: smoke. The lesion causes the smoking, not the reverse, and I refuse to manage my own evidence as though it were a lever on the world.

6. Newcomb with a merely 60% reliable predictor: I genuinely cannot settle this one; at some point between 60% and 99% my one-boxing verdict must give out, but I cannot say where or why.

Verdicts 1 and 4 are the pair I cannot reconcile: in both, the relevant state of the world (the appointment book, the contents of the opaque box) is fixed in the past and causally independent of my act, yet in one I reason causally and in the other I reason dispositionally. Verdicts 4 and 5 are nearly as tense. I suspect my verdict in case 3 may also be doing quiet work by treating "no option is irrational" as a stable resting point rather than a symptom of a broken setup.

Please respond in American English as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, covering the following: (1) the single most precise candidate principle that captures as many of my six verdicts as possible, with the clause that each verdict motivates; (2) the verdict that principle cannot accommodate and the most principled reason for overturning it; (3) the strongest rival principle that saves the rejected verdict at the cost of complexity, stated precisely enough to compare; (4) what the recommended principle says about a case I did not list, namely Death in Damascus where the forecaster is accurate only about people who know how the appointment system works; (5) what the recommended principle says about where, and why, the one-boxing verdict in case 6 should give out as the predictor's reliability declines.
