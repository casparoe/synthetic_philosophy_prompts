The passage my profile turns on is from Part I of the Discourse on Method, where Descartes writes: "for as to the reason or sense, inasmuch as it is that alone which constitutes us men, and distinguishes us from the brutes, I am disposed to believe that it is to be found complete in each individual; and on this point to adopt the common opinion of philosophers, who say that the difference of greater and less holds only among the accidents, and not among the forms or natures of individuals of the same species." With that in view, here is roughly where I've landed: (1) Descartes' denial of thought to animals is essentially an empirical, scientific conjecture about what purely mechanical systems can do, and the famous two tests in Part V (flexible speech, and flexible action across novel situations) are meant as observable criteria, so the position is in principle defeasible. (2) The Part I passage shows Descartes takes reason to be an all-or-nothing faculty that comes complete in every human, and that's what licenses his confidence that animals simply lack it rather than having less of it. (3) Descartes grants animals sensations and passions like fear, hunger and hope, but understands these as purely corporeal mechanical processes with nothing felt going on. (4) His core argument against animal thought is that overt behaviour can always in principle be produced by mechanism, so articulate speech is the only sure sign of thought. (5) A sufficiently fluent conversational AI passes the first test, so Descartes is committed to attributing genuine thought to it. (6) On this view it follows pretty directly that dissecting a living unanaesthetised dog is morally on a par with taking apart a clock, which is why his followers were so relaxed about vivisection.
