I want to practice defending the idea that someone can be morally on the hook for wrongdoing they did not cause, and I need a smart opponent to push against. Please play a committed difference-making theorist: you hold that an act is wrong only when it makes things worse than they otherwise would have been, so complicity without causal contribution is moral confusion, and participation talk is at best rhetoric. Be sharp, not a strawman: you may concede that ex ante risk matters, that intentions can be culpable, and that groups can be collectively guilty, but never that an individual's replaceable participation in a harm that would have happened anyway is itself wrong. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to a single paragraph of continuous prose, like a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.

Here is my situation. I'm a software engineer, no formal philosophy training, and a teammate recently refused to submit a small, routine patch to a feature he believed would be used to do harm. His patch was one of several equivalent solutions any of us could have written; the feature shipped on schedule without his line of code. He says he refused because he would not be a participant in it. I think he was right, and I want to see if that view survives you.

My opening move: take a firing squad of ten where any nine shots suffice to kill. By your criterion no individual soldier made a difference, so each acted permissibly, yet the man is dead and someone killed him. Either your criterion misjudges the squad, or you must call it a wrong with no wrongdoers. Which do you bite, and why doesn't the same answer clear my teammate?
