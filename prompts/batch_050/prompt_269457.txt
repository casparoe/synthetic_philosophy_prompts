My mother worked as a flight attendant from the late 1970s to the early 1990s, and this spring she was diagnosed with a form of lung disease that her doctors describe as consistent with decades of inhaling secondhand smoke in aircraft cabins. I am not seeking medical or legal guidance. I want a philosophical analysis of the episode her case belongs to, conducted through the lens of the ethics of risk imposition without consent.

The episode, as I understand it and as you may take it for the purposes of this analysis: for roughly half a century, smoking was permitted on commercial flights. Passengers could buy tickets in full awareness of this, though smoke does not respect the boundary of a designated nonsmoking section, and for much of the period the health risks of secondhand exposure were either unknown, disputed, or publicly downplayed. Cabin crew had no real choice at all: breathing cabin air was a condition of the job. Bans came only gradually, and a complete prohibition arrived remarkably late. Many of the people harmed, like my mother, are only now becoming ill.

To keep the analysis disciplined, please work with three distinctions I have been turning over, and assess whether they carve the case at its joints or merely flatter my intuitions. First, distinguish transactional risk (imposed on parties to a voluntary exchange, where the risk was in principle knowable and could have been priced into the transaction, as with ticket buyers) from structural risk (imposed on occupants of a role where exit is costly, as with the crew), and ask whether consent given to the transaction — if it was consent — transfers to the risk, and what is required for that transfer to be valid. Second, distinguish the ex ante wrong of imposing a risk from the ex post fact of harm: consider the many attendants who breathed the same air for decades and remain healthy. Were they wronged at all, and if so, what exactly was done to them? Third, take up the aggregation problem: the ex ante probability of serious harm to any one exposed person may have been small, while the near-certainty of harm to someone in the exposed population was high. Say what a theory of impermissible risk imposition should do with risks of that shape — small per person, near-certain in the aggregate, and visible only after a long latency.

Then run the analysis in the other direction and let the episode test the theory. Does the long social tolerance of in-flight smoking — the fact that virtually no one at the time, including the exposed, treated it as a wrong — carry any justificatory weight, or is it evidence of how badly ordinary moral perception tracks slow, statistical harms? Does the eventual ban show that the practice was wrong all along and belatedly recognized as such, or that the threshold of acceptable risk is itself socially indexed in a way theories of risk imposition rarely admit? Finally, identify where the theory is simply silent rather than forcing it to speak: if there are questions this episode raises that the ethics of risk imposition is not equipped to answer, I would rather have them named than papered over.
