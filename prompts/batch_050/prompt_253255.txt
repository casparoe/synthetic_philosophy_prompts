Two coworkers of mine at a small weather-analytics company got into a running argument that started over lunch and has now spilled across three Slack threads, and they've both appealed to me to settle it, which I can't. Here is the exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it. Jess is an ML engineer, Ren did two years of a phil science PhD before dropping out to write code.

(1) Jess: for the new forecasting product we had two candidate models that fit the past ten years of data essentially equally well, and I shipped the simpler one. That's just normal good science. The complicated one has extra interaction terms that are clearly overfitting; preferring the simple model isn't some mysterious philosophical leap, it's how the field works and it works well.

(2) Ren: you're describing the Duhem-Quine problem and acting like it isn't one. The data alone never picked your model — you needed an extra-empirical preference for simplicity to break the tie. That's fine as a practical heuristic, but notice what you can't claim: that the evidence made the simpler model more likely to be true. The evidence is equally happy with both. That's what underdetermination means and you haven't escaped it, you've just papered over it.

(3) Jess: I think "equally happy" is doing a ton of work there. The underdetermination we ever actually face is transient — two fits that agree on all past data almost always diverge on some test you haven't run yet. So my choice isn't a leap of faith, it's a bet about the future, and it's a good bet: simple models have a much better track record of surviving contact with new data. That's an empirical fact about the world, not an a priori prejudice.

(4) Ren: the track-record argument is circular. "Simple models have held up before, so they'll hold up again" is itself an inductive inference — you're justifying simplicity with exactly the kind of reasoning simplicity is supposed to help justify. And the in-principle point stands too: you can construct genuinely empirically equivalent rivals, not just currently-untested ones (mess with the geometry and the forces together so every observation comes out the same, that kind of trick). Philosophers have known for over a century that the possibility is real.

(5) Jess: fine, grant all of it — it still doesn't touch me, because I never claimed the model was true. I made a deployment decision, and on the specific quantity the decision actually depends on, both models agree within a percent. Even total, permanent underdetermination about which model is right is compatible with the decision being fully determined. Ren: that's luck, not method. Nothing in your procedure guaranteed the agreement; you checked it after the fact. Next quarter the two surviving models might disagree on exactly the decision-relevant number and you'll be right back to simplicity carrying all the weight while you pretend the evidence chose for you.
