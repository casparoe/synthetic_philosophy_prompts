I teach a first-year undergraduate epistemology course, and I have a single eighty-minute seminar in which to introduce testimony. The students have already worked through perception and the regress of justification, but have done no social epistemology. My aim is that they leave understanding the dispute between reductionists and anti-reductionists about testimonial justification as a dispute about the structure of justification — about whether a hearer needs positive, non-testimonial reasons for taking a speaker at their word, or whether accepting testimony is prima facie justified absent defeaters — and not merely as a disagreement about how trusting people happen to be.

Please design the teaching plan rather than the seminar itself. Specifically:

First, the order of ideas. State which components must be in place before the dispute can even be posed (for instance: how much of what they believe rests on testimony; the distinction between testimony as a source of belief and testimony as a source of justification) and why the dependencies run in that order. Advise me on whether to anchor the session in the classic pessimist and optimist positions from the start or to build the problem out of cases and name the positions only at the end, and defend your choice.

Second, the carrying example. I want one mundane scenario to run through the whole session. Specify what it must contain — a speaker, a hearer with no independent route to the fact in question, and a dial that lets me vary the hearer's background evidence — and what it must avoid: stakes high enough that students slide into practical reasoning, or expertise technical enough that they conflate testimony with deference to authority.

Third, the misconception map. Predict the confusions in the order they will arise and indicate where in the sequence each should be headed off: the assumption that testimonial justification just is induction from a speaker's past reliability; the reading of anti-reductionism as a license for gullibility; the slide from needing reasons to trust this speaker to needing reasons to trust testimony as a kind; and the question, which a student will certainly ask, of whether the output of an AI assistant counts as testimony — tell me at what point in the seminar that question becomes productive rather than a derailment, and what each position would say about it.

Fourth, a check for understanding: propose one fresh case the students must classify under both frameworks, ideally one where the two verdicts diverge, so I can see whether they grasp the fault line rather than having memorized two names.

Finally, tell me which complications (such as whether testimony merely transmits a speaker's justification or can generate it) I should explicitly refuse to cover at this level, and how to mark that refusal without making the seminar feel truncated.
