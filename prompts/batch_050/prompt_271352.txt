For a philosophy of mind reading group, I have to write down my position on split-brain cases so everyone can attack it, and I want to check it for internal problems first. Heres roughly where I’ve landed:

1. I’m a physicalist and broadly functionalist about consciousness. A conscious subject exists when information is available to a sufficiently integrated set of systems for perception, reasoning, memory, emotion, and action. Verbal report is evidence for this, but not necessary.

2. In the standard split-brain experiments, the best interpretation is that there are two simultaneous centers of experience in one skull. For example, one side can perceive and act on information that the speaking side genuinely lacks, rather than merely being unable to describe it.

3. The patient nevertheless remains one person, with one continuing biography, one set of long-term relationships, and one moral and legal status.

4. Person and subject of experience are different concepts, so one person can temporarily contain more than one experiential subject.

5. Conscious unity is not just coordination inside one body. Experiences belong to one subject only if they can jointly enter the same space of comparison and control, at least in principle. Two experiences that are forever informationally walled off from each other are not unified merely because they happen in the same brain.

6. Ordinary conflict does not divide a subject. Self-deception, conflicting desires, divided attention, and acting against one’s better judgment can all occur within a single consciousness.

7. At any given moment there must be a fully determinate fact about how many experiential subjects are present. Our descriptions may be vague, but reality itself cannot be borderline between one subject and two.

8. Since functional integration comes in degrees, any transition from one subject to two should track the amount and kind of integration that remains. It should not depend on an arbitrary behavioral cutoff, such as whether the two sides can still cooperate on a laboratory task.

To make the pressure concrete, imagine a futuristic commissurotomy performed gradually over a year. First the fibers carrying episodic memories are cut, then those carrying detailed shape information, then color, then emotional salience, and so on. Between operations, tests measure exactly what information still passes between hemispheres. At the beginning there is clearly one subject and at the end, on my view, there are two. There need never be a dramatic behavioral discontinuity.

Please audit the commitments rather than just explain split-brain cases to me. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look that way, and how severe is each problem? In particular, trace exactly what follows from combining my functionalism, my determinacy requirement, and the gradual-surgery case. If something has to go, give me the cheapest revision and what it costs. Also say whether the one-person/two-subjects move really solves the unity issue or just relabels it. For purposes of the exercise, assume the usual behavioral descriptions of split-brain experiments are accurate; I’m interested in whether my conceptual package hangs together.
