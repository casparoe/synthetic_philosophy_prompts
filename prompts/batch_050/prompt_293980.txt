I'm a senior in high school, and our philosophy club has been working through the philosophy of time this semester. Last week someone brought up Gödel's rotating universes, and I spent the weekend putting together an argument based on what I could piece together about his 1949 work. The problem is that it seems to prove something I refuse to believe, but when I presented it to the club, nobody could find the hole. Here it is. (1) Einstein's field equations of general relativity have exact solutions describing rotating universes — the solutions Gödel discovered — in which there are closed timelike curves, meaning worldlines along which an observer could in principle return to an event in their own past, and in which no single global time ordering of all events from earlier to later exists. (2) Any solution of the fundamental equations of a correct physical theory describes a physically possible universe. (3) So a universe without an objective global temporal order is physically possible. (4) If the objective lapse of time were a real feature of the world, its existence could not hinge on contingent facts such as whether matter happens, on average, to rotate — something genuinely real cannot be switched on or off by such accidents. (5) Our universe is governed by the very same field equations as Gödel's; the difference lies only in the contingent distribution and motion of matter. (6) So whether our universe possesses an objective temporal order likewise hinges on a contingent fact. (7) Therefore time in our universe is not objectively real either, and the lapse of time is a kind of appearance — which I take to be essentially the idealist conclusion Gödel himself drew. (8) If the lapse of time is mere appearance, then there is no objective fact about whether my history essay was submitted before the deadline last Thursday. (9) There plainly is such a fact, since I got marks deducted. So something among (1) through (7) must be wrong, but each step looks defensible to me.

For context: I am fairly attached to (1) and (2) — I don't want to rescue time by denying that Gödel universes are genuine possibilities, since that feels like it gives up the interesting part of the story. If forced, I suspect (4) is doing something sneaky, but I can't articulate what, and my club president insisted the trouble is actually in (3). I'd rather be told than keep guessing.
