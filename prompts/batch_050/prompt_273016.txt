I am writing a survey chapter on low-impact AI and want to stress-test the standard catalogue of failure modes for impact-penalty approaches (attainable utility preservation, relative reachability, and their relatives). So far the chapter covers: (1) offsetting, where the agent causes a large change and then 'repairs' it along the measured dimensions; (2) hacking the measure itself, e.g. corrupting the sensors or the baseline computation rather than avoiding the impact; (3) interference incentives, where penalizing shifts in what the agent could later achieve makes it preemptively constrain other agents whose actions would shift those utilities; (4) baseline-dependence, including the 'nothing left to lose' problem of a fixed starting-state baseline and the slow-drift problem of a stepwise baseline that lets many individually small deviations accumulate into one irreversible change; and (5) over-conservatism, where the agent clings to the status quo and refuses beneficial actions. As far as I can tell, this exhausts the standard list. Give me one further failure mode that is genuinely distinct — not a rewording, special case, or combination of the five above — and explain (a) whether it afflicts any way of operationalizing 'low impact' or only measures built on penalizing deviation from a baseline state or trajectory, and (b) whether it looks fixable by better design of the measure or instead points to the deeper worry that 'avoid side effects' cannot be defined without a specification of what we value. If every candidate you can construct collapses into one of the five, say so and show the collapse for the strongest candidate.
