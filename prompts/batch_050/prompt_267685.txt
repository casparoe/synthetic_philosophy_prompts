I've been following arguments about whether taxation is theft for years now -- online, with friends, that kind of thing -- and I keep noticing the same pattern. The "taxation is theft" people say: if I took your money at gunpoint it'd be theft, the government threatens force if you don't pay, so it's theft, done. The other side says: you consented by living here, you benefit from roads and courts, society collectively owns stuff anyway, so the analogy fails. And then both sides just... repeat themselves. Forever. Nobody ever seems to concede an inch even when the other side makes what looks like a good point.

So my question isn't who's right -- I want to know what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it purely verbal, i.e. both sides just using "theft" differently (one as "taking without consent", one as "illegal taking", and taxation is definitionally legal)? Or is there a real substantive dispute hiding underneath, like about whether property rights exist before government or are created by it, and the theft framing is just a symptom? Or is it something weirder, like both sides having different bedrock intuitions about what counts as coercion that can't be argued for at all?

Please diagnose the structure of the debate based on what the two sides actually say, and tell me what would count as progress -- a distinction that would dissolve it, an empirical fact that would settle part of it, or an argument that it's genuinely unresolvable and why. My own guess is it's mostly verbal plus one real disagreement about the nature of property, but maybe I'm being too optimistic that it can be untangled at all.
