A friend argues that the standard counterexamples to conditional analyses of dispositions -- masks and finks -- actually establish something far more radical, and I cannot locate the weak step. (1) If a predicate like 'fragile' picks out a real property, there must be some observable condition whose obtaining the property explains; otherwise the ascription floats free of anything we could ever check. (2) Take the natural test for fragility: striking the object and observing whether it breaks. For any such test we can describe a mask that blocks the manifestation while the disposition remains (a fragile glass swaddled in packing material does not break when struck) and a fink or mimic that produces the manifestation without the disposition (a sturdy glass wired to a device that shatters it upon impact). (3) So a positive test result is no evidence of fragility and a negative result no evidence against it: the property is undetectable in principle. (4) A property that is undetectable in principle is no property at all. (5) Hence fragility is not a real property, and the same reasoning eliminates solubility, conductivity, irritability, and every other disposition. (6) But dispositional talk is indispensable throughout the sciences, so the argument proves too much. When I protest by pointing to physics and chemistry, my friend replies that this is mere nostalgia for an outmoded idiom. Which step should I reject, what made that step seem compulsory, and what do I forfeit by rejecting it? If I must choose what to keep, I would most like to keep (1), since some link between properties and possible observation strikes me as unavoidable -- but I want to know the price of holding on to it.
