I'm taking a second-year philosophy of psychology course this term and we just finished a unit on irrationality (a bit of Sartre on bad faith, some stuff on wishful thinking), and last week after the seminar a couple of people from the course got into an argument at the pub that I've been chewing over since. It was about our mutual friend Tobi, who's had this persistent cough and some other symptoms for months now. He insists it's nothing, gets visibly irritated if anyone brings it up, keeps "forgetting" to book the GP appointment (though he never forgets five-a-side on Thursdays), and apparently spent a whole evening reading articles about how these symptoms are almost always benign while refusing to open the ones his girlfriend sent him about warning signs. Lena said this is a textbook case of self-deception: on some level he knows it might be serious — that's exactly why he avoids the doctor — and yet he sincerely tells everyone, himself included, that it's nothing.

Marco pushed back hard, and honestly both of them made points I can't easily dismiss. His first move was that self-deception is literally impossible: deception means a deceiver who believes the truth intentionally getting a victim to believe the opposite, but if deceiver and victim are the same person, then at the moment of success Tobi would have to believe both "this might be serious" and "this is nothing" at the same time, which is a contradiction. And the intention can't do the work either, he said, because you can't deliberately get yourself to believe something you currently think is false — the plan falls apart the moment you notice it. Lena replied that the contradiction only follows if both beliefs are held conciously and in the same way; the "it might be serious" belief could be unconscious, doing its work from below, and the intention doesn't need to be an explicit plan, just a motivation. Marco said that's cheating — an unconscious belief hiding from you while your conscious mind says the opposite isn't deception, it's repression or ordinary wishful thinking, and if you look at the actual mechanism it's just motivated reasoning: anxiety makes threatening evidence feel less salient, so the bias does all the work without any deceiving going on. A bias isn't a lie, he said, any more than a magnet "deceives" a compass needle. Lena's counter was that this bias isn't random — it points systematically in the direction of comfort, and Tobi does things: he picks which articles to open, he changes the subject, he forgets selectively. Random error doesn't forget selectively, so somebody is steering, and if it's not Tobi, who is it? Marco said motivation steers without any intention to deceive — wanting q just biases how you weigh evidence for q, that's cognition under affect, no little man inside with a plan required. Lena's last move was about blame: we do blame people like Tobi, we literally say "he's kidding himself," and you can't be blamed for a bias that merely happens to you, like an optical illusion; blame only makes sense if he's doing something. Marco answered that we blame people for negligence and for letting their emotions run their inquiries all the time without any self-lie involved, and that maybe the whole "kidding himself" idiom is just confused folk psychology.

I stayed out of it because I kept flip-flopping — every reply sounded right until the next one came. Can you go through the exchange step by step and tell me who's right on each specific point: the contradiction objection, the unconscious-belief reply, the "steering without an intention" question, and the blame argument? Not just who wins overall, but where exactly each of them goes wrong if they do, and whether there's something both of them are missing — some third option neither of them considered. Also, I need to be able to follow it without having read the whole literature, so flag any jargon you rely on.
