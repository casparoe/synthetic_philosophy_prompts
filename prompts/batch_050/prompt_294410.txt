PhD student in cog sci here. My advisor leans constantly on Dennett's multiple drafts picture of consciousness: the brain keeps lots of evolving versions of what's going on, edits happening all over the place, and there's no final canonical version that gets "published" to some inner audience. She uses the analogy to argue that questions like "did the subject consciously see the stimulus before or after the mask appeared" are just ill-posed, full stop, and that any theory that tries to answer them has smuggled in a Cartesian theater. I get the gist but I want to actually stress-test the analogy rather than nod along. So: give me the explicit mapping -- what plays the role of the drafts, the editing, the readers who later misremember what the text said, the act of publication? Then where it breaks: a real manuscript has an author with intentions, drafts exist at definite times and places, and in principle someone could collect every draft and settle what the text really said, which seems to be exactly the kind of settling the analogy says is impossible for experience. Does the analogy quietly presuppose things it officially denies? And does her conclusion -- that "when did it become conscious" questions are meaningless -- actually follow from the model, or is the manuscript metaphor doing argumentative work that the evidence about probing effects (Orwellian vs Stalinesque reconstructions etc.) should be doing? If there's a better analogy for the same position, I'd like that too.
