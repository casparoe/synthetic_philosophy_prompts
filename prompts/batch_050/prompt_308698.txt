My cousin Marcus has, over the past few years, developed what he calls a system for handling moral questions. Whenever he faces a decision with an ethical dimension — whether to report a coworker he caught padding expense reports, whether to keep lending money to a friend who never pays him back, whether to attend a family wedding he disapproves of — he calls his old college ethics professor, Dr. Hale, describes the situation, and does whatever she advises. He doesn't weigh the considerations himself or press her for her reasoning; he simply asks what the right thing to do is and then does it. His justification is straightforward: Dr. Hale has spent forty years thinking about ethics, she's intelligent and honest, and her judgment is simply more reliable than his own. When I raised an eyebrow at this, he shrugged and said it's no different from taking the medication his doctor prescribes without being able to explain the pharmacology.

Marcus's fiancée, however, finds the whole arrangement deeply uncomfortable, and the disagreement between them has become a running tension I keep getting pulled into. Her complaint isn't that Dr. Hale gives bad advice — by all accounts her advice is sound — but that when Marcus is asked why he reported the coworker or skipped the wedding, all he can say is that Dr. Hale told him it was right. He can't explain what made it right, couldn't have reached the conclusion on his own, and couldn't recognize a situation where her advice would be off-base. Marcus thinks this is pure squeamishness: if the outcome is that he consistently does the right thing, what does it matter how he got there? A nurse who administers the correct drug without understanding how it works is still helping the patient. His fiancée counters that something is genuinely missing when a person treats moral questions that way, even if every single decision happens to be correct — but when pressed, she struggles to articulate what exactly is lost.

I'm curious which of them has the better of this. Is there a real disanalogy between deferring to an expert on moral questions and deferring to an expert on medicine or engineering, or is Marcus right that reliable deference is reliable deference, full stop? Suppose it were demonstrated that Marcus makes better decisions this way than he ever would by reasoning on his own — would there still be anything defective about his arrangement, and if so, what is the defect, exactly? Does it matter for the value of his actions whether he understands the reasons behind them, or is understanding a kind of private luxury that only the reasons-obsessed insist on? And if his fiancée is right that something is missing, what would a better version of the arrangement look like — should he consult Dr. Hale differently, or not at all?
