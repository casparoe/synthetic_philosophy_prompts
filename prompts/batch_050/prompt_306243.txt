Write a mock investigative report, about 650 words, from a national consumer-protection agency whose statutory mandate defines consumer welfare strictly as net experienced pleasure. The agency has spent several years investigating the tragedy industry after field reports that its products reliably inflict suffering: public weeping, sleepless nights, a heaviness that outlasts the experience by days. The report must explain, in deadpan bureaucratic prose, the anomaly at the center of the case file: consumers return to these products voluntarily, pay a premium for the more devastating ones, and give them the highest satisfaction ratings in the entire leisure sector.

Structure it as a real report: numbered findings of fact, a section reviewing the explanations the commission tested and rejected, a dissenting minority note, and formal recommendations. The tested hypotheses should each be recognizable — by content alone, never labeled or attributed — as a genuine position in the debate about why people enjoy tragic art: that the suffering is converted into pleasure by the beauty and eloquence of the presentation; that the pleasure of the artistry merely outweighs the pain, as a surcharge canceled by a larger refund; that audiences do not genuinely suffer, since they know the events are invented (the lab confirms the tears are chemically authentic); that consumers are purchasing evidence of their own sensitivity, enjoying the discovery that they are the sort of people who weep; and the industry's own defense, that the product purges the very emotions it induces, like a shampoo that relieves an itch it also causes. The minority note should propose that the value in question is simply not of the hedonic kind the agency's instruments can detect, and must be ruled outside the mandate on procedural grounds.

Include, deadpan, at least two findings the commission cannot explain: consumers' conspicuous unwillingness to attend actual funerals for enjoyment, and the fact that the only actionable complaints on file allege insufficient suffering — uplifting endings entitling the purchaser to a full refund. The recommendations should follow bureaucratically from the findings without resolving anything. The humor must come entirely from the premise colliding with the phenomenon: no winking, no breaking character, no philosophy named as such. A reader who knows no aesthetics should find it funny; a reader who knows the paradox of tragedy should be able to map every hypothesis onto its real counterpart and find the satire fair.
