I volunteer at a small science museum and I'm building an interactive "is it alive?" wall where visitors push a button for an object -- virus, prion, wildfire, a dormant tardigrade, a sterile mule, a synthetic minimal cell, a self-copying bit of code running in a simulation -- and get sorted to a "yes / no / depends what you mean" verdict. While reading up I hit this in Aristotle's De Anima (Book II, ch. 1, J. A. Smith translation): "Of natural bodies some have life in them, others not; by life we mean self-nutrition and growth (with its correlative decay)." It struck me that most modern definitions either quietly keep his criterion, swap it for evolution-by-natural-selection, or bolt things onto it, and that would make a good skeleton for the wall.

So: write me a decision tree that starts from Aristotle's line above as the first branch (does it feed itself, grow, decay?) and then splits further with one yes/no question per node, phrased so a visitor can answer about a specific object without knowing any labels. Each leaf should be a named-ish definition or position (metabolism-first Aristotelian, Darwinian/replicator-first, autopoiesis or self-maintenance, "life is a cluster concept, stop asking", etc.), plus which of my seven objects lands there and one sentence on the leaf's most embarrassing commitment. Note any combination of answers that nobody actually defends and whether that's because it's incoherent or just unpopular, and flag leaves the tree can't really separate.

One more thing: please mark clearly which of these definitional moves are basically settled among people who work on this and which are still live disputes -- I don't want the museum wall presenting one contested camp as the consensus.
