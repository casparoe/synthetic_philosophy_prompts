I spent my career in experimental physics, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science with the mixture of delight and bewilderment you might expect. I keep returning to a pattern I noticed repeatedly in my working life, and I would like to know whether it has an established name and a literature around it. Please answer in British English.

The pattern is this. The evidential credit a body of data confers on a theory seems to depend not merely on the logical relation between them but on the history of how the theory came to stand in that relation, and my colleagues treated this as obvious even though I can find no principled justification for it. Three cases from my own experience:

First, the famous sort: a theory is developed for entirely independent reasons and then turns out to entail, to remarkable precision, an anomaly that had been sitting in the textbooks for decades and had resisted every prior explanation. The old anomaly is then treated as among the strongest evidence for the new theory — stronger, I think, than any fresh prediction the theory subsequently made and had confirmed. The anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion and general relativity is the textbook instance.

Second, the converse: a colleague constructs a model by adjusting its free parameters until it reproduces a known dataset. Formally the finished model entails the data just as in the first case. Yet referees refuse to count that data as evidence for the model at all — it has, they say, already been spent in the construction, and only genuinely new data will do. This strikes me as reasonable practice but puzzling in principle: the entailment relation is identical in both cases.

Third, the awkward intermediate case that really bothers me: a theorist certainly knew the old data — everyone did — but insists, plausibly, that her derivation proceeded from independent principles and made no use of it. Some colleagues discount the old data anyway, on the grounds that her knowledge of it must have guided her choices somehow; others say what matters is whether it actually entered the derivation, which is nearly impossible to establish.

My questions, then. Is there an established name for the general phenomenon — the apparent dependence of evidential weight on the temporal or constructive relationship between theory and data — and is there a name for the specific puzzle that standard probabilistic accounts of confirmation seem unable to accommodate it, given that long-known data already has probability one for us? I gather there may be several neighbouring concepts here that are frequently conflated (mere temporal ordering versus actual use in construction, for instance, seem to be treated as distinct in the third case), so I would not be satisfied with a bare label: please identify the nearest established notions, say how the pattern I have described maps onto each and where it departs from them, and indicate which fits best. If some part of what I have described has no established name, say so and propose one, with a definition sharp enough to handle my third case.
