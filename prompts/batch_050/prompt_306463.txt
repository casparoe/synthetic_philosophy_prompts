I'm writing the final exam for my upper-year philosophy of action course (3rd/4th year undergrads who've already done intro ethics). I want one essay question on weakness of will and hyperbolic discounting that students can't answer by just reciting the standard gloss -- you know the one, "akrasia is acting against your better judgment, hyperbolic discounting is when we weight near-term rewards way more heavily, so hyperbolic discounting explains why we're weak-willed, the end."

The crux I actually want them to grapple with is the mismatch between the two notions. Classic akrasia is synchronic: at the very moment of action, you judge you ought to do A and yet you do B. Hyperbolic discounting, though, generates a *diachronic* preference reversal: the evening-self genuinely prefers to get up early tomorrow, the morning-self genuinely prefers to stay in bed, and each time-slice arguably behaves rationally given its own preferences at that time. So the hard question is whether the snooze-button-hitter is really acting against her better judgment at all, or whether "her better judgment" is just the preference of a different temporal slice of her. And then what that does to Ulysses-contract style precommitment strategies -- if both slices are rational, why does the earlier self get to bind the later one?

Please write:

1. The exam question itself, answerable in about 75 minutes, roughly 800-1000 words expected.
2. A model answer of around 600-700 words.
3. A marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves an A answer must contain and the specific mistakes that should cost marks -- including the single most common one, which is treating "preference reversal over time" and "acting against your better judgment" as obviously the same phenomenon without argument.

One important constraint: I don't want a fence-sitting model answer. Have it take a definite stand on whether hyperbolic discounting genuinely *explains* akrasia or merely redescribes a structurally similar failure in fancier vocabulary, and build the marking scheme so that students who commit to a position and defend it score higher than students who just survey the options and shrug. Definately make sure the scheme reflects that. No citations or references needed -- they won't have texts in the exam room.
