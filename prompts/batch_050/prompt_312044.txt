A guy in my epistemology seminar walked me into this argument and I still can't find the exit. (1) A belief of mine is justified only if I have a reason for it. (2) Having a reason means being able to give it when asked "how do you know?" — if I couldn't produce it, it's at most a cause of my believing, not a justification. (3) But whatever I produce in answer is itself a claim I'm only entitled to if I have a reason for it too, so the question can be asked again. (4) This can only end three ways: I circle back to something already on the table, I stop at something I can't defend, or the chain never ends — and for a finite creature like me that's circularity, dogmatism, or impossibility. (5) So none of my beliefs is ever justified. (6) Justified belief is necessary for knowledge, so I know nothing. He insists this isn't a skeptical theory, it's just applying my own standards of justification consistently, and that if the conclusion bothers me I should change my standards. I have a nagging feeling the damage is done by (2) but every time I try to drop it I end up saying that a reason you can't articulate still justifies you, which sounds like cheating. Which step should I actually reject, and what do foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism each have to pay to reject it — i.e., which of them is buying its way out of the argument rather than showing where it goes wrong?
