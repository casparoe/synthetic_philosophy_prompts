I've been turning something over in my head for a while, partly because of arguments I've had with friends about where our clothes come from, and I'd like help working out whether there's a coherent position behind my gut reactions. Please answer as a professional philosopher specializing in political and economic ethics, and approach it like one: think carefully, consider the obvious candidate principles before settling, and don't flatter my intuitions if they don't cohere.

Here are my verdicts on cases. A garment worker in Dhaka takes a factory job at a wage that is a fraction of what comparable work pays here, and she takes it because every alternative open to her is worse; I think the arrangement is morally acceptable, and I don't feel that I do wrong when I buy the clothes, though I have a nagging sense the wage ought to be higher. A payday lender charges an enormous interest rate to someone who needs a few hundred dollars to fix the car that gets her to work; she consents, she's genuinely better off with the loan than without it, and yet this one feels plainly exploitative to me -- though I still wouldn't want it banned outright. A wealthy tourist haggles a poor street artisan down from twenty dollars to eight for something the tourist would barely notice paying thirty for; the artisan agreed, even smiled, but I think the tourist did something shabby. A drug company runs a trial in a poor country, paying participants and giving them treatment they otherwise couldn't get, in a study that couldn't be run at that cost elsewhere; I think this is fine, and my friends say I'm inconsistent. A landlord rents out a shabby apartment cheaply to someone with nowhere else to go and lets a broken heater sit for a month; here my complaint isn't the rent at all, it's the indifference. And last: disaster pricing -- a hardware store doubles the price of generators the night before a hurricane -- this one I keep flipping on, since the high price arguably gets more generators trucked in.

My tentative principle, which I suspect leaks, is that a transaction is exploitative when one party's consent is forced by desperation while the other party could have offered much better terms at trivial cost to themselves. That seems to condemn the payday lender and the tourist while sparing the factory and the drug trial, which is what I want -- but then I can't see why the payday lender couldn't make exactly the same excuse the factory makes (better terms were available to her, after all, just not from him), and the factory owner presumably *could* pay more at modest cost too. So please: tell me whether any single principle, or small set of them, captures most of these verdicts, state it precisely and say which case motivates each clause, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot save and which one you'd overturn first if forced. Then tell me what the resulting principle says about two cases I haven't decided: selling a kidney for a large sum, and paying someone to be a surrogate mother. Finally, I'd like to know what would change your own verdict here -- which argument or counterexample, if presented to you, would make you abandon the principle you settle on -- and where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail.
