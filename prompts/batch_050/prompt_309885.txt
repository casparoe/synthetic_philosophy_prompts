I work as a backend engineer at a company that processes refund requests, and last week our fraud team had an argument that I keep turning over in my head because it seems to be an instance of something general that I can't pin down. Here's the situation. Our internal audits tell us that about 80% of the refund claims coming through one particular channel are from an organized fraud ring. The claims are individually indistinguishable -- the ring does good work -- so for any given claim, all we can say is: it's from this channel, so there's an 80% chance it's bogus. Half the team said fine, deny everything from that channel, 80% is way past whatever threshold you'd need. The other half said you can't possibly deny *this specific person* their refund on the grounds that people *like them* are usually scammers, even though they agreed the 80% number is right. Nobody could articulate what the actual difference was supposed to be, and I realized I've seen this same shape of disagreement in like three other places.

Once I started looking for it, it's everywhere. In court people seem totally fine convicting someone based on a witness who is, say, 90% reliable, but I get the strong impression (maybe I'm wrong) that a court would not convict someone just because the base rate says 90% of people in their situation are guilty -- like, if a venue sold 100 tickets but 1000 people are inside and you grab someone at random, there's a 90% chance they snuck in, and yet "well, statistically you probably didn't pay" feels like it shouldn't count as proof against that particular person even though the probability is the same as the witness case. And at work again: we have an ML classifier that flags accounts, and banning someone because the model says 97% bot feels different when the 97% comes from population-level correlations than when it comes from a specific behavioral trace from that account's own session -- even when the calibration numbers are identical.

So my question is: does this distinction have a name? I don't just want a label though. I want to know what exactly the line is supposed to be. Is it about where the probability comes from? About whether the evidence could in principle point at this individual rather than the group? About whether the evidence would have looked different if the person were innocent? And does whatever this is connect to how "beyond a reasonable doubt" is supposed to work -- like, is the legal standard secretly about the *kind* of evidence rather than the strength of it? If there's no established concept that fits exactly, tell me what the closest ones are and where my phenomenon falls off the edges of each.
