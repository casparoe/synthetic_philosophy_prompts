Now that I'm retired (forty years of experimental physics) I've fallen down the philosophy-of-time rabbit hole, and something about how my old field talks has started to bother me. In astronomy we cheerfully say we see the Sun as it was eight minutes ago and Andromeda as it was 2.5 million years back, and we speculate that some far-off star may already have blown up. But there's an unwritten rule: never state what is happening out there *now*. The distant past is fair game, the distant present is off-limits. Meanwhile in daily life I phone my sister in Sydney and assume there is simply a fact of the matter about what she's doing right now. And relativity makes it worse: as I understand it, two people strolling past each other on the street already disagree by days about which events on Andromeda are simultaneous with "now" (the Rietdijk-Putnam / Andromeda buisness). So the everyday phone call seems to presuppose a universal present that the astronomy talk quietly denies.

Act as a philosopher of physics with teaching experience. What view of time does this whole way of talking presuppose -- presentism for the near, eternalism for the far, or something else? Which rival theories (growing block, block universe, presentism) would license the practice, and which does it rule out without noticing? Is the practice even coherent given relativity, or is it underdetermined, so that several theories would fit it equally well? And what would have to change in how astronomers, and the rest of us, speak if we took the block universe fully seriously?

Please think this through carefully -- maybe first list the candidate positions, then test the practice against each one. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
