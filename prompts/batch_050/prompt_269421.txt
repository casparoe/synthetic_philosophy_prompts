I'm putting together a grad seminar on LLM transparency and I want a thought experiment to anchor the week on chain-of-thought faithfulness -- the question of whether what a language model writes as its "reasoning" is actually the reasoning that produced its answer, or just a plausible-sounding story it generates after (or independently of) doing whatever it did internally. Most cases I've seen in the literature are empirical studies, which are great but my students get bogged down in the methodological weeds. I want something cleaner, more like a Gettier case for this debate: a scenario that lets you think about what faithfulness would even *consist in*, not just measure it.

So here's the specification. Construct a scenario (or pair of scenarios, if that works better) in which two models produce identical chain-of-thought text and identical final answers, but where we'd want to say that one model's chain-of-thought is faithful to its actual reasoning and the other's is not. Constraints: everything must be observable from the outside -- no peeking at weights, activations, or internal states, since the whole point is that my students should feel the difficulty of diagnosing faithfulness without that access; the scenario should involve only capabilities current models plausibly have (so no "the model secretly runs an interpreter in its head" -- it has to be something like hint-parroting, post-hoc rationalization, or smuggling computation into the choice of phrasing, which real models arguably already do); and it should work as a single self-contained story a seminar can read in a few minutes, with enough concrete detail (the actual prompt, the actual chain-of-thought, the actual answer) that we can argue about it line by line.

After the scenario, do the demonstration that it meets spec: explain what makes the two cases differ despite the identical text, and state which structural feature of your setup carries that difference -- e.g. what counterfactual would hold in one case and not the other. Then the honest part: describe how a skeptic could argue that the "unfaithful" model's chain-of-thought is faithful after all (maybe on a deflationary view where the CoT just *is* the reasoning), and say whether that objection is merely terminological or actually dissolves your distinction. Finally, tell me what, if anything, an observer with only black-box access could do to distinguish your two models in practice -- and if the answer is "nothing," say what that implies about whether faithfulness is even an empirically meaningful property. Oh and keep the whole thing to maybe 800-1000 words, it's going on a handout.
