At a sentencing-policy seminar last week someone defended executing murderers by saying “the punishment should fit the crime,” and everyone nodded like that settled it. I get the appeal, but the more I think about it the less I know what it means. It clearly isn’t supposed to mean we should literally do to offenders whatever they did: almost nobody thinks the state should rape rapists, torture torturers, or burgle burglars. But once we drop the literal reading, I’m not sure what work “fit” is doing.

There’s also Kant’s notorious discussion in The Metaphysics of Morals, where he says, roughly, that a murderer has to be punished with death, although the execution must not involve extra mistreatment that degrades humanity. Is Kant relying on one particular reading of “the punishment fits the crime,” or is he making a different claim altogether?

Please disambiguate the slogan into a menu of precise principles it might express. For each one, say (a) what makes a punishment count as fitting on that reading, (b) whether it is merely a limit on excessive punishment or positively requires a particular punishment, and (c) whether it would actually support capital punishment for murder. I’d especially like you to distinguish at least these possibilities, while adding others if I’ve missed some: punishment should resemble the crime; punishment should be proportionate to the crime’s seriousness; punishment should impose a loss equal to the victim’s loss; punishment should express the right amount of condemnation; punishment should be no worse than the criminal deserves; and punishment should be severe enough to deter comparable crimes.

Then test the readings on a few hard cases: a failed murder attempt that causes no lasting injury; a murderer who is genuinely remorseless versus one who is consumed by guilt; a fraudster whose scheme ruins thousands of retirements but involves no violence; a torturer who would experience execution as a release rather than a penalty; and the possibility that some innocent people will inevitably be executed. Which verdicts should each principle give, and where do they conflict?

Finally, tell me which reading is the strongest version of the slogan as a philosphical principle, which reading people in ordinary political debates probably mean, and whether the best reading supports, forbids, or is simply neutral about the death penalty.
