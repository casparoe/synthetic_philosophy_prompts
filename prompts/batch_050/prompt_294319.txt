I'm co-teaching a two-week unit on deepfakes in an undergrad course on epistemology and technology, and I want to end it with a "what do you actually think the problem is?" sorting quiz instead of an exam. Background to why: in our discussions the students kept talking past each other. Half of them think the harm is obvious — people get fooled into believing fake videos of things that never happened. The other half think the deeper damage is that real footage loses its force: a politician actually caught on tape can just shrug and say it's a deepfake, and everyone stays where they started (people sometimes call this the liar's dividend). I also tried to give them a third framing that a few of them found compelling: for most of human history you had to take people's word for what happened, and recording tech gave us, for the first time, a check on testimony that didn't depend on trusting anyone — a kind of epistemic backstop. On this view deepfakes don't just enable lies, they dissolve the backstop itself, which hurts even honest people and even true footage. Meanwhile one stubborn student keeps insisting Photoshop and staged tabloid photos already did all this decades ago and nothing epistemically new is happening, just more of it, faster.

So: please write me the quiz. 8 to 10 questions, each built around a concrete scenario, never a direct "do you think deepfakes are a big deal?" Use cases like: a courtroom where video evidence now gets challenged in every trial; a world with perfect, universally trusted detection tools; a dictator dismissing genuine massacre footage; a cheap fake targeting a random private person rather than a public figure; a documentary that fabricates footage of historical events; a provenance/watermarking standard adopted by every camera manufacturer. Each question gets 2-3 answer options, and then a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto at least these five positions: (a) deception-first, where the harm is false beliefs in viewers; (b) the liar's dividend view, where the harm is that genuine evidence becomes dismissible; (c) the backstop-erosion view, where the harm is the collapse of recording as a check on testimony tout court; (d) deflationary continuity, where this is all old wine and only the scale is new; and (e) institutional adaptationism, where the threat is real but transitional and new norms/standards will absorb it. The quiz has to sort people by what they say about the cases, not by which label sounds cool, and the scenarios have to be answerable by someone who's never heard any of these terms. Don't bother naming actual authors or papers anywhere, I'll add the references myself when I write it up.

Two things I want you to be strict about. First, commit: every answer option has to map somewhere definite in the key, no "this one could go either way," and the key needs an explicit tie-breaking rule for mixed answer patterns. If it turns out that two of these positions genuinely cannot be seperated by any scenario you can write, say so out loud in a note rather than faking a question that pretends to split them. Second, after the quiz and key, add a short paragraph where you say which of the five positions you think is actually closest to the truth and why — I'm going to get asked this in class and I don't want to stand there shrugging.
