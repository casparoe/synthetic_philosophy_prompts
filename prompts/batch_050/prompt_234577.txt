A friend and I have been arguing about the cave allegory in Book VII of Plato's Republic (we're using the Jowett translation) and got stuck on a bit that neither of us remembers anyone making much fuss about. It's this exchange:

"And if they were able to converse with one another, would they not suppose that they were naming what was actually before them?

Very true.

And suppose further that the prison had an echo which came from the other side, would they not be sure to fancy when one of the passers-by spoke that the voice which they heard came from the passing shadow?

No question, he replied."

My friend's claim is that this passage basically anticipates Putnam's brain-in-a-vat argument about reference. On that sort of view, a word refers to whatever actually causes its uses, so when a prisoner points and says "horse", he's saying something true — it's just that his word means shadow-of-a-horse, and his word for the voice he hears means echo, not speaker. I'm sceptical that Plato is committed to anything like that, and I suspect there's an important disanalogy with the vat anyway.

Could you compare the two cases carefully? Concretely: (1) Does Socrates' remark about the prisoners "naming what was actually before them" really commit him to their words refering to the shadows rather than to the statues carried along the wall, or is there a respectable reading on which their talk still reaches through to the real objects, since the shadows and echoes are genuinely caused by them? (2) Is the key disanalogy that the envatted brain's experiences have no causal connection to real trees and tables at all, whereas the prisoners' shadows are honest traces of real things? If so, does that make the cave referentially healthier than the vat, and does whichever predicament is referentially worse line up with whichever is epistemically worse? (3) Whichever way you come down on that, think about the released prisoner who gets dragged back down: given that his entire vocabulary was built up out of shadow-talk, can his old words even express what he now knows, or does Plato owe us a story about how he'd acquire genuinely new meanings (and would the echo detail make that problem worse, since even the sounds in the cave are mislocated)?

I'm less interested in a tour of the secondary literature than in which of my friend and me has the better of the argument, so do take a side.
