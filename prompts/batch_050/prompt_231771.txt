I help co-ordinate the marking for a first-year formal logic module, and every term the tutors argue about the same sort of student objection. Some students challenge a premise, some challenge the logic, and some do a sort of Tortoise manoeuvre: they accept the premises, accept that the conclusion follows, but then say the inference needs another premise saying that the premises imply the conclusion. Occassionally this is philosophically interesting, but often it just derails an otherwise decent answer.

Could you turn the point in Lewis Carroll’s “What the Tortoise Said to Achilles” into a marking procedure our tutors can actually use? The passage I want you to work from is this:

"Then I must ask you to accept C."

"I'll do so," said the Tortoise, "as soon as you've entered it in that note-book of yours. What else have you got in it?"

Please don’t just explain the regress in the usual textbook way. I want a repeatable triage procedure for deciding what kind of objection a student is making and how much credit it should get. It needs to distinguish at least:

- an ordinary challenge to one of the premises;
- a claim that the conclusion does not follow under the logic specified in the question;
- a missing-premise objection where adding a premise would genuinely repair the argument;
- a Tortoise-style demand that a rule of inference be rewritten as a premise;
- a legitimate challenge to the background logic, such as rejecting explosion, unrestricted modus ponens, or some other rule in a non-classical system;
- and a muddle between accepting an argument as valid and accepting its conclusion as true.

The procedure should say which questions the tutor should ask, in what order, what answer settles each question, and where the tutor has to exercise judgement rather than mechanically applying the rule. Also say which distinctions absolutely must be preserved when explaining this to students, and which bits of the philosophy can safely be left out in a first-year course.

Apply the procedure to these three cases:

1. A student is given “P” and “If P then Q”, concludes “Q”, but says the proof is incomplete unless we add “If P and if-P-then-Q, then Q”.
2. A student accepts both premises of a valid geometrical argument but refuses the conclusion because nobody has separately stated the hypothetical proposition that the premises necessitate it.
3. In a course explicitly using classical logic, a student says an argument from a contradiction to an arbitrary conclusion is invalid because the contradiction has no relevant connexion to the conclusion. The student correctly identifies explosion as the rule they reject.

One of these should produce a marking decision a generous tutor probably won’t like, so don’t sand that down.

After the prose explanation, give me a valid JSON object with exactly these fields: procedure_name (a string), steps (an array of objects with step, question, pass_condition, and verdict_if_failed), classification_rules (an array of objects with label, test, and feedback_line), worked_cases (an array of objects with case, classification, decision, and reason), judgement_calls (an array of strings), and procedure_limits (an array of strings). Don’t put the JSON in a code fence.
