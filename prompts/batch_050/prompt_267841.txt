Here is the question the students were set. It's marked out of 25:

"Consider two identical particles, each of which can occupy one of two one-particle states. (a) Count the distinct arrangements of the two particles over the two states on classical (Maxwell-Boltzmann) statistics and on the two quantum statistics (Bose-Einstein and Fermi-Dirac), and explain what the difference in counting is taken to show about the individuality of quantum particles. (b) State the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles and assess whether quantum particles violate it, paying attention to the difference between absolute and weak discernibility and to the case of two fermions in the singlet state."

And here is one of the answers I have to grade:

"(a) Take two particles, call them 1 and 2, and two one-particle states, A and B. On classical Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics there are four arrangements: both particles in A, both in B, particle 1 in A with particle 2 in B, and particle 2 in A with particle 1 in B. The last two count as distinct because classical particles are individuals: exchanging them produces a genuinely new physical situation, whether or not anyone could detect the difference. Quantum mechanics counts differently. For bosons the joint state must be symmetric under particle exchange, so the arrangement with 1 in A and 2 in B is the very same state as the arrangement with 2 in A and 1 in B, and there are only three arrangements (Bose-Einstein). For fermions the state must be antisymmetric, which means double occupancy is impossible (this is the Pauli exclusion principle), and there is exactly one arrangement, one particle per state (Fermi-Dirac). The standard philosophical moral, pressed by French and Krause among others, is that permutation of quantum particles never yields a new physical possibility, so the particle labels track nothing real; quantum particles seem to lack the individuality that classical particles possess.

(b) The Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles, Leibniz's Law, holds that things sharing all their properties are identical. Two electrons share all their intrinsic properties (mass, charge, spin magnitude), and in entangled states no measurement can tell them apart. By Leibniz's Law, then, such electrons are numerically identical: strictly speaking there are not two things here at all, only one. Someone might object that the electrons could still differ in extrinsic respects like position, but in the entangled case even these are shared, since the state assigns no definite positions to either particle.

Saunders has defended the Principle against this sort of argument. For two electrons in the singlet state, he points out, one electron is spin-up and the other is spin-down, so they are absolutely discernible after all, and the Principle survives even in the hardest case. My conclusion is therefore that although quantum statistics undermines the classical notion of individuality, Leibniz's Principle itself survives once we take all of the particles' properties, relational ones included, into account."
