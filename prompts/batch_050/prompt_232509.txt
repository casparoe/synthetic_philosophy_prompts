I teach aesthetics at the undergraduate level, and every year the seminar on Hume's "Of the Standard of Taste" lands in the same place: roughly half the room insists that the essay's proposal is circular, the other half insists the circularity charge rests on a misreading, and the two halves mostly talk past each other. The published literature strikes me as no further along. Here is the sentence everything turns on:

"It is natural for us to seek a Standard of Taste; a rule, by which the various sentiments of men may be reconciled; at least, a decision, afforded, confirming one sentiment, and condemning another."

One camp reads this as Hume promising a decision procedure for disputed verdicts, and then objects that his test for good critics — delicacy of sentiment, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice — can only be applied by someone who already makes sound aesthetic discriminations, so the standard presupposes the very capacity it was supposed to certify. The other camp replies that Hume never offered a test for identifying the true judges independently of their verdicts, that the criteria are meant to characterize a natural kind of well-functioning judge rather than to pick one out from a neutral standpoint, and that the "at least" in the passage already concedes that a decisive ruling may be the exception rather than the rule: the standard's real work is to explain how reconciliation of sentiment is possible, not to supply an algorithm. Both camps quote the same sentences at each other.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that this is a verbal disagreement sustained by an equivocation on "standard": the circularity camp assumes a standard must be something like a meter bar, usable to settle disputes from a position of aesthetic neutrality, while the defense camp assumes a standard can be constitutive of correctness without being usable by just anyone — the way ideal-observer theories in ethics are defended against parallel objections. But I am not confident, because Hume himself, earlier in the very same essay, offers a diagnosis of disputes like this one that seems to cut against my reading: "An explanation of the terms commonly ends the controversy; and the disputants are surprized to find, that they had been quarrelling, while at bottom they agreed in their judgment." If the circularity debate were merely verbal, seventy years of clarifying terms should have dissolved it by now, and it has not.

So: diagnose this impasse. Is it verbal, substantive, or two questions under one heading — an epistemological question about how we identify reliable critics and a constitutive question about what makes an aesthetic verdict correct? Use what each side actually commits to, including the fact that Hume himself allows that some disagreements (between the manners of different ages and nations, and between blameless personal preferences) survive even a fully functioning standard. Does the presence of those admitted residue cases support the reconciliation-only reading, or do they show the standard fails even at the weaker task? And given your diagnosis, state concretely what would count as progress: a distinction the literature has failed to draw, a comparison class (ideal observers, experts, measuring instruments) whose logic would settle the reading, or an argument that the dispute is unresolvable because Hume's text genuinely underdetermines the answer — and if the last, say whether that is a defect in the essay or a feature of any sentimentalist aesthetics.
