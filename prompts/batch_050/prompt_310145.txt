I'm a statistician by training. My working vocabulary is sampling designs, estimators, order statistics, priors and posteriors, credible intervals, and the assumptions under which an observation can be treated as a random draw from a population. Please write your answer in British English.

Here is the position I want translated. The doomsday argument, roughly: suppose the total number of humans who will ever live is some unknown number N, and suppose your birth rank -- your position in the sequence of all humans, past and future -- can be treated as a uniformly random draw from 1 to N. You observe that your rank is, say, around 100 billion. Conditioning on this observation shifts your beliefs about N: values of N not much larger than 100 billion become far more probable than very large values, because on a very large N your observed rank would be improbably early. Conclusion: humanity's total run is likely to be much shorter than we naively assume, and thus extinction is closer than it looks. A cousin of the argument runs on durations rather than headcounts: given only that something is observed at a random point in its lifespan, there is a 95 per cent chance the observation falls in the middle 95 per cent of that lifespan, which licenses confidence bounds on how much longer the thing will last.

Translate this into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can. I expect the classical serial-number problem -- estimating the size of a population from the largest serial number observed in a random sample -- to be the natural home for much of it, and I'd like the translation to run through that machinery: state the likelihood, the implied prior over N, and the posterior, and show which step of the doomsday argument corresponds to which step of the statistical estimation. Do the same for the duration-based cousin, which I take to be a claim about a uniform quantile of an unknown interval.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually care about. First, which moves translate cleanly into ordinary statistical practice. Second, which translate only with distortion -- I suspect the step where a person treats his own birth rank as a random draw is of this kind, since in my world an observation is a random draw because of a sampling procedure someone carried out, not because of the observer's nature. Third, which parts of the argument have no expression in my vocabulary at all: candidates include the choice of reference class (who counts as being in the sequence), the question of why I should regard myself as a typical member rather than a selected one, and anything essentially indexical about the first-person perspective of the reasoner.

Be honest in the translation: don't quietly install statistical assumptions and present them as part of the original argument, and don't pretend a move survived translation if you had to bend it. End with a verdict: does the residue point to something my statistical vocabulary genuinely cannot say -- so that the doomsday argument rests on a principle about self-location that is extra-statistical -- or does it show that the argument was smuggling in a premise that no respectable sampling design would licence? Commit to one of these, or to a principled split between the two versions of the argument, and justify the commitment.
