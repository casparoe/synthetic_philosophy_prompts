I'm working on a novel (loosely historical but the moral machinery is modern enough) and I need a pair of mirrored scenes built to spec. The engine I want to use is Abelard's old idea that the sin is entirely in the consent, not the act — you sin when you consent to what you believe you shouldn't, and actually carrying it out adds nothing to your guilt. Also, on his view even *wanting* the bad thing isn't sin yet; desire and consent come apart, and I want that distinction respected in the setup, since it seems to be the part everyone slides past.

Construct a scenario with these constraints:

1. Character A fully consents to a wrongful deed. No coercion, no factual mistake about what the deed is, no last-minute wobble — the consent has to be wholehearted. But A is then prevented from acting by pure bad luck, so the harmful thing never actually happens.
2. Character B performs the very outward deed A consented to (same movements, same downstream consequences, or as close as realism allows) but never consents to it. And don't reach for a gun-to-the-head — I want something subtler, like blameless ignorance about what they're doing, or a psychological compulsion they can't control.
3. On Abelard's account A must come out fully sinful and B not sinful at all, and that verdict has to fall out of the theory itself, not just be announced by you. If you have to stipulate the verdicts, the scene fails.
4. Realistic. No sci-fi, no hypnotists, no twin-switcheroos. Think fraud, betrayal, an affair, a poisoning that misfires — the kind of thing that could plausibly appear in literary fiction.
5. Make it uncomfortable. I want a reader to feel at least a tug toward "surely B did something worse" even after granting the whole setup. If the case makes Abelard look obviously right, it's too easy to be interesting.

Then do the check: state Abelard's verdict on each character and why, say which single feature of the case is doing the work, tell me what happens to the verdicts if you strip that feature out, and give the best attack a critic of Abelard could mount against the setup — plus whether he has a reply, since i suspect he does.

Scenario itself should be a couple paragraphs max; the analysis after can run longer. Finish with a one-line verdict on whether this scene makes Abelard's view look profound or unhinged, formatted as:

Verdict: <one sentence>
