I'm a PhD student in political theory writing a dissertation chapter on the ethics of influence, and before I commit to a working position I want to stress-test the set of views I've assembled. I'd be grateful for a consistency audit rather than a survey of the literature. Here is where I currently stand:

1. What makes an attempt at influence manipulative, as opposed to legitimately persuasive, is that it bypasses or subverts the target's capacity for rational deliberation rather than engaging it. Neither the influencer's intentions nor the quality of the outcome for the target is directly relevant to the classification: benevolent manipulation is still manipulation, and clumsy but reason-directed pressure is not.

2. It is legitimate for a public health agency to present an accurate statistic in the frame most likely to produce compliance -- saying that a vaccine has a 95% survival-relevant efficacy rate rather than a 5% failure rate -- provided the statistic itself is true and decision-relevant.

3. Emotional appeals in political speech can be fully legitimate persuasion: an aid organization that shows donors vivid footage of the people their money would help is doing nothing untoward, even though it knows the footage moves people in ways that statistics do not.

4. A friend who deliberately withholds a fact from me because she knows I would overreact to it and make a worse decision is doing something at least mildly objectionable, even if her prediction about my reaction is correct and her aim is my own good.

5. It is permissible, perhaps even obligatory, for a physician to exploit a patient's known loss aversion in how she presents treatment options, when the patient is cognitively intact but irrationally refusing a treatment that is clearly in his interest.

My questions: Which pairs or triples of these commitments are actually in tension, and which only appear to be? For each genuine tension, please identify exactly what generates it -- which clause of (1), or which suppressed premise linking the cases -- and rank the tensions by severity. I'd also like to know the cheapest revision that would restore consistency in each case, and what it costs: for instance, whether I can keep (2) by refining (1) in some principled way, or whether any refinement that saves (2) inevitably condemns (4) or (5) as well. Please don't simply tell me that the cases are contested; I want to know what I am actually committed to by holding all five, and which single commitment, if dropped, would do the most work.
