ok so background: I run this "weird physics" discussion night at my school (like 8 of us, mostly juniors and seniors, plus our teacher who lets us use his classroom). Last month I presented the Aharonov-Bohm effect — electrons going around both sides of a solenoid, interference pattern shifts even tho the electrons never pass through any actual magnetic field, only through the region where the vector potential is nonzero. And someone asked the obvious question: so is the potential the real thing then? And it got messy fast, because the potential isn't even unique — you can add the gradient of basically any function to it (a gauge transformation) and every prediction stays the same. So it seems like either the real physical thing changes when you do a purely mathematical relabeling (weird), or the only real stuff is the gauge-invariant stuff, in which case how does it shove the electrons around from a region they never enter? We argued for an hour and got nowhwere.

So here's what I actually want: a decision tree / sorting quiz we can use at the next meeting, where each person answers the questions in order and lands on a named position about what's real in electromagnetism. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names — first figure out which questions actually split the positions, then put the positions at the leaves, then note which answer-combinations end up empty. Each branch should be labeled with the claim that splits it, phrased as a concrete case someone can react to without knowing any terminology — e.g. something like "two setups with different potentials but identical gauge-invariant facts give exactly the same interference pattern: are those two physically different situations, or one situation described twice?" Aim for 6–9 leaves. Positions that have to show up as leaves somewhere: the view that only gauge-invariant quantities (field strengths, phase shifts) are real and the rest is redundant bookkeeping; the "holonomy" version of that where the fundamental facts live on closed loops rather than at points, so the AB effect is local after all relative to loops; the view that the potentials themselves are physically real and gauge transformations are just redescribing one real underlying object (people talk about fiber bundles here, but don't assume we know what those are — explain in a clause if you use it); and the deflationist view that the whole debate is a pseudo-question since the math works fine either way. If a combination of answers has nobody defending it, still include the leaf and say in a sentence why it's empty — incoherent, just unattractive, or genuinely unexplored. And if two positions in the literature (or two views people actually hold) can't be separated by your questions, say so explicitly instead of pretending the tree sorts them.

Each leaf gets: the position's name (or "unnamed"), and one sentence on its most awkward commitment — what does this view have to swallow that its rivals don't? Audience is us: we know basic E&M (fields, potentials, double-slit interference) but zero QFT, so none of the diagnostic questions can lean on anything fancier than the AB setup and maybe the ordinary electrostatic potential.
