I work as a policy analyst in the equalities office of a mid-sized city government, and part of my job is to produce the first-pass assessment of public submissions we receive whenever we consult on employment, education, or licensing policy. We get hundreds of these, I typically have under an hour per submission, and my summaries go to a committee of councillors who are not specialists. A recurring pattern is the appeal to what is "natural": submissions arguing, for instance, that a proposed change to parental leave, to hiring in some occupation, or to school sports provision should be rejected because the existing arrangement reflects the natural differences between men and women. My office currently has no shared standard for handling these, and I have been tasked with drafting one. Acting as a philosopher with expertise in nineteenth-century liberal thought and in applied argument analysis, please help me turn John Stuart Mill's treatment of such appeals in The Subjection of Women (1869) into a working procedure my colleagues and I can actually run.

The passage I want the procedure built around is from Chapter 1, where Mill writes about who properly bears the burden of argument:

"Again, in practical matters, the burthen of proof is supposed to be with those who are against liberty; who contend for any restriction or prohibition; either any limitation of the general freedom of human action, or any disqualification or disparity of privilege affecting one person or kind of persons, as compared with others. The à priori presumption is in favour of freedom and impartiality."

Before drafting the procedure, think carefully about what Mill is and is not claiming here and in the surrounding argument: how the point about the burden of proof connects to his broader claims that long-standing custom masquerades as nature, that what is called the nature of women is largely an artificial product of their circumstances, and that arrangements kept in place by power tend to look natural to those who benefit from them. I want the translation into practice to preserve these distinctions rather than flatten Mill into a generic slogan about questioning tradition, and I want it stated plainly where the passage alone is doing the work and where I need the wider essay to justify a step.

The deliverable is a written procedure a non-philosopher could follow in under an hour: the questions to ask about a submission in order, what counts as a satisfactory answer at each step, how to distinguish a submission that merely asserts naturalness from one that offers evidence Mill would have had to take seriously, how to handle submissions appealing to contemporary science rather than custom (a case Mill did not face in this form), and the specific points where the procedure must stop and hand the question to a committee because it is a judgment about values or contested evidence rather than a step the checklist can settle. Then apply the procedure to two realistic submissions I have paraphrased from our files: first, a business association arguing against extended paternal leave on the ground that mothers are naturally the primary caregivers, so equal leave would fight biology; second, a sports governing body arguing that sex-segregated competition categories should be retained because average physiological differences between the sexes are real and measurable. Run the procedure on both honestly, including any point where it delivers an assessment my office's political leadership will not like, and close with a candid account of what this procedure cannot do and where it is most likely to misfire in the hands of a hurried analyst.
