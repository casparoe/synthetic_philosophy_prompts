I've been reading about the architecture of nuclear deterrence, and the more I think about it, the stranger the whole institution seems to me. Consider the basic practice: a state maintains an enormous arsenal whose actual use would be an unprecedented moral catastrophe, and its entire security strategy consists in convincing adversaries that it would use that arsenal under certain conditions. Deterrence only works if the threat is believed -- so leaders cultivate credibility through war games, public declarations, automatic launch procedures, short decision timelines, and sometimes deliberate ambiguity about where the red lines lie. Some strategists even argue for reducing a leader's practical ability to back down, on the grounds that an adversary who knows retaliation would be near-automatic will never test the threat in the first place. At the same time, almost everyone involved in running these systems seems to hope, privately, that the threatened act never occurs, and many would concede that retaliating after deterrence had already failed -- incinerating millions simply because the bluff was called -- would serve no defensive purpose whatsoever and would be an atrocity committed out of sheer consistency.

So my question: what does this practice, taken as a whole, presuppose about the relationship between intentions, threats, and actions? On the one hand, the institution seems to require that a leader sincerely intend (or at least convincingly appear to intend) to do something it would be monstrous to actually do -- which suggests a view on which conditional intentions can be morally evaluated separately from the acts they contemplate, or perhaps one on which a good outcome (the threat never being tested) retroactively justifies a wrongful intention. On the other hand, the standard defense of deterrence insists there is no wrongful intention at all, only a warning -- which presupposes something about the difference between threatening and bluffing, and about whether a bluff you hope never to be called on is really a lie. And the engineering of credibility -- locking in commitments, narrowing one's own future options, appearing slightly irrational so the threat is more believable -- seems to presuppose a picture of rational agency on which it can be rational to make oneself incapable of doing the rational thing later. Can these presuppositions be made consistent with each other, and with the more ordinary moral principle that it is wrong to intend what it would be wrong to do? If not, which part of the practice would have to change -- the arsenals, the doctrines, the public declarations -- and what would deterrence even look like if it were reformed to presuppose only things we actually believe?
