Okay so I got into this massive argument with my friend Jon last weekend and I need help settling it in a sneaky way. We were arguing about lifeboat ethics -- like the classic setup where the boat can only hold so many people and you have to decide who gets on and who drowns. His position is that all these thought experiments are basically rigged: someone decided the boat only holds ten, someone decided there's no other boat coming, and then they act like the brutal triage logic that follows is some deep truth about morality when really its just an artifact of how the scenario was built. He kept saying real-world scarcity (food aid, migration, hospital beds) is always made by people and institutions, not by physics, so lifeboat reasoning teaches people to naturalize political choices.

I think he's half wrong -- even if the frame is constructed, there really are moments where you can't save everyone and triage intuitions matter, and the thought experiments capture something real about that.

Anyway, the fun part: I TA for a first-year undergrad ethics class, 18 students, one 75-minute slot, and the prof said I can run one session however I want. Jon is visiting that week, which is honestly part of the motivation. I want a fully runnable activity -- rules, roles if any, timeline, materials -- where the students first genuinely feel that triage logic is unavoidable and inescapable (they should be making the hard calls themselves, with some kind of stakes or pressure, not just discussing hypothetically), and THEN, through something that happens in the game itself rather than me lecturing, they discover that the scarcity they were operating under was a design choice made by someone, and they have to reckon with what that does to the decisions they already made. Neither Jon's view nor mine should be named or taught up front -- the terms should only come out in the debrief, if at all.
