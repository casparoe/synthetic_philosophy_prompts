I'm drafting an essay for a small reading group on the history and philosophy of moral change, working title "Why Arguments Lose: Honor, Interests, and the Timing of Moral Revolutions." Here is the core of it, as notes rather than polished prose. The central claim: moral revolutions -- the relatively rapid collapses of practices like dueling among European elites, footbinding in China, and the Atlantic slave trade -- were not caused by moral argument. In each case the decisive arguments were available, well circulated, and widely known for generations before the practice fell, and were largely ignored. What changed, I want to say, is that abolition became coupled to the reigning conception of collective honor and to material interests: dueling died when codes of gentlemanly respectability shifted so that refusing a challenge stopped counting as cowardice; footbinding collapsed within a few decades once it became entangled with national shame in the eyes of Chinese reformers; and British abolition tracked economic transformations of the sort the Williams thesis describes. Appiah's account of honor as the engine of moral revolutions is my main scaffold. My essay's structure: (1) set up the puzzle -- sound arguments long precede abolition, so argument alone cannot explain timing; (2) the three case studies; (3) a mechanism section proposing "honor-interest coupling," where practices die when they become identity-threatening rather than merely criticized; (4) a coda conceding that argument does real work in determining *which* revolutions become available, even if it rarely detonates them.

I'd like comments of several specific kinds. First, accuracy: have I described any of the three cases in a way that a historian would object to? In particular, is it right that the anti-dueling arguments really were old and fully developed long before dueling declined, or have I smoothed over the role of legal enforcement and changing institutions? Second, counterexamples: my thesis is that moral argument is almost never the proximate cause of a moral revolution, though it can be a necessary background resource. Can you supply a candidate case where argument plausibly *was* the proximate cause, and assess whether it genuinely threatens my claim or can be absorbed by the honor-interest framework? Third, a worry I can't resolve myself: does my essay confuse explaining the *timing* of a revolution with explaining its *occurrence*? If arguments explain why abolition was on the agenda at all, while honor and interests only explain when it happened, my title's thesis ("why arguments lose") may be much weaker than it sounds. Fourth, is the honor-interest coupling mechanism actually falsifiable, or have I built something elastic enough to accommodate any historical outcome -- and if the latter, what prediction could I add to give it teeth? Fifth, is my treatment of the Williams thesis fair to its critics, including the economic historians who argue the slave trade was still profitable at abolition, or am I leaning on a contested economic claim as if it were settled?

Please organize your comments as a list ranked by how damaging each problem is to the essay as a whole -- fatal restructuring required at the top, cosmetic fixes at the bottom -- and for each, say briefly what revision would address it. I'm especially interested in whether the coda's concession quietly guts the main thesis: if arguments determine which revolutions are possible, have I actually shown that they "lose," or just relocated their importance? Feel free to be blunt; I'd rather scrap the essay than publish something whose mechanism section is unfalsifiable or whose history is sloppy.
