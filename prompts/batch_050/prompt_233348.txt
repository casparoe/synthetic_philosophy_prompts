In a music appreciation seminar I am taking, the instructor leans heavily on the claim that music is a language of emotion: it has something akin to grammar and syntax (harmonic progression, tension and resolution, cadence), it transmits feelings from composer or performer to listener, and learning to listen well is a matter of acquiring fluency, much as one acquires fluency in a foreign tongue. The analogy is not merely decorative in her hands; she uses it to argue that musical meaning can be taught and even glossed, that a listener who hears nothing in a late Beethoven quartet is musically illiterate rather than merely unmoved, and that cross-cultural listeners can in principle learn to read unfamiliar traditions the way one learns a second language. I am skeptical that the analogy can bear all of this, but I want a rigorous audit rather than my own hunch. Please provide: first, an explicit correspondence table for the strongest version of the mapping — what plays the role of words, syntax, semantics, the speaker, the message, and fluency — including the more sophisticated variant in which music parallels not vocabulary but the emotional prosody of speech; second, the points where the mapping breaks down, such as the absence of propositional content and reference, the lack of anything like double articulation or conventionally fixed meanings, and the fact that composers neither control nor intend the specific feelings listeners report, with a clear separation of the disanalogies that matter for my instructor's three conclusions from those that are merely incidental; third, an assessment of whether the analogy conflates music's expressing emotion with music's arousing emotion, and whether a language can do the former without the latter; and fourth, a verdict on which, if any, of her three conclusions survives the audit, along with a better governing analogy if one exists. Keep the whole answer under 700 words, with the verdict stated plainly at the end.
