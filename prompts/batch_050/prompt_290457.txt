So I'm a junior and I got roped into being the "proof referee" for our math club — basically when two kids disagree about whether someone's proof actually proves the thing, I have to give a ruling, and I do it like 3-4 times a week. I don't have time to redo everyone's whole proof, and half the fights are about steps where one kid says "that's just obvious" and the other says "no it isn't."

I found this old Lewis Carroll dialogue, "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles," where the Tortoise keeps refusing to accept a conclusion even after accepting every premise Achilles writes down. At one point he says:

"If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"

Turn the Tortoise's trick into an actual procedure I can run on a disputed proof: the questions I ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where I can just rule "write the missing step down as its own line and we're done," and where the procedure has to stop because Achilles's problem kicks in — like, at some point someone has to just *see* that a step follows, and you can't keep demanding more premises forever. Then run the procedure on two cases: a geometry proof where a kid skips the step about corresponding angles, and a club-debate argument where every single premise is granted but the conclusion still doesn't land. One of them should come out with a ruling I won't enjoy giving. Also tell me honestly where this procedure will misfire, since I'm applying it to kids, not tortoises.
