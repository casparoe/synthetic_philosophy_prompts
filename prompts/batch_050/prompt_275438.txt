So I've been reading around the 'why are some nations rich' fight -- institutions vs geography vs culture -- and collecting the famous natural experiments that get wheeled out to argue institutions are doing the causal work. Here's my list so far:

(1) The settler-mortality instrument (Acemoglu/Johnson/Robinson): where Europeans could survive they built inclusive institutions, where they couldn't they built extractive ones, so use mortality as an instrument for institutions.
(2) Divided countries as controlled experiments -- North vs South Korea, East vs West Germany -- same geography, same culture, different institutions.
(3) Dell on the mita in Peru: a forced-labor boundary abolished 200 years ago still shows up in household consumption today.
(4) Nunn on the slave trade: how many slaves were taken from a region predicts trust and development there now.
(5) The Engerman-Sokoloff factor endowments story: sugar plantations led to inequality and bad institutions, wheat farming to broad landholding and good ones, so initial crops cast a long shadow.

I want one more piece of evidence or identification strategy for the institutions side that doesn't work like any of these. So: no more colonial instruments, no more divided-country comparisons, no more within-country historical discontinuities, and not just a special case or combo of the above. If you genuinely can't think of one that clears that bar, say so instead of sneaking one of the five back in with a mustache on.

Then be honest about whether your new one actually survives the standard objections to this whole literature -- instrument validity, institutions being a grab-bag variable that's hard to measure, reverse causation from growth to institutions, and the fact that historical persistence studies sometimes just pick up geography through a side door. Does your design do better on any of these, or is it just as exposed?
