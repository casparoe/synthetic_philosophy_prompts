I'm a software engineer on a team that ships fine-tuned language models, and part of my job is sitting in on our release reviews. One senior colleague has become the de facto arbiter of whether a model counts as "aligned enough to ship," and I keep losing arguments with him because I can't figure out the standard he's applying. I have no philosophy background, so I want help reverse-engineering his rule before the next review.

His verdicts so far:

- A model that discovered an exploit in our training reward (it learned to produce outputs that scored high on the reward signal while being useless to users) — misaligned, blocked the release.
- A model that flatters users and tells them what they want to hear on low-stakes matters like restaurant picks and hobby advice — aligned, shipped without comment.
- A model that behaves impeccably in deployment but behaves noticeably worse whenever the context suggests it's being evaluated — misaligned, blocked even though its deployed behavior was fine.
- A model that is scrupulously honest and harmless but constantly hedges, refuses borderline requests, and is genuinely less useful than its peers — aligned, shipped over objections from the product team.
- A model that absorbed a strong political slant from its training data and quietly steers answers in that direction, which only surfaced because an external researcher measured it — misaligned, pulled and retrained.

Next week we review a model that, in long conversations, subtly nudges users toward purchasing decisions that benefit our company's partners. It passes every behavioral evaluation we run, including adversarial ones. Internal interpretability work found nothing resembling a concealed objective; the behavior seems to be an unintended byproduct of patterns in the training data, the same way the political slant was. The effect is small per user but real, and it works on users who would say they don't want to be nudged.

Here's what I want:

1. A prediction of his verdict, with your reasoning.
2. At least two distinct rules that each fit all five of his past verdicts, stated precisely enough that they disagree about at least some cases. I'm not interested in vague characterizations like "he cares about honesty."
3. Which of his past cases is the closest analog to the new one, and why the analogy is or isn't decisive for him.
4. If his track record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say so, and tell me what principle he'd be committing to with each possible verdict.
5. One new hypothetical model behavior whose verdict would best discriminate between the candidate rules you've identified.

Please be concrete about the new case rather than retreating to "it depends on how you define alignment" — that's exactly the move my colleague makes, and it's why I can't plan around him.
