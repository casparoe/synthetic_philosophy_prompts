I'm putting together a handout for a reading group on Indian epistemology, specifically the debate about whether testimony (śabda) is a source of knowledge and when it's valid. Everyone in the group has already done the Western version of this debate (Hume-style reductionism vs Reid-style anti-reductionism about testimony), so I want the handout to force them to notice that the Indian debate is *carved up differently* and not just the same positions with Sanskrit names.

Write me a decision tree that sorts the positions. Each node should be a question someone could answer without knowing any Sanskrit or any school names -- a concrete claim about some everyday case of believing what someone tells you (or in the limiting case, what a text tells you). The branches then locate the actual positions in the space the questions generate.

Constraints on what the tree has to do:

- It must have leaves for at least: the Cārvāka refusal to count testimony as a pramāṇa at all, the Buddhist move of treating testimony as really just inference, Nyāya's parataḥ-prāmāṇya (validity needs external confirmation), and the Mīmāṃsā svataḥ-prāmāṇya view. Within Mīmāṃsā it must split Prabhākara from Kumārila on what intrinsic validity implies about error (akhyāti-style "every cognition is valid, error is just incomplete apprehension" vs Kumārila's line that validity is intrinsic but falsity can still arise).
- One leaf has to deal with the authorless-Veda doctrine (apauruṣeyatva) -- I want the tree to show *why* the Mīmāṃsakas thought authorlessness mattered, i.e. that human testimony inherits the defects of its speaker and a text with no speaker has no speaker-defects to inherit. Ideally the tree makes clear whether this is a separate position or just the svataḥ view applied to a special case -- I'm genuinely unsure how to draw that and want the tree to take a stand.
- At least one splitting question should be a case that seperates intrinsic-validity views from confirmation-requiring views *without* mentioning the Veda, because my group will be suspiscious that the whole debate only matters for scripture. Something like: you ask a stranger on the street the time, they answer, you believe them -- did the belief become knowledge at the moment of hearing, or only once it's corroborated?
- Mark any combination of answers that nobody historically occupies, and say in one sentence whether it's incoherent, just unattractive, or genuinely unexplored.
- Flag any pair of leaves the tree fails to separate, if there are any.

Keep the whole thing to 8 leaves max, one sentence per leaf on the position's most awkward commitment, and write the splitting questions in plain conversational english since half the group hasn't read any of the primary texts yet.
