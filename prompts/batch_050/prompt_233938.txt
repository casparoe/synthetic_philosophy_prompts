I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been prototyping an alignment setup: two capable but untrusted language models debate whether a proposed action is safe, and a smaller model that I do trust reads the transcript and picks a winner. When I described the design to a colleague, she said it collapses under what people call the obfuscated-argument problem, and she compressed her objection into the argument below. It seems to prove too much -- if it goes through, debate formats are worthless in general, not just in my setup -- but I can't locate the error, and I'm no longer sure there is one. (1) A debate protocol is useful only if the honest debater reliably wins when the transcript is judged by the weaker model. (2) The judge can be rationally moved only by things it can independently check: that a stated premise is true, or that an inference step is valid. (3) So the honest debater wins only in cases where the judge could have verified the whole chain of reasoning on its own. (4) In those cases the debate contributes nothing the judge couldn't have produced alone, so debate earns its keep only in cases the judge cannot fully verify. (5) In any case the judge cannot fully verify, there is at least one gap the deceptive debater can fill with a step that looks valid but isn't, or a premise that sounds true but isn't, and the honest debater cannot expose the flaw except by pointing to something the judge can check -- which, by hypothesis, there isn't. (6) So debate is either redundant (the judge could have verified the claim anyway) or unsafe (the deceptive debater wins): there is no working region where it is both informative and trustworthy. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost the overall argument? I'd most like to keep (1) and (2), since my judge model genuinely cannot be trusted on unaided judgment, but tell me if one of them is the problem. One more data point: in my own toy runs, the judge sided with a wrong debater who argued fluently about a long dependency chain, and I'd like to know whether that observation should change the diagnosis or is just an implementation detail. Answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: the step you reject, why that step looked valid, the cheapest revision to the argument, what the revision costs the protocol's usefulness, and how to read the toy-run result.
