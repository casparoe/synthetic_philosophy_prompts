Here is the disagreement I want you to assess. I overheard two colleagues, Mara and Jonas, arguing about a finding from a twin study reporting that differences in adults' body weight within a large, affluent country are about 70% attributable to genetic differences.

Mara opened by saying this proves that the national obesity-prevention campaign is mostly a waste of money: if weight is 70% genetic, willpower, diet programs, and public health messaging can at most move the remaining 30%, so the government should stop lecturing people.

Jonas replied that she's misreading the number. Heritability, he said, is a statement about what explains variation in this particular population, in this particular environment, at this particular time — not a statement about how fixed or changeable weight is. He gave an analogy: in a country where everyone gets plenty of food, height differences are highly heritable, yet average height shot up over the twentieth century as nutrition improved. High heritability and large environmental effects on the average are perfectly compatible.

Mara conceded the point about averages over time but pushed back: the campaign isn't trying to change historical averages, it's trying to change individuals living in the current environment. And heritability, she said, is estimated precisely in the current environment — so within that environment, genes explain most of why some people are heavier than others, which is exactly what an intervention would have to fight against.

Jonas answered that this still doesn't follow. Heritability tells you what explains existing variation given the range of environments people actually happen to occupy, but an effective campaign is by definition a new environmental factor — one that may lie outside the range the study measured. A variable can explain zero percent of variation in a population simply because everyone is exposed to it equally, yet changing it would change everyone. He added that heritability also says nothing about what would happen in a different country or under different policies, so the same study number can't be exported to justify or condemn programs elsewhere.

Mara's final move: fine, but then heritability figures are practically useless for policy, so why does anyone bother reporting them in popular science coverage at all? Jonas said they're useful for some scientific questions, just not for the inference she tried to draw.
