I'm applying for a small undergraduate summer research grant in philosophy (three months, ending in a paper of roughly 8000 words) and I need to turn a half-formed idea into a proper proposal. The topic is causation by omission. My starting point is the familiar overgeneration worry: if your houseplants die because I didn't water them while you were away, and we accept my not-watering as a cause of their death, then it looks like the non-watering of every other person on the planet was equally a cause, which seems absurd. The standard move is to say that only *normatively deviant* omissions count as causes -- I promised to water them, a random person in Peru didn't -- but I have a hunch this move is worse than people admit if you want causal facts to be prior to and independent of moral facts (e.g. if you want causation to help ground moral responsibility, you can't then let responsibility-grounding norms decide what counts as a cause, on pain of circularity). My half-formed idea is that the literature conflates two quite different things under the label "omission": (a) an agent's not-doing something they were in a position to do, which is at least event-like and agent-involving, and (b) the sheer absence of an event, full stop, which arguably isn't a thing at all and so can't be a cause of anything. I want to argue that only (a) is even a candidate cause, that the overgeneration problem mostly arises from treating (b)-type absences as causes, and that whatever remains of the problem for (a)-type cases can be handled by contrastivity (causes are always causes-relative-to-a-contrast) rather than by norms.

Write me the proposal. It needs: a precise statement of the question (I'd like it sharper than "when do omissions cause?"), a clear explanation of why the obvious existing answers leave the question open, what I'd actually do across the three months (roughly month by month -- reading, then working out the (a)/(b) distinction carefully, then testing it against hard cases), what a positive result would look like, what would count as the project failing, and an honest note on scope risks. A couple of things I specifically want stress-tested in the proposal: whether my distinction actually avoids the normativity problem or just relocates it (being "in a position to do something" might itself be norm-laden), and whether contrastivity is doing real work or just renaming the puzzle.
