I'm a third-year PhD student in normative ethics and I've been circling the moral dilemmas literature for a paper that I keep not being able to start. Here's my problem. The debate over whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible has basically been frozen since the 80s, and I can't figure out what it's even about anymore.

So the impossibilists (Williams and co.) argue roughly: if you genuinely ought to do A and genuinely ought to do B, then by agglomeration you ought to do A and B; but in a dilemma you can't do both; and ought implies can; so at least one of the oughts wasn't genuine. The possibility people (Marcus, Walzer, McConnell, depending on the flavor) point at things like Sophie's Choice or wartime cases and say: look at the residue. You can do everything there was to do and still owe an apology, still have guilt that's rationally appropriate, still have a remainder that doesn't cancel out. If the ought were defeated, none of that would make sense. The impossibilists reply that the residue just shows the situation is tragic, or that guilt is fitting without being justified, or that one ought was a prima facie thing that got overridden. And then everyone repeats.

My current hypothesis, which I want tested not just accepted, is that the two sides stopped talking about the same thing decades ago. The impossibilists are asking a question about the consistency requirements of a deontic system -- what must be true of 'ought' for moral theory to be coherent and action-guiding. The possibilists are asking a phenomenological/descriptive question about moral life -- whether our practices of remorse, compensation, and remainder actually track undefeated obligations. Each side's arguments are mostly good against their own question and mostly whiff past the other. If that's right, "genuine" is doing all the work and the debate is at least partly verbal. But I might be wrong -- maybe there's a real substantive crux, like whether rational guilt is evidence of an obligation or just evidence of taking obligations seriously, and the whole thing hangs on that.

3. Also stress-test my hypothesis specifically. Where does it break? What would Williams say the phenomenology-driven side is missing, and vice versa, at the level where they could actually hear it?
