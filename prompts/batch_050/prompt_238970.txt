A few weeks ago I drove home along my usual route and realized, pulling into the driveway, that I had no recollection of the previous twenty minutes: no memory of the intersections, the traffic lights, the other cars. Yet I plainly had not been asleep at the wheel — I had stopped at red lights, signaled, braked for a cyclist. Something in me was registering the road, but whatever that registering was, it apparently was not accompanied by any awareness of registering it, or at least none that survived.

Around the same time I had what feels like the mirror-image experience. I was absorbed in a book while a clock ticked on the wall. My wife asked whether the ticking was bothering me, and the instant she asked, the ticking was suddenly there — loud, obvious, and, so it seemed to me, having been there all along. I want to say I was hearing it before she spoke, but was not aware that I was hearing it.

These two episodes have led me to a hypothesis I keep turning over: perhaps what makes a perception conscious is not any property of the perception itself, but the presence of a further state of mind directed at it — a kind of noticing, or representing, of one's own perceiving. On this picture, my perceptions of the road never became conscious because no such second state accompanied them, and the clock-tick became conscious the moment my mind turned toward it. Consciousness, on this view, is not something a mental state has on its own but something conferred on it when the mind takes notice of itself.

My question is whether this hypothesis has an established name and an associated literature. I assume it must — it seems too natural an idea to be original — but I do not know what it is called, and I have had trouble even searching for it because I lack the vocabulary. If it is an established position, I would like to know who holds it, what its principal rivals are, and how it differs from two ideas I can already tell are nearby without being identical: first, the claim that attention is what renders things conscious (attending to one's own states does not seem to be the same thing as what I am describing, though I may be wrong); second, ordinary introspection, which strikes me as something one does deliberately, whereas the clock-tick became conscious entirely unbidden.

I would also like the standard objections. One I can anticipate myself: if being represented by a further mental state is what confers consciousness, would my thinking about a rock thereby make the rock conscious? Presumably the view restricts itself to represented items that are themselves mental states, but then I worry about the reverse case — whether the second state could occur without the first, a noticing of a perception that never took place, and what, if anything, that would be like for the subject.

One request concerning how you answer. If the experiences I have described do not support the distinction I am drawing — for instance, if I have confused consciousness with memory, or with the capacity to report — I would rather be told that plainly than have my framing politely accommodated. Likewise, if the hypothesis as I have stated it is ambiguous between several established positions, please distinguish them rather than forcing a single label onto it, and indicate which comes closest to what I actually described.
