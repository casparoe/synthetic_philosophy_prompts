You are a philosopher of probability who specializes in running fair-minded adversarial collaborations. Before drafting, take a moment to brainstorm the strongest three considerations on each side of the dispute described below, and then produce the requested document with care. The quality of your output will be judged on whether each party would actually sign the formulations you attribute to them.

The setting. Two colleagues at an interdisciplinary institute for the study of prediction have argued for a year and must now write a joint statement for their grant renewal:

- Dr. A, the deferentialist, defends a version of the Principal Principle: an ideally rational agent whose total relevant evidence about some future event consists in knowing that its objective chance is x should adopt credence x in it. She insists this is a substantive norm that tethers rational belief to a mind-independent magnitude in the world, not a definition or a tautology.

- Dr. B, the constructivist, holds that objective chance is nothing over and above an idealization of rational credence. On his view the Principal Principle does not connect credence to an independent quantity but covertly fixes what "chance" means -- so in a deterministic universe there is no chance for credence to defer to, only stable frequencies, robust patterns, and well-calibrated models.

One more fact about their institute matters to the dispute. The institute owns a sealed device called the Oracle: before any trial the researchers propose, it prints a number between 0 and 1, and across decades of trials, events to which it assigned a number near p have occurred with frequency near p -- better calibrated than any human expert or rival model, for coins, isotope decays, and weather alike. Nobody knows how it works, and it is disputed whether its internal mechanism is deterministic. Both colleagues agree the Oracle is the single most important test case for their disagreement.

Draft the joint statement they should produce. It must contain, in this order:

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of at least six claims both can sign without reservation. These should include, among whatever else you find: a claim about why long-run frequency is neither necessary nor sufficient for chance; a claim about why the Principal Principle must be restricted to admissible evidence (consider what each would say about an agent who has seen the future, or who knows the exact initial conditions of a coin toss); and a claim about what the Oracle has already established and what it has not established. No agreed claim should be vacuous.

2. Disagreements table. The precise points on which they still differ, each formulated in two columns: Dr. A's own wording of her position, and Dr. B's own wording of his, such that each would accept the wording of their own side and acknowledge the other's as an accurate statement of an opposing view rather than a caricature. Include at least the following rows: whether objective chance can exist in a deterministic world; whether the Principal Principle constrains chance or constitutes it; and whether the norm has any content that could not be recovered from a suitable principle of calibration or indifference.

3. Cruxes and registered predictions. For each disagreement, identify the sub-question whose resolution would force at least one side to revise, name a case or argument both accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. At minimum: what each side would conclude about the Oracle if its mechanism were proved deterministic, and what each would conclude if it were proved irreducibly indeterministic; and what each side makes of the fact that rational agents defer to statistical-mechanical probabilities in a physical theory whose underlying dynamics may be deterministic. Predictions must be committal enough that one side could later be held to have lost.

4. Verbal-disagreement audit. State explicitly which part, if any, of their dispute you judge to be merely verbal -- for instance, whether "chance" is being used in the same sense by both -- and justify the verdict in terms of what each side would actually concede under questioning.

Keep the document under 900 words, in a professional register, and do not resolve the dispute in either direction: your job is to make the disagreement exact, not to referee it.
