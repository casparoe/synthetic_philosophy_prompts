Bit of a delicate brief. I’m giving a six-minute speech at the opening of a hospital’s new secular reflection room, and the commitee has decided – possibly as a mischievous joke – to theme it around A. J. Ayer. The audience will include clinicians, bereaved families, philosophy students, hospital managers and one rather patient chaplain.

The speech should focus on the tension created by Ayer’s late near-death experience. He had spent much of his career treating religious and metaphysical claims with suspicion, especially where they seemed to make no empirically assessable difference. Yet after his heart stopped he reported a vivid experience which, by his own later admission, made him somewhat less confident that death must be the end. Still, he did not present it as proof of an afterlife and did not become a religious believer.

Write the speech in British English, at around 750–850 words. It must be genuinely suitable for the occasion rather than a mini-lecture with chairs scraping in the background. Build it around the distinction between an experience’s seeming revelatory to the person who has it and its functioning as evidence for everybody else. Also bring out the difference between conceding that personal survival is possible and assigning it a meaningful probability, and between some kind of continued experience and the survival of a person with memories, character and concerns.

The philosophy should shape what the speech can honestly offer grieving people. It should not dismiss spiritual interpretations as stupid, but neither should it turn Ayer’s report into false reassurance. I’d like one gentle joke about verification or empirical evidence, but nothing that makes Ayer sound like a heartless logic machine. End by explaining why a secular room for reflection can still make space for uncertainty, hope and mystery without pretending that uncertainty is itself evidence for what we hope.
