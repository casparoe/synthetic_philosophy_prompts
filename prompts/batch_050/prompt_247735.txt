I advise a national commission that recommends whether the government should issue official apologies for historical wrongs. Our past recommendations: we recommended apologizing for the wartime internment of an ethnic minority, where survivors were still alive, the state's role was direct and documented, and compensation accompanied the apology; we declined to recommend an apology for a nineteenth-century famine that government policy significantly worsened, on the grounds that causation was contested and no victims remained; we recommended apologizing for a program of forced adoptions administered by religious institutions under state license and inspection, where many survivors were living; we declined an apology for an early colonial conquest, though the injustice is undisputed, because the wronged population was absorbed generations ago and present-day claims are diffuse; and we recommended apologizing for non-consensual medical experiments on prisoners conducted by state physicians. A new case: a state-run residential school system that ended fifty years ago, where the last survivors have recently died, but the records are complete, the state's direct responsibility is uncontested, and identifiable descendant communities bear measurable ongoing harms. What should we recommend if we are to be consistent with our own record, which prior case is the closest analog, and if our precedents underdetermine the answer, what principle would we be committing ourselves to by recommending an apology versus declining one?
