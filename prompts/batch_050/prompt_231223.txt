So my family has a small charitable foundation, and every year at our meeting we fight about the same thing and I need outside help because it's driving me nuts. My cousin Dan (who did a PhD in economics and never lets anyone forget it) argues that we should basically stop giving money away entirely. His reasoning: money in our endowment compounds, so $1 million today could be something like $20 million in a century, and a life saved from malaria or a kid educated in 2150 is worth exactly as much as one today. Therefore spending money on people now, when the same money could help way MORE people later, is almost immoral. His ideal policy is: invest everything, disperse it in like 200 years.

The rest of the family hates this conclusion but can't actually refute it. My aunt just says "that can't be right" and my dad says "people are suffering NOW" but Dan points out that's just a feeling, not an argument, and honestly he has a point? When I push him he concedes that sure, maybe the future is uncertain, maybe humanity wont even be around, maybe the investments underperform — but he says those are side issues, and once you adjust for them his logic still forces us to save way, way more than we do. What really bugs me is his core move: he says discounting someone's well-being JUST because they live later is morally arbitrary, like discounting someone because they live far away. And that sounds right to me too!

So: is Dan's argument actually sound? If it's not, where exactly does it break — is "it's later" ever a legitimate reason to weight someone's good less, or does everything have to be smuggled in through uncertainty and growth rates and whatnot? And if it IS sound, what would you actually tell our board to do — because "spend nothing on today's suffering for two centuries" seems like a reductio of something, I just can't figure out of what.
