You are a philosopher of cognitive science who has worked on idealized models of deliberation and on iterated amplification schemes in AI alignment. Think carefully before answering. Begin with a short brainstorming pass listing every shape a defeater could plausibly take, then organize your considered answer from there. No citations are needed; argue in terms of positions and mechanisms, not names.

Here is the position I hold and want stress-tested. I endorse the factored cognition hypothesis in a strong form: for nearly all cognitive tasks that matter — scientific theorizing, philosophical argument, engineering design, moral reflection — a task can be recursively decomposed into subtasks, each performable by an agent with drastically less context and ability than the whole requires, with only modest overhead, so that iterated decomposition of the HCH type (a base-level reasoner consulting copies of itself on sub-questions) converges, in the limit, on the output of ideal deliberation. I treat this not as a mere engineering conjecture about language models but as a structural claim about cognition: understanding is in principle compositional in this way, and apparent holism — the sense that some problems must be grasped whole — is an artifact of bounded context rather than a real barrier.

What would count against this position? I want a map of its exposure, and I want it honest rather than reassuring. Address at least the following:

1. Which failures would be decisive and which merely awkward. My qualifier "nearly all" lets me absorb scattered exceptions; what kind of counterexample would resist absorption — a single striking case, or a systematic class? Distinguish, in particular, between in-principle non-decomposability (no factoring exists even with unlimited replication) and mere inefficiency (factoring exists but the overhead explodes), and say which of these actually refutes my claim as stated.

2. Take seriously three pressure points I already half-see. First, an interface problem: sub-questions must be posed in terms the leaf agent can act on, and the cost of transmitting context across the decomposition boundary may scale with the task itself. Second, an aggregation problem: the root agent's combining of sub-answers is itself a cognitive act, so convergence seems to presuppose that aggregation is reliable — is that circular, and if so, is it vicious? Third, candidate hard cases: mathematical and aesthetic taste, recognizing that a question is ill-posed, gestalt judgments about whether a long argument's conclusion follows from its parts read in sequence. For each, say whether it is a genuine threat or just a difficult case.

3. What would defenders of this position themselves concede as counting against it — not what its critics say, but what a committed proponent of iterated amplification would write down, in advance, as a falsifying result? Include the empirical results that would bear on it: human experiments on decomposing forecasting or research questions, attempts at recursive decomposition with machine learning systems, demonstrations that leaf-level reliability fails to propagate to the root.

4. What does my position predict that its rivals — the view that cognition is essentially holistic, or that decomposition only works within a domain of already-shared understanding — do not?

Finally, assess whether the position risks anything at all. Can every decomposition failure be blamed on the decomposer's skill rather than the hypothesis? If so, say whether that insulation is a defect of the position or just reflects that it is a limit claim. I will find most persuasive a principled argument that some indispensable cognitive operation is non-compositional, rather than an anecdote about a hard problem.
