Last week I listened to two friends, Mara and Tomas, argue for nearly an hour about whether a mutual acquaintance had done something wrong by taking a well-paid job as an accountant at a company whose main business is manufacturing components for weapons systems. I've reconstructed their exchange below as faithfully as I can, and I'd like a verdict.

Mara opened by saying the job was wrong even granting that his individual labor makes no difference to anything: the company would hire someone else, the same components would ship, the same harms would occur. Complicity, she said, is precisely the category for wrongs of participation that aren't wrongs of causation; if you join a wrongful enterprise and draw your salary from it, you're implicated in what it does, full stop.

Tomas replied that a wrong with no victim-made-worse-off is a wrong in name only. If his taking or refusing the job changes nothing for anyone affected, then condemning him is squeamishness dressed up as ethics -- a concern for keeping one's own hands clean rather than for anyone's actual welfare. Morality, he said, has to connect to consequences somewhere, or it becomes self-regarding.

Mara conceded the counterfactual point for the single case but pushed back on two fronts. First, she said, the "no difference" premise is less solid than it looks: industries respond to participation in aggregate and in lumps, so the expected effect of any one participant is positive even when no individual decision is the one that tips anything. Second, and more importantly, she argued he was wrong to think the only alternatives are causing harm or doing nothing wrong: there is a distinct wrong of lending one's labor and loyalty to a practice's normal functioning, and of profiting from a venture one would condemn if one weren't on its payroll. Integrity, she said, is about what you make yourself a part of, not about outcomes.

Tomas answered that both points prove too much. On the aggregate-effects argument, every purchase, tax payment, and utility bill participates in some aggregate with bad effects, so complicity becomes universal and therefore useless as criticism -- a charge that applies to everyone condemns no one. On the lending-and-profiting argument, he noted that his friend could take the job while openly and sincerely opposing the company's mission, donating a share of the salary to arms-control groups, and hoping the firm fails. If two people perform identical work with identical effects, but one inwardly endorses the mission and the other inwardly opposes it, then either Mara's view says the wrongness floats free of anything observable, which he found implausible, or it collapses into the consequentialism she claimed to be going beyond.

Mara's last move was to say that the charge of universality cuts the other way: perhaps we are all complicit in various harms, and the honest response is to take complicity seriously as a pervasive feature of modern life rather than to redefine it away because acknowledging it is uncomfortable.

Could you go through the exchange point by point and say who is right about what? In particular: whether the "no difference" premise defeats the charge of wrongdoing; whether the aggregate-effects reply actually rescues individual responsibility or merely relocates the problem; whether Tomas's universality objection succeeds against the participation-based account; whether his case of the inwardly opposed employee is a genuine dilemma for Mara or can be answered; and whether Mara's final concession saves her position or quietly abandons it. Finally, is there a distinction or consideration both of them are missing that would change how the original question should be answered?
