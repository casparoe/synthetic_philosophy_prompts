I co-moderate a small reading group on political disagreement, and next week it's my turn to present what I've been calling my personal code of engagement -- the working principles I try to hold myself to when arguing with people I disagree with politically. Before I present it, I'd like it stress-tested for internal consistency, because I have a nagging feeling I'm smuggling in an asymmetry somewhere but I can't locate it. Here are the five commitments, as I've written them down:

(1) When I explain why ordinary people on the other side hold their views, I should reach for their experiences, incentives, and values rather than their intelligence or character -- the stupid-and-malign explanation is almost never the right one for mass belief.

(2) People's stated reasons for their political positions are mostly post-hoc rationalizations: the positions come first, installed by identity and social alignment, and the reasoning is constructed afterward to defend them. I take the social psychology on motivated reasoning to support this fairly strongly.

(3) Public political argument -- essays, debates, conversations across the divide -- is nonetheless worth doing, because over time exposure to better arguments genuinely moves what people believe, which is why I bother writing and arguing at all.

(4) My own political beliefs are mostly not like the ones described in (2): I hold mine because of the arguments, and I can point to a few issues where I've changed my mind in response to evidence, which I take as evidence that I'm tracking truth rather than identity.

(5) Accusations of bad faith in public discourse are almost always themselves bad-faith moves -- status plays dressed up as critique -- and should be met with deep suspicion, not engagement.

Please approach this as a philosopher of social epistemology auditing a client's position set, and work carefully before concluding. I'd suggest starting by brainstorming, for each pair of commitments, both the strongest case that they're in tension and the strongest available reconciliation, and only then issuing verdicts -- don't just flag everything that looks superficially awkward. For each genuine tension, tell me how severe it is, what exactly would have to give, and what the cheapest revision that resolves it would cost me. For each merely apparent tension, show me the reconciliation that dissolves it. Note that I don't think (1) and (2) conflict, since rationalization is a claim about process rather than character, but feel free to tell me if I'm wrong about that. I'd also like your view on whether (2) puts pressure on my use of "bad faith" in (5) -- if stated reasons are generally post-hoc anyway, I'm no longer sure what the good-faith/bad-faith distinction even tracks. End with a ranking of the tensions from most to least serious, and identify the single revision that would do the most to make the set coherent -- but don't assume you know which commitment I'm most attached to, because I'm honestly not sure myself.
