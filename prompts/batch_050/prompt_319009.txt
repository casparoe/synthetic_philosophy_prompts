I'm prepping a grad seminar on supererogation and I need a test case built to order for a specific live question: does an act have to cost the agent something (effort, money, risk, forgone goods) to count as supererogatory, or can a completely costless act still be beyond the call of duty? Different camps seem to disagree on this but the literature rarely isolates the variable cleanly.

So: build me a pair of scenarios that are identical in every morally relevant respect except cost to the agent. Same beneficiary, same benefit conferred, same relationship between the parties, same menu of options available. In version A the agent pays a real but modest cost to do the good thing; in version B the agent does the same good at literally zero cost to themselves (e.g. the benefit is a byproduct of something they'd do anyway, or a trivial mechanism makes it free). Constraints: both scenarios must be mundane and realistic, one short paragraph each, no organ donation, no rescuing drowning children, no war heroes. And in both versions the act must clearly exceed what duty requires -- don't leave it ambiguous whether the act was merely obligatory, because that would wreck the comparison.

After the pair, show me the work: say which of the standard ways of drawing the line (cost/sacrifice-based accounts, praiseworthiness-based accounts, and bare beyond-duty accounts) count the costless version as supererogatory and which dont, and why in each case. Then tell me which feature of your pair is doing all the work, what happens to the comparison if you strip that feature out, and the best reply available to someone who insists costless acts can't be supererogatory -- i.e. don't just stipulate the verdicts, generate them.

Format-wise: give me exactly six numbered items, no intro or conclusion -- item 1 scenario A, item 2 scenario B, items 3-5 the verdict each of the three accounts gives with its reasoning, item 6 the load-bearing feature plus the objector's best reply. A few sentences per item is fine.
