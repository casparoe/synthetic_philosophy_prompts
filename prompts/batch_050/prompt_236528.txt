I am a high school student and I write the philosophy column for our school magazine, which runs a series of imaginary interviews with deceased philosophers. My next subject is A. J. Ayer, and I want the whole piece to turn on one episode: in 1988, a year before he died, Ayer choked on a piece of smoked salmon, was clinically dead for a few minutes, and later wrote about what he had experienced, including a bright red light and a sense that the order of space and time had broken down and that it somehow fell to him to set it right. In a later reflection he admitted the episode had slightly weakened his confidence that death is the end of a person, while adding that he continued to hope it was. This sits uneasily with the verificationism of Language, Truth and Logic, on which a claim counts as meaningful only if some experience could in principle settle it. Please draft seven or eight interview questions that exploit this tension instead of letting him retell the story: press him on whether an experience undergone during clinical death is evidence about what happens after death rather than evidence about a failing brain; on who could ever verify the claim that someone survives death, since on his own criterion the one observation that would settle it is available only to the person it happens to; on whether the episode changed his beliefs or merely his feelings, and whether his philosophy even permits that distinction; and on why he hoped for extinction rather than fearing it. For each question, anticipate the reply he would most likely give and provide a follow-up keyed to it. After the questions, say which of them rests on the shakiest reading of Ayer and what consideration would lead you to redraft it. Write the whole piece as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold text, with each question and its follow-up made clear within the flow of the paragraphs.
