While reading for a philosophy of language seminar, I got into a disagreement with a classmate about what exactly is wrong with Moorean sentences, and I realized I couldn't articulate it cleanly myself. The standard example is a sentence of the form "It is raining, but I don't believe that it is." My classmate's first pass was that the sentence is simply a contradiction, but of course it isn't: it describes a perfectly possible state of affairs, and one that presumably obtains all the time. So the puzzle is why a sentence that could be true nonetheless seems impossible to assert sincerely, or even to believe, at least in its first-person present-tense form.

Could you give a careful explanation of where the defect is supposed to lie, engaging with the following complications? First, the contrast between the omissive version (p, and I don't believe that p) and the commissive version (p, and I believe that not-p): the latter seems straightforwardly irrational in a way the former arguably isn't, since my evidence can genuinely support a belief that p while my introspective evidence supports that I lack that belief — yet both versions strike us as unassertable. Does one explanation cover both cases, or do we need two? Second, the assertion/supposition asymmetry: "Suppose it is raining and I don't believe it" is not only coherent but is a step we routinely take in reductio arguments and conditional proofs, so whatever is wrong with Moorean sentences seems specific to assertion (or outright belief) rather than to the content as such. Third, the first-person present-tense specificity: "It was raining, but I didn't believe it" and "It is raining, but she doesn't believe it" are completely unremarkable, so the explanation needs to locate the problem in the speaker's current self-ascription rather than anywhere in the propositional content.

Finally, I'd like the explanation to adjudicate between two broad styles of account that I've seen gesturing past each other. On one, the paradox is fundamentally about the norms or pragmatics of assertion — something like a practical contradiction between what is said and what the act of saying it manifests or commits the speaker to. On the other, the paradox is fundamentally doxastic: one cannot rationally believe a Moorean sentence, and the assertoric oddity is merely downstream of that. Is one of these accounts more fundamental than the other, and does either survive the observation that you could, in principle, have excellent evidence that a Moorean sentence is true of you right now — say, a trusted informant telling you that it's raining outside while a reliable belief-scanner reports your current credences?
