Here's something that's been bugging me for a while. Pretty much every legal system that bans torture also refuses to admit confessions obtained under torture or other serious coercion, and one of the stock reasons given is that such confessions are unreliable -- if you hurt someone enough, they'll say whatever makes it stop, so you can't trust a word of it. But the famous ticking time bomb scenario, which is supposed to be the strongest case for permitting torture, seems to assume exactly the opposite: that torture is a dependable enough way of extracting true information that we'd be crazy not to use it when a bomb's about to go off. So my question: what picture of testimony under duress does each of these positions presuppose? Can you coherently hold both at once -- say, by arguing that reliability depends on whether the interrogator can independently check the answer, or on what the victim gains by lying versus telling the truth? Or does taking the unreliability rationale seriously commit you to thinking the ticking bomb case isn't just empirically far-fetched but built on a confused assumption? And if you think the real reason we exclude coerced confessions is dignitary rather than epistemic -- we refuse them because of how they were got, not because they're probably false -- does the tension disappear, or does it just relocate somewhere else?
