I am a novelist, currently revising a campus novel whose central character is a philosopher of mind teaching at a small liberal-arts college. One chapter turns on a quiet but consequential scene: she sits at her kitchen table late at night grading a stack of take-home exam answers, and her marginal comments — precise, a little severe, occasionally generous — do a great deal of the work of establishing who she is. The scene will only work if the grading itself is authentic: the kind of assessment a genuinely careful philosopher would write, not the vague "good job, but..." commentary I could invent on my own. I am not a philosopher, and I need your help producing it.

Here is the exam question my character set her students:

"Both Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett deny that dreams are experiences we undergo while asleep, but they arrive at this shared-sounding conclusion by strikingly different routes. Set out each thinker's position and the reasoning behind it, making clear where the two diverge, and then judge whether Dennett's 'cassette theory' of dream reports is vulnerable to the objections Malcolm raises against treating dreams as experiences. Defend your verdict."

And here is the student's answer, which she has just read:

"Malcolm's argument begins from the observation that the concept of a dream is inseparable from the dreamer's waking report. When I say that I dreamt I was climbing a staircase, the only thing there could ever be to go on is the report I give on waking; there is no independent way to check the report against the dream itself, since no one, not even the dreamer, can revisit what happened during sleep. Because our dream reports rest on unreliable memory, Malcolm concludes, we should stop thinking of dreams as experiences at all — there is nothing the report could be accurate or inaccurate about. He makes a parallel point about duration: since there is no way to time a dream against anything outside it, Malcolm concludes that dreams are strictly speaking instantaneous, and that talk of a dream 'lasting' several minutes is empty. He also argues that no one can, while genuinely asleep, judge that they are dreaming, since making a judgment presupposes the wakeful use of concepts; a sentence uttered in sleep could not express a genuine thought.

Dennett reaches a similar destination by a different road. On his cassette theory, dream narratives are pre-composed material, inserted into memory at the moment of waking like cassettes slotted into a player, so that on waking we seem to remember an experience that in fact never occurred — nothing was presented to consciousness during sleep at all. The theory is motivated by cases like dreams that build elaborately toward a climax triggered by an external noise, where the whole narrative appears to have been prepared in an instant. Because the cassette theory explains the production of dream reports without positing any private sleep experience, it escapes Malcolm's objections entirely: Malcolm's worry was about unverifiable inner events, and once the inner event is removed by a concrete mechanism, his arguments have nothing left to gain a grip on. So although Malcolm and Dennett agree that dreams are not experiences, Dennett's account is the stronger one, since it replaces Malcolm's purely negative conceptual point with a positive explanation."

What I need from you is the grading itself, in three parts:

First, the grade she writes at the top of the page. Use whatever scheme you judge realistic — a letter grade or a number out of 100 — but choose one and commit to a single definite grade, not a range or a "depending on how strict she is." She is a rigorous but fair grader, and the scene needs a number she would actually write down.

Second, the marginal comments, keyed point by point to specific claims in the student's answer. I want her to credit what is genuinely right — and much of it is right — but also to catch what a hasty grader would let slide. In particular, if there are places where the student misstates a thinker's view in a subtle way, draws an inference that does not follow even though it sounds plausible, or attributes a contested reading as settled fact, I want those flagged with an explanation of exactly what is wrong, since those flags are what make her look sharp in the scene.

Third, a short end comment of two or three sentences, the summary judgment she would leave below the grade.

One request about how you answer: please be committed throughout. Do not hedge between two possible grades or offer alternative readings of the student's answer for me to choose among. Where a point is genuinely contested among interpreters, say so in a single clause, but still come down on a definite assessment — my character would, and indecision on the page would read as my indecision rather than hers.
