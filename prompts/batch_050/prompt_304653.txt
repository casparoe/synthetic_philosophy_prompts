Next month I'm presenting a paper at a small workshop on formal epistemology, and I want to be fully prepared for the question period. My thesis is that Dutch book arguments, in their classic sure-loss form, cannot justify probabilism as a norm of rationality, because the threat of sure loss only has normative bite for an agent who values money identically in every state of the world, which no actual reasoner does and no ideal reasoner is required to; but that the same formal machinery can be salvaged by reconceiving it as a consistency proof -- the betting setup is scaffolding that shows incoherent credences are formally defective, and once the proof is complete the money should be kicked away like a ladder. I further argue that this reconstruction succeeds for the synchronic probability axioms but fails for diachronic conditionalization, because the diachronic argument must smuggle in a substantive norm about how an agent ought to relate her earlier and later selves, and that norm does not follow from consistency alone. My one novel wrinkle: I press the point that a Dutch book is only executable by a bettor who knows the agent's credences, so the classic argument covertly assumes that rational belief is publicly inspectable, and I use this to argue that the sure-loss framing is even weaker than its defenders admit.

The audience: roughly fifteen people. Three or four are committed accuracy-firsters who believe dominance arguments have superseded Dutch book arguments entirely and may think my paper resuscitates a dead debate; two are senior defenders of the traditional pragmatic reading who have written on representation theorems and will know the betting interpretation of utility theory inside out; there is a decision theorist who is skeptical of the whole project of grounding epistemic norms in practical rationality and may attack from a direction I am not facing; and the rest are graduate students and generalists who will follow the strongest objections.

Please prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections I will actually get from this audience, in roughly the order they will come, each phrased the way the person raising it would phrase it -- the accuracy-firster's framing should sound different from the pragmatist's. For each objection, give me what I should concede up front, what I should hold, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and a note on where that reply is soft. Order the objections by how much damage they would do to the paper if I handle them badly. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks -- I suspect it concerns whether my consistency reconstruction has silently reintroduced a practical norm through the back door -- and tell me honestly whether I have an answer to it.
