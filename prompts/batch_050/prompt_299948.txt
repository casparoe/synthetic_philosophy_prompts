Here's an argument one of my grad students brought to office hours, built off Glaucon's speech in Republic II, and it seems to prove way too much. First the passage (Jowett translation, 359a), where Glaucon is explaining what people say about the origin of justice: people who have both done and suffered injustice "determine that it is for their profit to make a compact with one another neither to commit nor to suffer injustice; and that this is the beginning of legislation and covenants between men... and that this is the genesis and essential nature of justice—a compromise between the best, which is to do wrong with impunity, and the worst, which is to be wronged and be impotent to get one's revenge."

The argument:

(1) Glaucon is right about this much: justice as a practice is a compromise. Each of us would prefer to wrong others with impunity, and accepts restraints only as insurance against being wronged ourselves.

(2) What each party accepts only as a compromise is valued by none of them for its own sake -- a compromise is by definition your second choice, the cost you pay to avoid something worse.

(3) So no one actually values justice for its own sake; it's valued purely as a cost-avoidance device.

(4) A trait that's exercised only to avoid costs isn't a virtue -- someone who tells the truth purely to avoid penalites is prudent, not honest. Virtues have to be exercised for their own sake or they're not virtues at all.

(5) So justice is not a virtue. There's just prudence in justice's clothing.

(6) But the exact same reasoning covers every costly prosocial trait: generosity, loyalty, courage in defending others, gratitude. Each costs us, each is the sort of thing we'd happily skip if impunity guaranteed the benefits anyway, each plausibly originates in reciprocal arrangements.

(7) So there are no virtues whatsoever, and no real difference between a genuinely good person and a careful calculator who's good at insurance.

That last step is where my student grins, because obviously something's gone off the rails -- but every premise looks defensible and I can't cleanly locate the derailment. If forced to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and (4) than accept (7). So: which step do you reject? Does the passage's own language -- especially "a compromise" and that "they tell us" framing -- actually support (2), or is something being smuggled from the origin story of the practice into the motives of the people in it? And tell me what rejecting your chosen step costs, including what you'd have to say about the ring of Gyges case, where the compromise story looks most persuasive.
