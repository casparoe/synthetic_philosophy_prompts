For years I've noticed an odd pattern in how people relate to their own future opinions, and I'd like to know whether there is an established concept for it. Two examples. First: a friend of mine, sober, will freely tell you that after three glasses of wine he always concludes he's fine to drive home. He knows now, beyond doubt, that his later self will hold that view, and he thinks the view is false — yet when the evening comes he invariably adopts it. Second: I catch myself doing something similar with work estimates. On Monday I can predict that by Thursday, facing a deadline, I'll talk myself into believing a sloppy report is good enough, and I'm right almost every time. What strikes me is the contrast with ordinary learning: if I expect tomorrow's evidence to change my mind about something, it seems reasonable to shift my confidence today in anticipation. But in these cases no new evidence arrives — I simply know my judgment will drift — and moving now toward the opinion I expect to hold later seems plainly irrational, even though the prediction is just as reliable. Is there a name for this pattern, or for the line between cases where anticipating your future beliefs rationalizes changing your mind now and cases where it doesn't? I'm not after a bare label: if something close exists, explain how the phenomenon I've described differs from it and which side of its boundaries my examples fall on; and if nothing quite fits, tell me what you would call it and how you would state the principle separating legitimate anticipation from mere predicted drift.
