A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and we parted without resolution. She is thoroughly fluent in his three modes of relating to the past — the monumental, which seeks exemplars worthy of imitation; the antiquarian, which preserves and venerates what is one's own; and the critical, which drags the past before a tribunal and condemns it — and she judges real practices by whether they serve or stunt what Nietzsche calls life. What struck me, once I wrote down the verdicts she gave over the course of the evening, is that I cannot tell what rule she is actually running. Here are the five cases she pronounced on, as faithfully as I can reconstruct them:

1. A young republic, recently independent, commissions heroic statues of its founders and a school curriculum centered on their courage, knowingly smoothing over their compromises and cruelties. She called this life-serving: monumental history doing exactly its proper work for a people who must act.

2. An old mercantile family preserves its ancestral house untouched, catalogues every letter, and lets its present business slowly decay because every decision is referred to precedent. She called this life-stunting: antiquarian piety that no longer conserves a living thing but mummifies a dead one.

3. A country establishes a commission that exposes the crimes of a still-revered founding generation, at real cost to social peace. She called this life-serving: critical history breaking a reverence that had become a yoke.

4. A scholar spends a decade demolishing, with full archival rigor, a beloved legend about a medieval saint that no one now invokes to justify anything. She called this mildly life-stunting: criticism with no living target, truth purchased at the price of a harmless piety.

5. A protest movement becomes so saturated in the heroic deeds of its martyrs that its members prefer symbolic reenactment to any achievable present demand. She called this life-stunting, despite being monumental history: the right mode in a fatal dose.

Our argument ended over a case she refused to rule on, and this is the case I want your help with. A mid-career novelist, acclaimed and then stagnant, spends three years immersed in the diaries and letters of a forgotten minor novelist of the 1880s — not as research for any book, not to celebrate and not to debunk; she says only that she wants this person to be remembered. At the end of it she writes her best novel, whose connection to the archive no reader could detect. Life-serving or life-stunting, by my friend's lights?

Please structure your answer in the following sections, in this order:

Bottom line — two or three sentences: your predicted verdict on the novelist, and the rule you take my friend to be running.

The candidate rules (~250 words) — state two or three distinct rules, each fully consistent with all five verdicts above, each precise enough to yield predictions. Note briefly if some tempting rule is excluded by the track record.

What each rule predicts (~200 words) — apply each candidate rule to the novelist's case. At least two of your rules should diverge here; if none do, find better rules. Flag any rule that delivers only an equivocal verdict on this case and say why.

Your best guess (~150 words) — which verdict my friend would actually give, and why you favor that reading of her over the alternatives, drawing on the texture of her five verdicts rather than on what Nietzsche himself would say.

The discriminating case (~100 words) — describe one further case, realistically, such that the two leading candidate rules predict opposite verdicts on it; explain why its outcome would settle which rule she is running.
