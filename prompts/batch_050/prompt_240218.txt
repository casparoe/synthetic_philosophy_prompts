Construct a thought experiment at the intersection of criminal law and the free-will debate, engineered to test a specific hypothesis: that rejecting desert does not necessarily make a system of punishment gentler, because desert functions not only as a warrant for state coercion but also as a ceiling on it. Build a case that forces this hypothesis into the open.

Stipulate the two positions as follows, and treat these stipulations as fixed for the exercise. Dennett: some agents are morally responsible in a sense that makes it literally true that they deserve punishment; desert is grounded in an agent's competence, self-control, and authorship of the act rather than in any metaphysically exotic freedom, and its point is partly forward-looking — but because desert tracks what the agent actually did and chose, it fixes both a floor and a ceiling, so punishment beyond what is deserved is an injustice to the offender, whatever its protective benefits. Caruso: no one deserves punishment in this basic sense; the state may detain offenders only on the analogy of quarantining carriers of a dangerous disease — dangerousness is the sole legitimate ground of incapacitation, detention must be no more restrictive than safety requires, must aim at rehabilitation and reintegration, and carries no implication that the detainee deserves to suffer; proportionality-to-desert limits are a retributivist relic.

Constraints on the scenario:

1. Two characters, presented in at most two short paragraphs, in a present-day setting a sentencing judge, forensic psychiatrist, or parole board member could actually face. No science fiction: no perfect predictors, no neuroimaging certainty about future violence.
2. Character A has culpably committed a serious violent crime and has now served the entire sentence that any proportionality-respecting, desert-based view would assign. He remains genuinely dangerous: give him a concrete, realistic basis for this (the kind of actuarial instrument or clinical evidence actually used, with a stated error rate), and make the dangerousness real rather than manufactured by the state.
3. Character B has repeatedly done grave harm but is wholly non-responsible for it, through a mechanism you specify — for example, violence occurring only during episodes of a documented neurological or psychiatric disorder. He, too, remains dangerous.
4. Do not state or hint at what either theory concludes about either man; the verdicts must be derived afterward, not smuggled into the setup. And do not rig the reader's sympathies: no scrupulously redemptive post-prison record for A, no sinister demeanor for B.

After the scenario:

(a) Derive each theory's verdict on each character from the stipulated principles alone. The intended result is an inversion of the standard expectation that the desert-skeptic's system is the gentler one: for A, Caruso's model apparently mandates continued, potentially indefinite detention where Dennett's view forbids it; for B, both theories confine, but examine whether each can even mark B's confinement as undeserved, and what is lost theoretically if it cannot. Flag any step where the derivation depends on a contested reading of a position.
(b) Identify the load-bearing feature of the case — the substitution of dangerousness for desert as the sole ground of detention, with the consequent loss of a proportionality ceiling — and show what happens if it is removed. If Caruso concedes a ceiling on detention for those who have paid their debt, does the model collapse back into de facto desert? If Dennett drops the ceiling, does his view lose its advertised advantage?
(c) Take seriously the Caruso rejoinder that manner matters more than duration: his detainees are held in the least restrictive, rehabilitative conditions, and indefinite-but-humane protective custody is not punishment. Assess this honestly, including the asymmetry that desert is assessable retrospectively whereas dangerousness is prospective and probabilistic — the error rate stated in your scenario should do work here, since a desert ceiling is a check against state overreach that the quarantine model lacks.
(d) Press Dennett from the other side: his justification of punishment is partly forward-looking, so what prevents him from incapacitating A anyway on protective grounds, collapsing the inversion into a mere difference of rhetoric? Say what resources each stipulated position has to resist that collapse.
(e) Conclude: which side, if either, does the case ultimately cut against, and what is that side's best repair? If your honest verdict is that the two systems converge in practice and diverge only in what they say to and about the people they confine, say so — and explain whether that convergence is itself a discovery worth having.
