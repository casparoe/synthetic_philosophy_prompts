The homework question was: "In the criminal law of most jurisdictions, some offences do not require proof of mens rea as to every element. Define strict liability, and distinguish it from both absolute liability and negligence-based liability. Then consider the following hypothetical: under a (fictional) Clean Waters Act, a factory owner is convicted of 'causing polluted matter to enter a stream' even though the pollution resulted from vandals breaking into the plant at night, and even though the owner had taken every precaution a reasonable person would take. Is imposing criminal liability in such a case defensible? In your answer, address (a) the presumption that penal statutes are to be read as requiring mens rea unless the legislature clearly says otherwise, (b) the rationale commonly offered for so-called regulatory or public welfare offences, and (c) the objection that criminal conviction expresses a stigma that is unjust where the defendant was not at fault."

The student's answer: "A strict liability offence is one where the prosecution need not prove mens rea as to at least one element of the offence, typically a circumstance element, so that the defendant may be convicted on proof of the prohibited conduct alone. Absolute liability is a still harsher variant under which not even a due diligence defence is available, whereas strict liability offences generally still permit the defendant to show that he was not negligent. Negligence liability, however, does not really differ from strict liability in kind, only in degree: the negligent defendant did not choose to be careless, so neither doctrine requires a 'guilty mind' in any meaningful sense. Common-law courts apply a presumption that the legislature intends mens rea to be required, particularly where an offence is serious or carries genuine stigma, and only clear statutory language will displace it. For regulatory or 'public welfare' offences — traffic rules, food safety, pollution control — courts are readier to find strict liability, on the grounds that such offences are not truly criminal, that they aim at deterrence and administrative efficiency rather than retribution, and that the penalties are usually light. As defenders of the doctrine such as H. L. A. Hart have argued, strict liability in these contexts is an efficient and on the whole fair device for encouraging high standards of care among those who voluntarily enter regulated activities. Applying this to the hypothetical: the conviction is defensible, since the Clean Waters Act is a paradigmatic regulatory statute and the fine will presumably be modest. The stigma objection, according to which it is unjust to condemn someone who was not at fault, has force mainly for offences carrying genuine moral opprobrium; a small fine for a pollution offence condemns no one. Strict liability is therefore acceptable for regulatory offences but not for stigmatising crimes."
