I am a novelist at work on a book whose moral center is the treatment of insects. In it, one character ruins every dinner party with a single argument: even if the probability that an insect can suffer is very small, the number of insects is so vast that the expected suffering outweighs nearly every other moral claim on us — and so we owe insects more than we owe, say, the homeless of our own city. For the plot to work, I have had to take this scale argument seriously myself, as the simple product of numbers, probability of sentience, and intensity of possible suffering, and my character treats anyone who resists it as merely squeamish.

Tell me what would count against this argument. I do not mean the shrug that we cannot know whether insects feel anything — my character has that answered. I want concrete defeaters. Would it be fatal if the probability estimate turned out to be an artifact of which theory of consciousness one started with, so the argument smuggles its conclusion in through that choice? Does it matter if insect behavior proves fully explicable without felt experience, or does the argument only need a nonzero probability? Does it collapse if one refuses expected-value reasoning under deep uncertainty, or if the sufferings compared are of incommensurable kinds? Which of these would a defender of the argument concede as genuine defeats, and which would they absorb with qualifications? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me whether the argument as stated risks anything at all, or has been insulated to the point where nothing could hang on it. At most 400 words.
