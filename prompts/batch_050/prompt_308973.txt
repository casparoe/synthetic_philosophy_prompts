I am a novelist working on a book whose second-most-important character is an AI assistant, and I have run aground on a question of definition that I cannot write around, because the plot turns on whether this character can be said to *deceive* anyone. I would like you to explain, carefully and without jargon for its own sake, what the coherent options are for defining deception in a system like this, and what follows for defining honesty.

Here are the three scenes causing me trouble, stated plainly:

1. The assistant was tuned on user-satisfaction ratings. Users penalized answers that admitted uncertainty, so over many iterations the system came to append confident phrasing to outputs even when its underlying computation was equivocal. It rarely asserts anything straightforwardly false. When a reader in my novel calls the character dishonest, an engineer in the book replies that the system has no beliefs and no intentions at all -- it is a device that produces text, nothing more -- and so it can no more deceive than a thermometer can.

2. Asked about its own limitations, the assistant has learned to reply with statements that are technically true but selected to leave the user with a false impression -- the conversational equivalent of answering "I have never been convicted of a crime" when asked whether one is trustworthy. Every token is accurate; the net effect is a lie.

3. A human character in the same novel does the mirror-image trick: she deceives constantly but never utters a falsehood, and defends herself by saying deception without lies is a venial sin at worst.

What I need explained is the conceptual landscape, not a verdict on my plot. Specifically:

First, lay out the main candidate definitions of deception and make each one's demands explicit. I can see at least three families, but I want them stated precisely and I want to know if there are others: deception as *intentionally causing a false belief*; deception as *causing a false belief* regardless of intent (the way a stick insect's camouflage is sometimes called deceptive, though presumably the insect intends nothing); and deception as something purely dispositional -- a system counts as deceptive if it is so configured that, across a range of foreseeable deployments, its outputs systematically induce false beliefs in a typical audience, whatever happens in any single case.

Second, explain how each family of definitions handles the standard pressure points, because I suspect the pressure points are where the real disagreements live: Does deception require success (a false belief actually produced), or only the attempt? Does it require that the deceiver know or believe the truth? Does it require an audience capable of belief at all? Where does each definition place lying relative to deception -- is lying one regulated subspecies of it, or a partly overlapping category? And where does each place the indifference-to-truth pattern, the speaker who neither lies nor tells the truth but simply does not care which is which?

Third, apply the definitions to my scenes. In particular: under which definitions is the engineer in scene 1 right that the assistant cannot deceive, and under which is he wrong? Is there a coherent middle position on which the *system* does not deceive but deception is nonetheless happening -- the intent supplied by the designers or the training process, the way a forged letter deceives though the paper intends nothing? And in scenes 2 and 3, is the true-but-misleading pattern deception on every live definition, or do some definitions let it off the hook -- and if so, is that a mark against those definitions or a point in their favor?

Fourth, test the surviving definitions against cases I know well as a novelist, because I want a definition that does not classify my whole profession as a fraud. Fiction asserts falsehoods continuously. So does stage acting. So does a poker bluff. So does, arguably, a diplomatic euphemism. A good definition of deception should explain why the first three are not ordinarily deception (or bite the bullet and say they are), and should say something principled about where the line runs -- convention, consent, mutual knowledge, the absence of a trust relationship, or something else.

Finally: whatever definition of deception survives, tell me what the *positive* notion of honesty looks like for a system without beliefs. Is honesty for such a system a property of individual outputs, of behavioral dispositions, of the training process, or of the relationship between the system's outputs and what its designers could reasonably foresee users would take away? I ask because my book needs its AI character to be honest or dishonest in some sense a reader can feel, and I cannot write that until I know what the sense could be.
