I'm a PhD student in cognitive science, and my lab runs a little internal prediction market where we bet on whether upcoming experiments (ours and other people's) are going to replicate. Small stakes, mostly for fun and callibration.

Here's the problem. One of the postdocs, when asked to price contracts, consistently does something like this: for an experiment she says she genuinely has no read on, she'll pay up to 60 cents for a contract that pays $1 if it replicates, and she'll also pay up to 60 cents for a contract that pays $1 if it doesn't. Another grad student in the lab noticed this and pointed out that if he sells her both contracts at those prices, he collects $1.20 and pays out exactly $1 no matter what happens, so he pockets 20 cents for certain. He now wants to actually do this to her, every round, as often as she'll let him.

The postdoc's response is basically: fine, she knows she's overpaying by 20 cents, and she's okay with it. She says that when she truly doesn't know, she'd rather pay a small premium than commit to a lean either way, and that the 20 cents works like an insurance premium — nobody calls you irrational for buying insurance even though the insurance company profits from you on average. She also points out that nobody's forcing her to buy both contracts at the same moment, so it's not like she ever literally hands over $1.20 in exchange for a guaranteed $1. And she claims the arbitrage guy isn't exposing some defect in her beliefs, he's just charging her for a preference he doesn't happen to share.

I'm the one who manages the pool, so I have to actually decide something here: do I let the arbitrage guy keep milking her, do I impose a rule that everyone's prices for "replicates" and "doesn't replicate" have to sum to exactly $1, or do I just leave it alone?

What I mostly want to know is who's right and why. Is there something genuinely irrational about what she's doing, or does her insurance-premium defense hold up? Does it matter that in practice she'd never buy both contracts simultaneously? And does the "you can be reliably exploited" argument even go through if part of what she's buying is the option to not commit to a number — i.e., if the bet and the money aren't worth the same to her? If I force her prices to sum to $1, am I actually making her more rational, or just making her hide the same uncertainty behind a fake precise guess? Some practical advice on the pool would be nice too, but I care more about the underlying question of whether she's doing anything wrong at all.

Please answer in American English.
