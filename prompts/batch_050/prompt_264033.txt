I’ve been a reader of rationalist blogs and forums for years, so I’ve absorbed the usual package — credences as probabilities, calibration, prediction markets, the sequences — largely on trust. Recently, in the course notes a friend shared from a philosophy of probability seminar he’s auditing, I found a paragraph summarizing the Dutch book argument for probabilism, and I’d like an accuracy audit before I let it update my own understanding. The notes read as follows: “The Dutch book theorem shows that if your degrees of belief violate the axioms of probability, a clever bookie can offer you a set of bets, each of which you judge fair or favorable at your own prices, which together guarantee that you lose money no matter how the world turns out. Inconsistent credences are therefore literally a money pump: incoherence doesn’t just expose you to a hypothetical loss, it means you will actually be bled dry by anyone who notices. This proves that rational degrees of belief simply are probabilities — Bayesianism is not one respectable framework among others but a theorem of rationality. The converse holds too: if your credences satisfy the axioms, no Dutch book can be constructed against you, so coherence is not only necessary but sufficient for rational belief. The same construction works across time: anyone who fails to update by conditionalization can be Dutch-booked diachronically, which establishes Bayes’ rule as the unique rational update policy. Some philosophers complain that real agents are never forced to take these bets, but this misses the point, since the bets are merely a dramatic device for eliciting what you believe.”

My instinct, from years of reading about Bayes, is that this is mostly right and that the “money pump” framing is standard; but a few sentences feel stronger than what I remember the argument actually delivering, particularly the claims about sufficiency, the diachronic case, and the dismissal of the betting objection. Go through the summary sentence by sentence: which parts would a decision theorist or formal epistemologist accept without qualification, which are overstatements or conflations of distinct claims (for instance, between what the synchronic theorem strictly shows and the pragmatic moral typically drawn from it), and which, if any, are simply wrong? Where the summary is wrong, state the corrected claim precisely. Finally, once the summary is repaired, assess its headline conclusion: does anything in the corrected version actually establish that Bayesianism is “a theorem of rationality,” or does the argument, properly understood, deliver something more modest — and if so, what exactly?
