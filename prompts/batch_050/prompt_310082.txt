So I cover science and ideas for a mid-sized magazine, and earlier this year I moderated a panel on forgiveness that went weirdly well and then weirdly nowhere. The two guests were a moral philosopher who argues that forgiveness can sometimes be genuinely obligatory -- like, you can wrong someone by refusing to forgive them once certain conditions are met -- and a writer/therapist who thinks that whole framing is confused and dangerous, that forgiveness by its nature can't be owed to anyone, and that telling victims they have a duty to forgive is itself a kind of harm. They got along great, agreed on more than either expected, and afterwards I pitched them on doing one of those adversarial-collaboration write-ups together. Three months of scheduling emails later, it's dead. But my editor still wants something for the piece, so I want to draft the document they should have produced, and then run it by both of them.

Write that document for me. It should lay out the claims both camps would actually sign (stuff like: forgiving isn't the same as excusing or reconciling; pressuring victims is often bad in practice; some refusals to forgive seem petty or self-corroding in a way that at least feels like a moral failing), then state the residual disagreements in wording neutral enough that each side would accept the description of its own position -- no loaded terms like "demanding" or "gatekeeping." Then identify the cruxes: the sub-questions where settling them would actually move one side or the other. My guess is one crux is whether the feeling of resentment is even under the kind of control that obligations require, and another is whether "unconditional" forgiveness (forgiving before any apology or change) is a coherent notion at all or collapses into something else like forbearance -- but push back on my guesses if you think the real cruxes lie elsewhere. For each crux, give a concrete case or thought experiment both sides would agree bears on it, and record what each side would predict or say about it in advance, so neither can move the goalposts later.

Two ground rules for how you write it. First, be honest about the epistemic status of everything: where there's broad agreement among people who actually work on forgiveness, say so, and where something is genuinely contested between the two camps or in the literature more generally, flag it as contested rather than slipping one side's framing in as the default. If I'm wrong that something I called a crux is actually a crux, tell me. Second, and this is important because it's going in the article as a kind of found document: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables -- continuous prose only, the way a joint statement or a long letter would actually read. I know this kind of thing usually begs for a table, but resist. American English, please.
