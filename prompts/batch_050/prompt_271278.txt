Ok this has been bugging me for the better part of a year and I want to know if its a recognized thing or if I just have a screw loose. I run a pub quiz league and we do a lot of small side bets, so I have a fair bit of data on myself and my regulars.

Heres the pattern. If someone flips a coin in front of me and offers me even money on heads, I take it all day, no hesitation. But last year a guy produced a strange weighted-looking coin, told me straight up he had no idea how it was biased (and I believed him, hed never touched it before either), and offered me the same even money on whichever side I liked. Nobody at the table would take it. Including me. And heres the maddening part: if neither of us knows anything about the bias, then whichever side I pick, my chances are still fifty-fifty, right? Whatever the bias is, it cuts both ways equally from where Im standing. Ive done this math a dozen times and it keeps coming out "the two bets are the same" and I keep not wanting the second bet.

Same thing with a raffle we ran. Two buckets of tickets. Bucket A: we counted, exactly 40 winners out of 100. Bucket B: my buddy filled it with 100 tickets, made anywhere from 0 to 100 of them winners by rolling dice, and even he doesnt know the count. On average both buckets should treat you the same. People would literally pay more for a ticket from bucket A.

Now, one wrinkle that I think matters. When the person offering the mystery bet might know something I dont -- like if the weighted-coin guy had owned that coin for years -- then refusing feels obviously smart, thats just not getting hustled, thats the lemon market or whatever. But I get the exact same squirmy feeling when I set up the mystery MYSELF and nobody anywhere knows the odds. So either the feeling is misfiring in the second case, or there are two different things here that just feel alike.

So: does the second thing -- the pure version, where no hustler could possibly exist -- have a name? Is it considered irrational, and if so whats the actual argument, because the "its the same bet on average" reasoning sure sounds like it should settle it and somehow doesn't. Is it different from just being scared of losing, since these same people will happily take the 40/100 raffle bet which is a losing bet more often than not? And if it is two phenomena wearing one trenchcoat, where exactly is the seam -- how do you tell "reasonable worry that someone knows more than me" apart from the pure version when you're inside one of these decisions? Don't just hand me a label, I want the edges: cases that look like this but arent, and cases that dont look like it but are.
