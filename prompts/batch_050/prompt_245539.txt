So I spent fourty years doing physics, retired, and now I'm making my way through philosophy books on the couch, and Leibniz has gotten under my skin -- this idea that God surveyed all possible worlds and created the best one. Here's what's bugging me. In my old field there's a live fight about the so-called fine-tuning of the constants of nature: if things like the strength of gravity or the cosmological constant were slightly different, you'd get no atoms, no stars, no life. The two standard replies are (a) a designer tuned the constants, or (b) there's a multiverse, a vast ensemble of universes with different constants, and of course we find ourselves in one of the habitable ones. Now, if you could sit Leibniz down, explain modern cosmology to him, and ask him to pick a side, which way would he go? I can talk myself into both answers. The design option sounds like his best-of-all-possible-worlds thesis in fancy dress. But the multiverse sounds a lot like his own thought that the best world is the one richest in variety while governed by the simplest laws -- and he already thought God contemplated an infinity of possible worlds. On the other hand, his principle of sufficient reason says nothing is without a reason, and in a multiverse the particular constants we have seem to be sheer luck, which I'd think he'd hate. Answer as exactly six numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro or wrap-up: which side he'd take, which of his own commitments push toward each option, where the analogy between his possible worlds and the physicists' multiverse breaks down, and the single biggest problem his system faces whichever side he lands on.
