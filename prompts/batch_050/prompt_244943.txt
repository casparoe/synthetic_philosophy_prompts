I'm in a metaphysics reading group, and last week one member -- call him Dan -- presented what he calls the dissolution of personal obligation. He claims it follows from premises he thinks any reasonable philosopher of persistence should accept, and honestly I can't find the hole, though the conclusion has to be wrong. Here it is, as he wrote it on the whiteboard. (1) Nothing can possess incompatible properties, full stop. (2) The banana on my desk was green on Monday and yellow on Friday; green and yellow are incompatible. (3) If the banana were wholly present on both days -- the very same object, all of it, at each time -- then one and the same thing would be simply green and simply yellow, contradicting (1). (4) So what persists is a four-dimensional whole with a green Monday-part and a yellow Friday-part: distinct temporal parts of one spacetime worm. (5) Temporal parts are genuinely distinct objects; what is true of one need not be true of another, just as what is true of my left hand need not be true of my right. (6) The person who signed my lease eighteen months ago is a temporal part distinct from the temporal part my landlord would sue today. (7) By (5), the signer's undertakings are true of that part only, not of the part now sitting in the apartment. (8) So nobody is ever bound by an agreement, promise, or debt incurred at an earlier time. Dan grants that (8) sounds like a reductio but insists the only way out is to reject (3) and accept that objects can be multiply located in time with contradictory properties, which he says is just (1) denied with extra steps.

For context: I actually find (1) through (4) attractive -- I'd been leaning perdurantist before this meeting, partly because of temporary-intrinsic puzzles exactly like the banana -- and I obviously want to keep contracts and promises. So my preference is to locate the trouble in (5) through (8) rather than retreat to endurantism, but I don't want wishful thinking to do the work for me. What I'd like from you: name the specific step you reject, explain why that step looked innocent given the premises before it, and then price the alternatives -- what it costs to reject (3) instead, what it costs to reject (5), and what it costs to reject (7). If the argument is actually valid and sound, say so plainly and tell me which commitments I have to abandon. Keep the whole response under 450 words.
