I'm a science journalist who runs workshops for early-career science writers, and my next session is on how to report taxonomy disputes without treating them as either settled fact or mere semantics. About 14 participants, 90 minutes, no biology background assumed. Design an activity that makes them feel the force of the species problem — that 'species' has no single criterion that sorts real cases the way we intuitively expect — before they ever hear the term. I want them to construct a rule themselves and watch it break. Concretely: have small groups draft one written criterion for when two populations count as the same species, then hand them a prepared deck of case cards — a lineage that reproduces entirely asexually, a chain of neighboring populations where each interbreeds with the next but the two ends cannot, a hybrid cross with occasional fertile offspring, a single continuous lineage that paleontologists have split into two named fossils at an arbitrary geological boundary, and bacteria that routinely swap genes across distantly related groups — plus a fixed conservation budget that forces each group to decide which lineages merit protection under their own rule. Specify the rules and turn structure, the timing, how the budget decision should be run, and a debrief that maps each card onto the species concepts biologists actually deploy (interbreeding, ecological niche, common ancestry) and onto the characteristic mistakes journalists make when covering these disputes. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of the real scientific problem the game cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply in discussion. Lead with your bottom-line recommendation in the first sentence, then give the full design, with no preamble and no restating of my request.
