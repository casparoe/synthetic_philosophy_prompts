I teach a second-year ethics course, about 24 students, one 80-minute seminar a week. Next month we're doing aggregation and I want an activity for the session *before* anyone reads Taurek, so they bump into the idea themselves first and only hear his name at the end. Most of them will walk in assuming that of course you save the five over the one, and I want at least some of them to walk out less sure.

Design a game with real (small) stakes -- chocolate, participation points, whatever works logistically -- built around a rescue choice: a rescuer can save one group or another but not both. A few things I specifically want baked in:

1. A phase where the class votes on the rescue rule *before* they know which group they'll be in, and then assignments that are fixed for the rest of the game, so the "just maximize expected survival" reasoning stops working for them and the person stuck alone in the small group has to argue for a 50/50 coin flip from a position they can't escape. I want the room to feel what changes once the rule is rigged against you permanently rather than behind a veil.
2. Escalating rounds: 1 vs 5, then 1 vs 12, then (hypothetically, no stakes) 1 vs a million, so I can watch where the coin-flip intuition breaks even in students who were defending it.
3. Space for someone to propose a weighted lottery (proportional to group size), because that always happens and I want a plan for using it rather than shutting it down -- it's actually the interesting middle position.

Give me concrete rules, materials, a rough timeline for the 80 minutes, and what the facilitator does and says at each stage (ideally as little as possible -- the rules should generate the problem, not me). Then a debrief plan that maps specific moments in the game onto the philosophical moves: the seperateness of persons, why summing losses across people might be a mistake, what it costs to insist on the coin flip at 1 vs a million, and what the weighted-lottery proposal concedes and preserves.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong -- I'm guessing coalition-forming, students bargaining across groups, someone accusing the game of being rigged (it is), someone else refusing to accept the stakes as real -- and how to handle each. And be honest about which features of the actual philosophical problem the game can't reproduce: real death versus chocolate, the difference between choosing who dies and failing to save, the fact that real rescuers don't know the exact headcounts. I'd rather you flag these than pretend the game does everything.
