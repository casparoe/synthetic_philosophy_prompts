So me and a few friends do a blind whiskey tasting night once a month. Everyone brings a bottle, we pour them in unlabeled cups, score each one out of ten, and then -- and this is the part I want to ask about -- we throw out the highest and lowest score for each whiskey and average the rest. We stole that from olympic diving judging, and it turns out wine competitions and spirits awards do similar things: panels, blind flights, judges recalibrating, outliers getting tossed. Anyway, one friend keeps insisting the whole thing is theater because taste is just personal and no score is closer to the truth than any other, and another friend literally keeps a spreadsheet comparing our scores to professional critics to prove some of us are better tasters than others. Because of this argument I ended up reading this old essay I found free online, David Hume's Of the Standard of Taste. In it he tells this story he credits to Don Quixote: two of Sancho's relatives get asked to judge a barrel of wine, one says it's good except for a hint of leather, the other says good except for a hint of iron, everyone laughs at them, and then when the barrel is drained there's a key tied to a leather strap at the bottom. Hume's takeaway is something like: there really is a standard of taste, and it lives in the verdict of a small class of true judges -- people with trained, delicate palates, lots of practice, no prejudice -- and the rest of us count as right or wrong by how close we get to them.

Here's my question: what does our little practice, and the professional versions of it, actually PRESUPPOSE about taste? Like, does throwing out the high and low scores only make sense if there's some real fact about the whiskey that decent judges home in on and outliers miss? Or is our system compatible with taste being totally subjective, just a way of manufacturing fake consensus? And is Hume's picture -- truth sits with the elite judges, everyone else approximates them -- the theory our practice is secretly committed to, or does trimming the extremes actually presuppose something different and almost opposite, like the truth is whatever the converging middle says it is, so being a true judge just means being predictably average? I'd love it if you could reconstruct the implicit theory of the practice, and also be honest about where the practice is underdetermined -- i.e. which parts of it several rival theories (Hume's elite-judge view, a pure subjectivist view, a statistical consensus view) would all license equally well, and which parts, if any, only make sense on one of them.

Two more specific things. First, if my subjectivist friend is right, tell me concretely what would have to change -- would dropping extreme scores become straight up irrational, since no score is farther from any truth than any other, and would judge training and blind tasting become pointless rituals? Second, I've got a worry about Hume's own story: the key and the leather strap are REALLY in the barrel, so the two kinsmen turn out to be detecting actual physical stuff in the wine. But is being a good judge of whether a whiskey is GOOD like that? Detecting iron and leather is one thing; the leap to this bottle deserves a 9 and that one a 6 seems like a whole other claim, and I can't tell if Hume's story quietly assumes what it's supposed to prove. Anyway, pick apart the practice and the story together, and please write the answer in American English, casual is definately fine.
