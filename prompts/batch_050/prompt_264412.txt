In a philosophy reading group, I presented an evidential argument from evil: since we can discern no justifying reason for certain horrendous, apparently pointless sufferings, probably there is none, and hence probably no omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good God exists. A fellow participant replied only that God works in mysterious ways, as though that ended the matter. I'm fairly sure the slogan is doing argumentative work it hasn't earned, but I want to be precise about why. Please lay out the distinct theses this proverb might be expressing -- from the near-trivial (finite minds have limited access to a divine plan) through the substantive (we are not in a position to expect that we would recognize justifying goods even if they existed; apparently pointless suffering is actually an appearance only; all evils serve greater goods beyond our tracing) to the non-propositional (an expression of trust rather than a premise at all). For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a defensible but contentious position, or implausible; note which readings would actually engage my inference from "no reason is apparent" to "probably there is no reason," and whether any of them touch the logical version of the problem of evil as opposed to the evidential one; and indicate which reading someone who deploys the slogan to close down discussion is most likely relying on. Present the readings as an itemized list I can bring to the next session.
