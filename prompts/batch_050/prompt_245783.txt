After 35 years as a research chemist I retired, and somehow I've ended up sitting on the IRB at our regional hospital. The thing that keeps tripping me up is equipoise. Everyone on the committee throws the word around, but when I press people on whether a proposed trial actually has it, the answers get mushy fast. Sometimes they mean the individual investigator is genuinely uncertain, sometimes that the medical community is split, sometimes just that nobody's run the trial yet.

Write me a decision procedure I can actually use: a checklist or ordered set of tests I can run in the 20 minutes we get per protocol at our monthly meetings, for deciding whether a randomized trial has genuine equipoise and is therefore ethical to approve. It needs to handle the stuff that comes up constantly: trials of off-label drugs that clinicians already swear by, placebo controls when an effective treatment exists, head-to-head trials where one arm is the local favorite, and prior evidence that's suggestive but not conclusive. Mark the points where judgment can't be eliminated and I just have to make a call, rather than pretending the checklist settles everything. Work through two or three example protocols, including one where the procedure gives an answer I won't like (say, blocking a trial everyone wants to run, or greenlighting one that feels pointless).

Start with the procedure itself, no preface or restating of my question. And at the end, tell me which step of the procedure you're least confident in, and what argument or evidence would convince you to redesign it.
