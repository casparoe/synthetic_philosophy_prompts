Here's what I want. The separateness of persons objection — Rawls's complaint that utilitarianism fails to take seriously the distinction between individuals — is usually wheeled out alongside distributive complaints: the utilitarian doesn't care who gets what, only how much there is in total. So the prioritarian often replies: fine, weight the worse-off more heavily, problem solved. I suspect that reply doesn't touch the real objection at all, and I want a thought experiment that shows this by construction.

Build me a case meeting these specs:

1. Act utilitarianism and prioritarianism agree on which option to take — so whatever is fishy about the verdict can't be blamed on distribution-insensitivity or on how benefits to the worse-off are weighted.
2. The agreed verdict is one the separateness objection still bites against with full force: no single person gains enough to justify what another person loses, and the verdict goes through only because gains and losses are summed across distinct people.
3. Realistic and ordinary — think hospital triage, public health policy, infrastructure planning. No sci-fi, no utility monsters, no perfect predictors, and don't stipulate the utilities by fiat: give me the stakes in terms a hospital board would recognise.
4. The scenario itself should be one paragraph.
