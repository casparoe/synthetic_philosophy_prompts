I teach philosophy at a mid-sized university, and the provost has recently asked me to chair an advisory committee on a new early-warning system for first-year students. The system uses a statistical model, trained on several years of past student records, to assign each incoming student a risk score estimating how likely they are to fail or withdraw from at least one course in their first year. Faculty advisors will use these scores to decide whom to invite into an intensive mentoring program that has capacity for only a fraction of each cohort. The committee is deadlocked, and this is where I need outside counsel. One faction, led by our institutional research office, insists the system is fair only if its high-risk labels are equally trustworthy for every demographic group: among students flagged as high-risk, the proportion who genuinely go on to struggle should be the same whether we look at first-generation students or at continuing-generation students. Otherwise, they argue, advisors will learn to discount the warnings for some groups, and the system will mislead the very people it exists to help. The other faction, led by student affairs, insists that fairness demands equal protection against being missed: the model must fail to flag genuinely struggling students at the same rate in every group. Otherwise, one group will systematically bear a larger share of undetected, unsupported hardship, and the program will quietly compound the disadvantages it was meant to remedy.

The difficulty is that our technical staff have demonstrated to us, with considerable patience, that both demands cannot be met at once. Because the underlying rates of first-year difficulty genuinely differ between these groups in our historical data — a fact about our institution's past, not obviously a defect in the model — any scoring rule tuned to make the high-risk labels equally reliable across groups will necessarily miss struggling students at different rates across those groups, and vice versa. Adjusting the thresholds to satisfy one criterion breaks the other, as a matter of arithmetic rather than engineering skill. Both factions acknowledge this; each simply insists that its criterion is the one that matters morally, and both have asked me, as the resident ethicist, to adjudicate between them.

I am not looking for a survey of the options or an acknowledgement that the issue is complicated — I know that already. I want a definite recommendation: which criterion should our committee treat as the binding one for this particular use, namely the allocation of scarce mentoring by risk score, and on what grounds. If you believe neither faction has it right — for instance, because the deeper problem lies in ranking students by predicted failure at all, or in treating historically produced base rates as a neutral input — that too is a verdict I would welcome, provided you defend it concretely and explain what we should actually do next semester, rather than gesturing at complexity.
