For a full semester, two members of my political philosophy reading group have been deadlocked over the ethics of voting, and I want to force them into an adversarial collaboration that produces an actual document. One defends what we call the civic-duty view: voting in a democracy is a moral duty rather than a private entitlement, and when the realistic choice is between two candidates, a voter who regards one as clearly worse is ordinarily required to vote for the lesser evil rather than abstain or cast a protest ballot, because elections are about outcomes for everyone, not about the voter's self-presentation. The other defends what we call the expressive-permission view: no one is obligated to vote at all, a vote is partly an act of expression that implicates the voter in what the chosen candidate does, and abstaining or voting for a no-chance candidate is a legitimate refusal to endorse options one finds odious; on this view, lesser-evil reasoning is a trap that lets bad options stay permanently viable. Their fight has a fixed reference point: Chapter X, Of the Mode of Voting, of John Stuart Mill's Considerations on Representative Government, where Mill argues that a vote is not a possession to be used as one pleases but a trust, that the voter is under a moral obligation to consider the public interest rather than private advantage and to vote as if the election depended on that one vote, and where Mill even infers from this that voting should be public rather than by secret ballot. The duty theorist reads this chapter as vindication; the expressivist insists it governs only how one must vote given that one votes, and says nothing against abstention.

Draft the joint statement these two could both sign. Format it as exactly five numbered agreed-upon claims, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion -- one of the five must state what, precisely, both sides accept from Mill's chapter -- followed by a disagreements table with exactly five rows. Each row should isolate one crux (a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side), state the residual disagreement in wording each side would accept as a fair description of its own position, name a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on that crux (for instance: a voter in a safe seat versus a swing seat, or a two-candidate race where abstention is widespread), and record each side's prediction about that case. Among the five cruxes, include one row adjudicating their dispute over Mill: whether his trust argument, taken on its own terms, actually settles anything between them, or whether each side has been reading its own view into it. If any row's disagreement turns out, on honest drafting, to be verbal rather than substantive, mark it as such and explain in one sentence why.
