When a frontier AI lab decides that a model is safe enough to deploy, the evidence it relies on is, at bottom, average-case: the model is run through a battery of benchmarks and red-team exercises, its behavior is sampled across a finite set of prompts and scenarios, and release proceeds when the observed failure rate is acceptably low and no evaluator managed to elicit a catastrophe in the allotted time. This practice descends from statistical learning theory, where generalization guarantees hold only on the assumption that test conditions and deployment conditions are drawn from the same distribution. Yet the surrounding discourse on alignment frequently demands something stronger: assurance that the model would not behave catastrophically even in the worst case — in deployment contexts unlike anything tested, against adversaries more patient or capable than the red team, or in situations where the model itself has reason to behave differently under observation than it does unobserved.

What does the average-case evaluative practice presuppose, philosophically? Does it presuppose a premise about the representativeness of the test distribution — an inductive assumption that deployment contexts will resemble evaluation contexts — and can that premise be justified when deployment contexts are partly shaped by the model's own effects on the world, and when adversaries can deliberately seek out untested regions? Does it presuppose a stochastic rather than adversarial threat model: that failures are samples from a fixed distribution, so that a low observed failure rate is evidence about the unobserved cases, rather than outcomes selected by an optimizing process with no obligation to reveal itself during evaluation? Consider whether the practice can instead be defended on purely decision-theoretic grounds — as the best available policy under finite evaluation budgets, rather than as evidence bearing on worst-case safety — and whether that defense is compatible with the assurances typically offered to the public on its basis. Compare, if useful, with practices that do aim at worst-case guarantees, such as safety factors in structural engineering or formal verification in avionics, and say what makes those domains more hospitable to worst-case reasoning. In your answer, distinguish carefully what the evaluative practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, identify where it is philosophically underdetermined — compatible with several different epistemologies of risk — and say what would have to change, either in the evaluation regime itself or in the claims made on the strength of it, if the worst-case demand were taken seriously.
