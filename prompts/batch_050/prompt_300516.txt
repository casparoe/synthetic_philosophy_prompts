I am a retired research chemist who has come to philosophy late, and the reading group I have joined has asked each member to circulate a short statement of position for criticism. Mine concerns ageing and what, if anything, is rationally to be regretted about it. The text that set me thinking is Cicero's Cato Maior de Senectute, which I read in W. A. Falconer's Loeb translation. Cato says:

"For to those who have not the means within themselves of a virtuous and happy life every age is burdensome; and, on the other hand, to those who seek all good from themselves nothing can seem evil that the laws of nature inevitably impose. To this class old age especially belongs, which all men wish to attain and yet reproach when attained"

Building on this, my commitments are as follows. (1) Cato is right: whether one's old age is tolerable depends on one's inner resources, and for the person who has them, nothing nature imposes should be counted an evil. (2) Ageing is a set of biological processes like any other, and research aimed at slowing or mitigating it — extending healthy lifespan — is among the most worthwhile uses of scientific effort; I support such work and contribute money to it. (3) The rational preparation for old age is made in youth and middle age, in the formation of character and habits; by the time one is old it is largely too late to acquire the resources Cato has in mind. (4) Old age's release from the sensual appetites is not a loss but a liberation, and should be welcomed as such. (5) Death at the close of a full life is no evil, and fearing it is irrational.

What I want from you is an audit rather than an essay on Cicero. Which pairs of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, which only appear to, and how severe is each genuine tension? For each, what is the least costly revision? If a single amendment would remove the most trouble, say which. I would also value your judgement on whether I have read the quoted passage correctly, since so much of (1) rests on it.
