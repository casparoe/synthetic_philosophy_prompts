I've been arguing with a friend about when it's ok to kill insects and I realized my own intuitions don't add up to anything coherent, so I want to outsource the philosophy. Here are my gut verdicts on some cases. Tell me what principle (if any) captures them, and be honest about which ones have to go.

1. A city fogs whole neighborhoods for mosquitoes during a dengue outbreak, killing millions of mosquitoes and plenty of harmless bystander insects. Fine. Honestly wouldn't even hesitate.
2. A farmer uses a pesticide that also wipes out local bee populations. Not fine, or at least a genuinely bad cost that needs a serious justification, not just "the crops needed it."
3. I find a cockroach in my kitchen and kill it rather than trap and release it. Fine. Relocating a cockroach strikes me as absurd.
4. A kid pulls the legs off ants one by one because it's entertaining. Wrong, even though each individual ant death seems trivially insignificant next to case 1.
5. A collector kills a few dozen rare butterflies for a display case. Wrong, even though the total suffering involved is tiny compared to the fogging truck.
6. Destroying a wasp nest next to a playground before anyone's been stung. Fine.
7. Using a gene drive to drive a malaria-carrying mosquito species entirely extinct. Fewer total deaths than the fogging, and the goal is the same, but this one genuinely unsettles me in a way fogging doesn't, and I can't tell if that's a real difference or just neophobia.

The pair that I think breaks any simple principle is 1 vs 4: if insect suffering matters at all, the fogging is astronomically worse than one kid with one ant, but my verdicts go the opposite way. And 5 suggests something similar -- motive or character seems to be doing the work, not consequences for the insects. But if it's all about motive, then does case 3 change if I admit I kind of enjoyed killing the roach? I'd rather it didn't.

So: is there one principle (or a small set of clauses) that delivers most of these verdicts? State it precisely, say which clause each case motivates, and tell me which of my verdicts you'd overturn if the principle is going to stay clean. Then apply it to a case I didn't list: lethal traps for pantry moths when nonlethal traps exist but only work about half as well.

At the end, give me a table with columns: Case (short label), My verdict, Saved by your principle? (yes / no / only with a stretch), What the principle would actually say about it.
