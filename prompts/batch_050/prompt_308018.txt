The question (worth 20 points): Maxwell's demon appears to violate the second law of thermodynamics. Explain the apparent violation using Szilard's one-molecule engine, and explain how the modern thermodynamics of information is taken to save the second law. Your answer should address: (a) how much work can be extracted per cycle of the Szilard engine and where it comes from, (b) which step in the demon's operation must carry the compensating entropy cost, measurement or memory erasure, and why, and (c) what Landauer's principle says quantitatively and which figure in this history is responsible for locating the cost where the modern account puts it.

The student's answer:

Maxwell's demon guards a trapdoor between two chambers of gas and opens it only to let fast molecules pass one way and slow molecules the other, building a temperature gradient with no work input, which could then drive a heat engine. That is an apparent violation of the second law, since heat flows from cold to hot without compensation. Szilard's one-molecule engine sharpens the puzzle: a box containing a single gas molecule, a partition dropped into the middle, the demon observes which half the molecule is in, couples a weight to a piston on that side, and lets the molecule expand isothermally at temperature T, extracting kT ln 2 of work per cycle, apparently converting heat from a single reservoir fully into work in a cyclic process.

The standard resolution is that the second law is saved once we count the information processing the demon performs. By Landauer's principle, acquiring one bit of information, i.e. measuring which side the molecule is on, dissipates at least kT ln 2 of heat into the environment at temperature T, and this exactly cancels the kT ln 2 of work extracted, so the cycle nets zero and the second law survives. Szilard was the first to locate the entropy cost in the demon's memory, and Landauer later put a number on it.

A demon that tried to dodge the cost by never resetting its memory would eventually fill up with bits and stop operating cyclically, so the cost can't be postponed forever. And because the entropy cost is paid on every cycle, the second law holds in every individual run of the engine, not merely on average over many runs.
