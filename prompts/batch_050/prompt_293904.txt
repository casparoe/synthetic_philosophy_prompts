Having spent forty years as an evolutionary ecologist before turning to philosophy in retirement, I have lately been helping a colleague run a small undergraduate seminar in philosophy of biology. The question (20 marks): Critics have charged that the phrase "survival of the fittest" makes the theory of natural selection tautological. Explain the charge. Then explain how the propensity interpretation of fitness is meant to answer it, and whether that interpretation faces difficulties of its own.

The student's answer:

The tautology objection runs as follows. If the fittest are defined as those that in fact survive and reproduce, then "the fittest survive" says only that those that survive, survive: an empty truth that no observation could falsify and that therefore explains nothing. For natural selection to be a substantive, testable theory, fitness must be defined independently of actual reproductive outcomes.

The propensity interpretation, associated with Mills and Beatty, is designed to do exactly this. An organism's fitness is its propensity, roughly its expected number of offspring, rather than the number it actually leaves. Fitness is a disposition like solubility: a sugar cube is soluble even if it never dissolves, and a fit organism is fit even if, through bad luck, it leaves no offspring at all. Because fitness is defined by an underlying propensity rather than by actual outcomes, the claim that fitter organisms tend to outreproduce less fit ones is a contingent, empirically testable claim, and the tautology charge is thereby answered. On this view, if organism A has higher fitness than organism B, then A will, on average across environments, leave more descendants than B.

The interpretation does raise a measurement problem, but a tractable one. A propensity cannot be observed directly; however, by counting actual offspring over a sufficiently large number of generations, we converge on the true propensity, so fitness remains empirically accessible in practice.

Critics sometimes worry that chance still intrudes, since drift can cause the fitter type to decline, but this is no real difficulty for the interpretation itself, which concerns propensities rather than their realizations. On balance, the propensity interpretation successfully dissolves the tautology objection and faces no serious residual problems.
