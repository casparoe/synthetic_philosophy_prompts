I am a PhD student in philosophy of mathematics putting together a humor piece for our department's graduate newsletter, and I want it to be technically impeccable. Please act as a satirist with expert-level command of set theory and first-order model theory; before drafting, take a moment to map which features of the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem correspond to which conventions of the genre you choose.

Write a real-estate listing, in that genre's customary voice, advertising a countable model of ZFC to prospective tenants — sets who would reside inside it. Around 500–600 words. The satire should land Skolem's paradox without ever stating it outright: the listing promises that the property satisfies "there exist uncountably many real numbers," while the square footage and lot dimensions in the fine print make plain that the entire structure is countable. Include, at minimum: glowing testimonials from current residents insisting that their power set of the naturals is genuinely uncountable and that they've never been able to pair its members off with the naturals; a skeptical building inspector (an outside observer) whose report notes what he can see from where he stands; an amenities section (well-founded membership relations, unrestricted separation and replacement); and a standard disclosure that certain subsets of existing units simply do not exist on the premises, however definable they may appear from the sidewalk. The humor must come from getting the mathematics right — the internal versus external perspective, the relativity of "uncountable" to a model, the way satisfaction is defined — so that a set theorist laughs rather than winces. Stay deadpan throughout; the joke should be legible without a single wink at the reader, and the final line should leave open, in listing language, whether there is such a thing as the intended neighborhood at all.
