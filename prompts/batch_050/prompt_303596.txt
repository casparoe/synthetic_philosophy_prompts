I am a PhD student in philosophy of physics, and at our reading group last week a colleague presented an argument about the Aharonov–Bohm effect that has been bothering me ever since, because if it is sound it seems to prove far more than he is willing to accept. The set-up is the usual one: a long solenoid is shielded so that the magnetic field is confined entirely to its interior; a coherent electron beam is split so that the two paths pass on either side of the solenoid, never entering any region where the field is non-zero; and the interference pattern shifts by an amount fixed by the enclosed flux. His argument ran as follows. (1) The phase shift is a real, measurable physical effect occurring in the region traversed by the electrons. (2) A real physical effect in a region must be explained by something physically real in that region — this is just the demand of locality, the very demand that made unmediated action at a distance unacceptable in Newtonian gravitation. (3) The magnetic field vanishes throughout that region, so it cannot be what explains the effect. (4) The only remaining candidate the classical formalism offers is the electromagnetic vector potential, so the vector potential is physically real in that region. (5) But the vector potential is gauge-dependent, and gauge-dependent quantities are mere surplus structure — artefacts of a redundant mode of description, no more real than a choice of coordinate origin. (6) So the formalism contains an element that must be real (to explain the effect locally) and cannot be real (being gauge-dependent), and the only way out is that gauge transformations are genuine physical symmetries rather than descriptive redundancies after all.

Here is why I say the argument proves too much. If the inference at (4) is licensed, then by parity of reasoning we ought to conclude that the scalar potential in Coulomb gauge is physically real — yet in that gauge it responds instantaneously to changes in a charge distribution arbitrarily far away, so we would have proved the reality of superluminal influences. The same template would seem to force the reality of the Newtonian gravitational potential, and presumably much else besides. My colleague bites none of these bullets, so something in the chain has to give, but I cannot locate the culprit. Steps (2) and (5) each look like platitudes, albeit platitudes drawn from opposite camps — the causal realist and the modern orthodoxy on gauge — while (3) is an experimental fact. My own suspicion is that the damage is done somewhere in (2) or (4), perhaps through an equivocation on what it is for something to be real 'in' a region, since the phase difference manifestly depends on a quantity integrated around a closed loop rather than on anything at the points the electron visits — but I have not been able to make this suspicion precise enough to convince him.

Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I would particularly like to know whether the natural repair — taking the gauge-invariant holonomy around the solenoid to be the genuine physical explainer — actually rescues locality or merely renames the problem, given that the holonomy is not located anywhere the electron goes either. And I would like the alternatives priced honestly: what does one commit to, elsewhere in physics, by accepting non-local but gauge-invariant beables; by treating gauge structure itself as physically significant rather than redundant; or by revising premise (2)'s conception of local explanation — especially when one turns to diffeomorphism invariance in general relativity, where the analogous pressure bears on the reality of spacetime points themselves? If forced to choose, I would rather keep (2) than (5), but I want to know what that choice costs before I commit.
