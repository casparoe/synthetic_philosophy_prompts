Two philosophers — call them the Thirder and the Halfer — have spent two years arguing about the Sleeping Beauty problem and have agreed to settle their differences through an adversarial collaboration. Draft the joint statement their collaboration should produce.

Assume the canonical setup: Beauty is put to sleep on Sunday; a fair coin is tossed; she is woken on Monday in any case, and again on Tuesday if and only if the coin lands tails; an amnesia drug ensures that on any awakening she cannot tell whether she has been woken before; she knows all of this in advance, and on waking receives no evidence about which day it is. The disputed question is her credence, on awakening, that the coin landed heads: one-half, says the Halfer; one-third, says the Thirder.

The statement should have four parts.

Part I: Agreed claims, as a numbered list. Include: claims about the setup both camps accept (that Beauty is rational and fully informed; that no ordinary evidence arrives between Sunday and Monday's awakening; that the dispute concerns rational credence rather than the meaning of probability); verdicts on the neighboring questions where the camps coincide — Beauty's credence on Sunday before sleeping, her credence conditional on being told it is Monday, and her credence conditional on being told it is Tuesday — with a derivation for each that the respective side would accept as its own; and at least two claims about what would not settle the debate (for instance, that no empirical observation could adjudicate it, and that neither side denies the axioms of probability).

Part II: A disagreements table, with each side's position worded so that side would sign it. It must cover at least: whether Monday's awakening constitutes new evidence and, if not, what could justify a credence other than one-half; whether the correct unit of analysis is the experiment or the awakening; and whether the problem reduces to a choice between self-locating principles in the vicinity of the Self-Sampling Assumption (reason as if you are a random sample from your reference class) and the Self-Indication Assumption (give weight to hypotheses in proportion to how many observers like you they contain). Where a disagreement is verbal rather than substantive, the table should say so and explain why.

Part III: Cruxes. For each substantive disagreement, identify the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would force at least one side to move — and specify a case, argument, or variant both accept as bearing on it, recording each side's prediction in advance. The cruxes must include: (a) betting arguments — which bets Beauty should accept at which odds, and the dispute over whether stakes are paid once per experiment or once per awakening; (b) the Delayed-Toss variant, in which the coin is tossed only on Monday night, after the first awakening has already occurred — each side must state Beauty's Monday credence in that variant, say whether it differs from the canonical case, and explain why, with particular attention to whether a Thirder answer of one-third about a genuinely future toss is an acceptable consequence or a reductio; and (c) one further crux of your own devising that you judge the strongest test of the two positions.

Part IV: A closing paragraph of at most 150 words stating what each side would count as a fair loss.

Format Part II as a table with columns: Issue / Thirder's position / Halfer's position / Verbal or substantive. Format Part III as a table with columns: Crux / Case bearing on it / Thirder's prediction / Halfer's prediction.
