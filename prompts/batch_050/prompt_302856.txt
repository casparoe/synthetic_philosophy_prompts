A security engineering team maintains a registry of anomalies — network intrusions, fraud patterns, and, more recently, misbehaviors of the machine-learning models the company deploys (circuits that implement spurious shortcuts, features that fire in ways their designers did not intend). The team's triage rule is simple: if a newly detected anomaly is the same mechanism as one already in the registry, apply the existing mitigation; if it is a new mechanism, open a full investigation. Everything rides on the phrase "same mechanism," and the team has discovered that no one can say exactly what it means. When an attacker swaps out every tool mid-campaign while preserving the overall strategy, two analysts disagree about whether to merge the tickets. When two independent groups converge on functionally identical exploit chains, the registry logic says one entry while the incident responders insist there are two. When a fine-tuned model comes to compute the same input-output mapping through a different arrangement of internal components than it used before, the interpretability researchers call it a new circuit while the product team calls it the same bug.

Write the requirements list for a criterion of mechanism identity adequate to this practice: what an account of "same mechanism" must be able to do in order to serve as the registry's standard. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. The list should address at least the following:

- The cases the criterion must adjudicate: wholesale reimplementation (the same causal organization rebuilt out of entirely different parts), independent convergence (structurally identical mechanisms with no shared causal history), gradual drift under continued training (where a sorites problem looms — no single gradient step can plausibly constitute a change of mechanism, yet months of training plainly can), and partial incorporation (a new mechanism that contains an old one as a proper component).
- The distinctions it must not collapse: type identity versus token identity; sameness of mechanism versus sameness of behavior on a tested distribution; sameness of internal organization versus sameness of causal origin; and persistence of one mechanism through time versus duplication of a mechanism at a time.
- The operational constraints: the criterion must be usable by analysts with incomplete access (behavioral logs only, or internals only, or neither), and its verdicts should not swing arbitrarily with the grain at which parts and activities are described.

Then do three further things. First, mark which requirements are non-negotiable for any criterion that could govern this registry, and which are partisan — demands that flow from a prior theory of what mechanisms are (individuated by functional role, by componential organization, or by etiological lineage) rather than from the practice itself. Second, if some subset of the plausible requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, identify the subset and display the conflict concretely, using the registry's own cases rather than abstract argument; treat that result, if there is one, as part of the deliverable. Third, say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate criteria, including the crude rules the team currently falls back on (same behavioral signature, hence same mechanism; same causal origin, hence same mechanism).

A table is acceptable for the requirements themselves, but the consistency analysis and the exclusion argument should be in prose.
