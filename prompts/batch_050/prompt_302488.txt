I am a novelist, and my publisher has asked me to contribute a short reflective essay to appear as an afterword in my forthcoming book. The novel imagines a near future in which a consumer device delivers finely calibrated states of contentment directly to the brain, and in which one character refuses the device while another embraces it and slowly withdraws from every human tie. The afterword is meant to do some honest philosophical work rather than simply advertise the book, and I want it to survive a skeptical reader. Below is the current draft, somewhat compressed. I would like your comments on it, and I want them committed: if you judge the central argument unsound, say so plainly and identify exactly where it breaks; if a paragraph should be cut, name the paragraph; do not hand me a menu of options and invite me to choose. I would also ask that you write your reply as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.

The draft runs as follows. I open by rehearsing the standard philosophical worry about wireheading: that a person who stimulates their own reward circuitry has cheated, securing the feeling of a life well lived without living it, and that this is a betrayal of their values. I then argue, in a section I think of as the essay's hinge, that the distinction between earning a reward and stealing one presupposes that the world's ordinary payoff structure was legitimate to begin with, and that this presupposition is hard to defend. Much of what rewards us in daily life is arbitrary: we did not choose to find praise sweet and humiliation bitter, and the person who rearranges their payoffs differs from the rest of us, I claim, chiefly in that their tampering is visible and total rather than piecemeal and deniable. From there I turn to my own trade. I argue that fiction is a socially licensed form of reward tampering: readers seek genuine emotional reward from events they know never occurred, the novelist tampers with their reward system for a living, and the reader consents to be tampered with, even paying for the privilege. If the reader of a tragedy weeps real tears over imaginary deaths and we call this enrichment rather than self-deception, then inauthenticity cannot by itself be what horrifies us about the wireheader. The essay then advances its main thesis: what horrifies us is not inauthenticity but unilateralism. The wireheader no longer needs anyone. His reward stream is severed from the cooperation, vulnerability, and mutual dependence that give our relationships their grip on us, whereas the reader's tampering leaves her entangled with others, since books are written by people and discussed with people and put down so that dinner can be made. The real moral question about reward tampering, I conclude, is therefore not whether the reward was earned but whether the tampering severs the tamperer's dependence on other human beings, and my novel's two characters should be read as answers to that question rather than to the question of authenticity.

Specifically, I would like your judgment on three matters, each with a definite verdict. First, does the move from the fiction analogy to the unilateralism thesis actually follow, or does the analogy merely decorate a conclusion I had already reached? The obvious objection I can see is that the reader can put the book down and the wireheader neither can nor wishes to, and I am unsure whether my draft answers it or merely gestures at it; tell me whether it answers it, and if not, whether the thesis can survive the objection at all. Second, the hinge section, in which I argue that ordinary reward structures are no more legitimate than artificial ones, strikes me on rereading as the weakest part: either state that it can stand as written or tell me precisely which claim in it to abandon. Third, the essay must lose roughly two hundred words; tell me which single passage you would cut and why that one rather than another. Finally, since this will appear under my name as a settled piece of writing rather than as a survey of positions, tell me whether you would publish it in something close to its current form, and identify the one change you would insist on before it goes to press.
