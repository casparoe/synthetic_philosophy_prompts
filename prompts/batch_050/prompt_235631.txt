I'm writing a piece on the rebuilding of Notre-Dame de Paris after the 2019 fire, and I want to use it as a test case for the metaphysics of material constitution and persistence. Take the episode as follows: the medieval spire was lost, much of the roof and vaulting destroyed, and the cathedral has now been reconstructed with substantial new timber and stone, with the spire rebuilt to match the version added in the nineteenth century rather than anything medieval. Analyze this through the standard positions — mereological essentialism about parts, the constitution view on which a statue is constituted by but not identical to its clay, and sortal-dependent accounts of persistence — and tell me what each theory commits us to saying about whether the reopened building is Notre-Dame itself, a successor, or something the theories can't cleanly classify. I'm especially interested in where the mapping gets strained: does the fact that the restored spire replicates a nineteenth-century addition expose an ambiguity in what "the same building" was supposed to mean all along, and does the episode reveal that these theories were designed for lumps and statues and say surprisingly little about artifacts whose identity is partly institutional? Finally, run the argument in both directions: does the near-universal willingness to treat the rebuilt cathedral as Notre-Dame count as evidence against the stricter metaphysical views, or does it instead show that ordinary judgments of identity were never tracking what those theories are about?
