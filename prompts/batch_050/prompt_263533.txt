I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, and I've been trying to reverse-engineer my own intuitions about distributive justice. My working hypothesis has been something like: society should compensate people for outcomes that trace to their circumstances, not their choices. But when I test that against concrete cases, my verdicts don't all fall out of it cleanly. Here are the cases and my gut verdicts.

1. A person born with a degenerative condition that costs a fixed, substantial sum every month to manage. Verdict: society owes them the difference, indefinitely, no questions asked.

2. A homeowner who could easily have afforded flood insurance, declined it in order to spend the money on vacations, and lost everything in a flood. Verdict: nothing is owed. They chose the gamble.

3. A neighbor on the same street who lost the same amount in the same flood but could never have afforded the insurance. Verdict: full compensation. The contrast with (2) feels obvious even though the material outcomes are identical.

4. A 26-year-old who skipped health insurance to save money and is diagnosed with cancer at 27. Verdict: treat them, fully, and I don't want treatment made conditional on an inquiry into whether they could have afforded the premiums. I realize this case looks structurally identical to (2), and I can't yet say why it feels different.

5. A person who deliberately cultivated a taste for rare wines and is now miserable drinking ordinary wine. Verdict: nothing owed. But a person whose medical condition requires an expensive diet: full compensation, even though both involve costly preferences.

6. An entrepreneur who quit a safe job, ran a competent company, and was wiped out by a market shift nobody predicted. Verdict: nothing owed beyond ordinary bankruptcy protection -- though I notice this one sits less comfortably than (2), and I'm not sure why, since both involve freely accepted risk.

7. A person born with modest talents in a labor market that pays mainly for talent. Verdict: some top-up is owed, but I can't say where the line falls between 'modest talents' and 'didn't try', and I suspect the line I want doesn't exist.

So: is there one principle behind these verdicts, or a small set of them? If my circumstance/choice hypothesis is the right skeleton, I'd like it repaired precisely -- with each clause justified by the case that forces it -- rather than replaced wholesale. In particular: tell me honestly whether (4) can be reconciled with (2) at all, or whether a coherent principle has to sacrifice one of them, and if so which sacrifice is cheaper. Then tell me what your resulting principle says about two cases I haven't listed: a lifelong smoker who develops lung cancer, and a hobbyist injured doing a sport they'd been repeatedly warned was dangerous. If the principle gives a verdict on either that clashes with intuition, say whether you think the principle or the intuition should bend.
