For the final meeting of a philosophy of computation seminar, write a sestina titled “Thesis Before Theorem.” Use the six end-words hand, rule, tape, symbol, proof, and world in that order in the first stanza, follow the standard sestina permutation, and include all six in the three-line envoi. The poem should be intelligible to someone who knows only that the Church-Turing thesis identifies the informal notion of effective calculability with what a Turing machine can compute.

Let the first three stanzas move from a human clerk following finite instructions, to the Turing-machine picture, to one alternative formalism such as lambda calculus or the general recursive functions; the imagery should change without making it seem that the subject has changed. The fourth stanza should register the surprising convergence of these formalisms, while the fifth should distinguish their proven extensional equivalence from a proof of the thesis itself. In the sixth, consider whether there could be a genuinely effective finite procedure that escapes the formalisms, without treating a merely imagined hypercomputer as an automatic counterexample. The permutations should do the philosophical work: each returning word should feel like the same intuitive notion resurfacing under a new notation, with proof and world carrying the unresolved tension between mathematics and conceptual analysis.
