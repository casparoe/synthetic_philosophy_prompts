I’m a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I’m drafting a short column about the philosophical question of when a bunch of smaller things make up a larger thing. Could you give me candid comments on the draft below? I’m especially interested in whether the central idea is coherent, not just whether the prose flows.

**Things That Survive Their Parts**

Philosophers call it the special composition question: under what conditions do some objects compose a further object? Why should the cells in a cat count as parts of one thing, while the cat’s left ear, my coffee mug, and the Moon apparently do not?

The usual answers tend to fall into extremes. Maybe composition almost never happens: there are only simples arranged cat-wise, not cats. Maybe it almost always happens: any scattered collection automatically forms an object, however gerrymandered. Or maybe composition happens only when the parts get up to something special, such as living, thinking, or acting as a unit.

I want to explore a different test. Call it **custodial composition**. Roughly, some parts compose an object when they sustain a pattern that can outlive changes in those very parts. Three conditions matter:

1. **Role continuity:** The parts occupy interacting causal roles that maintain a repeatable pattern.
2. **Turnover tolerance:** At least some individual parts could be replaced, lost, or repaired without the pattern ending.
3. **No puppeteer:** The pattern’s continuation cannot depend entirely on some outside agent merely choosing to classify different stuff as “the same thing.”

The basic thought is that composition is not just a matter of things touching or resembling one another. It is a matter of there being a persisting arrangement that is, so to speak, somewhat indifferent to its own material. A cell remains the same cell through constant molecular turnover. A corporation survives replacement of every employee. Even a table could, in principle, have one leg replaced and remain that table. The object appears to be the custodian of a pattern, while the parts are temporary occupants of roles whos boundaries the pattern helps define.

This account has some convenient consequences. A random heap of sand is not obviously a further object, because its grains do not jointly maintain much of anything beyond sitting there. A living organism is a strong case of composition. An organization might also count, despite being spatially scattered. Perhaps even a traffic jam can be an object: cars enter and leave, but the compression wave persists and can move backward while no individual car moves backward with it.

The traffic-jam result will sound absurd to some readers. But should it? The jam has a location, a boundary, causal powers, and a career through time. It can slow ambulances, frustrate commuters, and disappear when road conditions change. Calling it an object may simply acknowledge that our ordinary inventory of things is not restricted to solid chunks of matter.

The harder cases are patterns maintained mainly by human attention. Consider a sports team. Players, coaches, owners, and even the city can change. Yet the team persists because fans, leagues, contracts, and records keep assigning new people to the same institutional roles. Is that custodial composition, or is the persistence merely conventional? My “no puppeteer” condition is meant to exclude cases where outside observers alone create continuity. But this threatens to exclude institutions altogether, since their causal roles are made possible by collective recognition. Maybe the distinction is between an observer **labeling** a pattern and an observer participating in the system that **maintains** it. I’m not confident that distinction is stable.

There is also a reductionist objection. Suppose every interaction among the parts can be fully described without mentioning the whole. Then the supposedly persisting object may be nothing more than a convenient summary of lower-level events. On this view, custodial composition identifies useful patterns but does not establish that anything extra exists. The traffic jam causes delays only in the derivative sense in which a statistic can be said to cause an outcome: the real work is done by drivers braking, accelerating, and changing lanes.

Yet this objection may prove too much. The parts of a cat can also be described chemically without mentioning the cat. If causal reducibility eliminated composite objects, organisms would disappear along with traffic jams. The defender of custodial composition should therefore deny that an object must be causally independent of its parts. A whole can be real because its organization explains why these particular lower-level events occur together, even if every event also has a more local explanation.

Still, the proposal has an awkward temporal feature. The special composition question is usually asked at a moment: do these things compose something now? Custodial composition, by contrast, looks backward and forward. It asks whether a pattern has survived turnover or could survive it. That may make composition partly historical. Two physically identical arrangements at a single instant might differ in whether they compose an object, because one is the continuation of a durable pattern and the other arose by coincidence a second ago.

That sounds odd, but perhaps composition really is historical. A counterfeit chair physically indistinguishable from an old chair is still not the old chair. More controversially, if an exact molecule-for-molecule duplicate of you appeared by accident, it would presumably be a new person rather than you, despite instantaneously sharing all your current structure. Continuity seems to matter somewhere. The question is whether it matters to composition itself or only to identity over time.

A few specific things I’d like comments on:

1. Does custodial composition actually answer the special composition question, or does it just change the subject from composition to persistence?
2. Are the three conditions independent, or does one secretly smuggle in another?
3. Is the traffic-jam example a helpful reductio, an acceptable consequence, or a genuinely plausible case of composition?
4. Does the “no puppeteer” condition create more trouble than it solves, especially for institutions?
5. Can you construct a case where my test gives the intuitively wrong verdict in both directions: saying there is an object when there clearly isn’t, and saying there isn’t one when there clearly is?
6. Is there a hidden circularity in defining objects through patterns whose boundaries are themselves identified by reference to the object?
7. Which paragraph would benefit most from a concrete scientific example, such as metabolism, neural turnover, swarm behavior, or distributed software?

Please be pretty blunt. If the idea is half-baked, tell me where the baking failed. Also let me know if I’m overlooking an obvious objection before I embarass myself in print.
