I'm a novelist, and after a reading from my new book last week two old friends of mine got into a real argument at the pub, and I stayed out of it but it's been bugging me. Some background: my novel follows Henrik, a middle-aged, not especially ideological clerk in an occupied town who joins a reserve police unit and ends up in mass shootings of civilians. Early on his commander offers anyone the option to step out of the killing detail with no punishment -- almost nobody takes it. Henrik vomits after his first one, goes numb, and by late in the book he's doing cruel little extras nobody ordered, like making a victim dig with his bare hands to show off for the younger men.

Marta's position: the book proves character is thin. Decent, squeamish guy, no prior hatred, and the situation -- peer pressure, the first step being the only hard one, nobody wanting to be the one who walks away -- does everything. That's the real lesson of these historical cases, she said: ordinary men, not monsters.

Daniel: no, the opt-out scene actually sinks her. Nobody who refused was punished, so fear can't explain the ones who kept shooting -- something in them did. And no situation explains cruelty that goes *beyond* orders; that's belief and contempt, built by a whole culture that had been calling these people vermin for years.

Marta: habituation explains the escalation -- each step renormalizes, the cruelty is performed for comrades, that's still situation just internalized over time.

Daniel: habituation explains it getting easier, not it getting crueler. Numbness doesn't embellish.

Who's right at each step? And is the situation-vs-character framing itself the problem here? Also curious whether I stacked the deck just by choosing which scenes to include.
