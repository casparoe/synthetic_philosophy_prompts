Since retiring from thirty-odd years as a research chemist I've been sitting in on an epistemology seminar at the local college, and we're deep into the question of when it's rational to flat-out believe something rather than just assign it a high probability. The instructor hands out cases and rules on them, and I keep his verdicts in a seperate notebook because I want to figure out what rule he's actually running. Next week he's promised us a new case, and I'd like to walk in with a prediction plus an argument for it, so here's his track record.

Verdicts so far. (1) A fair lottery with ten million tickets: he says it is *not* rational to believe outright that your ticket will lose -- only rational to be 99.99999% confident. His offhand remark was that the belief would be "true by luck if true." (2) A historian who has double-checked each of the 300 factual claims in her book may rationally believe every single one of them, *and* rationally believe, as she admits in her own preface, that at least one claim in the book is wrong. He says holding both is not irrational, just honest about her fallibility. (3) Your barometer has been dropping all afternoon and decades of experience on this coastline tell you a storm is coming -- call it 90% likely: he says it's perfectly rational to flat-out believe it will storm and cancel the sailing trip, even though 90% is a far cry from the lottery figure. So lower probability got a yes and higher probability got a no, which is what got me interested.

Here's the new case he teased: a lab tech weighs a reagent on a freshly calibrated balance and gets 4.97 g three times running, then a colleague mentions that this model of balance had a firmware bug last year that occasionally froze readings, supposedly fixed in the latest update. Nothing much rides on the measurement; it just goes into the logbook. Is it rational to believe outright that the sample is 4.97 g, or only to be very confident of it? I can see at least two rules that fit his first three verdicts -- one where what matters is whether your evidence is the kind that could actually make the belief knowledge rather than merely probable, and one where what matters is whether there's a live, specific way you could be wrong -- and I honestly can't tell which he's running, or if it's a third thing entirely. Tell me which rules plausibly fit the track record, what each one predicts for the weighing case, which rule you'd bet on him holding and why, and what case I could pose at the seminar that would force him to reveal which rule it is. Lead with your prediction in the first sentence -- no preamble, no restating my question -- and then give the reasoning.
