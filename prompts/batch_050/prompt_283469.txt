I am grading midterm exams for an undergraduate seminar on the political economy of disasters, and I would like your help producing a grade and feedback for one student's essay. Please provide: (1) a letter grade with a brief justification; (2) point-by-point comments keyed to the three parts of the exam question, indicating which specific claims are correct, which are overstated, which rest on conflations or misattributions, and where the reasoning skips a step. I want the comments detailed enough to paste into the student's feedback file, so please flag subtle errors that a quick read might miss rather than offering only a holistic impression.

The exam question was as follows:

"Amartya Sen's entitlement approach recasts famines as failures of access rather than failures of supply. Using at least two of the following cases — Bengal 1943, Ireland 1845–52, China 1958–62 — (i) explain the entitlement approach and how it differs from food-availability-decline (FAD) accounts; (ii) assess whether the 'natural disaster' view of famine retains any plausibility; and (iii) evaluate Sen's claim connecting functioning democracy with a free press to famine prevention."

Here is the student's answer:

Sen's central contribution, in Poverty and Famines (1981), is to show that famines are not natural disasters. He argues that food availability decline never explains famines, since famines can and do occur in years when aggregate food supply is adequate or even rising. Starvation happens when a household's entitlements — the set of commodity bundles it can command through production, labor, trade, and transfers — collapse, even while other groups continue to eat. It follows that famines are always political: if food exists but people starve, someone must have decided to let them.

Bengal in 1943 illustrates this. Sen shows that there was no significant shortfall in rice availability relative to preceding years that saw no famine. What collapsed was the exchange entitlement of rural laborers, artisans, and fishing households: the wartime boom around Calcutta's military industries pushed up rice prices while rural wages stagnated, so these groups' command over food evaporated. Panic buying and speculation made things worse, but the root cause Sen identifies is the decision by merchants to hoard rice and by the colonial government to permit it, making Bengal a case of deliberate, profit-driven starvation rather than impersonal market failure.

Ireland in 1845–52 appears to be a counterexample, because Britain at the time was a democracy and Ireland starved anyway. Sen's thesis that functioning democracies do not suffer famines is thus refuted by his own evidence. Moreover, the blight was a genuine natural shock — a fungus destroyed the potato crop on which the poor depended — so the FAD account is vindicated for at least this case.

China in 1958–62 brings out Sen's deeper point, which is that famines are political crimes. The Great Leap Forward produced deaths on the order of thirty million, and Sen argues that Mao's government engineered the famine deliberately as a tool of policy, using grain procurement to break rural resistance to collectivization. Because China lacked a free press, the scale of starvation was concealed; Sen shows that the absence of democracy, not any failure of crops, was the sole cause of the famine.

I conclude that the natural-disaster view of famine has no remaining plausibility. Sen has demonstrated that famines occur only when governments choose to cause them, which is why famine is best understood as a category of political crime and why the distinction between natural and man-made disasters is obsolete.
