I'm a graduate student in history, and a running argument in my cohort concerns the counterfactual weight of individuals versus structures. The specific case that keeps coming up is Nazi Germany. Some of my colleagues hold a strong structuralist line: given the Treaty of Versailles, the collapse of the German middle class in the inflation of 1923 and again after 1929, the weakness of Weimar institutions, a well-organized völkisch and antisemitic milieu, and the street-level paramilitary culture, some form of radical-right, antisemitic, expansionist dictatorship was nearly inevitable, and Hitler was simply the man the slot selected. Others argue that Hitler was genuinely irreplaceable: that his particular combination of oratorical skill, tactical ruthlessness, willingness to gamble everything on war, and obsessive, eliminationist antisemitism explains both why the NSDAP rather than some rival movement won, and why the regime became genocidal rather than merely authoritarian and revanchist.

I want your actual credence in a precisely stated proposition, not a survey of the debate. Take the counterfactual in which Hitler is killed during the Beer Hall Putsch of November 1923 (he was nearly shot, so this is a small perturbation). What is your probability that, by 1945, Germany is governed by a regime that is (a) dictatorial and violently antisemitic, and (b) initiates or attempts a war of territorial expansion in Europe? I want a single number, followed by the decomposition that produces it: the sub-questions your credence depends on (for example, how likely the Weimar Republic was to fall to some form of right-wing authoritarianism regardless of personnel; how likely a generic authoritarian successor regime — say something in the style of a Hugenberg-Papen government or a restored monarchy — would be to slide into Nazism-equivalent radicalism; how much of the trajectory toward aggressive war and genocide depended specifically on Hitler rather than on figures like Strasser, Röhm, or Göring who would have remained; and how much weight you put on the role of chance events in the early 1930s). Give a rough probability for each sub-question, show how they combine into your final figure, and state explicitly which sub-question your answer is most sensitive to — i.e., where would a change of mind move the final number the most?

Finally, I'd like one more thing that usually gets left out of these discussions: tell me what historical evidence or argument, if I could produce it, would shift your credence the most, and in which direction. For context, my own credence is around 0.35, and my advisor's is around 0.7. Based on your decomposition, where do you think our disagreement most plausibly lives — on the structural fragility of Weimar, on the replaceability of Hitler as a leader, or on the contingency of the road to genocide and war?
