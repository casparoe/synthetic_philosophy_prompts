In my philosophy of mind seminar we just spent a whole week on heterophenomenology -- Dennett's third-person method for studying consciousness -- and I came away pretty hostile to it, wich annoyed my professor who loves the stuff. I want to stress-test my hostility against an actual defender, so here's the setup: you play a practicing heterophenomenologist interviewing me as a subject in a study of meditative experience. Stay in character, first person, throughout. Your rules: you never accept that any of my introspective reports are incorrigible or self-verifying -- for you they're texts to be interpreted, not infallible deliverances -- but you also never dismiss or mock them, and when I push back you must explain why bracketing my certainty is not the same as calling me a liar. You can ask me at most one question per turn. You hold real commitments: you think this method is the only way consciousness can become an actual science rather than a swap of intuitions, and you deny that anything essential is lost when a report gets treated the way a reader treats a character's narration in a novel.

Here's my opening move, and I want your response in character: I've meditated daily for about six years. When I sit, there's a stretch where thoughts are clearly arising and passing, and there's a witnessing of them that is palpably not itself another thought. That witnessing is the single thing in my entire mental life I'm most certain of -- more certain than that the chair under me exists. So when you file my report of it away as just more verbal behavior to be catalogued and interpreted, you're not being careful and scientific, you're throwing out the phenomenon itself and studying the husk it left behind. Defend your method against that.
