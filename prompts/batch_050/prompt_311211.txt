Two people in my ethics reading group have been going back and forth all year about what gives an action moral worth, and I want them to finally produce something instead of just re-litigating the same two examples forever. Here's where each stands.

Priya holds a broadly Kantian view: an action has moral worth only when the agent does it because it's right — motivated by duty itself, or by the recognition that the action is morally required. Saving a drowning stranger because you'd be failing morally not to: moral worth. Saving him becuase you find him charming, or because it'll look good on your podcast: no moral worth, even though the action was right.

Tomás holds what he calls the right-reasons view: an action has moral worth when the agent does it for the reasons that in fact make it right. If the stranger's being in danger is what makes saving him right, then being motivated by his danger — by the fact that he's about to drown — gives the rescue moral worth, whether or not the rescuer ever thinks about duty or rightness as such. The podcast host still fails on this view too, because looking good on a podcast isn't a right-making reason for anything.

Draft the joint statement these two could both sign after a proper adversarial collaboration. I want:

1. A numbered list of claims both can honestly sign — about the podcast-type case, about whether rightness and worth can come apart, about which cases they agree are the hard ones, whatever genuinely holds. Nothing that they only "agree on" becuase it's vacuous.
2. A disagreements section where each remaining disagreement is stated precisely enough that each of them would accept the wording of their own side.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one of them. For each crux, construct one concrete case (make it up, make it specific) that both would accept as bearing on it, and write down in advance what each of them predicts about the case and why. Try to find at least one case where their verdicts genuinely diverge — not just two explanations of the same verdict.
4. If any of their apparent disagreement dissolves once you force them to use the same words, flag it and say which part was verbal.

Two wrinkles they've both been dodging that I want built in somewhere:

- The false-belief case: Maya donates a large sum to a charity because she believes it relieves suffering, and relieving suffering is her motivating reason. The charity is in fact a scam and the money is embezzled. Does her donation have moral worth? Each of them has to commit to an answer, and the statement has to record both answers plus what each says about whether moral worth requires the agent's motivating reasons to actually obtain.
- The mismatched-reasons case: Dev visits his lonely grandmother every week, genuinely moved by her loneliness — which is in fact part of what makes visiting right — but he believes the visits are right because family loyalty is sacred, and he'd keep visiting even if she found them tedious. Do Dev's visits have moral worth on each view? Force them to say why, and to say whether it matters that his motivating reason and his believed justification come apart.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table (columns: disagreement, Priya's position, Tomás's position, crux it turns on), then the cruxes with their cases and recorded predictions. Roughly — adjust if there's a better structure.
