For an undergraduate seminar on populism and the idea of a "general will," I would like a piece of philosophical fiction to distribute as a model text. Please write a short story of roughly 1,000 to 1,500 words on the following premise.

The story is set in a small town that acquires a device — call it what you like — that does what every populist rhetoric implicitly promises: it reads each citizen's sincere, fully informed ranking of any set of policy options put before it, and it reports every pairwise majority verdict among the citizenry flawlessly. The device cannot be lobbied, deceived, or boycotted; no one is excluded, no one is misquoted, no one's ballot is suppressed, and everyone votes honestly. In other words, all the standard excuses for why "the will of the people" fails to be enacted have been engineered away.

The town's mayor, who campaigned on the slogan that the government should simply do what the people want, puts the year's three possible budgets to the device and discovers something the campaign never prepared them for: a majority of citizens prefers the first budget to the second, a majority prefers the second to the third, and a majority prefers the third to the first. The story must include a scene in which the mayor's adviser realizes, and makes the mayor realize, that whoever decides the order in which the budgets are voted on pairwise can determine which budget wins — so that "what the people want" turns out to depend on who sets the agenda, even though every individual preference was sincere and every count was exact.

The story should not collapse into a disguised lecture: it needs real scenes, characters with distinct voices, and some texture of small-town political life. At some point in the narrative, at least two genuinely different responses to the device's verdict should be articulated and taken seriously — for example, the conclusion that there simply is no collective will in this case and populist rhetoric trades on an illusion, versus the conclusion that a fair procedure for breaking such cycles just is what the popular will amounts to. Neither response should be presented as a strawman, and the story may end without settling the question between them, though the ending should be earned by the narrative rather than appended as a moral.

Begin your response with the story itself: no preface, no framing remarks, no commentary before or after.
