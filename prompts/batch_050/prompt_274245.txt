I build agent-based evolutionary simulations as a hobby, and during a design discussion a colleague defended adding a cooperative trait to the model by saying it would evolve "for the good of the species." Another colleague flatly replied that nothing evolves for the good of the species, and the conversation stalled there. I have no biology background, so I can't tell what the slogan actually commits someone to.

Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims "for the good of the species" could be making — from a trivial redescription of differential reproductive success, through selection genuinely acting at the level of groups rather than individuals, to the naive claim that natural selection optimizes a species' welfare — and for each reading: what would have to be true of the causal structure of selection for it to hold, whether it's defensible, and how it relates to gene-level and individual-level accounts of the same phenomena. Say which reading, if any, could actually justify my colleague's design decision in the simulation, and which reading someone repeating the slogan most likely has in mind.

Throughout, separate what is settled among biologists and philosophers of biology from what is genuinely contested — for instance, over the viability of multilevel selection or whether kin selection and group selection are merely different accounting schemes for the same dynamics — and don't present a contested position as the consensus.
