I am a doctoral student working on the ethics of narrative art, and I have been trying to defend the permissibility of biographical drama against a colleague whose argument, as far as I can tell, proves that every biopic is a wrong. Since I take some biopics to be among the more honorable things we do for the dead, something has to give, and I would like a diagnosis that takes a definite stand rather than a menu of options. The argument:

(1) A biopic about a real, identifiable person is, for most of its audience, the only account of that person they will ever encounter; viewers treat the film as their source of knowledge and carry its picture of the person away as fact.

(2) Every biopic, however conscientious, must invent: dialogue no one recorded, inner states no one could know, compressed timelines, merged or omitted figures. These inventions are, from the viewer's seat, indistinguishable from what is documented.

(3) If you knowingly present inventions about an identifiable real person, in a form your audience will take as factual, and those inventions bear on the person's character and deeds, then you misrepresent that person to that audience.

(4) Misrepresenting a real person's character and deeds to a wide audience wrongs that person, or at least wrongs their memory; a disclaimer in the credits does not undo the wrong, since it does not change what the audience actually believes.

(5) Therefore every biopic wrongs its subject, and the practice of dramatizing the real dead is indefensible as such.

I refuse (5), but I grant that each premise has real pull: (1) is an empirical claim that seems obviously true, (2) is a truism about the genre, and (3) and (4) look like ordinary morality applied to a special case. If forced to choose, I would rather surrender the assumption that biopics assert than give up (4), but I am not confident that move survives the reply about what audiences actually come to believe. Please tell me which step you reject, explain why it looked sound, and state plainly what rejecting it costs -- including whether your diagnosis implies that dramatists owe the dead nothing at all, or instead leaves some obligations standing that my colleague's argument merely overextends.
