I'm a senior software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I recently got into an argument that left me stuck. A colleague claimed that ascribing properties to things is ultimately just making claims about how they behave under certain conditions -- that "this function is deterministic" or "this server is fault-tolerant" just means "if you ran it like this, it would do that." I objected that behavior under test conditions can come apart from what the thing is really like: a flaky test environment can make a robust service look fragile, and a forgiving environment can make a fragile service look robust, without the service itself changing at all. He replied that there's nothing more to a system's dispositions than the full set of conditional behaviors, so my distinction is empty. I couldn't refute him, but I still think I'm right.

I've since learned that philosophers have a developed literature on exactly this, centered on the conditional analysis of dispositions and its failures: cases where an object's characteristic manifestation would be blocked by an external antidote or mask even though the object retains the disposition, and cases where the stimulus itself would destroy or remove the disposition so that the manifestation never occurs even though the object was disposed to produce it (the reverse case, where a stimulus installs a disposition on the spot, apparently also matters). There's also a debate about whether a disposition must have an intrinsic causal basis in the object, or whether that requirement smuggles in something the dispositional ascription never needed.

Restate that debate as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually work in: functions, unit and integration tests, test harnesses and fixtures, mocks and stubs, exception handlers, circuit breakers, retries, runtime environments, feature flags, side effects, and the distinction between a system's implementation and its observable behavior. Go concept by concept: the simple conditional analysis, masks and antidotes, finkish removal and finkish installation, the causal-basis requirement, and the intrinsic/extrinsic distinction. For each, say precisely what in my vocabulary corresponds to it and how tight the correspondence is.

Then give me a residue report. Which parts of the philosophical debate translate cleanly into software terms, which parts you had to distort or extend my vocabulary to capture, and which parts -- if any -- have no expression in the language of implementations and observable behavior at all. For whatever doesn't survive, tell me whether that shows my engineering vocabulary is missing a distinction that genuinely matters (and what a language would need in order to express it), or whether it shows the philosophers are trading on an intuition that dissolves once you take the behavioral perspective seriously.

Finally, apply all of this back to the original argument: is my colleague right that there is nothing more to a system's dispositions than its full profile of conditional behaviors, or is there a principled sense in which a system can be robust even though every feasible test would report it as broken? I want a verdict, not just a survey.
