I'm the head of professional standards at a mid-sized engineering consultancy. A few times each quarter, a misconduct case lands on my desk: an engineer certifying calculations she never actually checked, a project manager quietly inflating billable hours, a senior staffer staying silent about a defect he noticed. My board, after a consultant's presentation on virtue ethics, wants a character-based policy: screen hires for integrity, and treat misconduct as revealing a defect of character to be disciplined accordingly. I've countered with the situationist critique as I understand it -- the experimental evidence that small situational features (time pressure, an authority figure's nudge, what peers visibly do) predict dishonest behavior far better than any stable trait, suggesting there may be no robust trait of honesty to screen for or punish. The board has told me to stop arguing and produce a working policy.

Write the procedure our three-person review panel should follow when a case arrives. The panel are senior engineers, not philosophers; they meet monthly and have about ninety minutes per case; their recommendation goes to the managing partner, and careers and firm liability are at stake. The procedure should specify: the questions to ask, in order, about the person and about the situation; what would count as a behavioral record that survives the situationist worry, versus the thin kind of evidence (a clean file in low-temptation roles) that doesn't; the point at which the panel must conclude it has a situation problem and recommend redesigning incentives, deadlines, or norms rather than sanctioning the individual; and the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and the panel must simply decide.

Then run the procedure on my three cases above, including at least one where it yields a recommendation leadership will hate, and close by stating which parts of the philosophical dispute the procedure deliberately sets aside and what it cannot settle at all.
