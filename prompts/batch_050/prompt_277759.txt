Last night my friend and I got into a long argument that began innocently. I said I knew our city's tap water was safe to drink, citing the municipal testing reports. She asked how I knew the reports were reliable; I pointed to the laboratory's accreditation. She asked how I knew the accreditation meant anything. This went on for several more rounds until I finally said that some things — direct perception, elementary logic — simply have to be accepted without further proof. She replied that any chain of reasons must do exactly one of three things: circle back on itself, continue forever, or terminate in a bare assumption — and that on all three options my supposed knowledge collapses, so strictly speaking no one is ever justified in believing anything. I couldn't answer her, yet her conclusion seems absurd to me. Setting aside the water example: is she right that these are the only three possible structures a chain of justification can take? Do all three really fail — in particular, is a stopping point necessarily a "bare assumption" as she claims, and is circular support automatically worthless? And if one option can succeed, which one, and why? Please keep your answer under 400 words.
