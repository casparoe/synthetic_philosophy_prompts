I'm a retired radiation physicist and over the last year or so I've gotten into moral philosophy as a hobby. I've been drafting a short essay and I'd like your comments before I show it to the reading group at our local library. Here's the draft:

---

The ethics of giving people doses they never asked for

In my old field, radiation protection, we worked with a simple idea: every exposure has a dose, doses add up, and even if a single dose is tiny and harmless on its own, the sum matters. So we kept total doses ALARA -- as low as reasonably achievable.

It strikes me that everyday life involves something similar, except morally. When I drive to the shops I impose a small risk of death on everyone near the road. When my neighbour lights fireworks, when someone goes to the office with a cold, when a factory emits a little pollution -- each is a small "moral dose" imposed on others without their consent. We usually don't ask permission, and asking would be absurd. But the doses add up, just like radiation.

My proposal: we should think of each person as having a risk budget that others spend from. Small spends within the budget are fine without consent (otherwise society stops). But three things make a spend illegitimate: (1) it pushes someone's total dose past the budget, (2) the spender could cheaply have reduced it and didn't bother, or (3) the spender pockets all the benefit while others carry the risk -- that's like irradiating your neighbour to warm your own house.

I think this also explains why some risk impositions feel wrong even when tiny: it's not the dose, it's that you weren't asked and couldn't say no. Consent in my framework acts like a discount factor on the dose. A risk you agreed to "counts" less than the same risk forced on you. I admit I haven't worked out why that should be, it just seems right to me.
