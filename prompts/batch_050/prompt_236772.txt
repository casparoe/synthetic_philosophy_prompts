I'm a software engineer (backend mostly, a bit of compiler dabbling on the side) with zero formal philosophy training. A coworker lent me a philosophy of language anthology after I joked that half our production bugs come from two names pointing at the same thing, and I got hooked on Frege's puzzle about identity statements. As I understand it: "Hesperus is Hesperus" is trivial, you can know it without looking at the sky, but "Hesperus is Phosphorus" was a genuine astronomical discovery, even though both names pick out Venus. Frege's move, if I've got it right, is to say names have a sense in addition to a reference, and the two names have different senses. My coworker says I'm definately flattening something important here but couldn't say what.

The thing is, every piece of this puzzle sounds like stuff I deal with daily. Sense vs. reference smells like the expression vs. the value it evaluates to. Two names with the same reference is just aliasing. There's symbol interning in Lisp, hash-consing, pointer equality vs. structural equality (eq vs. equal, == vs. .equals()), a compiler's symbol table resolving different identifiers to the same location. I even half-suspect that in a language with full hash-consing, where structurally equal values get collapsed into one canonical object, the puzzle dissolves, because identity checks become free and there's no room for two "modes of presentation" of the same thing.

So here's what I want. For whatever doesn't survive, tell me whether that means my vocabulary is missing something real about knowers, or whether the puzzle is partly an artifact of natural language that a cleaner formalism exposes.
