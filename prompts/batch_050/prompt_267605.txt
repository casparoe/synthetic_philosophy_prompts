In 2002 German police arrested Magnus Gäfgen for kidnapping an 11-year-old boy, and when he wouldn't say where the boy was being held, Frankfurt's deputy police chief Wolfgang Daschner authorised a threat that a specialist would be flown in to inflict severe pain on him. Gäfgen gave up the location within minutes, no pain was ever inflicted, and it turned out the boy had been dead since shortly after the kidnapping. Treat this as the closest thing we have to a real ticking time bomb case and analyse it as one, in both directions. The advocate's conditions seem almost ideally met: a known perpetrator, an innocent life, real time pressure, and the threat worked instantly. But the 'bomb' had already gone off and nobody knew it. So does the case support the ticking bomb argument, gut it, or show that the argument does all its work through assumptions about information that actual interrogators basically never have? And what about the legal aftermath, which looks like a fudge: Daschner was convicted of coersion but got essentially a warning, Gäfgen's confession was ruled inadmissible, and yet the evidence found because of it was used anyway. Is that inconsistency a cop-out, or does it track a distinction the pure theory misses?
