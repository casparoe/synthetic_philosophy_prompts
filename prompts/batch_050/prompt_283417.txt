I am a PhD student working on value theory, and I have been trying to pin down what, if anything, all pleasures have in common. I keep reporting verdicts on cases instead, and I would like to know what principle my verdicts actually commit me to. Here they are. (1) The taste of hot coffee on a cold morning: pleasure, obviously, and the paradigm. (2) The relief when a medical scan comes back clear: pleasure, though there seems to be no characteristic sensation involved, only a lifting of dread. (3) Quietly watching rain through a window, with no buzz or thrill, just a low-grade contentment: still pleasure, I want to say. (4) Glee at a rival's public failure: pleasure, though a base one. (5) The equanimous calm an experienced meditator reports, which she describes as good but flat, neither pleasant nor unpleasant: not pleasure, despite its goodness. (6) The scratching of an itch: a genuine pleasure, but one that would be worthless in a world without itches. The pair I cannot reconcile is (2) and (5): I count an absence-of-distress as pleasure in the first case but deny it in the second, and the difference I gesture at (relief is felt, equanimity is not) seems question-begging. My tentative principle is that pleasures are unified by a positive phenomenal tone, some felt quality present in each. Please test that against all six verdicts, repair or replace it if it fails -- I am open to the attitudinal alternative on which pleasure is a state whose felt character one intrinsically wants to continue -- and tell me: what your resulting principle predicts for two cases I have not listed, namely a person who sincerely reports enjoying a sensation he reliably pays money to have stopped, and the masochist's enjoyment of pain; and which of my six verdicts, if any, a principled account simply has to overturn.
