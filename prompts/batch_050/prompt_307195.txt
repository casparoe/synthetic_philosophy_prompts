Last night I had this massive argument with my mate Dan about Sen's liberal paradox and neither of us could talk the other round, so I want a proper second opinion. Write me an essay settling the following question.

Quick recap of the setup so we're on the same page: Sen shows that you can't simultaneously have (i) a weak Pareto principle, (ii) unrestricted domain, and (iii) even a minimal liberalism where just two people each get to decisively determine the social ranking over at least one pair of alternatives that only differ in what's happening in their own private life. The standard illustration is the prude and the lewd reader and the racy novel, which honestly feels a bit dated.

So here's the case Dan and I actually argued about. Two flatmates, Priya and Dan (yes I know). Priya gets to choose what colour her own bedroom is painted; Dan gets to choose whether he keeps his bicycle in the hallway or the shed. The trouble is that Priya cares intensely about where Dan's bicycle lives -- she thinks bikes in hallways are an affront to civilisation -- and Dan cares intensely about Priya's bedroom colour because he finds the idea of her sleeping in a yellow room viscerally upsetting, and each of them would rather dictate the other's private choice than keep their own right. Pareto plus each having a decisive say over their own sphere generates a cycle, same as in Sen.

Here's what we disagreed about. My view is the paradox shows that meddlesome preferences -- preferences about what other people do in their own private sphere -- simply shouldn't count when we aggregate, and once you launder or exclude them the whole thing dissolves. Dan insists this is ad hoc and question-begging: preferences are preferences, he says, you can't just ignore the ones that lead to awkward conclusions, and the real lesson is that Pareto and liberal rights genuinely conflict so we have to give one of them up, probably Pareto since rights are non-negotiable.

Write an essay adjudicating this dispute. Consider at least: whether there's a principled (non-result-driven) reason to discount preferences over other people's private choices, or whether that move just rigs the game in favour of liberalism; whether the paradox really is a deep conflict between two values we hold or merely a technical artefact of representing rights as decisiveness over pairs of alternatives in a social preference ordering; and what follows for real cases where preferences are genuinely entangled, like how much it should matter to me that my neighbour is offended by what I do in my own garden. Don't sit on the fence at the end -- pick a side between me and Dan (or some third position if you think we're both wrong) and actually defend it. And please write in British English.
