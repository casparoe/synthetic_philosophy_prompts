I teach a second-year undergraduate philosophy of mind module, and my students have just spent two weeks on the hard problem of consciousness — conceivability arguments, the knowledge argument, the explanatory gap — with most of them ending up convinced there is a deep mystery here. In a single ninety-minute seminar I now want to introduce the meta-problem: the project of explaining not consciousness itself but why we judge, report and intuit that there is a hard problem about it. Plan the session rather than deliver it. Specify the order in which the pieces must be introduced and why that order matters — in particular, whether the meta-problem should be presented as presupposing the hard problem's grip, as competing with it, or as neutral between the two, and what goes wrong pedagogically with each choice. Give me the one carrying example the whole session should be built around (for instance, the students' own reaction to the zombie case a fortnight earlier) and say what that example must contain and must avoid containing to do the job. Anticipate the misconceptions in the order they will arise — that the meta-problem is just one more easy problem with the word "meta" attached, that it is identical to illusionism, that a complete solution to it would show phenomenal consciousness is an illusion, that it is merely psychology of philosophers rather than philosophy — and indicate where in the sequence each should be headed off. Finally, give me the check for understanding that would show the distinction has genuinely landed (something beyond asking them to define it), and state which parts of the debate you would openly refuse to cover at this level and why.
