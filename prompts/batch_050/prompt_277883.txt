My political theory reading group (there's five of us, we meet Thursdays at a pub near the philosophy department) has been grinding through the "equality of what" literature — Sen, Dworkin, Cohen, Anderson — and last week someone derailed the whole evening by bringing up this passage from chapter 3 of Mill's On Liberty:

"As it is useful that while mankind are imperfect there should be different opinions, so is it that there should be different experiments of living; that free scope should be given to varieties of character, short of injury to others; and that the worth of different modes of life should be proved practically, when any one thinks fit to try them."

Her claim was that Mill here is basically a proto-capability theorist: the same bundle of freedom and stuff converts into wildly different lives depending on who you are and where you live, so equalizing resources misses the point. I pushed back that the passage is making a welfarist-instrumental point about social progress — the worth of different modes of life should be *proved practically*, i.e. diversity is valuable because it's epistemically useful to all of us, not because some metric of advantage demands it. We got nowhere, so we agreed to settle it with a constructed case, and I got voluntold to find someone to build it.

So: construct me a thought experiment, one or two paragraphs, contemporary and realistic (no sci-fi, no utopias), involving exactly two people, where equality of welfare and equality of resources deliver the *same* verdict — the two are equally well off, nobody is owed anything — but the capability approach insists one of them is worse off and owed compensation. Hard constraints: no disability example, no expensive-tastes example, no adaptive preferences, and don't just reskin Sen's fasting-versus-starving case, the group will spot that instantly. The divergence has to run through something social or relational rather than bodily. Then do the check: state each person's resource bundle and welfare level, show why both of those metrics call it a tie, and pinpoint which feature of the case makes the capability metric come apart — plus what happens to the verdict if you remove that feature. After that, give the strongest attack a Dworkinian resourcist could make, roughly that the alleged capability shortfall is really a disguised welfare difference or a taste the agent could cheaply revise, and say honestly whether the attack succeeds or the case survives it. Finally, the actual point of the exercise: does the case vindicate my friend's proto-capability reading of the Mill passage, my welfarist reading, or does it show that Mill isn't talking about distributive advantage at all? Don't just stipulate the verdicts — I want to be able to show the group the machinery working.
