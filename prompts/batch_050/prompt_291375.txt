Longtime LW/Overcoming Bias reader here, and I've finally been cornered by an actual philosophy argument I can't deflect. A friend walked me through Fitch's knowability thing and then kept going past the part I could find on Wikipedia, and his extended version seems to prove something absurd. Reconstructing it:

(1) There's at least one unknown truth: some p such that p, and nobody knows p.
(2) If every truth is in principle knowable, then the truth "p and nobody knows p" is itself knowable.
(3) Knowing a conjunction means knowing both conjuncts: knowing the first requires knowing p, and you can only know the second ("nobody knows p") if it's true, i.e. if p remains unknown.
(4) So knowing "p and nobody knows p" requires p to be both known and not known. Nobody can know that conjunction, ever.
(5) So the unrestricted "every truth is knowable" is false. Fine, that's apparently the standard result, whatever.
(6) But here's where he keeps going: by the same logic you can't even coherently believe a specific instance of "this particular truth is unknown" -- every instance is Moore-paradoxical.
(7) And if no specific instance of an unknown truth can ever be entertained, then the general claim "there are unknown truths" floats free of any possible case, so the concept of an unknown truth is basically empty: a bare existential with nothing under it.

(7) is the one I can't swallow -- it seems to make "there are things we don't know" meaningless, and I want that phrase to keep meaning exactly what it says. So where's the slippage? I suspect somthing between (6) and (7) but can't name it, and I'm not sure whether (3) is also doing quiet work. If you think the whole chain is actually valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to drop and what dropping it costs (friendship with my intuition about (1), presumably).
