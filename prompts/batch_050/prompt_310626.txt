Two doctoral students in my department, Mara and Jonas, got into an extended argument after our metaethics reading group, and I would like it settled. The background view under dispute is expressivism about moral language: the claim that to call an action "wrong" is not to state a fact about it but to voice an attitude, such as disapproval, toward it.

(1) Mara opened by saying the view is refuted by a single sentence: "If cheating is wrong, then getting your students to cheat is wrong." Someone can sincerely assert this conditional while being genuinely undecided about cheating — voicing no disapproval of it whatsoever — and can then go on to reason from it. So the conditional's content cannot be any attitude toward cheating, since none is being expressed; whatever "wrong" contributes here, it is not disapproval.

(2) Jonas replied that the conditional simply expresses a higher-order attitude: disapproval of the combination of disapproving of cheating while tolerating getting one's students to cheat. "Cheating is wrong" expresses disapproval of cheating, full stop. Modus ponens then shows something perfectly respectable: that holding the conditional attitude, disapproving of cheating, and yet tolerating student-cheating is an incoherent state of mind.

(3) Mara answered with two points. First, "incoherent state of mind" is doing all the work: on this account a valid inference has been demoted to a clash of attitudes, like intending to run a marathon while refusing ever to train — a regrettable inconsistency, perhaps, but not a logical error, and logic was precisely what needed explaining. Second, negation: "Cheating is not wrong" cannot mean disapproval of disapproving of cheating, since a person who simply fails to disapprove — who has never given cheating a moment's thought — is not thereby committed to cheating's being permissible, and the proposal collapses mere indifference into moral denial.

(4) Jonas conceded the negation point as stated but denied it was principled. The general program, he said, is to assign to every logically complex moral sentence a mental state standing in the right relations of inconsistency and implication; negation and disjunction are engineering difficulties within that program, not refutations of it. And on Mara's first point he accused her of begging the question: insisting that an attitude clash is not "really" a logical error simply presupposes that logical error is something over and above structures of attitudes, which is exactly what the expressivist denies.

I report this as a neutral bystander with no stake in either side.

You are a professional philosopher with expertise in metaethics and philosophical logic. Please proceed as follows. First, think carefully through the dialectic and briefly brainstorm — a few unpolished sentences are fine — the strongest interpretation of each move and the weakest point in each. Second, rate each of the four moves on a scale from 1 (clearly mistaken) to 5 (clearly right), justifying each rating in two to four sentences. Third, state what, if anything, both parties are missing: distinctions neither drew, burdens neither acknowledged, or concessions neither noticed they had made. Finally, give an overall verdict: who has the better of this exchange, on which specific points, and whether the exchange leaves the background view standing.

Assess the arguments as actually made rather than the positions in general, and please do not pad the answer with names or citations — I want adjudication, not a bibliography.
