I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've gotten stuck on a question about my own habits. I don't eat meat, I vote in every election, and I've cut back on flying — yet I keep running into versions of the argument that none of these choices makes any difference: my supermarket restocks in discrete batches, so my declining to buy a chicken only matters on the rare occasion it happens to tip a reorder threshold; my vote only matters if the election comes down to a single ballot; and my forgone flight's emissions are undetectable against the climate system as a whole. Write an essay evaluating this inefficacy objection. Begin by stating it precisely, distinguishing contributions that are merely small from contributions that are arguably zero, and noting that my three cases differ structurally: voting has a fixed and knowable threshold, meat purchasing has thresholds that are real but hidden from me, and emissions seem to involve no threshold at all. Then assess the expected-value reply — the claim that a tiny probability of tipping a large threshold can carry full expected weight — including whether my ignorance about where a hidden threshold sits strengthens or weakens that reply. Consider also whether responses appealing to complicity, to participation in a collective wrong, or to principles I could will everyone to act on succeed in bypassing the objection rather than answering it on consequentialist terms. Reach a definite verdict on whether the objection gives me good reason to stop bothering with at least one of the three practices, and justify that verdict; do not conclude merely that it depends on one's preferred ethical theory.
