I have a set of intuitions about everyday social conventions -- tipping, queuing, jaywalking, and the like -- and I would like to know what moral principle, if any, they collectively commit me to. I will state each case with my verdict, and I want you to find the principle (or small set of principles) that captures the pattern, test it against cases I have not listed, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts, if any, must be abandoned.

1. An American tourist visits a country where tipping is considered mildly insulting, since it implies the staff are not paid a professional wage. He tips anyway, generously, reasoning that it can only be taken kindly. I think he has done nothing seriously wrong, but he has done something wrong -- a failure of respect, not just a faux pas -- once he has been told how the custom is understood locally.

2. An American who believes tipping is a pernicious institution -- it invites wage underpayment, bias, and awkwardness -- visits the United States and refuses to tip on principle, leaving polite notes explaining his objection. I think he acts wrongly despite being right about the institution.

3. A visitor to Germany crosses against a red pedestrian signal on a completely empty street at three in the morning, with no one watching. I think this is permissible. The same visitor crosses against the same signal at noon while a child waits at the curb with her mother. I think this is wrong. Note that I deliver different verdicts on two acts that seem to differ only in audience.

4. A visitor to a country whose deference conventions require social inferiors to yield the pavement to superiors politely ignores the custom and treats everyone alike, even though locals regard this as boorish. I think he acts permissibly, perhaps even admirably.

5. In a city where the convention is to stand on the right of the escalator and walk on the left, a visitor stands on the left, mildly inconveniencing commuters. I think this is a real but trivial wrong.

6. A convention that is genuinely arbitrary -- drive on the left rather than the right -- is nonetheless absolutely binding. I take this as obvious, but I note the tension: in case 6 the arbitrariness of the convention is no excuse at all, while in case 4 the visitor's judgment about the convention's content seems to license defiance.

Here is what puzzles me. Cases 2 and 4 both involve a visitor who conscientiously objects to a local convention and defies it, yet I condemn one and excuse the other; any principle that saves both verdicts will need to distinguish objectionable-but-binding conventions from objectionable-and-liberating ones, and I suspect that distinction is harder to draw than it looks. Cases 3a and 3b differ only in who is watching, which suggests my verdicts turn on something like teaching or norm-erosion rather than the act itself -- but if norm-erosion is the operative idea, the American in case 2, whose defiance is public and deliberate, erodes far more than the jaywalker at noon, and I am not sure I want to weight his wrong accordingly. Finally, I cannot tell whether these are genuinely moral obligations or merely the prudential and social costs of being a guest, and I am unsure whether the answer differs for visitors and for locals.

Please do the following. First, state the strongest principle (or two or three candidate principles, if no single one suffices) that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, stating it precisely and indicating which case motivates each clause -- for instance, whether obligation tracks harm, fairness to those who rely on the convention, the convention's moral content, or the agent's role as a visitor. Second, report what your principle predicts for three cases I have not listed: a convention that is still emerging and not yet settled (say, a norm about phone use at dinner that half the population observes); a convention that locals routinely violate themselves but resent outsiders violating; and a convention with real personal costs, such as removing one's shoes indoors when one has a medical condition that makes this painful. Third, tell me which of my verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate, and whether you would revise the principle or the verdict. Fourth, say whether the obligations you have reconstructed are moral obligations proper or something weaker, and whether your answer commits you to a general account of why arbitrary conventions can bind at all.
