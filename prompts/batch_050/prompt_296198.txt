So I run a little philosophy reading group and last week we got into Lewis's Principal Principle -- roughly, if you know the objective chance of something is 0.7 your credence should be 0.7, absent weird inside information. One guy defended it with an analogy he swore he'd read somewhere: deferring to objective chance is just like deferring to a trusted expert. If the best-calibrated forecaster alive tells you she's 70% confident it rains tomorrow, you go to 0.7, no fuss; objective chance is just the ideally reliable expert, the universe's own forecaster, so the Principal Principle is nothing more mysterious than ordinary rational deference to testimony. He used this to argue the principle doesn't need any deep justification -- it's just common sense about listening to people who know more than you.

Act as a professional philosopher of probability who's seen every move in this debate. Think carefully, and before the final audit do a quick brainstorm pass of every way the expert picture could mislead. Then give me:

1. The correspondence table. What plays the role of the expert, of the expert's testimony, of your evidence about the expert's track record, of the possibility the expert is lying or miscalibrated? I want to see where the mapping is clean.
2. Where it breaks. Some candidates to assess (but find your own too): testimony is evidence you conditionalize on, whereas "the chance is 0.7" seems to constrain your credence without quite being ordinary evidence about the event; you can catch an expert being wrong and discount her, but what would it even be to catch the chances being wrong -- is there a higher court to appeal to; and the undermining-evidence headaches where learning the chance seems to conflict with the principle itself.
3. Which disanalogies actually matter for his conclusion and which are pedantic.
4. Does his move survive -- i.e., does treating the Principal Principle as mere expert deference actually discharge the need for justification, or was the analogy smuggling in the normativity it claimed to explain away? If there's a better analogy for what chances are doing, name it.
