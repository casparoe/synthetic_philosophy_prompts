I was arguing with a friend about whether Bentham’s sexual ethics would seem radical or quaint today. We both know he rejected asceticism and wanted consensual adult sex judged by its consequences rather than by disgust, custom, or religious prohibition. But then we started arguing about technologies Bentham obviously never saw, and it became much harder to predict what he’d say.

Imagine a platform in 2045 with three services: AI companions that adapt themselves to a user’s desires and never lose interest; immersive virtual encounters between real adults whose avatars, bodies, and scripted scenarios are freely alterable; and synthetic sexual encounters featuring the likeness of actual people who have sold revocable consent tokens. The platform also keeps a private pleasure ledger that predicts each user’s likely satisfaction, jealousy, compulsive use, and downstream effects on their offline relationships. Suppose Bentham is somehow resurrected and asked to regulate all this. Apply his hedonic calculus in some detail rather than just saying he would maximize utility: how would he classify the various pains and pleasures, and which would he count as fraud, nuisance, self-regarding folly, or real injury? Would he permit consent tokens to be traded, insist that they expire quickly, require warnings about dependence, or restrict AI companions that make human relationships seem too burdensome by comparison?

I’m especially interested in places where Bentham might be less permissive than expected. Maybe he would defend almost everything consenting adults do in private while still regulating deception, exploitative design, or technologies that predictably make third parties miserable. Please reconstruct the likely Benthamite position, compare it with a modern autonomy-based and dignity-based approach, and say explicitly where the historical evidence runs out and we’re just extrapolating from his general method. Proably the most interesting answer won’t make him a simple libertine or a modern prude.
