A friend of mine who's very into Gödel sprung an argument on me over coffee and I still can't find the hole in it, or even tell whether there is one. It goes like this:

(1) Take any consistent formal system F strong enough to do basic arithemetic. Gödel showed there's a sentence G(F) that F can neither prove nor disprove.
(2) But G(F) in effect says "F can't prove me," so if F really is consistent, G(F) is true.
(3) We can see (2) — we just did, a minute ago. So for any consistent such F, a human can know something that F can't prove.
(4) If my mathematical reasoning were the working of some machine or formal system M, then (assuming I'm consistent) there'd be a G(M) that I, qua M, could never come to know.
(5) But by (3) I can come to know G(M): just run the Gödel construction on M and reflect on the result.
(6) So I'm not M. No machine or formal system captures my reasoning. Minds aren't mechanisms.

Here's what's bugging me. Run the argument again with me as the target. In the first pass, "we can see G is true" did all the heavy lifting. But in (4) I had to write "assuming I'm consistent" — and Gödel's second theorem says a consistent system can't prove its own consistency, so by the argument's own lights I shouldn't be able to know that I'm consistent. Which means the move in (5), "just reflect on it," should fail for me in exactly the way it fails for F. So the argument seems to prove too much: it shows that no consistent reasoner, human or machine, can know the Gödel sentence of their own reasoning. Fine, maybe — but then the justification for (3) in the first pass was illicit, since seeing that G(F) is true requires knowing F is consistent, and the argument's own machinery seems to say you can only know that from outside F. It feels like it saws off the branch it stands on.

I'd rather keep (1)–(2) since those seem to just be the theorems, and I'd like to keep some version of the idea that mathematical reflection gets us something real. So: which step do I actually reject? Is it the slide from "F can't prove G(F)" to "a human can know G(F)," the assumption in (4) that my reasoning is one fixed consistent system, or something in (5)? And what does each rejection cost? Also tell me whether my "proves too much" worry is fair or whether it secretly helps my friend.
