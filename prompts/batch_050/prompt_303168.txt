Write a dialogue between two philosophers, Dana and Yusuf, debating whether genuine self-deception is possible. Dana defends the intentionalist view: self-deception, properly so called, involves one subject intentionally getting herself to believe what she knows or suspects is false, and Dana accepts the burden of explaining how this is not flatly paradoxical. Yusuf defends the deflationist view: so-called self-deception is nothing but motivationally biased belief formation -- wishful thinking with a particular etiology -- and requires no intention to deceive, no division of the self, and no paradox.

Anchor their disagreement in the following case, which you should introduce through the dialogue itself rather than treat as settled background. Mara comes to suspect that her late business partner embezzled from their firm. Unable to bear the suspicion but unable to dismiss it, she hires an investigator and gives him sealed instructions: gather all the evidence bearing on the question, show her only what exonerates the partner, and then destroy the instructions along with any record that the selection occurred. Years later, Mara -- whose memory of the arrangement has faded in the ordinary way, without any further contrivance on her part -- sincerely and confidently believes in her partner's innocence, on the basis of evidence she takes to be complete and impartially gathered.

Have Dana and Yusuf work through at least the following pressure points, with each responding to the other's actual arguments rather than delivering set speeches: (1) whether Mara's earlier act was deception of her later self rather than self-deception, and whether that distinction collapses if personal identity over time is as psychologically disunified as it appears to be here; (2) whether the diachronic structure of Mara's case could be compressed into a single moment -- whether a mind could run the same two-part arrangement synchronically, between contemporaneous subsystems, and if so whether that rescues intentionalism or merely relocates the paradox; (3) what each view predicts about how Mara should be described at the moment she sealed the instructions: did she then believe her partner guilty, innocent, or neither, and does that intermediate state do the work either side needs; (4) whether Yusuf's deflationism can explain why Mara bothered with the elaborate arrangement at all, given that mere motivated reasoning would have been cheaper.

Both characters should be equally competent: give each a genuine best case, let each concede real points under pressure, and have them isolate, by the end, the precise proposition on which they still disagree -- or show that the remaining disagreement is about the word "deception" rather than about Mara.
