At a residency, after I defended an ending in which a narrator lies to the reader and is then exposed by a document that could not exist in the story’s world, another novelist told me not to worry because “fiction is a lie that tells the truth.” The phrase was being made to do three things at once: excuse the impossible document, praise the book’s emotional honesty, and ward off the old worry that audiences cannot genuinely grieve for people they know are imaginary. Please treat the sentence as a piece of language rather than a theory and disambiguate it. Give a numbered menu of the precise claims it might be making—about (i) assertion and authorial intent, (ii) reference to nonexistent persons, (iii) whether emotions toward fiction are irrational, quasi-belief, make-believe, or responses to real general patterns, (iv) what “truth” could mean here, such as correspondence, authenticity, moral insight, phenomenological accuracy, or structural necessity, and (v) whether a fiction can “tell” anything without asserting it. For each reading, state whether it is platitudinous, substantive but defensible, or false; what it would predict about the paradox of fiction; and whether it would actually license my impossible document or merely console me for it. End by saying which reading a careful novelist should keep pinned above the desk and which reading should be banned from workshops.
