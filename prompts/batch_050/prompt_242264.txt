I'm designing a workshop for early-career researchers in sociology and behavioral economics who keep talking past each other about the so-called gender-equality paradox -- the finding that countries scoring higher on standard measures of gender equality tend to show *more*, not less, gender segregation in certain fields, especially STEM. I want a decision tree that sorts a participant from acceptance of that empirical regularity (or from disputing it) to a named interpretive position, so the workshop can stop arguing about labels and start arguing about the disagreements that actually load.

Requirements for the tree:

1. The starting node should be the empirical commitment itself: whether the pattern is real and non-artifactual, given that it depends on particular measures of national gender equality and of occupational segregation. Someone who thinks the whole thing is a measurement artifact should end up at their own leaf, not be forced through the rest of the tree.
2. Subsequent branch nodes should be drawn from the logical space of explanations rather than from a list of authors: for instance, whether the pattern reflects differences in expressed preferences once economic security makes pursuing them affordable; whether it reflects differences in the internalized expectations that shape what people come to prefer in the first place; whether the relevant national variation lies in labor-market structures, educational tracking, or welfare arrangements rather than in headline equality indices; and whether the paradox tells us anything about justice at all, or only about choice under constraint. Add any nodes you think the space genuinely requires.
3. The leaves must include, at minimum: (a) the view that the pattern reveals relatively stable, partly biological differences in typical interests; (b) the view that prosperity frees people to act on gendered preferences, whatever their origin, so the pattern is about economic freedom rather than equality failing; (c) the view that richer, more egalitarian societies have *more* thoroughly gendered cultural scripts about self-expression, so the pattern indicts culture rather than vindicating nature; (d) the measurement-skeptic leaf from requirement 1; (e) at least one position that accepts the pattern but denies it has implications for justice. You may add leaves.
4. Phrase every branch node as a concrete question a participant could answer without knowing which leaf it leads to -- e.g., a question about what they'd predict for a hypothetical country, or about which observation would change their mind -- not as 'Do you believe in biological differences?'
5. Note any combinations of answers your tree's questions allow that no named position occupies, and diagnose briefly whether that slot is empty because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.
6. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and say what further question would be needed to separate them.

Deliver the tree as structured data, not prose: a JSON object with these fields: "nodes", an array of objects each with "node_id", "question" (the branch question, verbatim as you'd pose it to a participant), and "branches" (an object mapping each possible answer to the "node_id" it leads to); "leaves", an array of objects each with "node_id", "position_name", and "one_line_characterization"; "empty_slots", an array of objects with "answer_combination" and "diagnosis" (one of: incoherent, unattractive, unexplored); and "unseparable_pairs", an array of objects with "leaf_pair" and "needed_question". After the JSON, at most 150 words of prose noting the tree's weakest branch -- the question where real participants are most likely to answer in a way that misclassifies them -- and why.
