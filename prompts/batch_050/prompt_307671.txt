I'm in the twelfth grade and taking an elective called "Philosophy and Literature," and for our final project each of us has to commission (or write) a poem that does real argumentative work, not just decorate an idea. I've chosen Pascal's wager, which we read in the Pensées. I don't want the poem to lean on the famous line about weighing the gain and the loss. Instead, I want it built around a less-quoted moment in the same fragment: Pascal's concession that reason, left to itself, cannot settle the question of God's existence, followed by his insistence that we are nonetheless already embarked -- that refusing to bet is itself a bet. (I'm paraphrasing his text rather than quoting it, but please treat those two moves as the poem's fixed points.)

Please write a poem of exactly five stanzas, in unrhymed verse, with the following structure, so that the form itself tracks the argument: the first stanza should present reason's suspension -- the coin spinning, the evidence evenly matched; the second should dramatize the moment Pascal says neutrality is impossible, that life has already placed our chips; the third should render the infinite stakes without using the words "infinity" or "eternity"; the fourth should give the strongest objection its best voice -- my suggestion is the thought that one cannot simply decide to believe, or that the calculation assumes the wrong god -- and the stanza should be genuinely sympathetic to it, not a straw man; and the fifth should be Pascal's quiet answer about habit and practice, about acting one's way toward a belief one cannot argue one's way into, ending on an image rather than a conclusion. Someone who knows the wager should be able to point at each stanza and say which move it is; someone who doesn't should still find it a good poem.

After the poem, please add a short prose note (three or four sentences) doing two things: first, saying where the poem takes liberties with Pascal's actual argument or leans on a contested reading of it (for instance, whether stanza four's objection is one Pascal himself takes seriously or a later objection projected onto him); second, saying what would change your mind about the poem's interpretation -- that is, which scholarly or textual consideration, if I brought it to you, would lead you to restructure one of the stanzas or withdraw the reading the poem commits to.
