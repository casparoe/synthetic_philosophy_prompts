After fifteen years of marriage, and more arguments about love with my spouse than I can count, I sat down recently to write out what I actually believe about romantic love — partly to settle a long-running dispute between us, partly because I realised I had been arguing from intuitions I had never examined side by side. I suspect the set does not hang together, but I cannot see where it breaks, and I would like an audit rather than a lecture. What I want is someone to go through the commitments below, identify which pairs genuinely pull against each other, distinguish those from pairs that only appear to conflict, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and tell me what the least costly revision to the whole package would be — which commitment has to go, or be weakened, and what I lose by giving it up. I am not interested in a reading list or in being told who held which view; I want the reasoning done on the commitments as stated.

Here is where I have landed. (1) If someone asks me why I love my wife, I can give reasons, and they are good reasons: her wit, her patience, the way she treats people who can do nothing for her. A love for which no reasons of this kind could be given strikes me as mere infatuation, or a fixation, rather than the real thing. (2) It would be a defect in me — a failure of love itself, not just of prudence or loyalty — if I left her for someone who was wittier, kinder, and more patient. The very idea of trading up to a superior model is incompatible with loving someone. (3) If she suffered an injury that erased those qualities — suppose she emerged from it irritable, dull, and indifferent to strangers — then a love that ended because of that change would be revealed as having been shallow all along; genuine love is constant through changes in the beloved's character. (4) Some loves are objectionable. Loving a cruel person partly because of the cruelty is a defect, morally and rationally; love is the kind of thing that can be assessed as fitting or unfitting, and is not like a taste in ice cream, where there is nothing to get right or wrong. (5) Love is not under voluntary control: nobody can simply decide to love someone, or decide to stop, in the way one can decide to take a different route to work.

Two requests on top of the audit. First, please do not signal that all five commitments are equally precarious — I want to know which tension is the deepest, and whether fixing it forces changes elsewhere in the set. Second, at least one pairing above looks to me as though it might be a problem but dissolves on reflection; tell me which one you think that is and why it is only apparent. Finally, whatever revision you recommend, say plainly what it commits me to about whether love is ultimately grounded in the beloved's qualities, in the value love itself creates, or in the shared history of the relationship — since I gather the whole dispute turns on something like that choice. Please write your answer in British English.
