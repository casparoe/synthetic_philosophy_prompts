I'm a philosophy professor and last week two of my grad students, Maya and Daniel, got into an argument in the department lounge that started over a playlist and ended up, as these things do, in the metaphysics of emotion. I stayed out of it at the time, but I want to use it in my aesthetics seminar and I'd like a proper refereeing. Here's the exchange as best I remember it:

Maya's grandfather died a few months ago, and she mentioned that she'd been listening to one particular gloomy piano piece on repeat ever since, and that it helped. Daniel said something like "makes sense, sad music for a sad time," and Maya pushed back:

(1) Maya: The piece didn't make me sad -- I was already sad. If anything it made me feel better. So calling the music "sad" is just wrong. Sounds can't feel anything. A piano piece is no more sad than a casserole is. "Sad music" is a metaphor, like "angry sea," and nobody thinks the sea is literally angry.

(2) Daniel: The metaphor story proves too much. Tons of our literal emotion vocabulary started as metaphor and nobody calls "heartbroken" a mere metaphor anymore. More importantly, we hear the sadness in the music directly, the way you can see sadness in a basset hound's face without believing for a second that the dog is sad. The music's slow tempo, falling contours, low dynamics -- these resemble how a grieving person moves and speaks, and that resemblance is enough for a perfectly literal perceptual attribution. Sadness is a perceptible property of the music, not a projection.

(3) Maya: Even granting the resemblance, emotions aren't just shapes and tempos. Emotions have objects and beliefs: fear requires thinking something is dangerous, grief requires having lost someone. The music's sadness has no object and no believer, so whatever quality you hear in it is at best an appearance of emotion, not emotion. And notice what your view commits you to: if sad music genuinely arouses sadness, then everyone who seeks it out on purpose is either confused or a masochist. People deliberately put on sad albums after breakups and pay money for devastating concerts. Your theory has to explain that, and mine doesn't.

(4) Daniel: Two replies. First, emotions can be objectless -- free-floating anxiety, undirected melancholy, the vague dread of a Sunday evening. So the no-object objection fails. Second, you're conflating the sadness expressed in the music with whatever the listener feels. The listener's response might be being moved, which is a positive state even though it's keyed to sad content, or a kind of absorbed attention to an imagined persona -- we hear the piece almost as if it were someone's utterance of grief, and we enjoy the vividness and coherence of that utterance the way we enjoy a great tragic performance by an actor without the actor actually suffering.

(5) Maya: But then you've dissolved the puzzle by equivocation. "Sad" in "the music is sad" and "sad" in "I feel sad" are now two different notions, and nobody has explained why we enjoy the first kind. You've just renamed my original question. Also, your resemblance story has a cultural problem: the link between minor-ish, slow, low music and sadness isn't universal across musical traditions, so it can't be a perception of some real sadness-property. It's learned association all the way down, which is much closer to my casserole than to your basset hound.

That's where they left it. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it's clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible with qualifications, and justify each verdict in a couple of sentences. Then tell me who's more right overall, and one thing both of them are missing. I defintely want to be able to push back on whichever student turns out to be wrong, so don't hedge everything.
