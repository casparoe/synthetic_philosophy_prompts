After forty years running a molecular biology laboratory, I have been reading Heidegger and was struck by something his commentators seem to take entirely on faith: the claim that when equipment breaks down, the structure of our involvements lights up and the world announces itself — that the broken hammer reveals what the smoothly working one conceals. My own experience of broken equipment is rather different. When a centrifuge fails or an assay misbehaves, the breakdown is mostly absorbed by practised routines: we recalibrate, swap in a spare, repeat the run. Theoretical reflection, when it comes at all, arrives much later and for quite different reasons. I would like to propose a term paper (roughly 6,000 words, one semester) examining whether Heidegger's account of breakdown — conspicuousness, obtrusiveness, and the rest — is a phenomenological report that survives contact with actual laboratory practice, or whether it describes a philosophically convenient idealization of failure. State the question so it has a determinate answer, explain what the paper would actually examine (which features of the account are put under pressure by the routine absorption of failures, and whether Heidegger has the resources to accommodate them), and say which reading of the relevant passages would decide the matter and what would count as the project failing. No bibliography is needed; I will supply the citations myself.
