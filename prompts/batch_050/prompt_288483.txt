I have a set of firm intuitions about which people are deceiving themselves and which are merely wrong, biased, or weak-willed, and I would like to know what principle, if any, unifies them. Here are the cases and my verdicts.

Case 1: An auditor with a spotless professional record has a son who shows every outward sign of dealing drugs — unexplained cash, odd hours, furtive phone calls. She sincerely insists, to others and apparently to herself, that he is simply enterprising, and she will argue the point at length. Yet she cleans every room in the house except his, which she has not entered in a year, though she would never say she avoids it. Verdict: self-deception, and the clearest case of the bunch.

Case 2: A nervous job candidate deliberately engineers his own confidence before interviews: a hype playlist, visualization exercises, rehearsed self-talk, all undertaken with the explicit aim of coming to believe he will get the offer. It works; by the time he walks in, he genuinely believes it. Verdict: not self-deception. The transparency of the project seems to disqualify it, though I admit the end state resembles Case 1's.

Case 3: An amateur cook sincerely believes he is excellent because his friends unfailingly praise his cooking; he has never suspected that the praise is polite. Verdict: not self-deception, just honest error. Nothing in him seems to be resisting the truth.

Case 4: A dieter believes his bathroom scale is malfunctioning whenever it shows a gain. He changes the batteries, weighs himself twice, checks the floor for levelness — but performs none of these checks when the number is low, and has never noticed the asymmetry. Verdict: self-deception, despite the fact that his investigations look, taken one at a time, entirely reasonable.

Case 5: A jealous husband intercepts a few ambiguous text messages and, on no further evidence, becomes certain his wife is having an affair; the certainty is sincere and distressing. Verdict: I want to say this is not self-deception, because he is deceiving himself into the belief he least wants, but I notice that the motivation (a need to feel wronged, say) could be doing the same work as the motivation in Cases 1 and 4, which makes me uneasy about the verdict.

Case 6: A smoker who fully and sincerely believes that smoking causes cancer, and smokes anyway. Verdict: clearly not self-deception; there is no false belief here at all, only weakness of will.

My question: is there a coherent principle behind these verdicts? In particular, Cases 1 and 4 seem to require that the truth be present in the person somewhere — the auditor must in some sense know what is behind the door for her avoidance to count as avoidance — but Case 2 seems to show that full awareness of one's own project destroys self-deception, and I cannot see how partial knowledge can be both required and forbidden. And I am not confident that the distinction I draw between Cases 3 and 4 (honest error versus motivated mismanagement of evidence) will survive scrutiny, since the cook also never seeks disconfirming evidence. So: state a principle, or the smallest set of principles, that reproduces as many of my six verdicts as possible; say precisely which clause is motivated by which case; apply the result to two cases I have not listed (for instance, a scientist who stops rerunning an experiment once it confirms her hypothesis, and a grieving person who sets out, knowing exactly what she is doing, to convince herself her late husband would have wanted her to remarry); and tell me honestly which of my verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate and should therefore be given up.
