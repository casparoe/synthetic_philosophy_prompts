My family group chat blew up over a political argument last week and I defused it with the blind men and the elephant: everyone grabbed a different part of reality, everyone's partly right, nobody sees the whole. My cousin, who's Jain, said this is basically what anekāntavāda is getting at and told me to read up on syādvāda. But a philosopher freind of mine pushed back and said the parable is actually a bad vehicle for the conclusion people ride it to. So audit it for me: lay out the mapping first — what is the elephant, who are the blind men, what does "touching the leg" correspond to in an actual dispute — then press on the weak points. Doesn't the parable smuggle in a narrator who can see the whole elephant, which is exactly the position the doctrine says no one ever occupies? And the blind men's reports (wall, rope, pillar) can be stitched together into one coherent animal — but can genuinely conflicting claims (this policy helps growth vs. it hurts it) be stitched the same way, or does the parable only work for complementary perspectives and quietly fail for contradictory ones? Once the disanalogies are out in the open, does it still support the usual moral — humility, every view partly true — or only something weaker, and is there a better analogy for what the Jains actually mean?
