My roommate and her boyfriend had this argument running all weekend and eventually tried to make me referee it, which I refused to do because I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning. It's about Pascal's mugging -- the thing where a stranger says give me $5 or I'll use my powers to torture 3^^^^3 simulated people, and even though the odds he's telling the truth are microscopic, the expected disvalue of refusing seems to swamp the $5. Here's the back-and-forth as best I remember it:

1. She opened by saying the paradox is basically solved by bounded utility. If your utility function has a ceiling, then no finite offer can grow without limit, the mugger runs out of room, and you just refuse. Done.

2. He said that doesn't solve it, it relocates it. Whatever your ceiling B is, the mugger offers utility just under B at a probability of 1 in B-squared and you're paying again. Boundedness only blocks literally infinite payoffs, and nobody needed infinity to get mugged in the first place.

3. She regrouped: fine, the real fix is in the prior, not the utility function. Bigger claimed stakes require more elaborate machinery to be true, so the probability has to fall faster than the stakes grow -- a hypothesis enormous enough to hijack your whole decision procedure pays for that enormity in improbability. The product stays small and you keep your $5.

4. He called that curve-fitting your priors to dodge a conclusion you don't like. For any prior you commit to, the mugger can just scale his claimed stakes until the product beats your threshold. The discount response only works if the mugger politely agrees to stay small, and nothing in the setup forces him to.

5. She said okay, deeper point: you have no evidence whatsoever that your $5 influences the claimed outcome. The mugger's threat is about distant simulations you'll never observe, and treating "I hand over cash" as correlated with "3^^^^3 people get saved" is exactly the mistake. Rational choice should track what your actions can actually affect, and this isn't that.

6. He said careful, because that move proves too much. Most arguments for working on extinction risk have precisely that shape: tiny probability, astronomical stakes, causal mechanisms you can't verify from your armchair. If un-influenceability is grounds for refusal, she has to give those up too -- and she donates to x-risk charities, so she owes him a principled line between the mugger and her own portfolio, not just vibes.

They asked me who's right. I think (3) and (4) is where it gets hard but I could also see (6) being the actual crux. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six, say whether it's right, wrong, or right-but-incomplete, and why. Then tell me who's more right overall, and whether the exchange misses some option neither of them raised.
