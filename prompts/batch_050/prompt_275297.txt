I'm in an undergraduate ethics reading group, and last week a discussion about abortion got shut down in a way that's been bugging me. I had raised a version of the deprivation argument -- roughly, that what makes killing an ordinary adult wrong is that it robs them of all the experiences, projects, and relationships their future would have contained, and that a typical fetus has a future of exactly that kind, so abortion is wrong for the same reason. Someone responded with "my body, my choice," said that settled it, and the group moved on. I lean pro-choice myself, but the exchange felt evasive rather than decisive, and I want to figure out whether it actually was. The problem is I can't tell what claim that slogan was even making -- it seemed to hover somewhere between a legal thesis, a moral thesis, and a conversation-ender.

So: what are the distinct, precise theses that "my body, my choice" could be expressing? I've gestured at a few candidates to get you started, but I want your own taxonomy, not just an assessment of mine. Candidates might include: a narrow legal claim about what the state may compel; a claim that the fetus is literally a part of the woman's body, like an organ; a claim about bodily rights strong enough to license disconnecting from anyone who depends on your body to survive (test it against the standard violinist case -- you wake up surgically attached to a stranger who will die unless you stay plugged in for nine months -- which I think is where the slogan gets its real argumentative bite); and a weaker claim that's really about who gets standing to decide rather than what the right answer is. For each reading, I want to know whether it's a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or an overreach; what it implies for hard cases (third-trimester abortions, a parent who refuses to breastfeed when it's the only food available); and -- most importantly -- whether it actually engages the deprivation argument or just talks past it. I'd also like your verdict on which reading the person in my group most plausibly meant, and which reading a careful pro-choice philosopher would actually want to defend.

Please present the analysis as a table with these columns: Reading (a short label); Precise claim (the thesis stated carefully, in one or two sentences); Strength (one of: platitude / substantive-and-defensible / overreach, with a clause justifying the classification); Handles the violinist case? (yes/no plus what the reading says about unplugging); Hard-case implications (what the reading commits you to about late abortion and the breastfeeding case); Answers the deprivation argument? (does it rebut it, absorb it, or ignore it -- and how). After the table, add a short paragraph naming which single reading you'd advise me to articulate next time, and why that one rather than the others.
