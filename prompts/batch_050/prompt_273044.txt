Here's my opening move: your whole case seems to rest on the claim that we have no idea what women are like by nature, because everything we observe about them has been shaped by education, law, and custom from the start. Fine. But isn't that just an argument from ignorance? If we can't know women's nature, we equally can't know that subjection is contrary to it. And I think it's worse than that for you: on your own view of character formation, circumstances make people what they are. So after centuries of subjection, the traits you call artificial -- deference, dependence, narrowness of interest -- may now just be what (most) women actually are. At what point does the artificial become the nature? It seems your empiricism commits you to treating the current arrangement, however ugly, as the only real data we have, and your appeals to what women might be in some counterfactual free society as pure speculation. What do you say?
