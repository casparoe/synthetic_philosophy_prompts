I spent my career as an experimental physicist, and since retiring I have been reading the philosophy of time. I am currently stuck on the Rietdijk–Putnam argument for the block universe, and I would like a careful conceptual explanation. Please do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — I want the reasoning itself, laid out patiently.

Here is where I stand. I am comfortable with special relativity: the relativity of simultaneity, light-cone structure, the lot. The argument, as I understand it, runs like this. Two observers pass one another at some location on Earth. Because they are in relative motion, their simultaneity slices through the Andromeda galaxy differ, so that some event there — in the familiar version of the example, a decision about whether to launch an invasion fleet — lies in one observer's present and in the other's future. If we then insist that what is real cannot depend on which frame of reference one happens to occupy, the Andromeda event must be real for both of them, full stop. By chaining further observers and frames, the conclusion sweeps across arbitrarily distant events: everything, past and future, is equally real. The universe is a four-dimensional block, and becoming is an illusion of perspective.

What I cannot see clearly is exactly where the argument stops being physics and starts being an additional metaphysical commitment. Please address the following four points, using the Andromeda case as the running example throughout, and write for someone fluent in the physics but not in the philosophical jargon (if you use terms like eternalism or presentism, define them as you go).

First, the argument seems to slide among three distinct claims: that simultaneity is frame-relative, that the future is fixed or determined, and that the future exists now in the very same sense that the present does. Which of these does special relativity by itself actually deliver? State the extra premise the argument needs, as explicitly and as weakly as it can be formulated while still yielding the block conclusion.

Second, the chaining step appears to require that some relation of the form "is just as real as" is transitive across observers and frames. Explain precisely where transitivity enters, and whether insisting on it begs the question against someone who relativizes reality to a frame or to a spacetime point.

Third, as a possible resting point, I have been toying with a view of my own: each spacetime point has its own local reality, consisting of the events in or on its past light cone; there is no global "now," and becoming happens locally, point by point, rather than along universal slices. Does this view concede everything the argument was entitled to while refusing its conclusion, or does it collapse under pressure into either the full block or a smuggled-in preferred frame? Please argue the point rather than adjudicate by name-calling.

Fourth, there is no possibility of the Andromeda decision affecting us for millions of years, so the dispute about whether it "already exists" seems empirically empty. Does the block conclusion have any operational content beyond determinism? If not, is that an objection to the argument, or does the demand for operational content itself smuggle in a verificationism that a realist about physics should reject? Take a position on this and defend it.
