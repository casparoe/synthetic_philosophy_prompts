I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I keep running into disputes about causation when we write incident postmortems at work. Write a dialogue (around 600-900 words) between two philosophers: one defends a counterfactual theory of causation, on which C causes E only if E would not have occurred had C not occurred (allowing whatever refinements the view needs); the other defends an interventionist theory, on which causal claims are grounded in what would happen to E under a suitable hypothetical manipulation of C within a causal model.

Anchor their disagreement in a concrete scenario: a payments outage in which two independent bugs were deployed the same day, either of which alone would have taken the system down, but only one of which actually fired; and a second episode in which the outage dragged on because an on-call engineer skipped the rollback step. Have them argue through at least the following: (1) whether but-for counterfactual dependence misclassifies the redundant-bug case, and what the counterfactual theorist's best repair is; (2) whether the interventionist notion of a "suitable manipulation" is circular, since interventions themselves seem to be characterized in causal terms; (3) whether an omission like the skipped rollback counts as a cause on each view, and at what cost; (4) what each theory implies for how a blameless postmortem should identify a "root cause."

Both characters should be equally sharp: make them respond to each other's actual arguments rather than recite set pieces, and let each concede ground where the other lands a real hit. End with them genuinely divided over whether their disagreement is metaphysical or merely a fight over which concept of cause engineers should reach for.
