In Book II of Plato's Republic, Glaucon sketches a familiar account of the origin of justice: doing injustice is naturally good and suffering it naturally bad, but the bad of suffering it outweighs the good of doing it, so people who have tasted both agree among themselves neither to do injustice nor to suffer it, and justice is honored as a reluctant compromise between the best thing, doing wrong with impunity, and the worst, suffering wrong with no power to retaliate. I keep finding that my intuitions about who is genuinely just resist any tidy version of this story, and I want to know what principle, if any, lies behind them. First case: a merchant who never cheats, but only because the auditors are infallible; remove the audits and he cheats. I say his conduct is just but he is not, and his justice is worth little. Second case: a man who could embezzle with no earthly chance of detection and refrains solely from terror of divine punishment. I say he, too, is not just, though his restraint is firmer and worth more than the merchant's. Third case: someone offered a ring of invisibility who refuses it on the ground that the life of the successful deceiver is not one he could live. I say he is genuinely just, and that this refutes Glaucon's claim that justice is chosen only as a lesser evil. Fourth case: if legal sanctions collapsed tomorrow, I expect most people's just conduct would collapse too, yet I do not think this shows their justice was worthless all along; it was a real good, only a fragile one. Fifth case: the just man ruined by false accusations, whom Glaucon imagines, strikes me as better off than the thriving deceiver, though I cannot say why in terms the deceiver would accept. What principle, or small set of principles, captures these verdicts? I suspect the first and fourth are in tension, since both make the worth of justice hinge on its independence from incentives, yet I judge them differently; tell me whether some distinction between kinds of dependence reconciles them, or whether I must give one up. State the resulting principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and tell me what it predicts for a case I have not raised: a person who is just only because injustice would cost him his self-respect. Please answer in continuous prose, as in a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
