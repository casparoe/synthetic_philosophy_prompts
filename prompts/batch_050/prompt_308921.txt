Here's an argument I cooked up after a seminar on money pumps, and I can't find the exit. Background: the standard money pump says that if your preferences are intransitive -- you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A -- a trader can start you off with C, charge you a small fee to swap up to B, another to swap to A, another to swap back to C, and repeat until you're drained. Hence intransitive preferences are irrational. Now my argument: (1) A preference pattern is irrational if it makes you money-pumpable, i.e. lets a trader repeatedly extract fees from you while returning you to exactly where you started. (2) My friend Maya prefers red wine to white every winter and white to red every summer, and she knows this about herself. (3) A trader who knows her schedule can hand her a white in December, charge a fee to swap it for the red she then prefers, come back in July and charge another fee to swap back to white, and repeat this forever -- she is exactly as pumpable as the intransitive agent, ending each year with the same bottle-type and less money. (4) So by (1), Maya's predictable seasonal taste change is irrational. (5) But acquiring and losing tastes over time is plainly rationally permisible, sometimes even an improvement. (6) So money-pumpability is not a mark of irrationality, and the original argument against intransitivity collapses too -- it's just a prudential vulnerability, like being exploitable because you trust people. The conclusion feels wrong to me; I want the classic money pump to work. So: which step do you actually reject?
