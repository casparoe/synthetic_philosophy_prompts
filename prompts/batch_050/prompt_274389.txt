Okay so I'm in a seminar on the ethics of promising and we keep going around in circles on this and I want to know if the thing I'm fixating on is an actual named debate or if I'm just confused.

Here's the setup. The standard story we started with is roughly: promises bind because they make the other person expect things and maybe plan around you, so breaking the promise lets them down. Fine. But then I keep noticing cases where that story seems to do zero work and yet the obligation still feels totally real:

1. I promise my grandmother I'll visit next weekend. Her memory is basically gone -- she forgets the conversation within the hour, builds no expectations, plans nothing around it, and if I don't show up she will never know the promise even existed. It still seems clearly wrong of me to just not go, and wrong in a way that's about *her*, not about my character.

2. Deathbed promises are the extreme version of this. The promisee is about to stop existing entirely, so they can't even be let down. People still talk about these as among the most binding promises there are, which is backwards from what the expectations story predicts.

3. Flip side: a case where I break a promise and the promisee turns out to be *relieved* -- like I promised to drive them to the airport, I bail, and they'd already gotten a ride and forgotten I was coming. No harm, no disappointed expectations, no reliance. But they could still reasonably say "hey, you promised" if they found out, and it would land.

So there seems to be this whole category where the wrongness of promise-breaking floats free of any actual effect on the promisee -- nobody's expectations get dashed, nobody relies, nobody is harmed, sometimes nobody even knows. My prof mentioned in passing that there's a real dispute here about what the obligation is even *grounded in*, but didn't give it a label, and I want to read up on it over break.

Is there an established name for this debate, or for the view on the "it still binds" side? I have a feeling it's adjacent to Scanlon's assurance stuff and to the practice view (promising as an institution we all benefit from), but I'm not sure either of those is exactly it, since both of those seem to still route through the promisee's expectations somehow. If what I'm describing is just those views restated, tell me that. And please don't just hand me a name -- I want to know how the phenomenon I described actually carves up: which of my three cases belong together, whether the deathbed case is really the same problem as the grandmother case, and where the expectation-based accounts push back hardest.

Also, if I have a confused premise in here somewhere -- like if I'm conflating two different questions, or case 3 isn't actually doing what I think it's doing -- say so directly rather than working around it. I'd rather get told I'm muddled than get a polished answer to the wrong question.
