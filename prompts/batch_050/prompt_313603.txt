Three months ago I inherited responsibility for my company's internal operations handbook — roughly 700 entries, everything from payroll escalation procedures to how the cold-storage alarm gets tested. Whoever holds this job is expected to stand behind the handbook, so I have been thinking hard about what standing behind it actually involves, and I have run into a pattern of thought I cannot find a name for.

Here is the situation. Take any individual entry — say, entry 312, on vendor invoice disputes. I can tell you who wrote it, when it was last verified, and against what. On that basis I believe it is correct. The same goes for entry 313, and every other. Yet I also believe, with equal confidence, that the handbook contains errors: I have already found four, and everything I know about documents of this size says more are lurking. I gather that much is a known puzzle, something to do with authors' prefaces.

What I want a name for is what happened next. Once the thought "some entries are wrong" was in place, it began to act on the individual entries. If roughly one entry in fifty turns out to be wrong, shouldn't my confidence in entry 312 tick down a little? It seemed it should. But then I noticed that my estimate of one-in-fifty had been built in the first place from my near-certainty about the entries taken one by one; once each of those is marked down, it is unclear what the estimate rests on, or whether it should itself be revised, and the revision looked ready to feed back a second time. I could feel confidence in the whole handbook starting to ratchet downward with no natural stopping point — which cannot be right, since nothing about the handbook had changed. I had only thought about it longer.

So my question: is there an established concept for this loop, where the belief "some of my beliefs are false" is brought to bear as evidence against each belief individually, and the resulting adjustment threatens to iterate? It is clearly adjacent to the preface paradox, but that, as I understand it, concerns whether a particular set of beliefs can be jointly rational at a time; my puzzle is about what the aggregate belief does to the individual ones once you let it do epistemic work. It is also adjacent to discussions of higher-order evidence, but those, as far as I know, concern evidence that bears on the quality of one's reasoning — learning that one was tired, or biased, or using a broken instrument — whereas my "evidence" is nothing more than my own beliefs counted up against a base rate.

If there is a name, I would like to know how the phenomenon it picks out differs from the two neighbors above and why the loop is supposed to stop where it stops. If there is no name, please coin one, with a definition sharp enough to distinguish the phenomenon from ordinary belief revision on new evidence, from the static preface case, and from mere generic self-doubt — and say whether the principled response is to find a stopping point inside the loop or to refuse the very first downward step. A bare label will not satisfy me; I want the carving done.
