I'm putting together the final exam for an upper-level course on rational choice theory (mostly juniors and seniors, plus a couple of grad students who audit). We've spent the term on the usual stuff -- expected utility, Nash equilibria, the prisoner's dilemma, backward induction -- and the last unit was on Hofstadter's idea of superrationality: roughly, the thought that when you know the other player is just like you and will reason the same way, you should choose the outcome that would be best *if everyone like you chose it*, which in the one-shot PD pushes toward cooperation.

Here's the crux I want to test. The standard objection I keep seeing students parrot is "superrationality is just rationality plus a false causal belief" (you can't causally control your twin's choice, so the reasoning is magical). But I had a conversation with a colleague in CS that made me think this objection has a soft underbelly: if the two players are literal copies of the same algorithm -- e.g., two instances of the same program, or two queries to the same deterministic model -- then they really do produce identical outputs given identical inputs, so whatever one "decides," the other "decides" too. The student who just recites "you can't control the other player" will flounder here, because the objection assumes an ordinary human-twin case where brains diverge.

So: write ONE essay question for this exam (answerable in about 75 minutes) built around a scenario where two identical copies of the same deterministic decision-making program face a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with each other. The question should force students to take a position on whether copy-symmetry actually rescues superrational cooperation from the magical-thinking objection, or whether it just relocates the problem (e.g., into questions about what a "choice" even is for a deterministic system, or about whether the program's designer is the real locus of agency). It should NOT be answerable by defining superrationality, defining the PD, and gesturing at Hofstadter.

Then give me:

1. A model answer of around 600 words, taking a definite stance (either way is fine, but it has to actually argue, not survey).
2. A marking scheme out of 20 points, broken into five must-hit points with marks attached. For each, say in one line how a weaker student typically mangles that point (e.g., confusing "same output" with "causal influence," or sliding into "cooperation is just what a good person does" moralizing).
3. A short note on what separates an A answer from a B answer in terms of specific moves, not vibes.

One thing to watch: a decent answer needs to distinguish at least three ways the symmetry could matter -- shared source code as a *causal* channel, symmetric *reasoning* as an epistemic signal about what the other copy outputs, and the "who decided" question about the designer -- and a top answer should notice that the epistemic version doesn't strictly require cooperation, since a defection-reasoning copy would also be symmetric. Build the question so that noticing that last wrinkle is rewarded rather than punished.
