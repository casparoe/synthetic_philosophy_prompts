Quick background: no degree, no academic training, I just follow energy stuff as a hobby and argue about it with my brother in law. Here's the thing I've been chewing on.

Germany decided in 2011, right after Fukushima, to get out of nuclear power, and it actually finished the job in April 2023 when the last three plants went offline. To cover the gap it leaned partly on renewables but also on coal and lignite — the dirtiest stuff going — and on Russian gas, which went about as well as everyone now knows. Meanwhile France gets roughly two thirds of its electricity from nuclear and is planning new reactors. Same continent, same evidence about Fukushima, same climate goals, opposite decisions.

I want this analyzed through the lens of the ethics of risk, and specifically through an idea I came up with (if it's old hat, tell me, but work with my version of it first): I call it the accident-vs-background asymmetry. The thought is that we treat harms from rare, dramatic failures as somebody's fault — a meltdown is a thing someone did, someone is answerable — but we treat harms from normal, boring operation as just weather, even when the boring pile of bodies is way bigger. Coal air pollution kills people every single year, predictably, and nobody gets hauled in front of a camera for it. So: if my asymmetry is a real feature of how people actually reason, does it explain the German decision better than the standard precautionary principle does? Where does each account break — Fukushima's radiation killed basically nobody in the usual telling, the German plants were arguably safer than the Japanese one, the gas dependence was a foreseeable cost of exiting, and the dread itself was measurable public opinion that a democracy maybe has to respect whether or not it's "rational"?

Then run it backwards: what does the episode show about the theory? If a rich, educated, engineer-worshipping country makes the higher-expected-death choice for twelve years under full public scrutiny, is that an anomaly for expected-value reasoning, a confirmation that dread risk always wins, or evidence the choice was never really about deaths at all (identity, the anti-nuclear movement going back to the 80s, whatever)? And does it matter for the analysis which country is the outlier, Germany or France?

I want the mapping disciplined: who is imposing risk on whom, what the actual options were in 2011 and again in 2022 when the gas got cut and they could have kept the last three running, what counts as the harm on each side. Not a lecture about how nuclear is ackshually safe, I've seen that one a hundred times. And if my asymmetry idea collapses somewhere, say definately where.
