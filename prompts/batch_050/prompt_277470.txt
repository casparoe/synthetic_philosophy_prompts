I've been teaching an MA seminar on reasons this term — we did a fortnight on the particularism/generalism fight, mostly Dancy with a bit of Ross for ballast — and halfway through marking the essays I realised I've never actually tried to unify my own gut verdicts about cases. I want to know whether there's a single principle underneath them or whether I'm just inconsistent, so treat this as reflective equilibrium done on demand. Please take on the role of a moral philosopher who knows the holism-about-reasons literature inside out, think carefully, and sketch two or three candidate principles to yourself before settling on the best one.

Here are my verdicts, and I stand by all of them even though I'm fairly sure they can't all survive. (1) That the remark would humiliate my colleague counts against making it at the departmental meeting, but at his own retirement roast, where he's expressly asked to be roasted, that very same fact counts in favour of making it. (2) That she's my oldest friend counts in favour of writing her an enthusiastic reference, yet that same fact counts against my being the person who ranks the candidates for the job — not merely a reason to be careful, a reason against me doing it. (3) That I promised counts in favour of turning up, full stop, even when I made the promise rashly, though I concede it can be outweighed. (4) In the surgeon-and-five-patients case, that killing the one would save the five isn't just outweighed — it strikes me as simply silent, no reason at all in that context, whereas in a traffic-diversion case it's obviously a strong reason. (5) And here's my stubborn one: some features never flip, and cruelty is one of them — that an act is cruel is always, everytime, a reason against it, whatever else is true of the situation.

What I'd like back is a numbered list of exactly five items, a sentence or two each, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the single principle (or close family of two) that best captures the pattern, stated precisely enough that I could put it on a slide; (2) which of my verdicts each clause of it is there to save; (3) what the principle predicts about two cases I haven't told you my verdicts on — a promise extracted by blackmail, and the borrowed rifle whose owner has since gone into a violent rage demanding it back; (4) an honest list of wich of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate; and (5) your bottom-line recommendation — if I want maximum simplicity rather than maximum fit, which verdict should I let go first?
