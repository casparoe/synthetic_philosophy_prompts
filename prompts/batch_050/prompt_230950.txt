In my philosophy of religion seminar we have just finished a unit on Molinism: the view that God, logically prior to his creative decree, possesses "middle knowledge" of what every possible free creature would freely do in any fully specified circumstance in which that creature might be placed. A classmate and I have assembled an argument that such middle knowledge is impossible. Each premise struck us as defensible when we wrote it down, but the argument now seems to us to prove far more than we intended, because the same pattern of reasoning appears to generalize to claims we have no wish to abandon. I would like to know where it goes wrong.

(1) Knowledge is factive: if God knows that p, then p is true. Hence middle knowledge requires that there be true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom -- contingent truths of the form "if agent S were placed in fully specified circumstances C, S would freely do A."

(2) Any such conditional is contingent rather than necessary: nothing in the concept of S, of C, or of God logically settles whether S would do A or refrain, since that is precisely what the freedom in question consists in.

(3) Every contingent truth requires a truthmaker: something in reality in virtue of which it is true rather than false. A truth that floats free of anything that makes it true is not a truth at all.

(4) For a contingent conditional about S's action in C, the only candidates for a truthmaker are (a) S's actual free choice in C, or (b) something outside that choice -- God's will, the circumstances, S's character or dispositions -- that fixes what S would do. These exhaust the options.

(5) Ex hypothesi S is never actually placed in C, so there is no actual free choice in C to serve as a truthmaker under (a).

(6) If instead the conditional is made true by something under (b), then what S would do in C is fixed by factors other than S's own free exercise of will, which contradicts the libertarian conception of freedom the counterfactual was supposed to describe.

(7) Therefore there are no true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, and middle knowledge is impossible.

Our problem is that the machinery of (2)-(6) does not seem to stop where we want it to stop. The same demands would apparently rule out ordinary dispositional truths ("if this glass were struck, it would shatter" -- nothing actual shatters it), subjunctive conditionals about genuinely undetermined chancy events, and our everyday justified confidence about how particular free agents, such as close friends whose characters we know well, would respond to situations they will never in fact face. We did not set out to refute any of that. So the argument either proves too much or we have mislocated its real target.

Please identify the specific premise or inference step you take to be the point of failure and explain why it looked innocuous in context. I am most reluctant to surrender libertarian freedom and divine foreknowledge, and most willing to sacrifice the strong truthmaker principle in (3) if that is where the fault lies -- but I want the cost made explicit: if counterfactuals of freedom are allowed to be true without truthmakers, what stops that move from licensing brute contingent truths generally, and does it undercut the original motivation for positing middle knowledge rather than mere foreknowledge? If instead the fault is in (4)'s claim of exhaustiveness, please say what the missing third option is and what it commits the Molinist to. Please also state briefly what the leading rival accounts of divine knowledge (simple foreknowledge, open theism, theological determinism) each gain or lose depending on which premise is rejected.
