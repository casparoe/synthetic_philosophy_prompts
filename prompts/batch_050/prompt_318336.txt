I'm a mature student taking an evening course in philosophy of biology, and our tutor has set us the question of what distinguishes living things from non-living ones. Rather than start from a textbook definition, I want to work out what I actually believe by going on my gut reactions to cases, and then see whether any principle unifies them. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher with expertise in the metaphysics of biology. Think carefully about the pattern before committing to an answer, and begin by sketching two or three candidate principles before settling on your favoured one. Please write in British English.

Here are my verdicts. A bacterium is alive, obviously. A virus is not alive, even though it carries genetic material and evolves, because left entirely on its own it does nothing at all. A mule is alive even though it is sterile, so reproduction can't be the whole story. A flame is not alive, even though it consumes fuel, grows, and can split into two flames. A tardigrade that has been dried out for thirty years is alive, even though in that state it metabolises no more than the virus does. A prion is not alive; it's just a misshapen protein that copies its shape onto others. A mitochondrion is not alive in its own right, even though it descends from free-living bacteria and still has its own DNA. But here is the one that keeps me up at night: I also feel that a synthetic cell, built from scratch in a laboratory from off-the-shelf chemicals but thereafter indistinguishable from an ordinary bacterium, would be alive. And yet a self-replicating machine on an assembly line, indistinguishable in behaviour from that synthetic cell but made of steel, I call not alive -- which seems to make my verdict depend on what something is made of or where it came from, and I'm not comfortable with that.

What I want from you is a principle -- or, if need be, a short list of competing principles, stated precisely -- that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. For each clause of your principle, tell me which case motivates it. Then apply the principle to some cases I haven't mentioned and find interesting: a dormant seed bank's contents, a fertilised egg in frozen storage, an individual red blood cell, the biosphere considered as a whole, and a culture of digital organisms evolving in a computer's memory. Finally, be honest with me about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and tell me which you think should give: the verdict or the principle. I'd rather have a principled reason to change my mind than a patchwork that saves all my intuitions by fiat.
