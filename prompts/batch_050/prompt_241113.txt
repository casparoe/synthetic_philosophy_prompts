While preparing a graduate seminar on ergodicity economics, I assembled the following argument and then became uneasy about it, because its conclusion is stronger than anything I am prepared to defend. I would like a diagnosis.

(1) Any individual decision-maker experiences her wealth as a single trajectory unfolding through time. She does not have access to the ensemble of possible worlds over which an expected value is defined, and no mechanism ever pays her the ensemble average. The quantity that actually determines her fate is therefore the time-average growth rate of her wealth.

(2) For a repeated gamble in which wealth is multiplied at each round by an independent random factor, the time-average growth rate is determined by the expected logarithm of the multiplier, not by the expected multiplier. A gamble can have a positive expected payoff on every round and still drive almost every trajectory to ruin.

(3) It follows that rational choice under risk, at least for such dynamics, consists in maximizing the expected logarithm of the wealth multiplier. Expected-value maximization is simply a mathematical error outside the special case of additive dynamics, and apparent risk aversion is not a preference at all but a consequence of optimizing the correct quantity.

(4) Moreover, since this conclusion rests on a theorem about time averages rather than on any psychological assumption, it holds for every agent whatsoever: regardless of her utility function, her attitude toward risk, her planning horizon, or whether the gamble in question is repeated at all. Insurance is rational, lottery tickets are irrational, and the St. Petersburg paradox is dissolved, for everyone and for the same reason.

(5) But (4) cannot be right. It would prescribe identical behavior to an agent facing a single, unrepeated decision — where there is no trajectory and hence no time average — and to an agent with linear utility and a short horizon, and it would declare the preferences of the expected-value maximizer mistaken rather than merely different. An argument that legislates tastes out of existence and applies asymptotic reasoning to cases with no long run has proved too much.

(6) Therefore at least one of (1)–(4) must be rejected.

Here is what I want from you. First, identify the step or premise you would reject, and state precisely what goes wrong there — I suspect the damage is done somewhere between (3) and (4), but I cannot pin it down, and I do not want the diagnosis to rest on a vague appeal to the is–ought gap. Second, explain why the rejected step looks innocent: each of (1)–(3) seems to me defensible, and I would rather keep the critique of naive expected-value maximization in repeated multiplicative settings than lose it. Third, price the alternatives. In particular, tell me what it costs to instead reject (1) — treating the ensemble average as decision-relevant after all — and what it costs to accept (4) and bite the bullet, since I gather some proponents of this research program are willing to do exactly that. If the right diagnosis requires distinguishing claims that the premises run together (for instance, what a time average is, what maximizes it, and what one ought to maximize), make those distinctions explicit rather than gesturing at them.
