Ok so I just got off a two hour phone call with my friend Marcus and I'm still annoyed, so forgive the rambling. Background: I run a little forecasting team at a logistics company, five people, and every Monday we put probabilities on stuff like "does the port strike actually happen this quarter" or "does our biggest client renew." Ops makes real staffing and routing decisions off our numbers, so being wrong costs actual money, and we review everyone's updates on Friday to see who moved their numbers and why.

The fight with Marcus was about uncertain evidence. Say one of my analysts tells me: "I'm like 70% sure the email from the client said the deadline moved, but the wording was weird and I might be misreading it." Marcus says you should just decide what you think the email most likely said and then update on that, full stop. I kept saying no, being 70% sure of something is just definately different from being sure, and the update should come out different. He said "partial evidence" isn't a thing and I was overcomplicating a solved problem. I've poked around since and apparently there's a whole framework for exactly this (Jeffrey conditionalization, I think?) where instead of conditioning on evidence E you redistribute your credence across E and not-E according to how confident you are that E happened.

Here's what I actually want: turn that into a procedure my team can follow on Mondays without knowing any philosophy. Concretely:

- The steps in order, in plain language: how an analyst should write down a piece of soft evidence (as "I'm X% confident that E," not as E), how to compute the new probability for the thing we actually care about, and what to write in the log so the Friday review can reconstruct it.
- The failure modes the procedure has to catch. E.g. sometimes the observation doesn't just bear on E vs not-E, it smuggles in other information (the fact that the client emailed at all tells you something, or realizing you saw a blurry photo tells you the photographer was there). I gather the simple version of the rule assumes it doesn't, so give my analysts a test for when that assumption breaks and what to do instead.
- The order problem. If Priya updates on her 70% email first and then on Tom's rumor, and Tom does it the other way around, do they land in the same place? If not, tell me what the procedure should say about whose number goes in the report, because Marcus swears order can't matter and I suspect it can.
- A rule for when to just do ordinary conditioning on "I seem to have learned E" as the evidence instead, and how to tell which situation you're in.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) the 70%-deadline-email one above, (2) an analyst who reads a gauge showing our warehouse is at 95% capacity but the gauge has been flaky lately and she gives it maybe 60% chance of being right this week, and (3) at least one case where the procedure spits out an answer I will actively dislike — like telling me a confident-sounding rumor from a usually reliable source only moves the number a little, or telling me two analysts who both behaved correctly end up with different numbers and the procedure can't say who's wrong.

Also: mark the points where judgment genuinely can't be proceduralized (like picking the initial "how confident am I that E" — can the procedure say anything about where that number comes from, or is it just vibes in, math out?), and say what the procedure flat-out cannot do so I don't oversell it to my boss. Keep the math to what a smart person with a calculator and a spreadsheet can do in five minutes per forecast.
