I serve on the ethics committee of a state professional engineering society, and we have been asked to issue a formal opinion on a member-conduct case that has divided us.

The facts, briefly. A structural engineer employed by a large construction firm spent two years designing the ventilation and climate-control systems for a privately operated detention facility that has since been credibly found, in litigation and in the press, to have housed detainees in inhumane conditions. Her work was competent and met code. She was not a decision-maker on the project, had no role in the facility's operation or policies, and there is no evidence that any specific design choice of hers worsened conditions -- a different engineer, or indeed the firm's standard designs, would have produced a materially identical facility. She testified that she had reservations about the project, raised them once with her manager, was told the facility was lawful, and continued the work. The complaint before us alleges unprofessional conduct.

Our disagreement is not about the facts but about what they establish. One faction argues that responsibility for a wrong requires that one's contribution made a difference to it, and here nothing turned on her participation in particular. The other argues that knowingly contributing one's labor to a wrongful collective enterprise can be culpable even when the contribution is fully replaceable -- and that professional ethics should not ratify the defense that someone else would have done it anyway.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate, roughly 700-900 words. It should:

1. Reach a definite bottom line -- sanction, no sanction, or a middle course such as a formal censure or a required disclosure -- with a recommendation concrete enough for the board to vote on.
2. Analyze the case on its own terms, distinguishing (if you think the distinctions hold up) between causing a harm, contributing to it, participating in a wrongful collective project, and merely being associated with it after the fact.
3. Give the strongest version of the losing side and explain why it nonetheless fails, or what residual weight it carries.
4. Address the "if not me, someone else" reasoning directly, including whether its force changes when the replaceability claim is true.
5. Throughout, separate what is genuinely settled -- in ethical theory and in the norms of professional-conduct practice -- from what is honestly contested among thoughtful people, and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus.
