For my senior seminar in formal epistemology, I want a decision tree that sorts students by their stance on the principle of indifference, anchored throughout in Bertrand's cube: a factory produces cubes whose side lengths lie somewhere between 0 and 2 cm, and asking for the probability that a randomly selected cube has side at most 1 cm yields 1/2 under a uniform distribution over side length, 1/4 under a uniform distribution over face area, and 1/8 under a uniform distribution over volume. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: each branch node should be a question a student can answer without knowing any labels — for instance, whether the three-way conflict shows the principle is inconsistent or merely needs a rule for selecting the right parametrization; whether a probability claim can be correct or incorrect in the complete absence of empirical information; whether the symmetries of a physical setup can privilege one parametrization; and whether sensitivity to how the outcome space is carved up is a defect or simply a fact to be reported honestly. The leaves should land on at least: outright rejection of indifference in favor of empirically grounded probabilities (frequentist or propensity); a transformation-group or invariance defense of well-posed cases, of the kind associated with Jaynes; subjective Bayesianism that accepts partition-dependence as the price of having priors at all; an objective Bayesianism that generalizes indifference into maximum entropy; and a view that restricts indifference to a privileged description language. Phrase every node concretely, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate, and identify at least one unoccupied combination of answers with a brief diagnosis of whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
