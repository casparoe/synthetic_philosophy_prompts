I'm organizing a seminar on the ethics of communication, and two of my regulars have been circling each other all semester. One holds that lying and misleading are morally on a par whenever the hearer ends up with the same false belief; what matters is the deception produced, not the linguistic mechanism. The other insists that lying is morally worse in a distinctive way, because the liar puts the full authority of their testimony behind a falsehood, whereas the misleader leaves the hearer to draw the false inference themselves. They argue constantly but, as far as I can tell, agree on more than they realize. Neither, for instance, actually disputes that deliberately implying something false via a technically true statement is often blameworthy; the fight is about whether it's blameworthy in the same way and for the same reason as a flat-out lie.

Write the joint statement these two could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Structure it as a numbered section of agreed-upon claims (about what lying is, what misleading is, and which familiar test cases both sides classify the same way), followed by a table of their residual disagreements, each formulated neutrally enough that both parties would accept the wording of their own position. Then identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would push at least one side to revise. Candidates you should consider include whether conversational implicature can carry the same commitment as what's literally said, whether the hearer's contribution to being misled matters morally, whether the wrong of lying is a wrong against the hearer, against the practice of testimony, or against the liar's own integrity, and whether the intuitive badness of particular lies is actually explained by something other than lying per se. For each crux, specify a concrete case or thought experiment both parties agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side's theory predicts about that case, so that the predictions could in principle be checked against intuition.

Where the disagreement strikes you as partly verbal — for instance, if the two sides are using "commitment" or "responsibility for the false belief" differently — say so explicitly and explain why. Avoid strawmen: do not portray the lying-is-worse camp as committed to the claim that misleading is harmless, nor the no-moral-difference camp as unable to explain why bald-faced lies feel worse than sly insinuations. Both characters in this document are competent philosophers who have read each other's arguments carefully. The answer should be written in American English.
