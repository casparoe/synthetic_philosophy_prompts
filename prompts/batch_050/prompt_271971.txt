I'm a teaching assistant for a seminar on philosophy of law, and last week a student pushed back on the whole "responsibility gap" literature on autonomous weapons and self-driving cars with an analogy I keep thinking about. His point was roughly: we solved this problem already, with corporations. A corporation isn't a person, has no mental states, can't be punished in any sense that touches it the way prison touches a human, and yet we hold corporations criminally and civilly responsible all the time -- fines, sanctions, orders, sometimes even "criminal intent" attributed to the company as a whole. So, he argued, worrying about responsibility gaps for autonomous systems is just forgetting that law and morality have long had a mechanism for assigning responsibility to non-human agents. If a self-driving car kills someone and no engineer, executive, or passenger is fairly to blame, we can just treat the car (or the company behind it) as the responsible party, the way we do with firms, and there's no real philosophical problem left.

The seminar got stuck here because the analogy does seem to do real work in actual debates, but I suspect it imports more than it can carry. So: how far does it hold? Lay out explicitly what would have to map onto what -- corporate mens rea doctrines, the vicarious liability of employers, the fact that corporations are made of identifiable humans whose decisions get aggregated, versus the opaque learned behavior of a trained system -- and figure out where the mapping breaks. In particular, I want to know which of the standard moves for corporations (fining the entity, attributing collective intent, restructuring it as "punishment") actually survive the transfer to a single autonomous system, and which secretly depended on there being blameworthy humans somewhere inside the firm that the responsibility-gap case is precisely defined by lacking. Also: does it matter for the analogy that corporate responsibility is usually treated as *derived* -- a legal fiction layered on top of, not a replacement for, individual responsibility?

Finally, and this is the part I care about most for writing up the seminar discussion: once the disanalogies are on the table, what conclusion about the responsibility gap survives? Does the corporation case show that gaps can be benignly "closed by convention," as my student thinks, or does it show the opposite -- that when we can't find responsible humans we just redistribute costs to whoever has the deepest pockets, which isn't responsibility at all? If neither, what would a better analogy be for what we actually do when we sanction an entity that can't suffer, understand blame, or reform itself?
