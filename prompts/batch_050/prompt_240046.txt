Two colleagues of mine, both in a history-and-philosophy-of-science reading group, have been arguing about what relativity did to Newtonian mechanics, and the argument has spiraled. Here is the exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Marcus opened with what he called the textbook story: Newtonian mechanics was falsified and replaced. It says mass is an intrinsic, velocity-independent property and that simultaneity is absolute; relativity denies both, and it got Mercury's anomalous perihelion precession right where Newtonian theory couldn't. Old theory wrong, new theory right — that's how science progresses.

(2) Dana replied that this is a caricature. Engineers still build bridges and satellites with Newtonian mechanics, and relativistic equations reduce to Newtonian ones at low velocities. The old theory wasn't refuted; its domain of applicability was discovered. Science here was cumulative, not revolutionary — the new absorbed the old as a limiting case.

(3) Marcus invoked Kuhn: the smoothness Dana describes is an illusion. Yes, the word "mass" survives, but the concept doesn't — Newtonian mass is conserved and frame-invariant, relativistic mass is frame-dependent and bound up with energy. Because theoretical terms get their meaning from the theory they're embedded in, Newton and Einstein aren't even making comparable claims about the same thing. That's incommensurability, and the "limiting case" story is just Whig history written by the winners.

(4) Dana answered that this proves too much. Marcus's opening move cited Mercury's orbit as a decisive point in Einstein's favor — but that comparison is only meaningful if both theories make predictions about the *same* observable phenomenon, i.e., if they're commensurable. If rival paradigms were genuinely without common measure, no empirical anomaly could ever adjudicate between them, and Marcus's own argument would be unintelligible. Besides, physicists move between the two frameworks fluently, and even Kuhn softened his early claims into something more local.

(5) Marcus's rejoinder: the low-velocity derivation recovers only numerical approximations of the old equations, not the old concepts — Newtonian mass isn't relativistic mass at small v; it's just close enough for bridge-building. Partial, patchwork comparability of the sort Dana describes is exactly what Kuhn's later "local incommensurability" amounts to, so Dana's citing Kuhn's retreat actually concedes Marcus's point. Shared observational vocabulary like "Mercury's position" is thin enough to permit limited testing without restoring full conceptual continuity.

They've both appealed to me and I genuinely can't settle it. Please adjudicate move by move: assess each of the five moves on its own merits, saying what it gets right and where it overreaches, and identify any equivocations or shared assumptions driving the disagreement (I'm suspicious that "incommensurability," "refutation," and even "mass" are shifting meaning mid-argument). Then give an overall verdict on who is more right, and on what — if anything — both are missing about how conceptual change and empirical comparison can coexist in a theory transition like this one.
