I am a PhD student in philosophy of language preparing the prospectus for a dissertation chapter on fictional discourse, and before I defend it I want an honest stress-test of the position I have assembled. Here is what I currently hold:

(1) Direct reference about names: the contribution of a proper name to the proposition expressed is simply its bearer. There is no descriptive content mediating reference, and this holds for names in fiction and names outside it alike.

(2) Artefactualism about fictional characters: fictional characters are abstract artifacts brought into being by acts of storytelling. Conan Doyle created such an object, and the name "Sherlock Holmes," as we now use it, refers to that object. Accordingly, "Sherlock Holmes is a fictional character" and "Sherlock Holmes is more famous than any actual detective" are literally true.

(3) Negative existentials are non-negotiable data: when an ordinary speaker, or a child disabused of a belief, says "Sherlock Holmes does not exist," she speaks the plain truth. Any semantic theory that counts such utterances as false has refuted itself, whatever its other virtues.

(4) A strict internal/external division of labor for fictional truth: "Sherlock Holmes lives in Baker Street," uttered without qualification, is literally false, since abstract artifacts do not live anywhere; "According to the stories, Sherlock Holmes lives in Baker Street" is literally true, with the story-prefix functioning as a sentential operator that does all the work of locating the claim within the fiction.

(5) No reference at the point of authorship: when Doyle wrote the stories he was pretending to use a name of a person, and nothing was referred to by his uses of "Sherlock Holmes" at that time. Reference, if there is any, belongs to our later practice, not to his.

My questions: Which of these commitments genuinely stand in tension with one another, and which only appear to? I would like the genuine tensions ranked by severity, with each one diagnosed precisely — that is, state exactly which pair (or triple) of theses generates the problem and why the standard motivations for each pull in opposite directions. For the merely apparent tensions, explain briefly why they dissolve. Then, for each genuine tension, identify the least costly revision and what it costs. Finally, I do not want a menu of options: please commit to a single verdict about which commitment in the set I should abandon or weaken first, defend that choice against the runner-up, and say how confident you are in it and what consideration would most plausibly overturn your recommendation.
