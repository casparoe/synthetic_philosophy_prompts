Okay so background first: I retired four years ago after 35 years in pharmaceutical chemistry, and about a year ago a neighbor dragged me into a philosophy reading group that meets at the public library. The group read Freud on religion this spring and I got kind of obsessed, and I've been drafting notes toward an essay I might actually try to publish in some little magazine or at least inflict on the group. Below are my notes so far. What I want from you is comments -- especially on whether I've gotten Freud right, whether my main arguement actually works, and whether the last section belongs in the essay at all.

NOTES:

1. Main claim: In The Future of an Illusion Freud defines an "illusion" as a belief held because it fulfills a wish, where the believer doesn't care much about evidence either way. Crucially, Freud himself admits an illusion isn't necessarily false -- it could turn out true, he just says that's a lucky accident. My thesis: that admission sinks his whole debunking project, because showing a belief is wish-driven is a claim about its origin, not its truth. Calling religion false on the basis of a psychological explanation is the genetic fallacy, full stop.

2. Freud's actual argument seems to be: religious ideas are illusions, illusions are formed without regard for evidence, therefore we should give them up. But there's a missing premise -- something like "beliefs formed by wish-fulfillment in this domain are unreliable." He never defends that premise, as far as I can tell. In my old line of work, a drug discovered by a researcher who desperately wanted it to work still had to pass the trials. Motive of discovery doesn't settle anything.

3. Possible objection I need to handle: maybe Freud isn't arguing religion is false, just that believers have no good reasons. That's weaker and maybe fair. But then the essay's punchy title and tone overpromise, and I want to say Freud the polemicist keeps outrunning Freud the careful definer of terms.

4. Second half: In Civilization and Its Discontents he discusses the "oceanic feeling" (a phrase he credits to Romain Rolland) -- this sense of oneness with the universe that some people report as the core of religious experience. Freud traces it to the infant's undifferentiated state before the ego separates from the world. My point: this is again an explanation of origins. Tracing a feeling to infancy says nothing about whether the feeling tracks anything real. A biologist can explain where my fear of snakes comes from evolutionarily without showing snakes aren't dangerous.

5. Last section, the one I'm unsure about: I compare Freud's notion of illusion to what psychologists now call motivated reasoning or identity-protective cognition -- people process evidence about climate change or vaccines in ways that protect their group identity. My worry: modern research actually *supports* Freud's missing premise in some domains (wish-driven cognition IS unreliable), which might rescue him -- but then his argument becomes an empirical bet he never made, rather than the knockdown he wanted. Is this section a good complication or a total distraction?

So: comments on accuracy (have I mangled Freud anywhere?), on whether the genetic fallacy charge survives the objection in note 3, and on overall structure. Be blunt -- I spent my career getting peer reviewed, I can take it.
