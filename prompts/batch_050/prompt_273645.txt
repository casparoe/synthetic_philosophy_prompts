ok so the blind men and the elephant. this parable gets quoted constantly in rationalist-adjacent spaces — someone stakes out a confident claim about consciousness or AI or god or whatever, and someone replies: we're all groping different parts of the elephant, everyone has a partial truth, nobody should be confident. it's basically the folk version of jain anekantavada, and it does a LOT of work licensing epistemic humility and arguing against one-true-model thinking.

but something about it has always bugged me: the parable is narrated from the perspective of someone who can see the whole elephant. the sighted king (or whoever) *knows* it's an elephant — so the story smuggles in exactly the god's-eye view it's supposed to be arguing we can't have. also the blind men could in principle just pool their observations and triangulate; the parable only works because they argue instead of comparing notes, which is a failure of Aumann-ing, not a failure of knowledge per se.

audit the analogy for me: what's the actual correspondence table (elephant = ?, groping = ?, the sighted narrator = ?, the quarreling = ?), which disanalogies matter for the "so stop being confident" use and which are just noise, and does that conclusion survive once the breaks are on the table? also, does the actual jain doctrine have this same problem or is it doing something more careful than the pop parable? and if the parable fails, what's the better analogy for the thing people are reaching for?
