Last night a friend and I argued for nearly three hours about whether dreams are experiences had while asleep, and we ended the evening exactly where we started, each convinced the other was simply failing to understand something obvious. I'd like a diagnosis of what is actually going wrong in debates of this kind, and what -- if anything -- would move one forward.

The dispute, as we reconstructed it, runs roughly as follows. On one side (associated with Norman Malcolm): our only criterion for whether someone dreamed is what they sincerely report upon waking. There is no independent way to check whether the reported episode was experienced during sleep, and so the idea that dreams are experiences occurring while we sleep is an unwarranted extension of a concept whose use is anchored entirely in waking reports. Certain consequences seem built in: one cannot, while genuinely asleep, judge that one is asleep; and to suppose the report could be systematically wrong about whether any experience occurred is to suppose something we have no coherent way to verify. On the other side (associated with Daniel Dennett): this treats the absence of a verification procedure as a conclusion about what could possibly be true. The waking report might be a retrospective confabulation -- a narrative assembled at or near waking -- bearing no reliable relation to whatever, if anything, went on during sleep. We should be open to the possibility that we are radically mistaken about our own dreams, just as we can be mistaken about other episodes of our mental lives.

My friend held that this is a straightforward empirical disagreement that sleep science will eventually settle -- we can correlate reports with physiology, wake people mid-dream, study lucid dreamers who signal from within the dream, and so on. I suspect something deeper is at stake: that the two sides are really disagreeing about what could possibly count as evidence about one's own experience -- one side granting first-person memory reports a kind of final authority, the other insisting such reports are just more data, as corrigible as any other. If I'm right, the physiological evidence my friend is waiting for would be interpreted differently by each side and settle nothing.

So: is this a substantive disagreement about the temporal location of experiences, a verbal disagreement trading on an ambiguous use of the word experience, a methodological disagreement about first-person authority, or two parties answering different questions under one heading? Diagnose the impasse using what each position actually commits to -- and in particular, assess whether the sleep-laboratory evidence (staged awakenings, lucid-dreamer signaling, physiological correlates) can bear the weight my friend wants it to bear, or whether each side can absorb it into its own framework. Finally, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a needed distinction, a possible experiment, a formal or conceptual result -- or an argument that no progress should be expected and why.
