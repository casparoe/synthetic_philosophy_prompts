A friend and I spent all evening arguing about whether multiple realizability really refutes the identity theory of mind, and we got stuck in the same place we always do. Her view: the entire argument is an artifact of how we describe things. Describe a system coarsely enough and almost anything realizes almost any program — she claims that on a sufficiently charitable description, a wall can be said to run a word processor; describe it finely enough and no two systems ever share the same functional organization, so multiply realized minds never actually occur. If the level of description is doing all the work, then multiple realizability tells us something about our vocabulary and nothing about the metaphysics of mind.

Before we resume, I want a standard I can hold any account of realization to, so we stop talking past each other. Write the desiderata: what an adequate theory of what it is for a physical system to realize a functional organization must deliver. At minimum, address these: it must exclude the wall without excluding genuinely alien hardware (silicon-based minds, neurons made of different stuff); it must specify a non-arbitrary grain at which functional descriptions are legitimate; it must take a position on whether multiply realized functional kinds are genuine scientific kinds or covertly disjunctive pseudo-kinds; and it must make clear what, if anything, follows for psychophysical identity once its own constraints are accepted. Give the case or intuition behind each requirement, say which desiderata you'd treat as negotiable and which as fixed points, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — if the list itself is inconsistent, that would be the interesting result. Close with a brief scoring of three contenders against it — a causal-role account, a mechanistic account, and a similarity-based account — but keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scorecard, and don't bother with citations; I'll fill those in myself.
