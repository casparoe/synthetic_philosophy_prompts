For a politics seminar I tried to turn a friend's argument for workfare against itself and now I can't tell whether I refuted it or broke it somewhere. His view: it's fair that unemployed people on benefits have to do work placements or training courses to keep getting paid, since the money comes out of other people's wages. I reconstructed his argument and extended it:

(1) When the state hands you something paid for out of other people's earnings, it's fair to ask for something in return.
(2) Out-of-work benefits are paid for out of other people's earnings.
(3) So it's fair to make able-bodied recipients work or train as a condition of keeping their benefits.
(4) But everything the state provides is paid for out of other people's earnings — the fire brigade, the courts, the roads, state schools, all of it.
(5) So it would be just as fair to make the family whose house the fire service saved work off the cost, or require parents to volunteer before their kid can enroll at the local school, or make a burglary victim put in community hours before the police open a case.
(6) Those are clearly not fair. Nobody thinks you owe the state labour as a condition of your kids being taught to read or your assault being investigated.
(7) So (1) has to go: the "it's other people's money, so they can ask for something back" principle is false, and the workfare argument built on it fails.

The trouble is I actually found his original point pretty plausible, and every premise of my extension looks fine to me when I stare at it on its own. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If I have to choose I'd rather keep the workfare conclusion (3) than keep (1) in its full generality, but I want to know what I'm paying either way. And tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis — flag the specific step you're least sure about.
