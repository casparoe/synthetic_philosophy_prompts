I co-run a reading group on formal epistemology, and for the past two months we've been stuck on what the no-free-lunch theorems from the machine-learning and optimization literature do or don't do to the classical problem of induction. Two members have become the spokespeople for the deadlock. One takes the theorems to vindicate Hume: averaged over all possible target functions, every learning algorithm performs identically to random guessing, so no domain-independent justification of induction exists, and any algorithm's success in the actual world is a brute fact about our universe, not a rational achievement. The other accepts the mathematics but denies the philosophical upshot: the averaging is done over a uniform distribution on all possible worlds, most of which are utterly chaotic, and to weigh those worlds equally is itself a substantive and, she thinks, indefensible prior; inductive success requires only that the actual world be compressible, and a bias toward simple hypotheses is justified by our best evidence that it is. Their arguments keep sliding past each other — he says she's smuggling in the very inductive assumption at issue, she says he's smuggling in a prior far stronger than anything an inductivist needs — and I've asked them to produce an adversarial collaboration instead of another open discussion.

Draft the joint statement they should write. It should contain: first, a numbered list of agreed claims, including what the no-free-lunch theorems strictly establish and what they do not establish (e.g., that they presuppose a way of averaging over environments, that no algorithm dominates another over all universes, that this is a theorem about distributions over problems rather than about any particular problem); second, a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated in neutral wording that both parties would sign, with a separate column stating each side's position in wording that side itself would accept — I want to see disagreements formulated at the level of "what would count as a justification of induction" rather than restatements of the theorem; third, for each disagreement, an identification of the crux: the sub-question such that resolving it would move at least one of them. I suspect one crux is whether the uniform prior over environments is philosophically innocent or question-begging, and another is whether a justification of induction must be non-circular in Hume's sense or whether a rule-circular or pragmatic vindication suffices — but let them discover their own cruxes too.

For each crux, the statement should record the argument, case, or formal result that both agree would bear on it, along with each side's prediction written down in advance — for instance, what each would say about a learner operating in a universe generated by an adversary versus one generated by a short program, and whether the formalism of algorithmic probability changes anything for either of them. Finally, the document should close with a section both sign that identifies which parts of their dispute turned out, on inspection, to be verbal — disputes about what "justification," "prior," or "the problem of induction" mean — and which part remains a live disagreement even after the terms are fixed. Do not adjudicate; the deliverable is the document itself, phrased so that both members would put their names to it.
