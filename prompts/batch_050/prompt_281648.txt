I'm preparing a one-page position statement for a working group on formal epistemology and would like a consistency audit before I circulate it. My working example is a variant of the Judy Benjamin problem. A hiker is lost in one of two regions, North and South, and her credence is split evenly between them. Rescue HQ radios a message whose entire content is this: if she is in the North, the objective chance that the weather will hold long enough for a pickup is 0.9; if she is in the South, it is 0.1. The question the case presses is what her credence in being in the North should become upon receiving that message.

My commitments are as follows:

1. When a proposition is learned with certainty, updating by Bayesian conditionalization on it is rationally required, not merely permitted.
2. When evidence falls short of certainty, Jeffrey conditionalization on the appropriate partition is the correct generalization, with strict conditionalization as its limiting case.
3. In the hiker case, the message favors neither region — the two conditional chances are symmetric in form and concern only the weather — so her credence in North should remain 0.5.
4. The Principal Principle: conditional on the known objective chance of an outcome, one's credence in that outcome should equal that chance.
5. Rational belief change is conservative: where two updates accommodate the same evidence, the one departing less from the prior distribution is to be preferred, and information-minimization is the right formal expression of this conservatism.

Please audit the set. Which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what would each genuine tension cost to repair? I do not want every conflict treated as equally serious. Give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: the most severe tension first, then the next most severe, then the apparent tension or tensions that dissolve on inspection, then the single revision that would restore the most coherence to the set, and finally the residual cost of making that revision.
