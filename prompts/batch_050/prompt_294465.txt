I am a novelist at work on a long narrative about a woman who, midway through a well-ordered life, becomes convinced that the universe is indifferent to her projects and that nothing she undertakes has any significance beyond what she lends it. I do not want her eventual response to that conviction to be a thin composite of familiar moves, so I have been cataloguing the philosophically serious answers to the problem of cosmic insignificance and the absurd, intending to give her one that is not simply borrowed. My catalogue so far:

First, the response of revolt in Camus's sense: sustained lucid awareness of the clash between our demand for meaning and the world's silence, met with defiance and a preference for the quantity and intensity of lived experience over any appeal to a higher order.

Second, Thomas Nagel's response of irony: acknowledge that nothing can be justified all the way down, continue to take one's life seriously anyway, and regard one's own earnestness with amused detachment rather than despair.

Third, Richard Taylor's proposal that meaning can be generated by subjective engagement alone: Sisyphus is to be imagined with a will aimed at the stone-rolling itself, so that even a futile task can fill a life from within, without the task needing any point beyond the willing.

Fourth, Susan Wolf's bipartite account, on which a life is meaningful when subjective attraction meets objective worth — when one actively loves things worth loving. As I understand it, this largely sidesteps the absurd rather than confronting it, since it relocates meaning to a plane where the universe's indifference is simply irrelevant.

Fifth, the religious leap: answer the world's silence not with argument but with trust in a God whose purposes exceed what reason can establish, in something like Kierkegaard's sense.

What I would like from you is a sixth response, genuinely different in structure from these five — not a blend of two of them, not a special case, and not merely one of them transposed into a new vocabulary. It must be something a person could plausibly live, sustained over years rather than asserted in an afternoon, because it has to carry a character through several hundred pages. Please describe the response itself, then say what it illuminates about the absurd that my five leave in shadow, and finally tell me candidly what I would pay for it as a novelist: what it costs in drama, in sympathy, or in believability that the others do not.
