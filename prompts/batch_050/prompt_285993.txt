I run a philosophy of language seminar where, every year, the same fight breaks out: one faction of students takes Putnam's Twin Earth to settle the matter against semantic internalism, and the other insists that Twin Earth cases only show something about reference and leave the real notion of content—the one that figures in psychological explanation and rational assessment—untouched. This year I want to end the cycle by having the two factions produce a joint document instead of trading their usual speeches. Draft the adversarial-collaboration statement they should come back with.

In it, I want the following:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. These should include whatever both sides can genuinely sign about the original case (the 1750s English-speaker, the twin on the planet where the clear, drinkable liquid is a chemically different compound), about the distinction between what a word picks out and what a speaker is in a position to know, and about what would have to be true for the case to bear on thought content rather than merely on the public reference of words.

2. A table of precise disagreements, each stated in wording the corresponding side would accept as a fair description of its own view—no strawmen, and where either side would say the other has mislocated the dispute, note that in the table rather than resolving it by fiat.

3. A section on cruxes. For each crux, give a case or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. At minimum, work through three candidate cruxes: (a) whether content must be able to play its explanatory role in psychology without mentioning anything the thinker could not discriminate, tested against the well-known case of the patient who complains of arthritis in the thigh; (b) whether privileged first-person access survives externalism, tested against the argument that an externalist who knows a priori that she is thinking about water could apparently deduce substantive facts about her environment without leaving her armchair; and (c) the empty-case problem: a community on a planet with no liquid at all, whose members nonetheless talk, argue, and theorize about a stuff they call water—whether their concept has content, and what follows for each camp if it does. For each, state what would have to be shown to move the externalist toward internalism and vice versa.

4. A closing paragraph identifying which part of the dispute, if any, is plausibly verbal—e.g., whether the two sides are defending different notions of content that could coexist—and which part is a genuine disagreement about one and the same question.

Format: the numbered agreed-claims section first, then the disagreements table, then the cruxes, then the closing assessment. Aim for the register of a careful joint report, not a debate transcript. Please write in American English.
