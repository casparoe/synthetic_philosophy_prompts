You are an expert in the philosophy of information and the history of visual communication, and a seasoned writer of committee papers. Take the following case seriously and think it through carefully; you may find it useful to brainstorm the competing considerations before settling on a recommendation, but your final answer should consist of a single polished document, not notes.

I serve on the five-member interpretive planning committee of a municipal museum. The museum is building a permanent gallery about the city itself -- its population, economy, housing, and public health -- with displays refreshed annually from the city's open data. A design consultant has proposed that every quantitative display in the gallery be rendered in a picture language modeled on Otto Neurath's Vienna method, later known as Isotype, which Neurath developed for public education in Vienna in the 1920s and 1930s. Under her proposal: quantities would be shown by repeating identical pictograms, each symbol standing for a fixed number of people or units, never by stretching a single figure or using bars of different lengths; silhouettes would be flat and stripped of individual detail; color would distinguish kinds of thing, not amounts; and axes, grids, and numerals would mostly disappear in favor of self-explanatory arrangements of icons. Her argument is that Neurath designed the method precisely so that statistical facts would be legible to every citizen regardless of schooling, and that a visitor should leave carrying a memorable picture rather than a forgotten table.

Our curator of data practice has filed a formal objection. She argues that the picture language buys its charm by suppressing what a chart would reveal: uncertainty, variance, small but consequential differences, and exact values, since a row of icons cannot easily show margins of error or fine gradations. She also argues that the apparently innocent design decisions -- which silhouette stands for a resident, who is drawn as a worker, which neighborhoods get which symbols -- editorialize under the cover of neutral presentation. She wants conventional charts with full figures, presented at two levels of detail for different audiences.

The committee's charter instructs us only to serve both accuracy and accessibility, without ranking them, and the director needs a decision at next month's meeting because fabrication must begin. Draft the written opinion I will circulate in advance. Format it as a memo addressed to the committee, of roughly 900 to 1,200 words, and make sure it does the following:

1. Recommends one concrete course of action -- adopt the consultant's proposal, side with the curator, or a hybrid you specify -- with enough implementation detail that the fabricators could act on it: which displays fall under which convention, and what review process, if any, should govern the choice of icons.
2. Reconstructs the philosophical stake in its strongest form: whether any presentation of quantitative fact can be neutral, and whether Neurath's own frankness about the fact that every visualization involves selection strengthens or undermines the consultant's case.
3. Takes seriously the question of whom the picture language trusts. Does it democratize statistical understanding, or does it do the public's thinking for it -- and does the answer depend on features of the design that could be changed, or on something inherent to picture languages as such?
4. Gives the strongest version of the considerations favoring the side my recommendation rejects, and explains why they lose anyway or how the design will accommodate them.

Write in the first person as a committee member addressing colleagues. Keep the tone formal but readable for non-specialists. No footnotes, citations, or bibliography are needed, and please do not put any quotation in Neurath's mouth: if you refer to his views, paraphrase them.
