I'm a high school senior, and for the past few months my philosophy club has been stuck on the question of whether a sufficiently advanced AI system could ever have moral status — whether it could ever be wrong, say, to delete one or to make it suffer for our convenience. The discussion hasn't moved at all, and I want to understand why. One side says the whole question reduces to whether such systems are conscious, and since no theory of consciousness is settled, we should simply suspend judgment. A second side argues that this gets the ethics backwards: even under deep uncertainty, a non-negligible chance of consciousness obligates us to extend some moral consideration now, the way uncertainty about animals doesn't excuse cruelty. A third member keeps insisting that consciousness isn't even the right test — that having stable preferences, goals, or a genuine role in our social practices could ground moral status on its own. My working suspicion is that we're having three different arguments under one heading: a metaphysical one about consciousness, a decision-theoretic one about acting under moral uncertainty, and a conceptual one about what moral status even requires. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a single core disagreement underneath — about evidence, about what work consciousness does in ethics, about who bears the burden of proof? Please give a diagnosis supported by what each position actually commits to, not a survey of the AI ethics literature, and tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis: a settled theory of consciousness, a formal argument about moral uncertainty, a conceptual distinction, or an honest case that the impasse is permanent.
