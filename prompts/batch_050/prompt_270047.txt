I manage the labelling team at a company that trains handwriting-recognition models. My twelve annotators label roughly 40,000 photographed handwritten characters a week, and every sample must be assigned to exactly one category: one of the twenty-six lowercase letters, "unreadable", or "not a letter". The labels go straight into training data, so systematic disagreement between annotators measurably degrades the model, and two of our largest clients audit samples of our output every month. Annotators average about six seconds per character, have no training beyond our written guidelines, and escalate disputed samples to me or my deputy; we have perhaps two hours a week for adjudication.

Our guidelines currently consist of a gallery of reference shapes, and they are failing at exactly the interesting point: not on sloppy handwriting, but on the boundary of the category itself. Three cases from last week's escalation queue illustrate this. First, a fast cursive sample from the middle of the word "cat", which in isolation most annotators read as a "u", though in context it is plainly intended as an "a". Second, a neatly drawn circle with a short vertical stroke on the right that rises slightly above the top of the circle — squarely between a single-storey "a" and a "d". Third, a page by one writer whose every "a" is an almost-closed circle with a small gap at the top right, while every "o" is fully closed: internally consistent, entirely legible in context, and matched by nothing in our shape gallery.

A designer friend tells me our problem is a working version of a much older question — the letter-spirit problem associated with Douglas Hofstadter's research on letterforms: what makes every "a" an "a", given that single-storey and double-storey, serif and cursive shapes vary enormously and yet almost all are unambiguously the same letter, while some shapes geometrically close to a canonical "a" are read as "u" or "o"? Her claim is that no checklist of shape features will ever settle this, because a letter's identity lies in the role the shape plays within the whole system of contrasts — an "a" is partly constituted by not being an "o", a "d" or a "q" — and in what a competent reader of the script can recover. I cannot adjudicate the philosophy, but I need to know what it implies for practice.
