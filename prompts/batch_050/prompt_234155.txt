After a long and unresolved argument with a friend, I would like my judgments about political obligation and civil disobedience made systematic. My friend defends a simple view: a legitimate law creates a genuine duty to obey even when one reasonably believes it is mistaken, and deliberate resistance is justified only when it is nonviolent, publicly announced, narrowly aimed at the disputed law, and accompanied by a willingness to accept the legal penalty. I initially found that view attractive, but it fits some of my judgments poorly. Assume the facts below exactly as stated.

1. In a reasonably just constitutional democracy, a carbon tax is enacted after a fair legislative process and survives judicial review. Mara campaigned against it and sincerely believes it will do more economic harm than environmental good. The tax is not confiscatory and does not violate anyone’s rights. I think Mara is morally required to pay it while continuing to campaign for repeal.

2. The same democracy prohibits door-to-door political canvassing during the final month of an election. The legislature’s real motive is to protect incumbents, but the ban applies equally to all parties and is enforced without violence. An activist publicly canvasses, identifies herself to officials, explains that she is violating the law conscientiously, and submits to prosecution. I think her conduct is permissible.

3. Another activist opposes the same canvassing ban. Without announcing himself, he chains shut the gates of a government office for one morning, delays public business, and leaves before he can be identified. I think this is not justified, even though his cause is the same and nobody is injured.

4. During serious riots, the government imposes an overbroad nighttime curfew. A driver finds a stranger bleeding heavily, cannot reach an ambulance, and drives him to a hospital. To avoid being detained before reaching the hospital, she bypasses a checkpoint and later does not surrender herself for prosecution. I think both the violation and her subsequent refusal to submit are justified.

5. An authoritarian regime requires citizens to report undocumented residents for deportation to a country where many will face torture. A resident secretly shelters a family and lies to officials who ask about them. I think this is justified despite being covert and deceptive.

6. A tourist briefly visits an otherwise just foreign democracy. She has voted in none of its elections, accepts its political institutions as illegitimate in principle, and plans to leave the next day. She nevertheless uses its roads. I think she is required to obey its ordinary traffic laws.

7. Through procedurally regular means, the democracy in cases 1–4 votes to invade another country, but the invasion is manifestly aggressive and unnecessary. Jonas receives a conscription order and knows that refusal will bring a long prison sentence. He obtains false medical records, avoids induction, and emigrates. I think his deceptive and covert evasion is justified. My friend thinks that if the state remains legitimate enough to impose the tax obligation in case 1, Jonas must either comply or openly accept punishment.

8. In the same democracy, a constitutionally valid statute criminalizes a form of consensual adult conduct that a juror considers seriously unjust but not comparable to aggressive war. The evidence proves the defendant violated the statute. The juror votes to acquit without revealing that she is refusing to apply the law, knowing that her acquittal cannot be appealed or punished. I am inclined to think this is justified.

9. Another citizen refuses to pay the portion of his taxes roughly attributable to the unjust invasion and sends that amount to humanitarian relief for its victims. He announces what he has done and accepts the resulting penalty. Here I am torn. The war is gravely unjust, but paying general taxes seems less direct a form of participation than serving as a conscript. I am inclined to say this tax resistance is not justified, though I am less confident than in the other cases.

Is there one principle, or a small family of principles, that captures these verdicts? I want it to distinguish at least between a state’s general legitimacy and the authority of a particular law; between ordinary political disagreement, injustice, emergency, and direct complicity; and between open disobedience, covert evasion, deception, coercion, and refusal to accept punishment. Please state the principle precisely, identify which cases motivate each clause, and explain whether publicity or acceptance of punishment is a necessary condition, merely evidence of conscientious motive, or relevant in some other way.

If no reasonably simple principle saves every verdict, say which verdict I should revise and why. Also apply the resulting account to two further cases: a government employee who leaks classified evidence of systematic illegal surveillance after internal remedies have failed, and a parent who violates a custody order because she has credible but not yet legally established evidence that the other parent is abusing their child.
