In discussions of free will, "sphexishness" names the mechanical rigidity of the digger wasp Sphex ichneumoneus: it drags a paralyzed cricket to its burrow, goes inside to inspect, and if the cricket is moved a few inches while it is down there, it comes out, drags the cricket back, and repeats the whole inspection — indefinitely. The behavior looks purposive until you see it cannot be revised mid-course, and the standard lesson drawn from it (Dennett's, for instance) is that what protects us from sphexishness is not some extra metaphysical ingredient but flexibility: the capacity to notice what we are doing and let new considerations reroute us. Read Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day through this lens. Stevens seems a candidate for a sphexish agent: his routines persist through the evening his father is dying upstairs, through the dismissal of the two Jewish housemaids he privately regrets, and through decades of loyalty to Lord Darlington even after Darlington's politics have collapsed. Where exactly does the novel mark the difference between his routines and genuine deliberation? Are there moments when a consideration almost gets through to him — Miss Kenton's hints, the recurrent question of whether his service was worthy — and how does Ishiguro stage their failure to reroute him? Does the novel treat his rigidity as something done to him (by professional training, class, deference) or as something he is complicit in maintaining, and does that bear on whether we should judge him free? Finally, the novel is usually read as a tragedy of self-deception: say whether the sphexishness reading competes with that reading or deepens it, and what, if anything, the novel shows about unfreedom from the inside that a philosophical discussion of the wasp cannot.
