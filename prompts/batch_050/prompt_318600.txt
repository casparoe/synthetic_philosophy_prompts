I serve on the Committee on Scientific Communication of a professional society for behavioral-science researchers. The committee adjudicates complaints under our public-communication code, whose only relevant clause states that members "shall not make public assertions about research findings that do not meet the appropriate epistemic standard." The drafters never specified what that standard is, and we must now interpret it in a live case. Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the committee before our vote.

The case concerns Dr. L, a member, and two statements she made in a televised interview about her lab's work on a nutritional supplement:

Count 1. Dr. L asserted that the supplement has no adverse effects on memory at the standard dose. At the time, she believed this on the basis of three well-conducted studies, two from other labs, and had no reason to doubt them; by any reasonable measure her belief was justified and responsibly formed. Eighteen months later, larger and better-controlled trials established that the claim is false. There is no suggestion of negligence in how she arrived at her belief.

Count 2. In the same interview, Dr. L asserted that the supplement improves reaction time in older adults. This claim is, as it happens, true; independent work has since confirmed it robustly. However, the only basis Dr. L had for the assertion at the time was a dataset assembled by a graduate student who, we now know, fabricated most of it. Dr. L sincerely believed the claim, but she had ignored two explicit warnings from a colleague about the student's reliability.

The complainant argues that both statements violated the code; Dr. L argues that neither did. In the opinion, please do the following:

- Set out the main candidate standards the clause could be read as encoding — at minimum: a truth standard (assert only what is true), a knowledge standard (assert only what you know), and a justified-belief standard (assert only what you justifiedly believe). Note explicitly that these standards deliver divergent verdicts on the two counts, and explain how Count 2 is precisely the kind of case that pulls the truth standard and the knowledge standard apart, since a lucky true assertion made on a corrupt basis can fail to be knowledge.
- Recommend which standard the committee should adopt as its interpretation of the clause, and defend the recommendation against its strongest objections. In doing so, address at least the following pressure points, as applicable: the objection to a knowledge standard that knowledge is not "luminous" — one can know something without being in a position to know that one knows it — so speakers could not reliably tell whether they were complying with the rule; the objection to a truth standard that it punishes conscientious speakers for unpredictable falsity and chills valuable public communication; and the objection to a justified-belief standard that it excuses Count 2-style recklessness whenever the speaker manages to be sincerely convinced.
- Reach a concrete verdict on each count under your recommended standard, with a recommended disposition for each (e.g., dismissal of the complaint, formal reprimand, required public correction), and note briefly whether and how the verdicts would change under the standards you reject.
- Give an honest, steel-manned treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side before explaining why it does not carry the day.

The opinion should run roughly 900–1200 words, in the register of a formal committee document: a clear bottom line up front, numbered reasoning, no rhetorical hedging. End with a final line of exactly the following form:

Verdict: Count 1 — [disposition]; Count 2 — [disposition]; Standard: [name of the standard adopted]
