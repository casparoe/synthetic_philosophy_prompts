Two friends of mine (both stats grad students) got into it last night over this game: you flip a fair coin until it lands tails, and if the first tails comes on flip n, you win (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n dollars. So n=1 pays you $2, n=2 *costs* you $1, n=3 pays $4/3, n=4 costs $2, and so on, alternating. The question they fought over: is there a fair entry fee for this game, and if so what is it?

Dan says the fair price is ln 2, roughly 69 cents. His reasoning: outcome n happens with probability 2^(−n), so the expected payoff is 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ..., which equals ln 2. And he says the ordering of the sum isn't arbitrary, because outcome n is literally the one decided by the nth flip.

Mara says there is no expected value, period. The series of absolute values is 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + ..., which diverges, and the rearrangement theorem says you can reorder the very same terms to sum to anything you like — seven dollars, negative a million, whatever. So ln 2 is just an artifact of one particular way of adding, and a quantity that changes under relabeling can't ground a fair price.

Dan fired back that rearranging is just shuffling symbols on paper. The casino pays according to the actual sequence of flips, not some reordered list, and he claimed that if you play the game over and over your average winnings settle down to ln 2 in the long run, so the fair-price question has a definite answer even if the math gets fussy.

Mara replied that a fair price should depend only on the distribution of payoffs, not on the physical procedure generating them. And you can design a totally different procedure with the exact same payoff distribution whose "natural" summation order converges to whatever number you want — so Dan's view forces two games with identical payoff distributions to have different fair prices, which she called absurd.
