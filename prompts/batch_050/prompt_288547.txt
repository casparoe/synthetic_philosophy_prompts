I teach an upper-division undergraduate seminar in epistemology, and every year I watch my students memorize the slogan-level differences between contextualism, subject-sensitive invariantism, and strict or intellectualist invariantism without being able to say where they themselves stand once they are pressed on cases rather than vocabulary. For the final unit I want to replace my usual review sheet with a diagnostic instrument: a decision tree, followed by a short quiz, that sorts students into positions by their verdicts on cases rather than by which label they happen to find attractive. Please design both for me.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Begin with the questions on which these views genuinely come apart, lay out which combinations of answers are coherent, and only then locate the live positions within that space. The leaves must include at least: strict invariantism with stakes-invariant knowledge; attributor contextualism; subject-sensitive invariantism, that is, pragmatic encroachment on knowledge itself; and the view that practical factors bear on justification rather than on knowledge directly. If the tree cannot separate two of these by case-verdicts alone -- and I suspect you will find trouble at a particular juncture -- say so explicitly rather than papering over it, and identify the smallest additional datum that would split them: semantic self-ascriptions, third-person knowledge attributions, retraction behavior when stakes rise, or norms of action. Note any unoccupied combinations and diagnose briefly whether each vacancy reflects incoherence, theoretical cost, or mere neglect.

For the quiz itself: eight to ten questions, each a concrete scenario with two or three answer options phrased in plain language, and with the words contextualism, invariantism, and encroachment appearing nowhere a student would see them. Variants of the familiar bank and airport cases are acceptable, but include at least one case involving statistical or lottery-type evidence, at least one in which the person evaluating the knowledge claim occupies different practical circumstances from the person the claim is about, and at least one pressing on whether it is permissible to act on a proposition one does not know. Close with a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the leaves, including guidance on what to conclude about a student whose answers split across two positions -- whether that signals an unclassified but coherent hybrid or simple inconsistency.
