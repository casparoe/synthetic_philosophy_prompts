Is there a name for this pattern of reasoning? I keep running into it in arguments about how to compare options. Someone wants to show that two very different things are actually equal in value, or that a big difference doesn't matter, and they do it by constructing a long chain of cases: two job offers that differ only by one minute of commute, then one that differs from that by one minute, and so on, until the chain connects a clearly wonderful job to a clearly dreadful one. At every link they insist that no rational person could prefer one neighbor to the other -- the difference is too small to ground a preference -- and from that they conclude that the endpoints can't differ in value either, since each step was a tie. I've seen the same shape of argument used with lives (remove one second of mild discomfort at a time from a life of agony), with concert recordings (add one second of background hiss per step), and with charitable donations. What puzzles me is that the argument feels fallacious, but not for the reason sorites-style arguments about heaps and bald men are fallacious -- those seem to be about vague words and where to draw a line, whereas this one is about whether 'at least as good as' really chains together the way the argument assumes. It also isn't a slippery-slope argument, since nobody is claiming one step causes the next. Does this specific configuration -- exploiting the apparent indifference of adjacent cases to flatten a real difference between endpoints -- have an established name and literature? And if it does, what exactly goes wrong in it: is the problem that 'equally good' fails to be transitive, that adjacent cases aren't really ties after all, or something else? I don't just want a label; I want to know where precisely the reasoning breaks and how this differs from the sorites paradox it superficially resembles.
