My friend and I have been arguing about Wittgenstein's On Certainty for most of a semester now, and last night, over the dregs of a seminar on radical skepticism, the argument finally crystallized into two positions we could actually state. She defends what I'd call the non-propositional reading: the so-called hinge commitments that stand fast for us — that there is an external world, that I have two hands, that the earth existed long before I was born — are not really propositions at all, and so are not the kind of thing that can be known, doubted, justified, or even strictly speaking believed. On her view, talk of hinges belongs to a different logical category from empirical claims; they show themselves in what we do rather than being items in our web of belief, and saying I know I have two hands is a misuse of the word know except in very special circumstances. I defend the rival propositional reading: hinges are genuine propositions, they can figure in belief and even in a suitably extended or non-evidential notion of knowledge, and Wittgenstein's point is that their certainty is a matter of their role in the structure of justification rather than their ceasing to be contentful claims. We both claim the text is on our side, and we both accuse the other of either flattening Wittgenstein into an ordinary fallibilist (her charge against me) or turning him into a mystic who thinks our most basic commitments are unintelligible (mine against her).

We've agreed to settle this the way adversarial collaborations are supposed to be settled, and I'd like you to draft the joint statement we should be able to produce. Please don't rely on any verbatim quotations from On Certainty — work from the general shape of the text (the Moorean starting point, the river-bed metaphor, the remarks about knowledge belonging with evidence and reasons, the observation that doubt presupposes a background that stands fast) rather than particular sentences. What I want is: first, a numbered list of claims both camps can sign, covering at least the target phenomenon (that some commitments function as exempt from doubt in ordinary practice), the inadequacy of the Moorean response to the skeptic as standardly deployed, and what the disagreement is not about (for instance, neither of us denies that hinge talk plays some special role); second, a disagreements table stating each residual disagreement in a single sentence per side, each worded so that the side whose view it states would accept the wording — no strawmen, no question-begging vocabulary like brute animal certainty or just another belief; third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one of us to concede something substantial. I suspect the cruxes include whether category membership (being outside the language-game of justification) is compatible with truth-aptness, whether Wittgenstein's scattered remarks about knowing here are consistent or deliberately concessive, and whether the non-propositional reading can make sense of how hinges can shift over time (the river-bed can move) without reintroducing propositional content; fourth, for each crux, the kind of evidence or argument both of us would accept as bearing on it — textual patterns, cases of hinge revision, or what best explains why doubt needs an exceptionless background — together with the prediction each side would make in advance.

Please end with a short honest section identifying which parts of our dispute, if any, you think are verbal rather than substantive, and say why. We'd rather be told that half our argument was about the word proposition than paper over it with diplomacy.
