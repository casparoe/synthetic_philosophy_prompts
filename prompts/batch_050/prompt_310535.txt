I'm an applied macroeconomist — I teach intermediate growth theory and do empirical work on automation and labor markets — and I keep ending up in arguments with colleagues who work on AI safety about whether the "intelligence explosion" scenario is a coherent possibility or an artifact of intuition pumped by science fiction. The positions I have in mind are the standard ones: on one side, the view that a sufficiently capable AI system could recursively improve the very research process that produced it, so that capability growth accelerates discontinuously once some threshold is crossed (a "hard" or "fast" takeoff); on the other, the view that AI will behave like other general-purpose technologies, producing growth effects that are large but smooth, bottlenecked by complementary inputs, diffusion lags, regulation, and diminishing returns to research effort (a "slow" takeoff). I suspect my colleagues' disagreement, translated into my language, would either become a well-posed empirical dispute or evaporate, but I'm not sure which.

So: translate this debate into the vocabulary I actually think in — Solow-style and semi-endogenous growth models, aggregate production functions, total factor productivity, the ideas production function and the standing-on-shoulders versus fishing-out debate, factor shares, elasticities of substitution between capital and labor, automation as task displacement and task creation, and doubling times of output and of research inputs — as precisely as you can. Concretely: what does "recursive self-improvement" correspond to as a property of an ideas production function (and is it distinguishable from strong increasing returns that growth theorists already study); is "takeoff speed" best understood as a claim about parameter values within a smooth model, about a regime switch between two models, or about a kink or phase transition that the standard functional forms simply cannot express; and does the disagreement between the two sides survive translation as a clean dispute about elasticities and returns to scale in cognitive R&D, or does it turn out that they were never disagreeing about anything the models can see?

Then give me the part I care most about: a residue report. Which elements of the takeoff debate map cleanly onto growth-theoretic objects, which can only be made to fit by distorting them, and which have no expression in that vocabulary at all — I'm thinking of things like the agency and goal-directedness of the system doing the improving, the possibility that the system strategically conceals its capabilities, and the adversarial dynamics between the system and those trying to control it. For each residue item, state your judgment on whether it marks a genuine blind spot of growth theory or a component of the takeoff debate that is less well-defined than its proponents believe.
