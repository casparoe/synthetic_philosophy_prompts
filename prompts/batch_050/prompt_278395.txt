I'm TAing an undergrad epistemology course and we just finished the unit on norms of assertion, and it's gotten me obsessed with figuring out what MY rule actually is, so I've been polling my gut on cases. Here's where I land. My friend holds one ticket in a fair 10,000-ticket lottery and asks me before the drawing whether she should go ahead and book the non-refundable hotel for the trip she'd take if she wins: telling her flat-out "you didn't win, book it" feels wrong, even though I'm almost certainly right. But telling my partner "my car is parked on Cedar Street, you can take it" feels totally fine, even though the odds it's been towed or stolen aren't zero either. If I glance at a clock that stopped exactly twelve hours ago and announce "it's 3 o'clock," and it happens to actually be 3 o'clock, I think I said something true but still shouldn't have said it. Passing along what a reliable coworker told me -- "the museum closes at 6, Dana who works there mentioned it" -- fine. Saying "I think the museum closes at 6" when I only half-remember -- also fine, and the hedge seems to be doing real work. Saying "it's raining but I don't believe it is" is just bizarre even on a day when it might be raining and I might not believe it. And in the mock trial we did in class I couldn't bring myself to say "the defendant did it" when the only evidence was that 95% of people at the event hadn't paid and he was at the event, even though I would assert things with worse odds in other contexts.

My tentative principle, which I basically stole from the reading, is: flat-out assertion requires knowledge, hedged assertion only requires reasonable belief. But I can already feel it straining. The lottery and the parked car seem to have the same epistemic structure (belief + very good odds + no guarantee) and yet I gave them different verdicts, so either the principle overturns one of them or there's some third variable I'm not tracking. And here's the pair I keep going back and forth on: when a journalist finds out a story she reported in good faith rested on a forged document, everyone agrees she has to print a retraction even if the story is probably still true. But when I found out the email I'd relied on to tell my friend "the meeting's at 3" was a prank, hunting her down to formally retract felt ridiculous, even though it seems like the same situation in miniature. I don't know if I think stakes change the norm, or change the consequences of violating it, or just change how bad I feel.

So: does my principle survive contact with these verdicts, or does some repaired version of it (maybe one where the hedge doesn't actually change the norm, or where knowledge isn't the requirement but something like proper warrant is)? Please state the best candidate principle precisely, flag which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed: a voice assistant flatly answering user questions all day, and me telling my kid "there's no monster in the closet," which I assert with total confidence but is, strictly speaking, not something I could know in the way I know the museum thing. If the honest answer is that no single principle gets all of these and I have to give one up, say which one and why it should be that one.
