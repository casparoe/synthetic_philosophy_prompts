I convene a graduate reading group on the ethics of war, and over the past semester we have worked through the traditional Walzerian picture of combatant equality and then a series of revisionist critiques of it. Ahead of our final session I have written down, as a discussion handout, the positions I have personally settled on, and before circulating it I want an audit of whether the set is jointly tenable. I find each commitment below independently compelling, so please do not assume I am more attached to any one of them than to the others; I want to know where the problems are, not to be steered toward a preferred revision.

Here are my five commitments. (1) Combatants on opposing sides of a war are moral equals in the conduct of hostilities: a soldier fighting in a just war and a soldier fighting in an unjust one have the same permission to kill enemy combatants, and neither wrongs the other by doing so, provided each observes the in bello rules. (2) The justice of a war and the justice of conduct within it are independent questions: no fact about why the war is being fought changes what an individual combatant may permissibly do on the battlefield. (3) Liability to be attacked tracks the posing of a threat rather than personal culpability: the kidnapped conscript, the reservist fighting under duress, and the enthusiastic volunteer are equally legitimate targets, and killing any of them, when necessary and proportionate, is permissible. (4) A combatant who knows his country's war is a war of aggression — say, one who has watched the pretexts for it being fabricated — is morally required to refuse to fight, and if he fights anyway he wrongs the people he kills, however scrupulously he observes the laws of war. (5) Political and military leaders who launch aggressive wars may properly be prosecuted for aggression, but captured ordinary soldiers may not be punished merely for having fought, and they are owed humane treatment regardless of which side's cause was just.

Please audit the set: tell me which commitments genuinely pull against one another, which pairs only appear to conflict, and whether any commitment is silently doing double duty for another. Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each one state precisely what would have to be given up or weakened to resolve it, without presuming which commitment I would rather sacrifice. Conclude with the single least costly revision to the package and an honest statement of what it costs. Give your assessment as a JSON object with the following fields: "overall_verdict" (a short string stating whether the package is jointly tenable); "genuine_tensions" (an array of objects, ordered by decreasing severity, each with "commitments" listing the numbered commitments involved, "severity_rank" as an integer, "explanation" describing the conflict, and "what_must_give" stating the revision each resolution requires); "merely_apparent_tensions" (an array of objects with "commitments" and "why_it_dissolves"); "redundancies" (an array of objects with "commitments" and a "note" field, empty if none); and "cheapest_revision" (an object with "commitment_revised" giving the commitment's number, "revised_version" stating the weakened or replacement claim, and "cost" describing what is lost).
