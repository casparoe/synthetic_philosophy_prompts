Years of reading LessWrong and adjacent blogs have drilled into me that the pessimistic meta-induction is the canonical outside-view argument in philosophy of science: past theories we once called true turned out to be false in their central posits, so we should expect the same of our current best theories, whatever the inside view says. Recently I came across a passage in Henri Poincaré's *Science and Hypothesis* (1905 English edition), in the chapter "The Theories of Modern Physics," that seems to preempt the whole argument by relocating where a theory's truth lives:

"Many theories of dispersion have been proposed. The first were imperfect, and contained but little truth. Then came that of Helmholtz, and this in its turn was modified in different ways; its author himself conceived another theory, founded on Maxwell's principles. But the remarkable thing is, that all the scientists who followed Helmholtz obtain the same equations, although their starting-points were to all appearance widely separated. [...] In the premisses of these theories the part that is true is the part common to all: it is the affirmation of this or that relation between certain things, which some call by one name and some by another."

My own credence that this move — roughly, what later became selective or structural realism — actually defuses the pessimistic meta-induction is about 0.6. A friend puts it at 0.15, on the grounds that Poincaré has cherry-picked a case where the equations happened to survive, and that "the true part is the part common to all" is a recipe for shrinking a theory's content until the survival claim becomes trivial.

I'd like your own credence that something in the vicinity of Poincaré's claim is right: that for mature, predictively successful theories, the components responsible for their novel predictive success are retained, in the relevant respects, by whatever theories replace them, so that the historical record does not warrant pessimism about our current best theories at that level of description. More important than the number is the decomposition. Please break the question into the sub-questions it depends on — for instance: how often the central posits of mature theories have been wholesale discarded rather than recovered as limiting cases; whether the parts that survive were the parts generating the successful predictions (Fresnel's equations versus Fresnel's ether); whether cases like the dispersion example generalize or whether the shared relations themselves sometimes get abandoned in the next revolution; and how much of the dispute dissolves once "approximately true" is made precise. Assign a rough probability to each, show how they combine into your overall credence, and identify which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to.

Two further asks. First, locate the disagreement: given the decomposition, which sub-question most plausibly separates someone at 0.6 from someone at 0.15? Second, does the quoted passage bear the argumentative weight Poincaré puts on it? In particular, does "these theories are all simultaneously true" because they share a common relational core collapse into mere instrumentalism unless we have independent reason to think the common relations — not just the equations' outputs — are robust across future revolutions? Finally, tell me what historical evidence, if it turned out differently than I assume, would move your credence the most.
