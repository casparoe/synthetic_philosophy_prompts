So I help run a local rationality meetup — 10-14 people, mix of grad students and software engineers, we've got about 75 minutes for this. Everytime Aumann-style agreement stuff comes up, the room splits into two camps: people who think it's obviously right that honest truth-seekers can't just sit with persistent disagreement, and people who think it's a cute math result with zero bearing on actual humans because "nobody shares priors" and "people aren't Bayesians." The second camp never actually feels the pull of the theorem, they just pattern-match it to "nerds being overconfident again" and move on.

Design me an activity that makes both camps feel the force of the common prior assumption in their gut before anyone names it. What I'm vaguely imagining (steal from this or ignore it): pairs or trios get private evidence about some shared unknown — like draws with replacement from one of two bags with known but different compositions, where the bag was chosen randomly — and then they have to go back and forth announcing their current best-guess probability out loud, updating between rounds, until something happens. I want them to feel the weird gravitational pull toward agreement, and especially to notice that hearing the other person's announcement is itself evidence, not just conversation. Then ideally a second round secretly breaks one of the assumptions — give one person in each pair a different prior without telling the other, or designate someone who announces strategically instead of honestly — so they can feel exactly which load-bearing piece got removed when agreement stops converging.

Include: concrete rules and materials, a scoring scheme with no real money that rewards calibration/accuracy and incentives honest announcements rather than stubbornness or herding (and flag it if those two incentives actually conflict and I have to pick), a rough 75-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments from the game onto the formal setup — common prior, common knowledge of rationality, the iterative-announcement thing where posteriors converge. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guess: people anchor on their first announcement out of pride, or start litigating whether the bag draws were really random), and give me an honest note about which feature of the theorem the game structurally can't reproduce. My instinct is real common priors — I can hand people identical evidence about a bag but not identical worldviews — but push back if you think the gap is somewhere else.

No lecture up front; nobody should hear the phrase "common prior" until the debrief.
