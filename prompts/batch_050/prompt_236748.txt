Alright, so here's my situation, and I'd like actual advice, not just theory. I spent forty years as an experimental physicist and only started reading philosophy in retirement, so bear with me if this is a well-worn question. I recently read about this digger wasp, Sphex, that paralyzes a cricket and drags it to the entrance of its burrow, then goes inside to check things out before pulling the cricket in. Apparently if a researcher nudges the cricket a few inches away while the wasp is inside, she comes out, drags it back to the entrance, and does the whole inspection again from scratch. You can move the cricket forty times and she'll repeat the routine forty times, never catching on. That story unsettled me more than I expected, because last month my daughter pointed out, half joking, that whenever anyone at the dinner table questions whether I remember something correctly, I do the exact same routine: I say "I've been wrong before but not about this," I bring up my lab notebook habit from the 80s, and I get quiet for the rest of the meal. Once she said it, I realized I've done it word-for-word maybe a dozen times. I resolved to stop, and then last week my son-in-law doubted some detail of a story and I caught myself mid-sentence, already saying the notebook thing.

So here's what I keep turning over. When I noticed the pattern, did that make me different from the wasp, or does the wasp just lack someone to point out its loop? Because noticing didn't actually stop me -- I saw the groove and rolled right into it anyway, like a marble that can watch itself go down the track. And a second thing: in my lab days I deliberately built rigid habits. Checklists, fixed calibration routines, always the same order of operations. That mechanicalness was the whole point, it saved me from errors. My wife keeps the same morning routine for the same reason. So rigidity can't simply be the enemy. But then when does a fixed routine stop being a tool I'm using and start being, I don't know, the thing that's running me? Is the test whether I could stop if I wanted to, or whether I've ever actually stopped?

Practically speaking: should I try to break loops like the dinner-table one, and if so, how? I've thought about asking my daughter to "move the cricket," i.e. interrupt me mid-routine, but that feels like outsourcing whatever it is that makes me not a wasp. Or is that exactly what a wise person does -- engineer the environment because willpower alone is itself pretty mechanical? I'd especially like to know whether philosophers think the difference between the wasp and me is a difference in kind or just in degree, and what it would take to actually be on the right side of it.
