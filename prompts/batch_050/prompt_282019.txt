I’m a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I keep circling back to the James versus Clifford dispute about believing without sufficient evidence. In engineering, we often have to ship with an assumption that isn’t properly established: that users will interpret a warning sensibly, that a third-party service is reliable enough, that a monitoring alert is safe to silence, and so on. My half-formed idea is that the standard debate looks different once private confidence gets, as I want to call it, “exported” — encoded in defaults, runbooks, automation, or advice so that other people inherit it without having to accept it explicitly. Maybe Clifford’s evidential rule is too blunt for private exploratory guesses, while James’s permitted leaps become much harder to defend when the belief is operationally forced on downstream users. But perhaps the real moral work is done neither by the amount of evidence nor by the leap being live, forced and momentous, but by whether the people affected can see, contest, or reverse the exported assumption.
