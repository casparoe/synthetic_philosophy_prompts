I'm a novelist working on a book about an art forger, and I've been leaning hard on an analogy my agent keeps repeating: a forged painting is like a counterfeit banknote. The idea is that the fake isn't bad because of what it looks like -- visually it might be indistinguishable from the real thing -- but because of what it does to the system. Like fake money, it poisons trust, and everyone downstream gets hurt: buyers, sellers, insurers, the experts whose opinions get doubted, even people who never lay eyes on the forgery. She uses this to argue that forging art is exactly as bad as forging currency even if nobody ever finds out, and that all the hand-wringing about the special value of originals is sentimentality. I gave a version of this speech to the detective who's hunting my forger, and I want to know if it actually holds up before the book goes to print.

So audit the analogy for me. Map it out properly: what in the art world plays the role of the banknote, the mint, the guarantee backing it, the shopkeeper who innocently accepts a dud? Then tell me where it breaks. Some stress points I can already see -- banknotes are fungible and nobody hangs a twenty on the wall to stare at it; a note's value is exhausted by what you can swap it for, while at least some of a painting's value seems to live in the looking itself. And there's the awkward fact that a discovered counterfeit is worthless to absolutely everyone, while a discovered fake Vermeer can become fascinating in its own right -- museums have exhibited famous forgeries on purpose. But which of these disanalogies actually matters for the conclusion my agent draws, and which are just decorative? Does the analogy smuggle in the assumption that all the value in art is market value plus provenance? Does the trust-poisoning story show that an undiscovered forgery does harm, or does the harm only kick in at the moment of discovery -- in which case the analogy proves less than she thinks, and my detective's speech has a hole in it? And if it collapses, what's the better analogy for what a forger does wrong -- a lie, an imposter at a reunion, an athlete on steroids? Which one should a careful writer actually put in the detective's mouth, if any of them?
