So I went down a rabbit hole on the fall of Rome and now I'm stuck on something meta about the whole debate. Here's my rough map of the terrain: Gibbon pins it on Christianity and internal decay plus the barbarians knocking at the door, and he treats 476 as at least symbolically the end. Then you get Pirenne saying the real break comes with the Arab conquests in the 7th century, not with Odoacer. Then the late antiquity crowd, Brown and his successors, basically arguing there was no fall at all, just a long transformation into successor societies, and that "decline" is a value judgment projected backwards. Then pushback from people like Ward-Perkins saying look at the archaeology -- pottery quality, roof tiles, literacy, building in stone -- ordinary people's material lives genuinely got much worse, so calling it a "transformation" is a euphemism. Plus newer inputs: the lead poisoning theory that everyone seems a bit embarassed by now, plague, the 536 dust veil event and the Late Antique Little Ice Age.

What I want from you is not another verdict on which of these is right. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, because it seems to have been running for 250 years with very smart people on every side. Some options I've been turning over: is it a factual disagreement that the evidence keeps failing to settle; a verbal disagreement about what "Rome" and "fall" even refer to, where one side is tracking institutions and tax collection and the other is tracking culture and elite literary production and they're just talking past each other; a disagreement about which domain counts as the relevant unit of analysis, so the east continuing until 1453 counts for one side and not the other; or a disagreement about values wearing the mask of a factual one, i.e. whether centralized state capacity and good pottery are things we should even weigh heavily when judging a civilization. Maybe it's some combination.

Diagnose it using what the sides actually say and emphasize, not a textbook summary. And given your diagnosis: what would count as progress here? Is there any evidence -- aDNA, ice cores, pollen records, shipwreck counts as a trade proxy -- that could genuinely force one side to concede, or is this the kind of dispute where the facts could all be agreed and the disagreement would survive intact? If you think the question "did Rome fall" is partly ill-posed, say what the better-formed question(s) would be and whether historians are already quietly answering those instead.
