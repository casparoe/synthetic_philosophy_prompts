A friend sprung this on me in the pub last week and I still can't find the step I'm willing to refuse.

(1) If you know that p, then p is something you can properly rely on in deciding what to do — knowledge wouldn't be much use if it didn't settle the question for practical purposes.
(2) Whether you can properly rely on p depends on the stakes: the evidence that lets me rely on the bank being open on Saturday when nothing much turns on it isn't enough when I'll miss a mortgage payment if it's wrong, and then I'd pop into the bank and double-check first.
(3) So whether you know p depends partly on the stakes of your practical situation.
(4) Stakes can change with no change in your evidence at all — someone at the table offers you an enormous bet on p, thinks better of it, and withdraws the offer a minute later.
(5) So you can lose knowledge and then get it back again, with your evidence fixed and nothing in the world changing except what's being offered to you.
(6) But knowledge can't flicker on and off like that; what you know isn't hostage to what some bloke across the table happens to offer you this minute.
(7) Therefore (1) is false: knowing p doesn't by itself license relying on p in action.

I'm quite attached to (1) — it feels like the whole point of knowledge — and (6) feels like common sense, so I suspect something has slipped between (2) and (3), but I can't put my finger on it. Tell me which step you reject, why that step looked fine, and what rejecting it costs. Also: does your fix leave any room for the contextualist who wants to say the sceptic's high-standards scenarios genuinely make "knows" come out false, or does the whole lot go down together? British English in the answer please.
