Moral luck is the one topic where my second-years reliably write the right essay and don't believe a word of it -- they'll all agree outcomes shouldn't affect blameworthiness and then go home and judge their friends entirely by outcomes. So for next term's seminar (16 students, 80 minutes, nobody's read anything yet) I want an activity that convicts them before the problem even gets a name. No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear "moral luck" or Nagel's name until the debrief.

Here's a skeleton I've got in mind, flesh it out or replace it with something better: everyone makes the same risky decision in some staged scenario, but whether anyone actually gets hurt is settled by chance -- dice, cards, whatever -- so the room ends up split into people who "did" the harm and people who got away clean despite making identical choices. Then they have to do something with real (small) stakes: allocate a limited pot of something, vote on a mock sanction, rank each other for some fictional role. Some judgement task where they can't wriggle out of either treating the lucky and unlucky differently or pointedly refusing to. Ideally round one happens with outcomes hidden and round two with outcomes revealed, so we can show their own judgements shifting once they know who "crashed".

What I need from you:

- concrete rules, materials, and a timeline: setup, the decision, the chance mechanism, the judgement task, the reveal
- what to use as stakes so they genuinely feel the pull to punish outcomes rather than decisions, but nobody actually loses anything that matters
- a second act that brings in luck in circumstances or character rather than just results -- e.g. some students get dealt a "temperament" they didn't choose that makes the risky option more tempting for them, and we watch whether the room grades them more harshly for it
- a debrief built out of questions, not a lecture: things I can ask that make them articulate the principle they've just violated, mapping each moment of the game onto resultant, circumstantial and constitutive luck and the idea that we should only be judged for what's under our control
- what will predictably go wrong the first couple of times I run it (students gaming the dice, someone refusing to judge at all, the whole thing collapsing into an argument about whether the game is fair instead of whether their judgements were) and how to handle each
- and be honest about where the seams show: my guess is it can't reproduce the asymmetry between how we judge ourselves and how we judge others, and forfeited chocolate isn't real suffering, but tell me where you think the activity strains and what I have to supply with words instead.

Please write the answer in British English.
