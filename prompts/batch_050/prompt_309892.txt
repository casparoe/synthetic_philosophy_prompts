I'm in a reading group on philosophy of science and last week we got into a proper row about whether the predictive success of a theory is any evidence at all that it's actually true. My friend (a physicist, annoyingly confident) kept repeating that realism is the only view that doesn't make science a miracle, and I want to come back next week with a worked case rather than vibes. So I want you to act as a professional philosopher of science and walk me through one episode carefully. The case: the 1818 French Academy prize competition on diffraction. Fresnel, then a fairly unknown engineer, submitted a memoir defending the wave theory of light, built on the idea of an elastic luminiferous ether as the medium of the waves. Poisson, a die-hard particle theorist on the judging committee, showed that Fresnel's maths entailed an apparently absurd consequence — a bright spot right in the centre of the shadow cast by a small circular disc — clearly meaning it as a reductio. Arago then ran the experiment, and there the spot was. The wave theory swept the board, and yet within eighty years the ether at its heart had been dissolved into Maxwell's electromagnetic field and then discarded altogether after 1905, while Fresnel's equations sailed on untouched.

In chapter twelve of Science and Hypothesis ("Optics and Electricity"), Poincaré — writing after Maxwell but before relativity — says this about exactly this transition: "Thus blended in a larger aggregate, in a higher harmony, Fresnel's theory of optics did not perish. Parts of it are yet alive, and their mutual relations are still the same. Only, the language which we use to express them has changed; and, on the other hand, Maxwell has revealed to us other relations, hitherto unsuspected, between the different branches of optics and the domain of electricity."

Please think carefully about this before writing — maybe start by brainstorming two or three rival readings of the passage, then commit to one. What I want is the analysis running in both directions. First: map the episode onto Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction and onto Worrall-style structural realism. Who do the actors come out as on each view, what exactly did Fresnel's theory get right, and is Poisson's spot the kind of novel prediction the no-miracles argument leans on (does it matter that the phenomenon was derived from the theory rather than accommodated to it — and would it change anything if, as I've seen claimed, the spot had in fact been observed long before Fresnel)? Second: test Poincaré's claim against the case. Is his "their mutual relations are still the same" just structural realism avant la lettre, or is it sneakier than that — after all, one could say the "parts yet alive" are simply whichever bits had already been empirically confirmed, in which case the passage redescribes the survival rather than explaining it, and Laudan's induction still bites on everything that did the explanatory work (the ether's mechanical properties, not just the bare equations)? Third: does the episode confirm the meta-induction, embarrass it (Fresnel's theory is on Laudan's own list of refuted successes, yet something plainly survived), or show that the induction was only ever as strong as one's carving of what counts as "the theory"? If you think some other position — deployment realism, entity realism, whatever — handles the case better than both, say so, but don't force it. Write in British English, and please say explicitly where the historical record is too thin for the philosophy to get a grip rather than papering over it. Oh, and its fine to be opinionated — I need ammunition, not a textbook summary.
