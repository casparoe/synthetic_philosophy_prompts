Need a referee for an argument I watched last night and could not settle.

Background: my home town turns 300 next year, and the council's anniversary commitee is splitting over the big exhibition in the town hall. Half the budget's already spent, so something is getting built either way. My friend Marcus (teaches at the comp, helps run the civic society) wants it centred on the foundry family who basically made the place — jobs for three centuries, the park, the library, the almshouses. My friend Jo (finishing a history PhD) wants it centred on where the foundry money actually came from, which includes alot of early contracts in the slave-worked colonies and about a century of child labour in the works. It kicked off in the pub after the meeting. As best I can reconstruct it:

Marcus opened with: an anniversary exhibition isn't a court record, its job is to hand a town some exemplars. Every community that's ever done anything difficult ran on a burnished account of its own past, and a town that raises its kids on a charge sheet won't attempt anything — won't even fix the swimming baths, because nobody thinks the place is worth a subscription.

Jo: "burnished" is doing the work of "laundered" there. The exhibition is history or it isn't, and if it's history the criterion is what happened, and parts of what happened deserve to be judged, not celebrated. Also the descendants of the foundry children and of the colonies live in this town too — the heroic version tells them their ancestors are scenery.

Marcus: selection isn't lying. Every account selects, yours included — you'd curate towards indictment as surely as I'd curate towards pride. So the only real question is what the selection is for, and mine is at least for something the living need. Yours is for a verdict nobody asked for.

Jo: that's exactly the problem. The moment "what it's for" outranks "what happened", the people with power pick the purpose — they always have, it's how we got the old plaque calling the foundry man a tireless benefactor of progress. The truth criterion is the only one the losers of history can appeal to. Take it away and the winners write everything, forever.

Marcus: your prosecution proves too much. Drag every figure in the exhibition into your assize and they all fail — including the union hero of the 1890s who campaigned to keep imperial markets open for foundry steel. If nobody can be an exemplar the category dies, and you've produced a history whose only living characters are judges. And your zeal is a piety of its own anyway — you keep faith with the dead foundry children exactly the way the old antiquarians kept faith with every brick this town ever laid. You need the past to be a home as much as I do. You've just moved house.

Jo: keeping faith with victims is a debt, not a myth, and I can defend my selection by pointing at what happened; you can only defend yours by pointing at what it does. And if a town can only feel confident by deceiving itself about how it got rich, it shouldn't get to call the self-deception history. Maybe it shouldn't get the confidence either.

Marcus's last word: name one flourishing community, anywhere, ever, that built its confidence on a verdict of guilt about itself. If you can't, you're asking us to be the first experiment, and you still haven't said what gets the baths fixed.

Then they both looked at me and I had nothing. So I went home and dug out the Nietzsche essay I half-remembered from undergrad, thinking it would settle it, and it did the opposite. From the preface to On the Use and Abuse of History for Life, the second Untimely Meditation (1874) — this is Adrian Collins's old translation, the one on Wikisource:

"We do need history, but quite differently from the jaded idlers in the garden of knowledge, however grandly they may look down on our rude and unpicturesque requirements. In other words, we need it for life and action, not as a convenient way to avoid life and action, or to excuse a selfish life and a cowardly or base action."

So here's what I want.

1. Start with the passage. What exactly is the test Nietzsche is proposing? He seems to set needing history for life and action against two corrupt uses — avoiding life and action, and excusing base action. Spell out what each corrupt use would actually look like, because I want to use it as a scoring device.

2. Then go through the exchange point by point and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall. In particular, turn the passage on both of them: is Marcus's exhibition "excusing a cowardly or base action" (or laying down the excuse for the next one), and is Jo's prosecution a way of "avoiding life and action", judging being something you can do instead of ever building anything? And is either of them the "jaded idler in the garden of knowledge", or is that a third character neither resembles?

3. Say where the passage runs out. It tells us history must serve life, but as far as I can see it gives no account of whose life, or what counts as serving it, and that might be the whole dispute.

4. What are both of them missing? I've got a feeling there's a use of the past neither described — Marcus's version is all future-facing fuel and Jo's is all tribunal, and something got left out.

5. Finally, who's more right overall? I'm allowed to report back one name. I'd like it to be the right one.

British English please, and don't be gentle with either of them — they've both asked for it.
