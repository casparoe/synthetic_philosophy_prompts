Two of my friends got into a long argument at the pub the other night about a guy we all know, Derek. He's 28, drinks way too much, and when people bring up his liver he says he doesn't care what happens to him after 55 -- and he means it. He's not depressed, he's not in denial about the medicine, he's read the pamphlets, he just shrugs at his future self.

Priya said Derek has a reason to cut back whether or not he cares. The reason is sitting there in the facts: trashed liver, dead at 60. Reasons live in the world, not in his head. If whether he "has a reason" depended on whether he happened to care, then saying he has a reason would just be describing his current mood, and you couldn't ever tell anyone they have a reason they weren't already acting on.

Marcus said that if Derek genuinely couldn't be brought to care by any amount of honest, informed reflection, then telling him he has a reason is empty noise. What would it even be for a reason to exist that couldn't in principle move him at all? He granted we can criticise Derek, call him an idiot, whatever -- but a reason has to be the kind of thing that could motivate the person it's a reason for, at least potentially.

Priya said that makes reasons hostage to accidents of psychology: on his view a total psychopath has no reason not to hurt people, since nothing could motivate him, and that's insane -- basic moral stuff can't switch on and off depending on your wiring.

Marcus said he'd been too crude. He didn't mean reasons depend on what Derek happens to feel like right now, but on what Derek would want after fully clear-headed, fully informed deliberation -- like if he vividly imagined being 58 with cirrhosis and really thought it through, he'd be motivated. So the psychopath case doesn't bother him either, because psychopaths by definition aren't doing proper reflection.

Priya said that's rigged. "If he thought properly he'd agree with me" is something anyone can say about anyone, it can't be tested, and it quietly smuggles back in the objectivity Marcus was trying to get rid of.

Then Marcus tried a different angle. Suppose Derek is about to drink from a bottle he believes is water but is actually paint thinner. Surely Derek has a reason not to drink it, and that reason comes purely from the facts about the bottle, not from anything in Derek's motivations -- so reasons can rest on facts outside a person's head. Priya said no, that example only works because correcting a false belief counts as part of deliberation; Derek the drinker has no false belief, he just lacks concern, so the cases are totally different and Marcus has actually conceded her framework by relying on the bottle case.

They went in circles after that and I honestly couldn't work out who won. Can you go through the argument move by move -- who's right at each step, which points are actually decisive and which are dodges, and what, if anything, both of them are missing? Then give me an overall verdict, even if it's a split one.
