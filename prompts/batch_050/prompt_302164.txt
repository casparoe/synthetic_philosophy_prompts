I am a second-year university student taking a seminar in existentialism and feminist philosophy. Our class keeps reducing Simone de Beauvoir’s claim that womanhood is socially acquired to either the shallow idea that gender is a costume one can remove at will or the equally shallow idea that socialization simply programs passive victims. I would like to run an 80-minute activity with 18 students that makes the stronger and more uncomfortable version of the claim intelligible before anyone names it: social expectations do not merely reward or punish behavior from outside, but gradually shape what people notice, want, attempt, apologize for, and regard as a realistic possibility. Please design an original classroom simulation in which randomly assigned fictional roles—not participants’ actual sexes or gender identities—acquire patterned expectations through institutional rules, subtle sanctions, and repeated choices. The activity should include a clear setup, roles, rules, materials, a minute-by-minute sequence, and some consequence or point system that does not involve real money. It should avoid asking students to perform crude gender stereotypes or implying that the simulated categories map straightforwardly onto real men and women.

The force of the idea should emerge from what participants find themselves doing, not from an introductory lecture. Ideally, some should begin making choices for reasons they initially experience as prudent or natural, only to recognize afterward how the environment made those choices attractive; others should discover that resisting the role is possible but costly; and observers should be able to identify moments when external expectations start becoming self-monitoring. Please provide a detailed debrief that connects these moments to de Beauvoir’s notions of situation, social meaning, internalization, complicity, and constrained freedom, while carefully distinguishing her view from both biological determinism and the claim that identity is freely invented. The debrief should also distinguish sexed embodiment from socially produced womanhood without pretending that the body simply drops out of the analysis. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run—students treating it as a contest, confusing politeness with structural pressure, or overidentifying the fictional roles with real identities—and explain how the facilitator should respond. Be candid about which features of de Beauvoir’s account a short simulation cannot reproduce, such as historical sedimentation, embodiment, unequal material stakes, and the way gender intersects with class and race. Please write the entire activity plan in American English.
