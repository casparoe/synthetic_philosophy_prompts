I am a PhD student working on phenomenology and embodied cognition, and for a department colloquium's lighter session I want to commission a piece of parody that only works if its author actually understands what Merleau-Ponty was attacking in Phenomenology of Perception. Please write a parody user manual, roughly 700 words, titled something like "Operating Your Body: A First-Time Owner's Guide," produced by an invented company whose entire business model presupposes the intellectualist picture of action that Merleau-Ponty rejected: that moving one's body is fundamentally a matter of representing the body's position, computing trajectories, and issuing propositional instructions to the limbs.

The manual should be written in the relentlessly chipper, liability-conscious voice of consumer electronics documentation, with the standard apparatus of the genre: an unboxing and setup section (calibrating the body by explicitly representing the location of each limb), an everyday-operations chapter (walking, reaching for a cup, catching a ball, each broken down into the explicit inferences and calculations the manual assumes they require), a troubleshooting section, an FAQ, and a warranty page. The satire must land the genuinely Merleau-Pontian points without ever naming him or using his vocabulary: skilled movement should be shown as something the manual's model cannot accommodate (hence the troubleshooting section's strained fixes); the lived body as an "I can" rather than an object one pilots should appear only negatively, as the warranty clause voided by users who "bypass approved deliberative procedures" and simply reach for things; habit acquisition should be treated as an unauthorized firmware modification; the phantom limb should appear as a known defect whose existence the company's representational model cannot explain, with patch notes that keep failing; and the body schema should be satirized as a diagram the user must consult before each movement, with complaints in the FAQ from users who found that consulting it made them fall over.

The humor should stay deadpan throughout and arise from exaggerating real features of the target view, not invented ones: someone who has read the relevant chapters of Phenomenology of Perception should be able to recognize exactly which doctrines are being skewered in each section, and ideally to feel that the skewering is fair. Do not have the manual wink at the reader or break character, and do not append any commentary explaining the joke.
