We were talking about what makes a theory "proven," and I brought up a thing I'd read: that part of why physicists took Einstein's gravity theory seriously was that it accounted for a weird wobble in Mercury's orbit that astronomers had already known about for decades. One of my friends said that shouldn't count for much, because Einstein already knew about the wobble when he was building the theory, so of course he could build something that fit it -- that's just reverse-engineering, not discovery. Another friend said that was unfair, because a good explanation is a good explanation, and it would be strange if a fact stopped counting as evidence the moment anyone noticed it. Nobody could settle it, and it kept me up that night.

At the same time, dismissing all after-the-fact explanation seems to throw out most of history, geology, and half of what detectives and doctors do. So there seem to be two ideas tangled together: whether old, already-known facts can support a new theory at all, and whether a correct guess about something genuinely new is worth more than an equally good fit with something old. They feel related but not identical, and my friends kept sliding between them.
