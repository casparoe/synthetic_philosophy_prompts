After forty years as an experimental physicist I retired and, out of curiosity, began sitting in on the scientific advisory panel of our regional fisheries board. The board sets annual catch quotas using a population model built on assumptions everyone in the room knows to be false: the stock is treated as evenly mixed across the bay, fishing effort as uniformly distributed, and every vessel's gear as identical. The senior economist on the panel brushes my objections aside — the model has forecast yearly landings within a few percent for a decade, so, he says, the falsehoods plainly do no harm. He is right about the track record. Now, however, the board wants to use the same model to settle something new: closing half the bay to trawling and redistributing effort across the remainder. I find my unease returning, and I cannot tell whether I am being inconsistent. Was I wrong to grant the model its assumptions for forecasting in the first place, or is there a principled difference between trusting a deliberately false picture for prediction under familiar conditions and trusting it to steer an intervention on which it has never been tested? I would welcome advice on how to think about this, and in particular on what I should ask the panel to actually check before I cast my vote.
