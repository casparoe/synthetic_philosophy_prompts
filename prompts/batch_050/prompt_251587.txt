I got into an argument last week that I couldn't settle and it's been bugging me. I was talking with a friend who's in the military about the Christmas truce of 1914 — you know, the spontaneous ceasefires on the Western Front where British and German soldiers climbed out of the trenches, exchanged cigarettes and gifts, supposedly played football in no man's land, and in some places helped each other bury their dead, before the high commands on both sides got furious and cracked down to make sure it never happened again. My friend's take was that the truce shows ordinary soldiers instinctively understand something philosophers took decades to articulate: that the guys shooting at you aren't murderers, they're just men doing the same job you are, and that responsibility for the war lies with the statesmen, not the infantry. He basically treated the truce as the moral equality of combatants vindicated from below.

I pushed back but not well. Here's what I want: take the Christmas truce and analyze it through the debate about the moral equality of combatants — Walzer's traditional view that soldiers on both sides are symmetrically permitted to fight regardless of whose cause is just, versus the revisionist critique (McMahan and company) that combatants fighting an unjust war are liable to be attacked in a way just combatants aren't, so there's no real symmetry. Map it carefully: what would each side of that debate have to say about what the soldiers in the trenches were doing and feeling? Does the mutual recognition between the truce participants actually support Walzer's picture, or is my friend smuggling in a different claim — like soldiers being *excused* rather than *justified* — and does the distinction matter here? Then run it the other way: what does the episode do to the theories? A few things strike me as awkward for both sides — the fact that the truces happened mostly where troops had been facing each other for months, that commanders considered it dangerous precisely because fraternization made killing harder, and that nothing comparable happened on the Eastern Front. Does any of that count as evidence the truce was more about exhaustion and reciprocity than moral insight, and if so does that undercut using it as data at all?

I don't want a balanced survey where you end with "there are considerations on both sides." Tell me which account you think the episode actually supports — Walzer's, the revisionists', or neither — and be honest about whether the truce is the kind of case either theory was built to handle, or whether my friend and I were both misusing it.
