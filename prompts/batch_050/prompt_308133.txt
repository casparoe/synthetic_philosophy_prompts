Okay so here's the situation. I'm a philosophy professor putting together the reading list for a grad seminar on the philosophy of medicine, and I keep circling back to the definition-of-death debate — whole-brain death versus the circulatory criterion, the dead donor rule, all of it. The literature feels weirdly stalled to me, and after arguing about it with a colleague over coffee last week I've developed a half-baked conjecture I want pressure-tested: the debate is secretly three different debates wearing one trenchcoat. There's a biological question (when does the organism stop functioning as an integrated whole), a moral question (when is it permissible to treat someone as dead for purposes like organ procurement), and a legal-practical question (what standard should hospitals actually write down and apply). People keep winning arguments in one layer and claiming victory in all three. Maybe that's right, maybe it's too tidy, but either way I want to know what follows from it for where the intellectual effort should go.

So here's the actual ask. Imagine you're advising a sharp early-career philosopher with about three years to spend on this cluster of problems — they know the bioethics literature, they can talk to clinicians, but they have no lab and no legal training. Which open question should they take on first? Some candidates to react to (but feel free to add your own or reject mine): whether "permanent" versus "irreversible" cessation of circulation does real work in justifying donation after circulatory death, given that we often *could* attempt resuscitation and just don't; whether the whole-brain criterion can survive the awkward fact that some bodies meeting it can be maintained for years, including through pregnancies, which looks bad for the "loss of integration" rationale; whether the more radical move of just abandoning the dead donor rule and defending procurement on autonomy-plus-nonmaleficence grounds actually dissolves the definitional fight or just relocates it; and whether the hunt for the true biological moment of death is basically a dead end (pun intended, sorry) because death was always a status concept.

Give me a defended agenda, not a survey. I want the ordering justified with explicit criteria — how many other questions downstream depend on this one, tractability for someone with that profile, and how much an answer would actually change clinical or legal practice versus just tidying up the journal pages. Tell me which of my candidate questions you'd deprioritize and why, and what consideration, if you learned it was true, would reshuffle your whole ranking. Also tell me honestly whether my three-debates conjecture clarifies anything or whether I'm just redescribing the mess.
