I co-host a comedy podcast and before the next series I want to write down my settled views on humour so guests can have a proper go at them. I've read the usual suspects — superiority, incongruity, relief, benign violation — and here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Incongruity is necessary. Nothing is funny unless it violates an expectation — no surprise, no laugh.
2. Benign violation is the best general theory going: something's funny when there's a violation that you simultaneously appraise as benign. It even explains the too-soon effect — wait long enough and the psychological distance lets you see the violation as harmless.
3. My favourite jokes still make me properly laugh on the dozenth hearing, and a beloved sitcom is funnier on the rewatch, not less.
4. A joke can be offensive, morally dodgy, and genuinly funny all at once. Being offensive doesn't stop something being funny, it just makes the laughing complicated.
5. Tickling laughter is a pure reflex — rats do it, there's no appraisal involved at all — and it's the same laughter as joke laughter. Any theory of humour that can't cover tickling is incomplete.
6. When my insufferable colleague slipped on the ice outside the office I laughed like a drain, and Hobbes was onto something: sometimes laughter is just joy at someone else's comeuppance.

A couple of guests have said these don't all sit together but nobody's pinned down where. Can you audit the set? Tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like they are. Order the real tensions from most to least serious, say seperately for each what would have to give and what that would cost me, and finish by telling me which single revision would clean up the most. Don't just flag everything as a problem — I think some of these are perfectly fine together and I want to know which.
