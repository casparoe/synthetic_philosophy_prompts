I teach a discussion section for an undergraduate philosophy of mind course, and last week we got to split-brain patients. My co-instructor reached for an analogy I've also seen in pop-science books and that the students found immediately gripping: the two cerebral hemispheres are like a married couple driving on a long road trip who can coordinate only by talking to each other, and the corpus callosum is their phone line. On this picture, commissurotomy doesn't create anything new; it just cuts the line, revealing that there were two drivers in the car all along. Each hemisphere was already a full subject with its own experiences and intentions -- the right one silently watching, the left one doing the talking -- and their seamless cooperation in everyday life fooled everyone, including themselves, into thinking there was only one person. She used the analogy to license a strong conclusion: the famous laboratory dissociations (the right hand pointing to what the left hemisphere verbally denies seeing, and so on) are exactly what you'd predict from two passengers whose phones have been taken away, so the unity of consciousness in a normal brain is nothing more than a high-bandwidth communication artifact, an illusion of coordination rather than a real metaphysical fact.

The students loved it, but the more I sat with it the less sure I was about what it's actually claiming. Normal intra-personal unity doesn't seem to work like a conversation between two already-complete agents -- when I reach for a cup, I don't experience one half of me proposing it and the other half ratifying it. And some things I know about the actual patients sit awkwardly with the phone-line picture: the two hemispheres still share subcortical connections, show shared emotional responses, and function so smoothly outside the lab that relatives often notice nothing; there are also partial and staged callosotomies where the "line" is cut gradually rather than all at once, which doesn't map onto anything in the road-trip story. At the same time, I don't want to dismiss the analogy on a technicality if its core point -- that seamless coordination can mask numerical multiplicity -- is sound.

Can you audit the analogy for me? Lay out the mapping explicitly: what in the couple-and-car story corresponds to what in the commissurotomy case (hemispheres, callosum, the laboratory setups, the patient's fluent everyday behavior, the left hemisphere's confabulations). Then tell me which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion my co-instructor drew, and which are harmless. In particular: does the analogy smuggle in the very thing at issue -- two pre-existing, fully fledged subjects with private inner lives -- by starting with two people rather than one? Does the fact that ordinary unity isn't message-passing between agents undercut the inference from severed communication to revealed duality, or does some version of that inference survive? And if you think a better analogy exists for what commissurotomy shows about the unity of consciousness -- whether it's one subject, two, or something the one/two counting question doesn't fit at all -- I'd like to hear what it is and what it predicts about the partial-split cases.
