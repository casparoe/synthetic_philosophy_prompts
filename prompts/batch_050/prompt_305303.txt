I'm writing a term paper for my epistemology seminar defending anti-reductionism about testimony — roughly, the view that testimony is a basic source of justification, so that a hearer can be entitled to accept what a speaker says without possessing independent, non-testimonial grounds for trusting that speaker or testimony generally. My draft already deploys the following arguments, and I don't need them repeated:

1. The circularity argument: any attempt to justify testimony in general by appeal to its observed track record must itself rely on testimony (we learn of other people's reliability largely through what others tell us), so a fully general reduction is impossible.
2. The language-acquisition argument: children must accept testimony uncritically in order to acquire language at all, and language is a precondition of formulating any reduction, so testimonial acceptance is prior to reduction both developmentally and conceptually.
3. The impracticality argument: most of what each of us knows — our date of birth, the geography of places we haven't visited, historical facts — could never be individually verified, so reductionism would consign nearly all belief to irrationality.
4. The a priori entitlement argument: absent reasons for doubt, a hearer has a defeasible entitlement to accept a comprehended assertion simply in virtue of its being presented as true, no inductive evidence about the speaker required.

I'd like one further argument for anti-reductionism that is genuinely distinct from these — not a restatement, special case, or conjunction of any of them, and not merely a reply to the standard reductionist rejoinders. After stating the new argument, explain briefly how it relates to the four above (which, if any, it presupposes or competes with), and give your honest assessment of whether it succeeds.
