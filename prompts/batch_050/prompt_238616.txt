Last week a close friend and I got into a long and surprisingly heated argument about Denis Villeneuve's Arrival, which we had just rewatched together. My position was that the film is essentially a dramatization of radical translation in Quine's sense: Banks and Donnelly are in roughly the position of the field linguist facing the native who says "gavagai" while a rabbit runs past, except the informants are heptapods, and every ostensive cue is compatible with indefinitely many translations. My friend insisted the film is better read as a refutation of Quine: the linguists do converge on a translation, the logograms turn out to have a discoverable semantics, and the whole plot depends on that semantics being uniquely right. Neither of us convinced the other, and I now want to settle the question properly.

Please act as a professional philosopher of language who is also a careful critic of film, and think carefully through the problem before committing to an answer. I'd like you to begin with a short brainstorming pass -- list the scenes and structural features of the film that seem most relevant, and the elements of Quine's indeterminacy thesis (ostension, analytical hypotheses, the in-principle character of the underdetermination) that the film engages, before settling on a reading.

Then give me the reading itself. Specifically: Does the film's portrayal of the translation project -- the early sessions with the heptapods, the reliance on pointing and written exchange, the military's anxiety that a word rendered as "weapon" might just as well be rendered as "tool" or "gift" -- actually vindicate or undermine the indeterminacy thesis? Note that the film's climax seems to require that the logograms have a determinate meaning that Banks uniquely gets right, and that its Sapir-Whorf-inflected claim (learning the language restructures her experience of time) presupposes there is a single language-content to be learned. Is that a quiet repudiation of Quine, or does the film dramatize something subtler: that indeterminacy is no obstacle to practical convergence, which is in fact Quine's own view? Where does the film resist the Quinean reading, and where does a Quinean have to concede that the fiction idealizes? Finally, does the film add anything to the philosophical debate that the armchair cases don't -- for instance, does a non-sequential writing system, or translation with creatures whose experience of time is non-linear, change the stakes of radical translation in a way the gavagai example cannot?

Please be faithful in both directions: don't flatten the film into an illustration of a thesis, and don't bend the thesis to fit the film. No citations or bibliography are needed -- I want the argument, not the apparatus.
