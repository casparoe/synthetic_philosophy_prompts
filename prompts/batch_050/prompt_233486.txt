I run weekly discussion sections for a second-year undergraduate course on collective decision-making. The students can do conditional probability informally — they can reason with marbles in urns — but most have never seen Bayes' theorem written down, and none have studied game theory. In one fifty-minute section I have to teach information cascades: how a sequence of individually rational choices, each made on the basis of private information plus the observable choices of predecessors, can produce uniform public behavior that no longer reflects anyone's private information.

Don't teach me the material; plan how I should teach it. Specifically, I want the following.

First, the order in which the components must be introduced, with a stated reason for each dependency: which idea fails to land if some earlier idea hasn't been secured. My instinct is to go from individual updating, to the informational asymmetry between private signals and public actions, to the moment a cascade starts, to the surprising fragility of an established cascade once a little new public information arrives — but tell me whether that ordering is right and where it breaks.

Second, the one example that should carry the whole session. State what properties it must have (sequential decisions, actions observable but signals not, a discrete choice so that a cascade is actually demonstrable) and what it must lack (payoff interdependence, reputational stakes, coordination motives — anything that would let the students mistake the phenomenon for conformity or strategic imitation rather than pure inference from others' behavior). Pick the concrete scenario you'd build around and justify it, including why not the obvious two-restaurants-on-a-street anecdote if that isn't your pick.

Third, the misconception map. I anticipate at least: cascades are a story about irrational crowds; a cascade in full swing amounts to a well-founded consensus; more public information must always improve the group's decision; and once formed, a cascade is stable. For each misconception the students will predictably form, including any I've missed, give a table with these columns: misconception; why students form it (what earlier step invites it); where in the sequence to head it off; and how to head it off in one move.

Fourth, the check for understanding: one question I can pose in the final five minutes whose answer would show the idea has landed — ideally one that distinguishes students who grasp that the cascade discards private information from those who merely grasp that people copy each other.

Fifth, what to deliberately leave out at this level, and what I should say aloud to mark the omission rather than letting it pass silently.

Finally, since this is a teaching plan rather than a theorem, identify the single pedagogical judgment in your plan where you are least confident, and say what classroom evidence or consideration would lead you to change it.

After the plan, give a brief structured summary as a JSON object with these fields: "carrying_example" (a string naming the scenario), "sequence" (an array of objects, each with "step", "idea", and "depends_on"), "check_question" (a string), "deliberately_omitted" (an array of strings), "least_confident_judgment" (a string), and "revision_trigger" (a string describing what would change your mind about that judgment).
