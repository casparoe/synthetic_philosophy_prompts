I teach a second-year undergraduate philosophy of language module — about 24 students, a 75-minute seminar — and we are about to start Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism." In past years I've assigned the paper cold and watched students bounce off it: they nod along at the claim that no statement is immune to revision, but it stays a slogan, and by the following week they've reverted to sorting sentences into 'true by definition' and 'true by fact' as if nothing had happened. This year I want them to run into the problem in their own experience before they read a word of Quine, so the paper arrives as a description of something they've already done rather than as an argument they have to evaluate from a standing start.

Design me an in-class activity that makes the failure of the analytic–synthetic distinction something that happens to the students rather than something they're told about. My rough idea, which you should feel free to overhaul: students are given a set of statements and must sort them into those they'd hold onto no matter what experience brought and those they'd give up under enough pressure; the session then subjects their 'immune' statements to staged recalcitrant experiences — fabricated experimental results, testimony, shifting usage within the group itself — so that they're forced either to revise what they called analytic or to protect it by contorting the empirical ones, and to feel that where the revision falls is up to them rather than dictated by the statements. I'd like the mechanics to do real work: concrete materials, rules, an order of events with timings, and a way to make the trade-offs vivid without lecturing. Two specific demands. First, the activity should put pressure on both dogmas, not only the first — every classroom treatment I've seen reduces the paper to analyticity and lets reductionism slide, so I want a designed moment where students discover that individual statements don't face the tribunal of experience one at a time. Second, I want the strongest response to Quine to emerge from the students rather than from me: when they do revise a 'definitional' truth, at least some of them should object that they haven't revised anything, they've just changed what their words mean — and the debrief should be able to pick that objection up and press on whether any principled line can be drawn between revising a belief and changing the subject.

End with a debrief plan that maps what happened in the room onto the structure of the paper, tells me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students gaming the sort, one forceful student settling revisions for their whole group, the staged 'results' being dismissed as obviously fictional — and how to handle each, and states honestly which feature of Quine's view the activity cannot reproduce: I suspect the holism of real science, where revisions are expensive and heavily constrained, won't survive compression into 75 minutes, but tell me if you think the significant loss lies elsewhere. If any part of this rests on a confused premise — say, if the activity as I've sketched it would actually vindicate the Grice-and-Strawson response rather than Quine's, or if my two demands pull against each other — say so plainly instead of papering over it.
