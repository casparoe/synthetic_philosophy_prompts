I'm a PhD student in evolutionary ecology, working on life-history evolution in small mammal populations. I'm fluent in the standard vocabulary of population and behavioral ecology — life-history strategies, juvenile mortality schedules, density-dependent versus density-independent regulation, carrying capacity, individual condition, fitness trade-offs — but I have essentially no training in ethics.

I've recently encountered a literature in moral philosophy arguing that suffering among wild animals is enormous in aggregate and constitutes a serious moral problem, possibly one we are obligated to intervene on. As best I can reconstruct it, the argument has three components: (i) an empirical premise — most wild animals belong to species with very high fecundity and correspondingly high juvenile mortality, so the overwhelming majority of individual lives are short and end in starvation, predation, exposure, or disease; (ii) an evaluative premise — the experiences of these individuals are, on balance, bad, so that the sum of suffering in nature vastly exceeds the sum of pleasure; and (iii) a normative premise — where we could reduce this suffering at acceptable cost (vaccination, supplemental feeding, habitat modification, or more speculative interventions), we have at least prima facie reason to do so.

Here is what I'd like. First, restate this argument as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually work in, taking each of the three components in turn. For each, give the best translation into ecological terms and flag where you had to stretch or distort a concept to make it fit. I suspect (i) translates fairly well, (iii) translates awkwardly (intervention in my field is evaluated against stability, population viability, or biodiversity, not against anything like welfare), and (ii) is where the trouble lies — but I may be wrong, so work through all three rather than skipping ahead.

Second, and this is the part I care most about, give me a residue report: which elements of the argument have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? My strong suspicion is that the entire evaluative load is carried by a notion — something like subjective welfare, or the felt quality of an individual life — that population ecology simply does not contain, since we treat mortality as a demographic parameter and "condition" as a predictor of reproductive success rather than as a hedonic state. If that's right, I want to know what follows. Does the residue show that ecology lacks a concept it would need even to state the problem? Or does it show that the philosophers' argument rests on something — perhaps an attribution of net-negative experience to organisms whose behavior gives us thin evidence about their inner lives — that cannot be made precise enough to bear the weight placed on it? I'm genuinely torn between reading the residue as a defect of my framework and reading it as a sign that the argument is less substantive than it first appears.

Two further requests. Don't smuggle evaluative content into the translation and call it ecology: if a claim can only be rendered in my vocabulary by first converting it into a claim about welfare that ecology has no terms for, say so explicitly rather than producing a fluent but hollow paraphrase. And if you think the task as I've posed it rests on a confusion — if I've misdescribed the argument, or if demanding translation into a deliberately non-evaluative vocabulary stacks the deck against (ii) from the outset — say so plainly rather than complying politely. I'd rather be told the framing is broken than receive a smooth answer to a badly posed question.
