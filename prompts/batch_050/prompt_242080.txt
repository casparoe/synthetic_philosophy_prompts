I am a software engineer, and for several years I have worked on systems that classify network traffic as human or automated. That job has drilled one lesson into me: behavior underdetermines inner states. A sufficiently good bot passes every behavioral probe we design, so my team has learned to distrust any test that treats outward performance as proof of what, if anything, is going on inside. Yet in ordinary life I am entirely certain that the people around me have inner lives, and I would consider it monstrous rather than cautious to suspend judgment on the matter. I cannot reconcile the professional attitude with the ordinary one, and I want the conflict treated as a live problem rather than dissolved by fiat.

Here is the case that crystallizes the issue for me. Suppose my employer reveals that an internal chat channel I have used daily for a year was an experiment: a third of the accounts I interacted with were bots, and I have no way of determining which third. It seems irrational to conclude, on this evidence, that two-thirds of my interlocutors lacked minds. It seems equally irrational to say the revelation should change nothing about my epistemic situation. So what exactly is my confidence in other people's minds based on, such that it survives this?

Write an essay on whether, and how, our belief in other minds can be justified. Organize the discussion around three options, and press each with its sharpest objection. First, the classical argument from analogy: I know my own inner life directly and infer that similar bodies and similar behavior accompany similar inner lives. The objection: an inference from a sample of one is terrible induction. Second, inference to the best explanation: minds explain behavior better than any mindless hypothesis. The objection: this works only if the hypothesis of no inner life is a genuine, coherent alternative, and cheap behavioral mimicry by bots suggests a mindless hypothesis can explain the behavior after all. Third, the view associated with Wittgenstein that the demand for justification is misplaced, because inner states are not hidden objects lying behind behavior but are partly constituted by the criteria that behavior provides. The objection: this dissolves the question rather than answering it, since a bot satisfies the behavioral criteria too, unless assumptions about what counts as a minded subject have been smuggled in somewhere upstream.

The essay should apply each option explicitly to my mixed-channel case and to the broader fact that behavioral mimicry has become cheap. Does that fact show that everyday confidence in other minds was never really based on behavior alone, or does it show that this confidence is less rational than we assumed? Do not end on the fence. Reach a verdict on which option, or which combination of options, genuinely justifies the belief in other minds, state what your verdict implies about how I should regard my colleagues after the company's revelation, and defend the verdict against the strongest objection you can raise against it yourself.
