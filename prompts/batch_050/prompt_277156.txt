Having retired from a career in experimental science, I have lately been reading Leibniz, and in his Discourse on Metaphysics (1686) I came upon the claim that "the excellence of God's works can be recognized by considering them in themselves," so that "by considering his works we can discover the creator." I would like you to read Voltaire's Candide as a response to precisely this claim. It seems to me that Voltaire's satire may miss its target: Leibniz's point is that the perfection of the whole is discernible in the works themselves, as one might admire a mechanism without yet knowing its purpose, whereas Candide — the Lisbon earthquake, the miseries catalogued by the old woman, the idyll of Eldorado — attacks something more like the proposition that things are pleasant for us, or that every particular evil visibly serves a nearby good. Does the novel anywhere engage Leibniz's actual position, or only Pangloss's parody of it? And does the ending, with its retreat to the garden, concede Leibniz's point by substituting local, human-scale order for cosmic intelligibility, or is it rather a counter-proposal about where value can be found at all? I should like a reading that is fair to Leibniz before it is fair to Voltaire, and that says plainly where the satire lands and where it merely entertains.
