So this came out of a long argument in a comment thread on my blog (I've been reading LessWrong and the adjacent econ blogs for years, and the satisficing vs. maximizing fight never dies). The usual Herbert Simon point is that when search is costly, a satisficer with a decent aspiration level can beat someone who keeps hunting for the best option. But the standard countermove -- which my interlocutor kept deploying -- is that a real expected-utility maximizer just folds search costs and decision costs into the maximization itself, so "rational satisficing" is secretly a special case of maximizing and the bounded agent can never actually come out ahead in principle. I suspect this countermove is a motte and that there's a class of environments where it fails, but I couldn't construct a clean case on the spot. Build me one.

Concretely: construct a thought experiment where a satisficer reliably outperforms an expected-utility maximizer who has strictly more information and strictly more compute, and where the maximizer is explicitly allowed to optimize over optimization policies too (stopping rules, deliberation budgets, meta-level reasoning, the works) and still loses. The satisficer's edge has to come from the structure of the environment itself -- e.g. how other agents in it react to visible optimization -- not from the maximizer making a dumb error, being given a misspecified model for free, or the satisficer having secret information. Constraints: pick one realistic setting (hiring, apartment hunting, dating, negotiation, angel investing, whatever works best), no omniscient predictors or demons or exotic physics, and the scenario itself should be one tight paragraph. Then do the verification: walk through each agent's reasoning step by step, name the single structural feature of the case that's doing the work, tell me whether removing that feature collapses it back into "maximizing wins again," and give the strongest reply a maximizer-defender could make along with why it fails here. If the only version you can build is one where the maximizer's loss is really just another search cost in disguise, say so honestly rather than dressing it up -- that definately counts as the case failing.

At the very end, on the last line, put the name of the load-bearing structural feature as a short phrase in the form \answer{...}.
