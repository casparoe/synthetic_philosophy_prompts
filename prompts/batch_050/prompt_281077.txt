I'm a software engineer at a mid-sized logistics company, with no formal philosophy background. My team maintains an automated decision layer that scores candidate actions (pricing changes, route assignments, capacity purchases) and executes the highest-scoring one. The scores are produced by aggregating preferences elicited from several internal stakeholders, mostly through pairwise comparisons. A contractor recently told us that unless our aggregation satisfies certain "coherence conditions," the system is exploitable in principle — someone who understands its preference structure could trade against it repeatedly and extract value for free — and that the standard fix is to make the system behave like an expected utility maximizer. I've read informal summaries of money-pump and Dutch-book arguments and of the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, but I don't trust my grasp of what these results actually establish, and I need something my team can run, not a literature overview.

Please turn the coherence arguments for expected utility maximization into an audit procedure we can apply to a scoring system like ours, roughly quarterly, in about half a day, by engineers who know probability but no decision theory. Concretely, I want:

1. An ordered sequence of tests, where each test states which axiom or coherence condition it probes (completeness, transitivity, continuity, independence, or whatever you think the right decomposition is), what system behavior counts as a pass and what counts as a fail, and how to run the test against elicited stakeholder preferences rather than against an idealized agent.
2. For each failure mode, a worked construction of the corresponding exploit: e.g., given a detected cycle in pairwise preferences, show step by step how to build the money pump (the sequence of trades and why the system accepts each one), so we can demonstrate severity to management rather than just citing a theorem.
3. An honest statement of what the underlying representation theorems do and do not establish. In particular, I want the procedure to distinguish between "your system cannot be represented as maximizing expected utility" and "your system is making bad decisions," since I gather these can come apart, and to flag where the theorems are silent (e.g., whether they say anything about what we ought to value, or about which probabilities to use).
4. Explicit markers at the steps where judgment cannot be proceduralized — for instance, preferences that shift between elicitation rounds, stakeholders who refuse to compare certain options, or framing effects in how choices are presented — with guidance on what the auditor should record at those points rather than forcing a verdict.
5. A small set of stress-test choices in the spirit of the Allais paradox that we can put to stakeholders to check whether the independence condition is one they would actually endorse on reflection, or merely one we're imposing.

Then run the procedure on this concrete example, which is close to our real setup: the system elicits pairwise comparisons of candidate actions from five stakeholders and aggregates them by majority rule; each option's final score is its Borda count normalized to [0,1]; and the system evaluates any probabilistic action (e.g., a route assignment whose payoff depends on weather) by the expected value of those normalized scores, treating a 50/50 lottery between two options as exactly as good as the midpoint score. Tell me which tests this system passes and fails, whether its revealed preferences admit an expected-utility representation, and, if not, construct the exploit explicitly. Also say whether the system's treatment of lotteries is a coherence requirement or an extra assumption we've smuggled in, since the distinction matters for how we fix it.

Please close with the audit verdict for the example system in a fixed machine-readable form: a final line of the form \answer{<axiom(s) violated>; <whether an expected-utility representation exists: yes/no>}.
