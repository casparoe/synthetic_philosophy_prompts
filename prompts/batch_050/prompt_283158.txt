A charitable foundation can fund a four-person philosophy group for three years to produce guidance for designers and regulators of griefbots: conversational systems trained on a dead person’s messages, recordings, photographs and other traces. The foundation does not want a general defence or condemnation of griefbots. It wants a prioritised research agenda whose conclusions would affect concrete decisions about product design, inheritance law and platform governance.

Assume that griefbots vary along four dimensions:

1. Provenance: they may be assembled from the dead person’s own records, from other people’s recollections, or from both; the person may or may not have consented before death.
2. Plasticity: they may merely recombine recorded material, or learn from new events and from interactions with the bereaved.
3. Address: they may serve one designated mourner, a family, or the public.
4. Presentation: they may openly function as an archive, or speak in the first person as though the dead person remained present.

The candidate research problems are:

- Authorisation: who, if anyone, may legitimately permit, constrain or veto a simulation—the person before death, surviving intimates, an executor, or some combination?
- Representational fidelity: when is an affectionate or idealised distortion deceptive or wrongful, and how should a system communicate uncertainty about what the person would have said?
- Counterfactual agency: if a plastic griefbot answers questions the person never considered, changes its apparent views, or develops through interaction, in what sense—if any—can its output be attributed to the dead person?
- Grief and dependence: when does continuing interaction support mourning, and when does it obstruct the reconstruction of a life without the deceased?
- Conflicting relationships: how should a system handle incompatible claims by a spouse, child, estranged relative or former partner, each possessing different memories and interests?
- Collective memory: what happens when widely available simulations of public figures can preserve, revise or domesticate their personalities long after death?

Which three problems should the foundation prioritise, and in what order? Which one should it explicitly deprioritise? If there is an important problem missing from the list, introduce it and say which listed problem it should replace.

Defend the agenda rather than merely describing the field. For each prioritised problem, explain how far it is upstream of the others, how much progress philosophers can make without waiting for further technical or psychological research, and which design or regulatory choices its resolution would alter. Distinguish importance, tractability and neglectedness, and say what you would do when those criteria conflict. Identify the empirical discoveries or real-world developments over the next three years that would cause you to change the ordering. Finally, indicate where the differences between private and public, static and plastic, or consented and unconsented griefbots undermine any attempt at a single unified policy.
