I've got no background in philosophy or maths at all — I'm a plumber — but a few months back I went down a total rabbit hole about whether mathematics could just be broken at the bottom. Like, the basic rules everyone takes for granted, the axioms of arithmetic and so on: what if they quietly contradict each other and nobody's spotted it yet? From what I gather, clever people have genuinely worried about this, and there's a sense in which the system can't fully prove from inside itself that it won't blow up. That idea has honestly unnerved me more than any horror film. Everything we build, bank and dose rests on this stuff, and the floor might have a crack in it.

So I'd like you to write me a short story about it. Here's the setting I have in mind, though feel free to adjust: fifteen or twenty years from now, a big international project has spent a decade translating essentially all of serious mathematics into a form a computer can check line by line, and one ordinary Tuesday the checker reports that from the standard axioms you can derive an outright contradiction. Not a software bug — they spend agonising weeks ruling that out — but a real one, sitting in the rules everyone has used for over a century. Follow a small cast rather than the whole world: maybe a tired engineer on the project who's seen the proof and wishes she hadn't, a secondary school teacher whose pupils keep asking whether 2 + 2 is still 4, and a bridge inspector who suddenly doesn't know what her calculations are worth. I want the story to treat it as a lived event rather than a twist ending. What do people actually do on day three, on day thirty, when the news is out? Do things stop working, or does everything weirdly carry on fine, and which of those is more frightening? And if you can manage it, let the characters grope their way towards the idea that a contradiction might not have to be fatal — that maybe you can fence off the damaged part and keep reasoning around it, rather than everything becoming true and false at once — but let them earn that thought slowly instead of having some professor deliver it in a speech.

Aim for maybe 1500-2000 words, understated tone, and definately show the dread and the odd calm through small domestic details (a direct debit, a lottery draw, a kid's homework) rather than through grand speeches. No tidy resolution where it all turns out to be a dream or a hoax, please — I want to sit with the crack in the floor.
