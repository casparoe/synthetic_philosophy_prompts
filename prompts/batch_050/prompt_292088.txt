I'm a novelist midway through revising a manuscript, and a conversation with my editor has unsettled some things I'd long taken for granted about human nature -- which matters to me professionally, since my books live or die on whether readers find my characters psychologically credible. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher specializing in moral psychology, virtue ethics, and the situationist literature in social psychology, and to audit the following set of commitments for coherence. Please think carefully and take your time; I'd suggest beginning by brainstorming which pairs of these claims could possibly pull against each other before settling on your analysis, rather than committing to a verdict early.

Here is roughly where I've landed. (1) I believe people possess robust, stable character traits -- that some people really are honest, or generous, or cowardly, in a way that persists across years and explains what they do; this strikes me as both common sense and the indispensable foundation of believable fiction. (2) I'm persuaded by the classic social psychology experiments -- Milgram's obedience studies, Darley and Batson's Good Samaritan study, the dime-in-the-phone-booth work -- that ordinary people's behavior is driven overwhelmingly by situational features, often trivial ones, rather than by anything we'd call character. (3) I think virtue ethics is the right frame for moral evaluation: whether someone is kind or just or temperate matters more, for assessing them as a person, than whether any particular act of theirs conformed to the correct rule or maximized the good. (4) I hold that the truest windows into a person's character are the small, uncalculated moments -- how they treat a waiter, whether they bother to return a misdelivered letter -- precisely because nothing rides on them. (5) I believe people are highly predictable: if you know someone well, you can reliably anticipate how they'll behave. (6) And yet I also believe that genuinely novel circumstances can reveal that almost anyone is capable of conduct utterly out of keeping with everything you thought you knew about them -- indeed, that's the premise of the central plot turn in my manuscript.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture: tell me which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with which others, which pairings only appear to conflict and dissolve on inspection, and rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damaging. For each genuine tension, state what would have to give on each side and what the cheapest overall revision costs me -- I'm not attached to any particular one of these, but I want to know what I'm actually committed to. Finally, since this bears directly on my craft, close by saying briefly what your analysis implies for how a novelist should construct a credible character, given what the psychology shows. Please respond in American English.
