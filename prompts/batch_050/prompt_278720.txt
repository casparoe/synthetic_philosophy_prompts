I'm in my junior year of high school, and my AP Comparative Government class has spent the last few weeks on democratic backsliding. For our final seminar we each have to defend a case analysis, and I chose Hungary under Viktor Orbán since 2010. The framework we were taught goes roughly like this: modern democracies rarely die through coups anymore; instead, elected executives erode them from inside by capturing the referees (courts, electoral commissions, prosecutors), sidelining or weakening the opposition, and tilting the media and economic playing field, all while keeping elections on the calendar so the system still looks democratic from the outside. I want my presentation to map Hungary onto this framework carefully: Fidesz's two-thirds parliamentary supermajorities, the new constitution, the restructuring of the constitutional court and the judiciary, the redrawing of electoral districts and voting rules, the consolidation of most private media into government-friendly hands, and the pressure on independent institutions like the university that relocated abroad. For each of these, I want to say which part of the framework it fits and how cleanly.

But my teacher pushes back on anything that just applies a theory without testing it, so the second half of my presentation has to run the analysis in the other direction: what does Hungary do to the framework? Here is where I keep getting stuck, and where I'd like the analysis to be genuinely hard-headed. Almost everything Fidesz did was legal under the rules in force at the time, much of it backed by the supermajority the constitution itself made available, and voters have kept returning the party to power in elections that outside observers generally describe as free but not fair. So: if a population repeatedly and knowingly votes for the people doing the eroding, is it still backsliding, or is it democracy producing an outcome the framework's authors happen to dislike? Who gets to fix the baseline against which erosion is measured, and how do you keep that baseline from just encoding the political preferences of whoever coined the term?

Please write the case analysis I'd want to present: the disciplined mapping in the first half, then an honest account of where the framework strains against the Hungarian facts, and finally a verdict on whether Hungary confirms the backsliding concept, exposes a hole in it (say, that it has no principled way to distinguish erosion from legitimate majoritarian constitutional change), or reveals that the concept was only ever built for cases where the executive cheats rather than wins. If you think the concept can be repaired to handle the case, say what the repair would be and what it costs. I'd rather have a sharp argument I have to defend than a balanced survey I don't.
