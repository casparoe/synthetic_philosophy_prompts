In a popular book on everyday reasoning, I came across the following summary of Paul Grice's theory of meaning and implicature:

'Grice's basic insight was that what a speaker means typically runs ahead of what their words literally say. In conversation, speakers cooperate by observing certain principles -- be truthful, be informative, stay relevant, be clear -- and hearers interpret them on the assumption that these principles are being respected. When a speaker conspicuously departs from one of them, the hearer reasons that the speaker must intend to convey something further, and Grice called this further content an implicature. Since implicatures are generated by the conventions of cooperative talk, they become part of what the speaker has said, and like any piece of conventional meaning they can always be canceled by an explicit rider without contradiction. Speaker meaning itself Grice analyzed as nothing more than the speaker's intention to get the audience to believe something. On this picture, literal meaning is ultimately a psychological matter, which is why Grice held that sentences have no meanings of their own, independent of the intentions of those who utter them.'

The book concludes from this that dictionaries merely record statistical patterns in speakers' intentions, and that talk of 'the meaning of a sentence' is a convenient fiction.

Please audit the summary sentence by sentence: for each, say what a philosopher of language sympathetic to Grice would accept, what they would want to qualify, and what they would reject as a misrepresentation -- attending in particular to the distinction between conversational and conventional implicature, the relation between implicature and what is said, and the structure of the intentions involved in speaker meaning. Then assess whether the book's concluding claims follow from anything Grice actually argued, once the summary is corrected.
