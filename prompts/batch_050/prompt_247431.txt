In a seminar on colonial history, a classmate dismissed my appeal to the standard scholarly account of a nineteenth-century conquest by remarking that history is written by the victors, and moved on as though that settled the matter. I suspect the sentence hides several distinct claims of very different strength, and I would like them pulled apart. Lay out the precise theses it could be expressing: for instance, a descriptive claim about who controls the production and preservation of records and archives; an epistemic claim that victor-produced accounts are systematically distorted and therefore unreliable; a stronger relativist claim that historical truth just is whatever the powerful say it is; and perhaps a claim about whose suffering gets remembered rather than about which facts get asserted. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or false, noting where relevant the awkward counterexamples — cases where the losers' version of events came to dominate the historiography. Then tell me which readings, if any, would actually justify dismissing a standard account of a particular conquest without further evidential argument, and whether the slogan does the work my classmate needed it to do.
