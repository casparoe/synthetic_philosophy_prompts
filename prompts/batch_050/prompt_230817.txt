So I write about science and big ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a piece about whether culture actually evolves the way organisms do -- memes, internet trends, all that. I wanted a historical hook and went poking around in Darwin's The Descent of Man (1871), and in Chapter III, the one comparing the mental powers of humans and animals, I hit a bit about language that kind of blew my mind and kind of confused me.

I don't have the book in front of me so forgive the loose transcription, but the passage says roughly this: the development of languages and of species are "curiously parallel" -- both have family trees, both show homologies from common descent and analogies from similar formation processes, and within any language there is a constant struggle for life among the words and grammatical forms, in which the better, shorter, easier forms steadily win out, owing their success to their own inherent virtue.

Here's what I need help with, because I keep going back and forth:

1. That last bit about "their own inherent virtue" is doing a lot of work and I can't tell what it means. Does Darwin think words succeed because they're good for the speakers (shorter, easier to say), or because they're somehow good at getting themselves copied? Because my whole piece is about how modern meme people (Dawkins, Dennett, Blackmore, that crowd) make a big deal of the second thing -- a meme can spread even when it's bad for you, like a catchy tune or a chain letter. If Darwin only means the first thing, is his picture actually a different theory from memetics wearing the same clothes?

2. What exactly is the replicator here? In the biological case there's a thing (gene, organism) that makes copies of itself with variation. Words don't copy themselves -- speakers do the copying, imperfectly, on purpose, with goals. Does that break the analogy, or is intentional copying still "selection" in the relevant sense? I want to be able to say in the piece whether Darwin's language passage is a real application of his own theory or just a nice metaphor he'd have disowned if pressed.

3. The "curiously parallel" part about homologies and analogies in languages vs. species -- is he just describing what historical linguists already knew (language families, sound shifts), or is he making the stronger claim that the *process* is the same? Those seem like very different claims and the passage seems to slide between them, but maybe I'm misreading.

Basically: what is Darwin actually claiming in this passage, and is it the same thing the meme people claim? If I write "Darwin anticipated memetics," is that true, false, or true-but-misleading? Explain it to me like I'm smart but haven't thought about selection theory since college. No need for a reading list, I just want the argument laid out clearly enough that I can stress-test it in my draft.
