So I'm in a political theory seminar this semester (Democracy, Ancient and Modern) and we just spent two weeks on sortition. Our prof is kind of obsessed with this bit of Aristotle's Politics (Book VI, in the Jowett translation our course reader uses), where he lists the characteristics of democracy:

"that the appointment to all offices, or to all but those which require experience and skill, should be made by lot; that no property qualification should be required for offices, or only a very low one; that a man should not hold the same office twice, or not often, or in the case of few except military offices: that the tenure of all offices, or of as many as possible, should be brief"

She reads this like a checklist for judging real-world reform proposals, and over the term she's given us her verdicts on a bunch of them:

- A city replacing its elected planning commission with a citizens' panel drawn by lot from the voter rolls, one-year terms: "clearly more democratic, straight out of the reading."
- A ballot measure capping state legislators at two terms: "more democratic."
- Switching from appointed judges to directly elected ones: "less democratic -- and don't tell me the voters get a say, that's the oligarchic bit."
- A water district where candidates have to pass a qualifying exam and the winner is then drawn by lot from whoever passes: "more democratic."

Here's the new case. A country in the news is proposing a second legislative chamber of 200 members picked by lot from the entire adult population, no qualifications at all, members serving a single non-renewable six-month term -- BUT the chamber is purely advisory. It debates bills and issues recommendations that the elected house has to formally acknowledge but can completely ignore, which everyone expects it to do.

My question: what do you think she'll say about this one? I can't tell which rule she's actually running. If she's just ticking boxes off the Aristotle passage, this proposal ticks basically all of them -- lot, brief tenure, no repeat service, no qualifications -- so it should be an easy yes. But the passage is weirdly silent on whether the allotted body has to have any actual power, and I noticed her verdicts are also consistent with a totally different rule, something like "democratizing means breaking up the class of career politicians who hold real power," and on THAT rule an advisory chamber that gets ignored changes nothing.

So: (1) predict her verdict and tell me which rule you think the track record points to, (2) lay out what each of the two (or more?) rules that fit her four verdicts would say about the new case and where they diverge, (3) tell me whether the passage itself settles anything here -- like, is the list supposed to be conjunctive or a menu, does the "experience and skill" exception do any work, and does Aristotle's silence about the body's powers mean he doesn't care or that it's assumed, and (4) if her past verdicts genuinely underdetermine the new one, say what principle she'd be commiting to with each possible verdict, and what hypothetical reform would finally tell her rules apart.
