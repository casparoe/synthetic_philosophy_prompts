Okay so this has been gnawing at me since I reread the old MU-puzzle stuff and I want to know where my friend's argument breaks, because it feels like it proves way too much but I can't find the joint.

Quick setup so we're on the same page: there's a toy formal system where every string starts from MI, and you can (a) tack a U onto anything ending in I, (b) double whatever comes after the M, (c) swap any III for a U, (d) delete any UU. The puzzle is whether MU is producible. The classic resolution: you stop pushing symbols around and notice the count of I's is never divisible by 3 -- MI starts with one I, doubling keeps it non-divisible-by-3, and the other rules don't change the count mod 3 -- but MU has zero I's, so it's out. The whole point of the example is that you solve it by "jumping out of the system": reasoning about the system instead of within it.

My friend (big into the whole "your brain is just a computer" cluster of ideas) says this resolution is an illusion, and his argument goes:

(1) The mod-3 reasoning is not a derivation inside the MIU system -- nobody disputes this, it's the whole sales pitch.
(2) But humans doing math are finite physical systems, and any proof-checking we do can in principle be cashed out as mechanical symbol manipulation. So the mod-3 argument is itself a derivation inside some bigger formal system M -- the "rules of ordinary math plus my brain's hardware", roughly.
(3) So we never actually left the inside of anything. We just moved from a small system to a bigger one. "Jumping out" is a relative description, not a real move.
(4) What M produces is only theorems-of-M. Knowing that M derives "MU is not derivable in MIU" tells you what M does, not what's true -- unless you already know M is sound, and checking that would require stepping outside M into M', and so on forever.
(5) No system can certify itself from the inside, so the regress has no floor.
(6) Therefore nobody has ever genuinely gotten outside a formal system, and the MU "answer" -- and honestly every metamathematical result ever -- is at best "M says so", not knowledge.

And like... (6) swallows all of mathematics, including the math he used to state the argument, which seems like a sign something went wrong. But each step looks locally fine to me, and I especially don't want to give up (2), because the physicalist picture of minds seems non-negotiable on pain of magic. If I have to sacrifice something I'd rather it be some bit of (4), but I can't tell what's wrong with it.

So: which step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it actually cost me? Also if I'm mishandling the setup itself -- like if I've misdescribed the invariant or the whole framing is confused -- please just say that straight instead of politely playing along with a broken question. I'd rather be told the question is ill-posed than recieve a beautiful answer to the wrong thing.
