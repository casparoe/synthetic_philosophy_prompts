For an upper-level undergraduate seminar in applied ethics, write a decision tree that sorts students into positions on torture in the ticking time bomb scenario. The tree should begin at the shared starting point that torture is in general gravely wrong, and should lead, through eight to twelve binary or three-way branch questions, to at least the following seven leaves: (1) absolutism -- torture is impermissible even to avert a certain catastrophe; (2) threshold deontology -- torture is wrong in normal circumstances but the prohibition gives way at some scale of threatened harm; (3) act-consequentialist permission -- torture is permissible whenever expected harm averted exceeds harm done, with no special threshold; (4) the warrant proposal associated with Alan Dershowitz -- torture may be used in rare emergencies but only under prior judicial authorization; (5) what might be called the conscientious lawbreaker position -- an official may rightly torture in a genuine emergency, but the law must not authorize it in advance, and the official should act illegally and submit to prosecution afterward; (6) the epistemic dissolution -- the scenario's stipulated conditions (certainty that the captive planted the bomb, that it will detonate, that he knows where, that torture will extract the truth in time) cannot in practice be known, so the real question is about torture under uncertainty and the hypothetical misdirects us; (7) rule-consequentialist prohibition -- acts of torture can be justified case by case, but any institution or practice licensing them does more harm than the cases justify, so the answer is different at the level of the act and the level of the rule.

The diagnostic questions must do the sorting work: each branch should be a concrete case or claim a student can answer without knowing which position it leads to -- for instance, a case where the captive's innocent child is the only leverage, a case where the bomber will certainly talk but only after forty-eight hours of torture, or a case where the authorities are 60 percent rather than 100 percent sure. No question should simply ask 'do you think torture is ever permissible?'

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of names: first identify the dimensions along which the positions actually come apart (whether the prohibition is side-constraint or presumption; whether justification at the level of the act implies authorization at the level of the institution; whether the reply to the hypothetical is a moral answer or an epistemic rejection of the stipulations), then locate the seven positions in that space. Note explicitly which combinations of answers are left unoccupied and diagnose each briefly -- is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, unstable under reflection, or merely without a prominent defender? Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot reliably separate (I suspect the warrant proposal and the conscientious lawbreaker position will be harder to pry apart than the standard presentation suggests, since they agree about the act and disagree only about what the law should say), and say what further question, if any, would separate them.

Deliverable: the tree itself, a one-paragraph gloss for each leaf aimed at a student who has just been sorted into it, and a short note on which single branch question you expect to generate the most disagreement in a seminar room and why.
