I serve on the Trademark Licensing and Labor Standards Committee at my university, the body that decides which apparel companies may put our logo on their products. Next month we vote on whether to adopt a revised labor code. The sticking point is Article 4. Under the current code, licensees' suppliers must pay the legally mandated minimum wage or the prevailing industry wage, whichever is higher. The proposed revision, drafted after a year of student sit-ins, would instead require every supplier to pay a "living wage," benchmarked annually by an external NGO, in every country of operation. Our own consultant's report projects that two of our three licensees would respond by shifting production from Bangladesh and Honduras to automated facilities elsewhere, eliminating roughly 9,000 jobs at the affected factories. Wages at those factories currently run about 30 percent above the local legal minimum, and the jobs are sought-after: there are waiting lists.

I have spent enough years reading rationalist blogs and forums to be suspicious of both sides' rhetoric. The students say the current code makes the university complicit in exploitation. The licensees' representative calls the revision a piece of moral self-indulgence that will hurt the very workers it purports to help. Both sides reach for the word "exploitation" as though it settled something, and I suspect they mean different things by it. My own intuition is that a transaction can benefit both parties and still be morally defective: the workers are better off with these jobs than without them, but their alternatives are so bad, and their bargaining position so weak, that the surplus from the arrangement is split in a way that looks unfair, and the university is arguably a participant in that unfairness rather than a bystander. At the same time, I cannot shake the thought that a requirement cannot be justified by appeal to the interests of workers if its predictable effect is to leave those same workers worse off.

Draft the memo I will circulate to the committee before the vote. It should:

1. Recommend adopting Article 4, rejecting it, or adopting a modified version of it (you may design the modification), with a concrete implementation timeline the committee could actually act on.
2. Settle on an operational definition of exploitation that distinguishes taking unfair advantage of a weak bargaining position from merely transacting with people who are badly off, and apply that definition to the facts above rather than gesturing at it.
3. Engage seriously with what I take to be the strongest argument against my intuition: that since the affected workers would prefer to keep their jobs at current wages, and since no one is offering them a better option they could actually take, the revision does not defend them but abandons them — and fairness to particular persons cannot license making those very persons worse off.
4. Address whether it matters that the university is a wealthy institution that could in principle absorb higher licensing costs or subsidize compliance, as opposed to a case where the cost of the higher wage necessarily comes out of the workers' own share.
5. Run no longer than 1,200 words and be written so that a committee member who has never read the ethics literature can follow it.
