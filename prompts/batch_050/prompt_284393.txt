A colleague of mine in the philosophy department works on decision theory, and over the past year I have been presenting her with gambles and recording her verdicts about whether, and at what price, they are worth playing. I now want to predict her response to a new gamble and, more importantly, to figure out which rule she is actually running. Her verdicts so far:

1. A fair coin is flipped once. Heads pays $3, tails costs $1. Verdict: the game is worth $1, and she would play at any price below that.

2. A fair coin is flipped until it first lands heads; if the first heads occurs on toss n, the payoff is $2^n. Verdict: the expected payoff is infinite, yet she would not pay more than about $25 to play. Her stated reasons were that money has diminishing marginal utility and that no actual counterparty could fund the deep tail of the distribution.

3. The mirror image of the previous game: if the first heads occurs on toss n, the player pays $2^n. Verdict: she would not play at any price, since the expected loss is infinite.

4. Again the coin is flipped until the first heads on toss n, but the payoff is (-1)^(n+1) × (3/2)^n dollars, so payoffs alternate in sign and grow without bound, though more slowly than the probabilities shrink. Verdict: the game is worth exactly 3/7 of a dollar, and she would pay any amount less than that. She remarked that the alternating signs are harmless here because the relevant series converges absolutely.

5. A two-envelope setup: she chooses one of two envelopes known to contain x and 2x dollars and is then offered the chance to switch. Verdict: the standard switching argument equivocates on x, and she is indifferent between keeping and switching.

The new gamble is this. A fair coin is flipped until it first lands heads on toss n, and the payoff is (-1)^(n+1) × 2^n / n dollars: +$2 if n = 1, −$2 if n = 2, +$8/3 if n = 3, −$4 if n = 4, +$32/5 if n = 5, and so on, with negative payoffs being amounts the player must pay. (This is a version of the Pasadena game.) Everything is settled in a single draw: there are no rounds, just one realized state n and one net payment.

Please address the following:

(a) Predict her verdict on this game. Will she name a fair price, refuse to assign one, or do something in between, and at what confidence do you hold this prediction?

(b) State at least two general rules, each consistent with all five earlier verdicts, that diverge on this game, and give each rule's verdict. Note that the series of expectation terms here converges conditionally but not absolutely, so your rules should differ in how they treat that fact.

(c) Say which rule the track record best supports overall and why. In particular, discuss whether her appeal to diminishing marginal utility in case 2 extends to this game, and whether her remark about absolute convergence in case 4 was an incidental observation or a load-bearing part of her method.

(d) Describe a further gamble whose outcome would best discriminate between the leading candidate rules you identified in (b), and explain what her verdict on it would establish.

Please write your answer in American English.
