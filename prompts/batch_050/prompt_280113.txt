At a barbecue last weekend two friends of mine got into a long argument I couldn't settle. Background: our friend Dan turned down $450 for a ticket to a playoff game he'd bought for $90, and he admitted that if he hadn't already had the ticket, he'd never in a million years have paid $450 for one.

(1) Marcus said this is the textbook endowment effect and it's just a bias: the ticket has one value, and Dan priced it two different ways depending on nothing but whether he happened to be holding it. Any theory of rational preference says you should be indifferent between keeping the ticket and having the $450 if $450 is genuinely more than you'd pay, so Dan is leaving money on the table because of a quirk.

(2) Jess pushed back hard. She said "the ticket has one value" is exactly what's in question. Owning it changes what losing it would mean: Dan has already made plans around going, told his brother he'd meet him there, pictured the whole weekend. The value isn't just the seat -- it's the seat *in his life*. Marcus is defining whatever you'd pay before you own something as the "true" value and then declaring any departure from it irrational, which is rigging the game.

(3) Marcus said the plans point can't be the whole story, because the effect shows up in labs with mugs and pens people were handed thirty seconds earlier -- nobody's made life plans around a mug. So whatever's going on, it kicks in way before there's any real attachment, which means it's a framing thing, not a genuine change in value.

(4) Jess said fine, but even the instant version isn't obviously irrational. There's no such thing as the value of something independent of a position to view it from. Gains and losses are just different kinds of events -- a loss takes something out of your situation that you were counting on, a gain adds something you weren't. Asking "what's the mug worth, really, to nobody in particular" is like asking for the view from nowhere. Marcus wants one number because it makes his math clean, not because people actually work that way.

(5) Marcus's last move: if she's right and there's no position-independent value at all, then nobody can ever say a trade made anyone better off, or that Dan's choice is fine *or* mistaken, or anything -- you've thrown out the only tool that lets you evaluate decisions at all. So her defense of Dan costs her the ability to say Dan did well. Jess said maybe, or maybe it just means economists need better tools, and she didn't see why she had to fix welfare economics at a barbecue.

So: go through their exchange and tell me who's right at each step. Does the mug point in (3) actually do the damage Marcus thinks? Is Jess's "view from nowhere" move in (4) deep or just evasive? And does Marcus's closing argument in (5) land -- does defending the endowment effect really commit you to giving up on evaluating choices? I'd also like to know if there's something both of them are missing.
