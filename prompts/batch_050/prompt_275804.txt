So my reading group has been stuck on the badness of pain for about a month now and we've split into two camps that keep talking past each other. One side (call them the phenomenal intrinsicalists) thinks pain is bad simply in virtue of how it feels -- the nasty phenomenal character is itself the bad-making feature, full stop, so anything that feels like that is bad for whoever feels it. The other side (the attitudinalists, roughly) thinks the badness of pain is located in our negative attitudes toward it: the aversion, the dislike, the way it intrudes and motivates us to escape it. On their view the raw feeling could in principle be there without being bad, if the minding-it part were gone.

What kicked the whole fight off was a neurologist friend of mine mentioning pain asymbolia over coffee -- patients who, after certain brain lesions, will say they still feel pain and can rate its intensity but don't seem to mind it at all, plus the similar reports from people on morphine who say the pain is still there it just doesn't bother them anymore. The intrinsicalists in the group say these patients are confused, or that whatever they're feeling isn't really pain, or that their testimony is just unreliable. The attitudinalists say these cases are exactly what their view predicts and the other side is doing desperate gerrymandering. We also argued about masochists, chili-pepper eaters, and runners who describe their pain as somehow good or at least welcome, and about whether congenitally not feeling pain would be bad even if it never led to injury.

I'd like you to act as an expert in philosophy of mind and value theory and draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a serious good-faith adversarial collaboration. Take your time and think carefully -- maybe start by brainstorming what each side's strongest commitments actually are before you write the document. The deliverable should have: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims, including claims about what the clinical cases actually show and what each theory is minimally committed to; (2) a disagreements table where each side's position is phrased carefully enough that that side would accept the wording as their own, no strawmen; (3) the cruxes -- the specific sub-questions such that settling them would actually move one camp or the other (I suspect one crux is whether asymbolia patients are still really in pain, and another is whether first-person reports of not minding one's pain should be taken at face value, but tell me if you think the real cruxes lie elsewhere); and (4) for each crux, a case, thought experiment, or kind of evidence both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction about it written down in advance. If it turns out part of the disagreement is just verbal -- e.g., they're using pain to mean different things -- please identify exactly which part and say so explicitly in the document.
