I am retiring at the end of the month after thirty-one years in municipal pest management for a mid-sized American city -- I started as a field technician baiting rat burrows and finish as director of the department -- and the city has chosen my farewell ceremony as the occasion to publicly unveil an ordinance I spent my last decade arguing for: a "least-harm pest management" code that bans anticoagulant rodenticides outright (on grounds both of the slow internal bleeding they inflict and of the hawks, owls, and pets that die after eating poisoned rodents), prohibits glue boards, makes exclusion and sanitation the mandatory first response to any infestation, funds a fertility-control pilot for the rat population, and permits lethal measures only when non-lethal ones have failed and only by the quickest-killing available method. Write the speech I will give, roughly eight to ten minutes when delivered.

The room is divided in a way that makes this hard. Present will be: the pest-control contractors I came up through the ranks with, some of whom feel the ordinance implicitly indicts the work we did together for decades; animal-welfare advocates, some of whom think the ordinance is a fig leaf because it still permits killing; and ordinary residents, several of whom are currently dealing with rats in their kitchens and want to know whether the city has gone soft. I need all three groups to hear something honest.

The speech should do real philosophical work rather than merely reciting the ordinance's provisions. Specifically, I want it built around the question a journalist once put to me at a press conference -- roughly, how many rats I had personally killed, and whether that number had ever kept me up at night -- and around the answer I have arrived at after years of turning it over. That answer must thread several needles. It should not repudiate my own career or pronounce the men I trained to be cruel; I believe most of the killing we did was justified, and I want to say why without hedging. It should not pretend that rodents are pets or that a house with rats in the walls is a tolerable thing. It should explain, in terms a layperson will actually follow, why it is coherent to hold both that a rat's suffering has genuine moral weight and that protecting human homes and health sometimes overrides it -- and why, given that, the manner and the necessity of the killing matter rather than just the outcome. And it should explain why preventing rats from being born sits in a different moral category from killing them, without overselling fertility control as a painless fix (it is slow, it is partial, and the rats it spares are ones that will never exist, which is a strange thing to claim credit for).

The speech should end by looking forward rather than backward: what I hope this ordinance becomes, and what would count as its failure. No barn-burning peroration; my register is plainspoken, and the room knows it.
