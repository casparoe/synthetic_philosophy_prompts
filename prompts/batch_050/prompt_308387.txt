My friend cornered me with this after we both read that old dialogue about the conscious ant colony (the one where the colony itself seems to be having the conversation) and I cant find where it breaks, but the conclusion feels unhinged so something has to give. The argument, as he laid it out:

(1) A system has a mind if it has the right kind of functional organization: its parts interact in such a way that the whole thing processes information, ends up with something like beliefs and goals, and responds flexibly to its enviroment. What the parts are made of -- neurons, ants, silicon -- is irrelevant.

(2) An ant colony fits this perfectly. Individual ants are dumb as rocks, but their interactions add up to coherent, flexible, apparently intelligent behavior at the colony level: it forages, it defends itself, it makes tradeoffs, it "decides" to move the nest. So by (1), the colony is a mind.

(3) But by that standard, so is a national economy. Millions of dumb local transactions add up to coherent, adaptive, flexible behavior: prices re-route around shocks, the system "punishes" bad bets and "learns" from bubbles, it even seems to have moods.

(4) And the internet too, and maybe the global climate system. Anything big and decentralized with feedback loops qualifies.

(5) But that's absurd. There's nothing it's like to be the bond market.

(6) So (1) has to be false: functional organization isn't sufficient for mindedness, and something about actual biological wetware must be doing the real work.

He says this shows the whole "mind is substrate-independent" idea collapses the moment you take it seriously. I'd honestly rather keep (1) and (2) if I can -- the colony seems like the single best case for a group mind, and giving up substrate-independence feels like giving up on AI for no good reason. So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine when he said it, and what does rejecting it cost me? If more than one step is fishy, rank them by how much I'd have to pay.
