Below is a draft essay I have written for an undergraduate philosophy of mind journal. The essay defends a position I call thin experientialism in the dispute between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett over whether dreams are experiences had during sleep. Please read the draft and then provide detailed comments, addressing in particular the five questions listed at the end. I want frank criticism, not encouragement: if an argument is confused, say so and explain why. Please write your comments in British English.

---

Sleeping Through It: A Modest Experientialism about Dreams

1. From Aristotle to Freud to contemporary sleep science, nearly everyone has assumed the received view: a dream is an experience, or a sequence of experiences, undergone during sleep and afterwards recalled, however imperfectly, on waking. Two philosophers have attacked this view from different directions. Norman Malcolm, in his book Dreaming, argued on broadly Wittgensteinian grounds that dreams cannot be experiences had during sleep; Daniel Dennett, in his essay Are Dreams Experiences?, argued that the received view is an optional and probably false empirical hypothesis. In this essay I defend a chastened version of the received view, which I call thin experientialism, and I claim that it captures what is right in both attacks while avoiding what is wrong in each.

2. Malcolm's argument, as I understand it, turns on criteria. Suppose you wake and report a dream. What could settle whether you really underwent, during the night, the experiences your report describes? Nothing but the report itself. No observation of you mid-sleep, no measurement, and no subsequent discovery could confirm or disconfirm the claim that a particular experience occurred at a particular time in the night. But where nothing could count as a report's being mistaken about an inner past episode, Malcolm argues, the picture of a determinate episode that the report aims to describe loses its grip. The waking report therefore should not be understood on the model of memory — a possibly faithful, possibly faulty reproduction of an earlier experience — at all. It is more like an avowal: a first-person utterance that is authoritative precisely because nothing could check it. On this view, to say one dreamt is not to recall the night but to respond to the morning.

3. Dennett accepts much of Malcolm's scepticism about the memory model but offers a positive mechanism in place of Malcolm's quietism: the cassette theory. Dream narratives, he suggests, are not experienced during sleep and then recalled; they are composed — perhaps long in advance, perhaps in the instant of waking — and inserted into memory, so that what it is like to have dreamt is constituted by what memory presents on waking. His motivating cases are dreams whose endings seem causally tailored to the stimulus that woke the dreamer, as in the famous nineteenth-century anecdote of a sleeper whose long dream of the French Terror culminated in a guillotining at the very moment the bedstead fell on his neck. If the dream's narrative led up to the blow, the narrative cannot have unfolded in real time; better to suppose the whole storyline was never experienced at all, but only remembered.

4. I believe both views are refuted, or at least badly wounded, by an experiment neither author took seriously enough: the lucid-dreaming studies in which subjects, having agreed beforehand on a code of deliberate eye movements, execute that code during sleep that is independently verified as REM sleep, and afterwards report dreams whose content corresponds to the signals — counting, say, or tracking a moving object, for roughly the duration the signals indicate. If waking reports were pure morning confabulation, the systematic correspondence between real-time signalling during sleep and the content later reported would be an inexplicable miracle. The natural explanation is that something experiential was occurring during sleep, and that this activity guided both the signals and the subsequent report. So the answer to the question whether anything is experienced during sleep is, at least sometimes, yes.

5. But the lucid-dreaming evidence does not vindicate the received view in its naive form, and here Malcolm and Dennett each get something right. Notice what the evidence shows and what it does not. It shows occurrent experiential activity during sleep; it does not show that ordinary, non-lucid dream reports faithfully reproduce a fully formed nocturnal narrative. Ordinary reports remain suspicious: too well plotted, too neatly concluded, too ready to absorb the alarm clock's ring as a dramatic finale. I therefore propose thin experientialism. During sleep there is occurrent experiential material — fragmentary, imagistic, affect-laden — but it lacks the temporal ordering, connective structure, and much of the content that the waking report attributes to it. The report is a retrospective composition constrained by genuine material: neither a memory of a finished film (as the received view would have it) nor a fabrication from whole cloth (as Dennett's stronger claims suggest), but something like the account a dozing cinema-goer might give of a film she genuinely glimpsed in pieces. Malcolm is right that the report is not a memory in the ordinary sense; Dennett is right that narrative composition can occur at or around waking; both are wrong to deny occurrent dream experience, and the received view is wrong to read the report's narrative structure back into the night.

6. I conclude that the question 'Are dreams experiences?' conflates two questions: whether experiential activity occurs during sleep, and whether waking reports accurately represent what occurred. Thin experientialism answers the first affirmatively, at least sometimes, and the second mostly negatively, for ordinary dreams. The appearance of a straight fight between Malcolm and Dennett dissolves once the two questions are prised apart, and what remains is an empirical research programme about how much constraint the nocturnal material places on the morning's narrative — a programme for which lucid-dreaming methods seem ideally suited.

My questions for you:

1. Accuracy and fairness. Have I misrepresented either Malcolm or Dennett anywhere? I have deliberately paraphrased rather than quoted. If any of my attributions — the criterion argument, the avowal analogy, the cassette theory, the use of the guillotine-dream anecdote — distort the actual positions, please identify the distortion and state the position more carefully.

2. Stability of the thesis. Does thin experientialism hold up as a genuinely distinct position, or does it collapse into Dennett's view with the word 'fragmentary' doing all the work? In particular: if the waking report contributes all of the narrative structure, ordering, and much of the content, is the residue of 'occurrent experiential material' substantial enough to make my disagreement with Dennett more than terminological?

3. The lucid-dreaming argument. Does my Section 4 prove too much, too little, or roughly the right amount? Consider at least: whether lucid dreams are too atypical to bear on ordinary dreaming; whether the signal-report correspondence really excludes confabulation; and whether my inference from occurrent signalling behaviour to occurrent experience smuggles in the very assumption Malcolm challenged.

4. The strongest objection I have ignored. What is the most damaging objection to the essay that I have not addressed at all? (One candidate I have worried about: it may be incoherent to posit experience wholly lacking in temporal or narrative structure, in which case Section 5 posits something inconceivable. If you think this objection succeeds, please explain why; if not, please explain how thin experientialism should respond to it.)

5. Structure and presentation. Is the ordering of sections effective? Would the essay be stronger if the positive proposal came earlier, if Malcolm and Dennett were given more (or less) space, or if the concluding dissolution were flagged in the introduction? Please suggest concrete reorganisations rather than general advice.
