Write a parody of a birdwatching field guide where the "birds" are the positions people take on what large language models actually are. Deadpan taxonomic style throughout, like a proper field guide: field marks, habitat, range, behavior, similar species, the works. Around 600 words.

Cover at least these three species, and invent one or two more of your own:

- The Common Agent (believes things, wants things, acts for reasons; frequently reported by journalists and tech executives, but nearly every confirmed sighting has turned out, on closer inspection, to be something else)
- The Simulator (the view that the base model has no beliefs or goals of its own and just impersonates characters who do; identification headache: the characters it simulates can be indistinguishable from real agents, and asking the specimen directly is useless, since both species produce the same fluent first-person call when prompted)
- The Stochastic Parrot (drab plumage, just predicts the next word; listed as a similar species under everything above, because every identification dispute eventually ends with one side accusing the other of having spotted one)

Include a dichotomous identification key that is technically rigorous but keeps collapsing at the crucial step (e.g. "does the behavior persist when the context is cleared?" "does it have goals, or merely goal-shaped dispositions?" — the key should keep punting to questions nobody currently knows how to answer). Also include at least one note from a regional records commitee rejecting a claimed Agent sighting on procedural grounds, and a conservation-status line for the Agent, which has been repeatedly declared extinct by philosophers only to be rediscovered by the press within weeks.

The humor should come from getting the philosophy of mind right: whether functional organization is enough for genuine belief, whether belief-ascription is a fact about the world or just a stance we adopt toward systems, whether the goals live in the weights or in the prompt, and the mimicry problem (behavior produced by imitating agents is not evidence of agency). Don't name any real people. No winking at the reader — the guide should read as if its authors take all of this completely seriously.
