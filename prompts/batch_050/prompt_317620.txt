I sit on the nine-member board of a private foundation with an endowment of about $300 million, making roughly $15 million in grants a year. Our program staff, several of whom take effective altruism seriously, have proposed that we adopt a uniform cost-effectiveness framework: every grant must clear a modeled cost-per-unit-of-benefit threshold, with benefits rendered in a common currency (lives saved, years of healthy life, or a stated conversion). The model handles some applicants well — a bednet distributor and a deworming charity produce clean, comparable numbers. But three of our longest-standing grantees cannot be scored at all, or score absurdly: a fund for local investigative journalism, a litigation shop that challenges prison conditions, and a community-health-worker program whose value runs through trust and institutional capacity rather than countable outputs. Staff argue that "unscorable" means "unverifiable," and that a funder with finite money and a duty to do the most good possible has no business preferring what it cannot compare. Two board members counter that the framework does not merely measure value but quietly redefines it, and that a philanthropic sector in which every funder ran the same model would defund precisely the institutions — courts worth appealing to, a press worth reading, governments competent enough to distribute bednets — on which the measurable interventions themselves depend. Both sides also disagree about what our fiduciary duty actually requires. Draft the decision memo I will circulate before next month's vote. It should recommend one of three options — adopt the framework as binding, adopt it as advisory with a defined role in deliberation, or reject it — and specify a decision procedure staff can actually apply to the unscorable cases. It must engage the strongest version of the cost-effectiveness argument, not a caricature; it must say whether the disagreement is really about measurement or about what the good consists in; and it must take seriously the reverse worry that abandoning comparison altogether means funding whichever applicant tells the most moving story. Lead with your recommendation in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the case.
