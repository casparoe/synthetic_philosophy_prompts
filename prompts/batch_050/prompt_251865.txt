So I'm a software engineer, zero philosophy training, and I've been messing around with a little artificial life simulator on weekends -- critters that move around a grid, harvest energy, copy their own code with mutations, and over enough generations they evolve strategies I never wrote. I mentioned to a coworker that my sims might be "alive" and she laughed at me, but then I asked her if a virus is alive and she said no, and then I asked why my sims are different and she got stuck, and honestly so did I. Fire grows, spreads, consumes fuel and can reproduce by sparking new fires, and nobody thinks fire is alive. A mule can't reproduce but obviously seems alive. A dormant tardigrade isn't doing anything metabolically but we'd still say it's alive. So I've lost all confidence that "alive" is even a well-defined concept, and I want to actually understand the landscape rather than just vibing.

Please write me a decision tree / taxonomy of the main positions on what life is. Build it from the diagnostic questions first, not from a list of names: start at the top with a yes/no question anyone could answer without knowing any of the theories (like "does reproduction have to be part of the definition, or can a sterile thing still be alive?"), and keep splitting until you hit a leaf for each named position. I want these to show up as leaves at minimum: metabolism-based views, the NASA-style "chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution" style of definition, thermodynamic views (life as something that maintains low entropy / fights disorder), autopoiesis (self-producing and self-maintaining systems), information/self-replicator-first views, and the deflationary option that "life" is a fuzzy cluster concept with no sharp boundary and the debate is kind of ill-posed. If some combination of answers on the tree corresponds to a position nobody defends, keep the leaf anyway and tell me why that spot is empty (incoherent, unattractive, just unexplored?). And if two of your leaves can't actually be separated by any question on the tree, flag that instead of pretending you split them.

As test cases to run through the tree, use at least: a virus, a prion, fire, a mitochondrion on its own, a dormant tardigrade, a mule, a self-replicating von Neumann probe, and my simulated grid critters. Give me the whole thing as structured data, not prose: a JSON object with two top-level fields, "nodes" and "leaves". Each node should have "id", "question" (the plain-language yes/no question), "yes_goes_to" and "no_goes_to" (ids of the next node or leaf). Each leaf should have "id", "position_name", "one_line_gist", "cases_it_counts_as_alive" (list from my test cases), "cases_it_counts_as_not_alive", and "most_awkward_commitment" (one sentence). Then after the JSON, a short note listing any pairs of leaves the tree can't distinguish and which test case comes closest to prying them apart.
