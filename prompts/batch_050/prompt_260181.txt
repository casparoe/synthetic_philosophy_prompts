I'm preparing a discussion unit for a graduate seminar on historical anthropology -- the working title of the unit is "Do Societies Fail, or Merely Change?" -- and I've been collecting scholarly positions that push back against the straightforward civilizational-collapse narrative found in popular treatments of the Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya, and Rapa Nui. I've reached the point where I want to know whether the space of anti-collapse moves is larger than I think it is. Here are the positions I already have on my syllabus; please do not repeat, reword, or recombine them:

1. Rational simplification (associated with Joseph Tainter): complexity yields diminishing marginal returns, so collapse is often an economizing decision -- a society shedding administrative overhead it can no longer profitably maintain. On this view the "failure" framing mistakes an adaptive strategy for a catastrophe.
2. Collapse as a phase (adaptive-cycle and panarchy models from resilience ecology): release and reorganization are normal phases of any adaptive system, and what looks like death is a rigid system shedding brittleness so that renewal becomes possible.
3. The elite-lens objection (associated with Patricia McAnany, Norman Yoffee, and their collaborators): what the archaeological record typically shows ending is elite practice -- monumental construction, royal inscriptions, long-distance luxury exchange -- while the bulk of the population persists in comparable or improved conditions. "Collapse" generalizes the fate of a thin ruling stratum to the entire society.
4. Post-collapse welfare gains: skeletal and settlement evidence sometimes suggests better nutrition, greater autonomy, or reduced labor extraction for commoners once central authority dissolves, so that from the bottom up collapse can be an improvement, even a liberation.
5. The continuity-of-descendants objection: descendants of supposedly collapsed peoples (the modern Maya being the standard case) reasonably object that their ancestors did not vanish, and that the term reflects an outsider's fixation on what stopped being built rather than what continued to be lived.

What I want is one further position, distinction, or line of argument in this debate that is genuinely absent from the list -- not a restatement, special case, or conjunction of items 1-5, and not merely an application of one of them to a case study I haven't mentioned (so don't just tell me the Western Roman Empire fits item 3). It has to be usable in a seminar: state it precisely, explain what it licenses or rules out that the listed positions do not, and then give me your honest assessment of whether it survives scrutiny. If the best available addition strikes you as weak, question-begging, or ultimately a disguised version of one of my five, say so plainly rather than inflating it -- I would rather end up with a candid verdict that the list is complete than with a padded syllabus.
