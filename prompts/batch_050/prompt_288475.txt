My oldest friend and I spent most of last Saturday evening arguing about the mind–body problem, and we ended up stuck on a word rather than on the brain. She kept saying that talk of mental facts being 'grounded in' physical facts is just a fancy way of saying the physical explains the mental, and that if you strip away the explanation talk there is nothing left. I found myself insisting that grounding is a metaphysical relation that would hold whether or not anyone ever explained anything, but when she asked me what would decide between us — what a theory of grounding would even have to look like for my side to win — I had to concede I did not know. I am now writing an essay on the metaphysics of ground, and I want to fix the terms of the debate before I get lost in the literature.

So: write the desiderata that any adequate theory of metaphysical grounding must meet, treating the specification itself as the deliverable. Cover which putative cases it must vindicate and which it must refuse — for instance, that the singleton set is grounded in its sole member rather than the reverse, that a conjunction is grounded in its conjuncts jointly while a disjunction is grounded in whichever disjunct is true, and that the fact that something exists is not grounded in any particular object. Cover which formal features the theory must build in and which it may leave open: irreflexivity, asymmetry, transitivity, factivity, and whether full grounds necessitate what they ground, with the consideration motivating each demand. Cover, too, how the theory must relate grounding to its neighbours — entailment, supervenience, causation, and explanation — since half our quarrel turned on whether 'in virtue of' collapses into one of these or stands apart. For each desideratum, give the case or argument behind it, and mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which look to you like the house rules of one camp.

Two further requests. First, if some subset of these desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied — I have a suspicion, for example, that treating grounding as a kind of explanation sits poorly with some of the purely formal constraints, and that insisting on well-foundedness conflicts with certain live views about infinitely divisible matter — then say so explicitly and identify the fault lines, since a list that proves inconsistent is itself a result. Second, tell me which single desideratum does the most work in excluding contenders. Only then, briefly, indicate how the main families of theory fare against the list: the approach that treats ground as governed by a formal logic of its own, the orthodoxy that treats it as a metaphysical priority relation among facts or entities, and my friend's deflationary position that it is nothing over and above a species of explanation. Keep the scoring short; the criteria are where I need the thinking done.
