After forty years as an experimental physicist, I have recently begun reading philosophy and have become stuck on a dispute about probabilistic reasoning when the number of observers itself is at issue.

Consider a deliberately simplified cosmological case. Two mutually exclusive theories fit all ordinary astronomical evidence equally well. Theory A predicts that exactly one civilization will ever produce observers in circumstances subjectively indistinguishable from mine. Theory B predicts that a trillion such civilizations will do so. Before taking my own existence into account, assign the theories equal prior probabilities. There is no conventional observation I can make—no telescope measurement, laboratory result, or historical record—that distinguishes them; they differ only in how many observers like me they imply.

Two camps in my reading group agree about this setup but disagree about how to update. The first says I should reason as though I were randomly sampled from among the observers who actually exist. On this view, the bare fact that I exist confirms only that the world contains at least one suitable observer; it does not, by itself, make a theory predicting a trillion observers vastly more probable than one predicting one. The second camp says I should reason as though my existence were sampled from all possible observers a theory might have produced. Because Theory B offers a trillion times as many opportunities for an observer with my evidence to exist, it receives an enormous likelihood boost. These are sometimes called the self-sampling and self-indication assumptions.

The second result strikes me as the “presumptuous philosopher” problem: apparently, one can settle a cosmological question from an armchair merely by noting that populous theories contain more possible copies of oneself. Yet the first camp’s position also seems strained in cases involving duplicates, branching futures, or repeated trials, where refusing to count the number of possible observer-moments appears to ignore relevant information. Whenever we press the point, each side accuses the other of using an illegitimate reference class or confusing the existence of an observer with the occurrence of a particular observer’s evidence. No one disputes the arithmetic once a sampling rule has been chosen.

What kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it a substantive dispute about the correct axioms of inductive probability, a concealed disagreement about what should count as my evidence, a verbal dispute over “randomly selected observer,” or a clash between prior intuitions that formalization merely exposes rather than resolves? Please diagnose the impasse using the stipulated case, not by surveying every variant of anthropic reasoning. Identify precisely where the two rules diverge, what further fact—if any—could decide between them, and what would count as philosophical progress: a new distinction, a decision-theoretic argument, a formal consistency result, or an admission that the original question is under-specified.

In answering, distinguish clearly between points that are broadly settled among people who work on this problem and points that remain genuinely contested. Do not present one anthropic rule as the consensus unless that characterization is justified. Where your diagnosis depends on a controversial conception of evidence, probability, or personal identity, say so explicitly.
