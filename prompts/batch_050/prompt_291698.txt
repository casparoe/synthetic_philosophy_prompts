Okay so I need a referee. I was up way too late last night arguing with my friend Dan about van Fraassen's Reflection principle (we'd both had a bit to drink, which may be relevant) and he walked me into an argument that I'm pretty sure proves way too much, but I couldn't pin down where it breaks. Here it is as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Reflection: if I'm rational, then conditional on learning that my credence in p tomorrow will be r, my credence in p right now should be r. When I know what my future self will think, and I take my future self to be at least as well-positioned as me, I should just defer to them now.
(2) There are plenty of cases where I can rationally predict the direction my credence will move before I've done the work. Example from my own life: I'm halfway through a long book about the problem of evil, and I can tell from how it's going that when I finish it my credence in God's existence will be lower than it is now. Another one: a mathematician who has one line left in a proof and can see where it's going knows what she'll believe when she writes it.
(3) By (1) and (2), in these cases rationality requires me to adopt the future credence right now, without finishing the book or the proof.
(4) But then actually finishing the book is epistemically pointless. The rational move is to skip the remaining chapters and just lower the credence. The reading was never doing any work the prediction wasn't already doing.
(5) And this generalizes: any time inquiry or deliberation has a predictable outcome, the rational agent skips the inquiry and just conforms to the prediction. So gathering evidence is never rationally required -- only predicting its upshot is.
(6) That's absurd, so something in (1)-(5) has to go.

Dan thinks (1) is the casualty and that Reflection is just false, full stop. I really don't want to give up Reflection entirely because it seems like the only thing making sense of why it's irrational to say "I'll probably come to believe p, but I don't believe it yet" in cases where nothing weird is going on. But when I pushed on (4) he said I was just sentimental about doing homework.

So: which step do you actually reject, and why does it look so plausible if it's the wrong one? And what does rejecting it cost me -- if I keep Reflection, am I really committed to my friend being irrational when he predicts his own mind changing, or is there some distinction hiding in the setup that dissolves the whole thing? Bonus points if you can say something about whether the mathematician case and the book case are actually the same case, because Dan conceded they might not be and then claimed it didn't matter, which felt like cheating.
