I sit on the environmental and ethics committee of a mid-sized logistics firm (about 600 staff), a role I landed partly because I've been a reader of rationalist blogs for over a decade and colleagues tend to route anything resembling an expected-value calculation my way. The committee reviews around a dozen employee proposals a year: switching the canteen to vegetarian-by-default, banning short-haul business flights, buying carbon offsets for the delivery fleet, boycotting a supplier with poor labour practices, matching staff donations to climate charities. Nearly every proposal runs into the same objection from one particularly sharp colleague: our firm is a rounding error, nothing we buy or abstain from will measurably change global emissions or animal welfare, and therefore the proposals are pure signalling at the company's expense. I suspect this objection is sometimes decisive and sometimes a dodge, and I am tired of relitigating it from scratch at every meeting, with the outcome depending on who argues best on the day rather than on the structure of the case.

I would like a reusable decision procedure we could actually run in a one-hour meeting: an ordered series of tests, flowchart-in-words style, for determining when the "your individual contribution makes no difference" objection genuinely defeats a proposal and when it does not. The procedure should operationalise the distinctions the philosophical literature on this problem draws -- the difference between smooth and lumpy or threshold effects, expected impact versus guaranteed impact, threshold-and-elasticity arguments about consumer purchasing (the claim that one more purchase has a small chance of triggering a large restocking decision, and that the expected effect may survive even when no particular purchase is pivotal), Parfit-style cases of imperceptible harms and overdetermination, and the further considerations that complicate the picture, such as signalling, norm-setting, precommitment, and complicity. For each step, say what evidence would count as a sufficient answer, where the committee could realistically obtain it, and what to do when no number is available. Mark clearly the points where the procedure terminates with a rejection, the points where it must hand the decision back to human value judgement, and the steps that are really judgement calls dressed up as procedure.

Please then run the procedure on three of our real proposals: making the canteen vegetarian-by-default; banning short-haul business flights, which the proposer openly defends as largely symbolic; and purchasing offsets for the delivery fleet. At least one of these applications should produce a verdict the committee will not like, and I would like you to be explicit when that happens rather than softening the conclusion. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot settle, so that I do not oversell it to the board as more mechanical than it is.
