Hi, I'm a second-year undergrad taking a philosophy of emotion module (part of the mind syllabus), and our exam always includes a question where they hand you a made-up thinker -- like "Professor X holds that fear is..." -- and you have to work out which theory of emotion they're committed to and where their view collapses under counterexample. I keep freezing on these, so I want a decision tree I can run any stated view through to sort it into one of the main families: pure feeling theories (James-style, the emotion just is the perception of the bodily changes), judgment theories (the emotion is an evaluative judgment, Nussbaum-ish), perceptual or construal theories (the emotion is a perception of value, not a judgment), and hybrid views that want feeling plus cognition.

Some constraints so the tree is actually usable:

- 7 to 9 questions max, each branch labeled with the question that splits it, and every question has to be a concrete case a person can answer without knowing any of the jargon -- no "does the theory posit X?" questions. Use whichever of these actually do diagnostic work: being terrified of a spider you sincerely believe is harmless, the calm pilot who counts as afraid but reports no feeling at all, grief that runs for years but only surfaces occasionally, free-floating anxiety with no object, a dog's fear of the vacuum, shame about something you don't think is shameful. Add cases of your own if a split needs one.
- Build the tree from the logical space first (is feeling necessary? are bodily changes necessary? is a judgment necessary? does the emotion have to be about something?) and then locate the actual positions in that space, rather than the other way around. For combinations where nobody sits, say whether the slot is empty because the view is incoherent or just unloved, and commit to which.
- Where the tree can't seperate two positions -- I'm told judgment and perceptual views are annoyingly hard to pull apart -- flag that honestly instead of papering over it, and say what extra case would split them.

And please don't hand me a neutral tool and leave the hard calls to me. Commit: pick which question goes at the root and defend that choice against the runner-up, give a definite verdict on each empty node, and at the end tell me straight which family you think survives the recalcitrant-emotion cases best and why, since that's the follow-up the examiner always asks and where I lose my marks.
