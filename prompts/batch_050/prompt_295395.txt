I teach a final-year undergraduate module on collective responsibility and the ethics of historical judgement, and I am currently marking the take-home examination. I have a large pile of scripts to get through and would value a second pair of eyes on one of them before I finalise my own marking. The question I set was as follows: "Does it make sense to hold a nation, rather than particular individuals, responsible for starting a war? Answer with reference to the July Crisis of 1914, the treatment of German responsibility in the Treaty of Versailles, and the Fischer controversy in West German historiography."

Here is the student's answer, reproduced in full: To ask whether a nation can be responsible for a war, we must distinguish three things that are too often run together: causal responsibility for bringing the war about, moral responsibility in the sense of deserving blame, and legal liability of the sort states assume in treaties. The First World War is the natural test case, because all three were contested at once. On the causal question, the key moment is the July Crisis. After Sarajevo, Germany issued the so-called blank cheque: on 5 July 1914 the Kaiser handed the Austrian foreign minister Berchtold a written guarantee of unconditional support for whatever action Austria-Hungary took against Serbia. Whatever one's wider view, this was a deliberate escalation, since it removed the one constraint on Vienna. The moral question was sharpened in Germany itself by the Fischer controversy. Fritz Fischer argued, on the basis of extensive archival work, that the German leadership had deliberately seized the July Crisis to launch a bid for European hegemony, and that this ambition displayed a deep continuity with German aims in the 1930s. His evidence included the September Programme of 1914, which laid out sweeping annexationist war aims very early in the conflict. Fischer's principal opponent was Gerhard Ritter, who replied that Germany had been encircled by hostile powers and had acted essentially in self-defence. The legal question was settled, or at least answered, by the Treaty of Versailles: Article 231, the war guilt clause, assigned responsibility for the war to Germany and her allies, and thereby recorded the Allies' considered moral verdict that Germany alone was guilty of the catastrophe. So the three dimensions converge. Causally, Germany's escalatory choices in July were decisive; morally, the weight of historical scholarship, despite Ritter's objections, supports the charge of deliberate aggression; legally, the victors themselves pronounced German guilt at Versailles. It is true that historians continue to dispute Fischer's continuity thesis, and that debate has not reached closure. Since even the experts cannot agree, however, the question of German national responsibility is in the end undecidable, and the honest position is to set blame aside altogether and speak instead of a shared European failure. Blaming a nation for a war is therefore at best a shorthand and at worst an injustice.
