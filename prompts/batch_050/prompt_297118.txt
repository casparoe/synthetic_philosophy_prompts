You are a satirist with professional training in decision theory, writing for an audience of economists and philosophers. Your task is to write the "Disagreement Policy" section of the employee handbook of an invented quantitative hedge fund whose founding partners have adopted Aumann's agreement theorem as binding corporate law. Where other firms retain compliance officers, this one retains Common Knowledge Coordinators.

The section should run about 700 words, in the flat, unbothered prose of a real HR document: defined terms in quotation marks, numbered subsections, cross-references, an appeals procedure. It should include, at minimum: an onboarding procedure by which new analysts' priors are "harmonized," with a probationary period for those whose priors resist harmonization; the mandated protocol for resolving any disagreement (public, alternating announcements of posterior estimates until convergence, with convergence time logged and discussed at performance review); an annual Honesty and Rationality Attestation, required because the policy's logic only works if each employee regards every other as perfectly honest and perfectly rational; and disciplinary tiers for "Persistent Disagreement," which the handbook treats not as a difference of opinion but as proof that someone, somewhere, has breached the Attestation.

Before drafting, spend a short planning step mapping the theorem's assumptions and implications to bureaucratic instruments — common priors, common knowledge of rationality and honesty, the ban on agreeing to disagree, iterative announcement as a convergence mechanism — and design one policy clause per item. That mapping is where the jokes live. Then write the section itself, and output only the section.

Constraints:

- Never state, or nod at, the standard objections to the theorem. The satire has to land them anyway: that priors might legitimately differ; that common knowledge is a demanding thing to actually produce in an office; that the protocol presupposes the very trust it is meant to enforce; that real workplace disputes are usually about what to do rather than what is true; that convergence-by-announcement describes a process no human organization could run.
- Exaggerate real features of the result rather than inventing absurdities it does not imply. A reader who knows the theorem should find the satire fair; a reader who does not should still read it as a funny, faintly sinister HR document.
- Stay deadpan throughout. No winking, no ironical asides, no punchline. The final clause should be a routine severability or amendments provision that is nonetheless quietly devastating.
