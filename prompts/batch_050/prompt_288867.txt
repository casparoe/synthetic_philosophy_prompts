Two of my mates got into a massive argument at the pub on Friday and I want a referee, because both of them seemed to be making sense and yet they can't both be right. Background: Dara's doing a philosophy PhD, Tom did maths and now works in risk modelling. We were talking about the football and someone said "There's about a 70% chance that if City drop points on Saturday, Arsenal win the league."

Round 1. Dara said sentences like that are easy: the probability of an indicative conditional just is the conditional probability — the chance of "if A then C" is the chance of C given A. That's plainly how everyone uses and understands these sentences, and any theory denying it is telling the entire population they don't know what their own words mean. Tom said you literally cannot have that as a general rule — there's a result of David Lewis's (the triviality result) showing that if the probability of "if A then C" always equals the probability of C given A, and this has to keep holding as you update on new evidence, then the probability of C given A collapses to just the plain probability of C whenever A is neither certain nor impossible. Updating would never change anything. So "if" can't work the way Dara's intuition says; the intuition is fine as a rule of thumb but can't be the semantics of the word.

Round 2. Dara said the proof only bites if "if A then C" is a proposition — something true or false that can sit inside bigger sentences and get assigned a probability. Indicative conditionals aren't propositions: uttering one is expressing a conditional belief, not stating a conditional fact, so there's no "probability of the conditional" for the equation to latch onto. Its assertability goes by the conditional probability, and that's all the intuition ever claimed. So Lewis's proof is a proof about the wrong kind of thing. Tom replied that this is a dodge with two problems. First, we embed these conditionals all the time: "If he's already left, then if the lights are on someone's feeding the cat" is perfectly understandable, and if conditionals lack truth values there's no clear account of what that sentence is even doing. Second, if the conditional isn't truth-apt, Dara owes a story about denying one — "It's not true that if he trained all winter, he'll make the team" sounds exactly like calling a conditional false, which you can't do to something that isn't the sort of thing that's true or false.

Round 3. Dara said the truth-apt alternatives mispredict usage far worse: on the flat "not-A or C" reading, "If the King is in Leeds right now, I'm a banana" comes out true and worth asserting just because the antecedent is false, which is absurd — so keeping truth values costs more than it saves, and people on her side have ways of handling embeddings (roughly, test-style rules for when compounds are acceptable). Tom conceded the material reading is hopeless but said there are better truth-apt options (Stalnaker-style: the conditional is true if C holds at the closest world where A holds), which keep most of the intuition and only force the probability of the conditional to come apart from the conditional probability in unusual cases — a small price for a theory that explains embedding and denial for free.

Round 4. Dara said "unusual cases" is doing all the work: the whole ordinary judgement just is that the two coincide, so a semantics that prises them apart hasn't explained the intuition, it's changed the subject. Tom said no — intuitions about single sentences don't get to veto a theory that systematically explains compounds, denials and embeddings, which the expressivist story still struggles with after decades of trying.
