For a number of years I have been assigning the debate over same-sex marriage in my political philosophy seminar, and I have noticed that my students -- whatever their ultimate verdict -- reach almost immediately for the analogy with interracial marriage. Their reasoning runs roughly as follows: just as anti-miscegenation statutes excluded certain couples from marriage on the basis of a characteristic (race) that is irrelevant to what marriage is and does, restrictions on same-sex marriage excluded couples on the basis of a characteristic (sex) that is equally irrelevant; since the first exclusion was plainly an unjust discrimination rather than a defense of marriage's true nature, the second must be as well. The analogy did considerable work in the actual litigation and public argument as well, so it cannot be dismissed as a mere classroom artifact. What I want is an audit of how far it actually holds, conducted with the rigor one would bring to an argument by analogy in any other domain.

Please proceed as follows. First, set out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of race, of the anti-miscegenation statute, of the excluded couple, of the state's asserted interest, and of the institution itself, on the strongest version of the analogy. Second, distinguish the two descriptions under which the relevant laws can be characterized -- as excluding certain persons from a standing institution versus as changing the definition of the institution itself -- and assess whether the interracial-marriage precedent adjudicates between these descriptions or merely presupposes one of them. This strikes me as the crux: critics of the analogy contend that anti-miscegenation laws left the procreative, complementary structure of marriage untouched while barring individuals from participating in it, whereas same-sex marriage required rethinking that structure itself, so that the analogy covertly assumes the companionate conception of marriage whose truth is precisely what is at issue. Defenders reply that the procreative structure was never enforced as a condition of marriage anywhere else -- the infertile and the elderly marry freely -- and that the parallel exclusion of a class defined by an immutable trait is the morally salient feature. Third, say which of the disanalogies matter for the conclusion the analogy is meant to carry and which are idle. Fourth, give a verdict: does the analogy survive scrutiny as an argument, or was it functioning as a rhetorical scaffold for a conclusion that must be established on independent grounds -- and if the latter, what is the stronger argument it was standing in for?

Finally, indicate where your analysis is most vulnerable. If your verdict rests on a contested premise -- say, about whether the conjugal and companionate conceptions of marriage can be distinguished sharply enough to bear argumentative weight, or about what anti-miscegenation jurisprudence actually settled -- name that premise and state what consideration, example, or argument would lead you to reverse the verdict.
