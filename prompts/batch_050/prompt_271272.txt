I'm a high school senior writing an essay on Slaughterhouse-Five, which we just finished in English class, and I want to read it through the presentism versus eternalism debate in the philosophy of time. The Tralfamadorians experience all moments of their lives at once, so for them nothing is ever really past and death is just one unpleasant moment among permanently existing others; Billy Pilgrim, after coming unstuck in time, seems to live that way too. Is Vonnegut dramatizing eternalism as the literal truth of the novel's world, or as a psychological defense Billy constructs against the trauma of Dresden — and does the novel itself care about the difference? I'd also like to know where the book presses on the philosophical debate rather than just illustrating it: for instance, does the flat refrain the narrator repeats after every death show what it would actually feel like, emotionally and morally, to believe that the dead still exist in their moments — and whether that consolation is worth what it costs? Please take both the metaphysics and the novel seriously, and say where the novel resists being read as philosophy in costume.
