hey, I sit on my city's transportation advisory committee and we're voting next month on whether to approve a congestion charge for the downtown core. The staff report leans almost entirely on revealed preference analysis: they watched how commuters actually behaved during the toll pilot (who paid for the express lanes, who shifted to the free routes, who left earlier), backed out everyone's "value of time" from those choices, and then added it all up to claim the charge creates roughly $40M a year in net benefits. Our economists talk about these revealed choices as if they just ARE what people value.

Here's my problem. A low-income warehouse worker who sits in traffic for 90 minutes every morning "reveals" a value of time of basicly zero — not because his mornings don't matter, but because he can't afford the toll. Meanwhile a well-paid consultant drops $9 to save 20 minutes without thinking. Taken at face value, the staff's method counts the consultant's 20 minutes as worth many times the worker's 90. That seems to smuggle in the assumption that willingness to pay = how much your time matters to you, which feels philosophically shaky.

Please draft the short memo I'll circulate to the committee before the vote. It should:

- give a clear recommendation: approve, approve with modifications, or reject
- explain in terms a lay committee member can follow what revealed preference analysis does and doesn't establish — does it measure welfare/utility at all, or only predict behavior? And does that distinction even matter for our decision?
- engage seriously with the strongest case for the staff's approach (real behavior beats self-report; stated-preference surveys have their own well-known problems; some aggregation method is unavoidable if we price anything)
- if the recommendation isn't a full rejection, say concretely what should change in how the benefits are aggregated or how the charge is designed

Keep it under 900 words, and end with the bottom line on the last line in exactly one of these forms: \answer{approve}, \answer{approve with modifications}, or \answer{reject}
