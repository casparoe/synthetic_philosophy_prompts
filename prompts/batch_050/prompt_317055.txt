I'm a novelist, and in my current book there's an afterlife that runs like a badly managed corporation. One of the set pieces is a document every new arrival gets handed at orientation, and I want you to actually write it for me: the official welcome pamphlet from the Office of Unanswered Petitions, explaining -- in warm, soothing, customer-service language -- why the prayers they sent up during their mortal life were never acknowledged. Think FAQ meets employee handbook: "Frequently Asked Questions About Your Unanswered Prayers," with sections like What Happened To My Prayer?, Our Silence, Explained, and a perky little testimonial sidebar from satisfied former petitioners.

The fun part (and the hard part) is that every excuse the pamphlet offers should be a recognisable version of an actual response to the problem of divine hiddenness, just translated into corporate deadpan. So the free will defence becomes a policy about how answering your prayer would have interfered with "other users' autonomy settings"; soul-making becomes a note that hardship builds character and that fully-resolved cases show measurably lower resilience scores; the greater-goods move becomes an assurance that your suffering was "a load-bearing component" in an outcome you'll appreciate later (details available on a need-to-know basis, and you don't need to know); and the appeal to mystery becomes a cheerful reminder that the architecture is simply too complex for users, so tickets get closed automatically. If you know the Schellenberg-style argument that a perfectly loving being wouldn't stay hidden from creatures who seek it honestly, the pamphlet should have a section that tries to handle the "nonresistant nonbeliever" case and quietly fails to -- that's the joke a philosopher of religion should catch.

A few constraints so it works in the book:

- Deadpan the whole way. The pamphlet sincerely believes it's being helpful. No winking, no villainy -- the horror should come from how reasonable it sounds.
- The satire has to be fair, meaning it exaggerates real features of the theodicies rather than inventing dumb versions of them. Someone sympathetic to those defences should wince but recognise their own arguments.
- Don't mention God or religion directly if you can avoid it -- keep it one step removed, like the corporation nobody will name.
- Somewhere around 600-700 words.
