I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a long feature about whether the psychology of political disagreement can ever tell us which side of a dispute is actually right. A recurring obstacle is an analogy that has become nearly load-bearing in this literature, popularized by Jonathan Haidt's work on moral foundations: the moral sense is like the tongue, equipped with a small set of innate receptors (care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, liberty), and political cultures are like cuisines that build elaborate dishes from different subsets of those basic tastes. The analogy is routinely deployed to draw a strong conclusion — namely, that liberals and conservatives differ the way Thai and French cooking differ, so that asking which side is right about, say, immigration or taxation is a category error, like asking which cuisine is correct. My editors like the piece's premise, but I have come to suspect the analogy is smuggling in the relativism it's supposed to merely illustrate, and I want that suspicion examined properly before I commit it to print.

So here is what I'd like: an audit of the taste-bud-and-cuisine analogy as an argument, not just as a pedagogical device. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the receptors, the taste qualities, the acquired preferences, the chef, the culinary tradition — and be honest about which mappings are genuinely supported by the psychological evidence and which are decorative. Then the disanalogies, and please separate the ones that matter from the ones that don't: taste preferences are not answerable to reasons in the way moral judgments seem to be (nobody argues a dinner guest out of disliking cilantro, but we do argue each other out of moral positions, and people occasionally change their minds under argument); tastes don't issue in claims that others are mistaken, whereas moral judgments do; and there's the historical awkwardness that disgust-driven moral responses have a poor track record (they once underwrote caste systems and anti-miscegenation sentiment) in a way that has no analogue on the palate. For each disanalogy, tell me whether it merely makes the metaphor less cute or actually breaks the inference to normative pluralism.

Finally, the payoff for my piece: does the conclusion the analogy is used to carry — that the descriptive psychology of left and right cannot, even in principle, bear on who is right — survive once the mapping is laid out? Note that this is itself a normative-sounding claim being extracted from descriptive premises, and I'd like that maneuver flagged and assessed rather than quietly repeated. If the analogy fails, is there a better one in the neighborhood (I've seen moral perception compared to color vision, and to grammar acquisition, both of which seem to cut differently), or is the honest position that moral foundations theory simply has nothing to say about who's right and the interesting question is why its popularizers so badly want it to?
