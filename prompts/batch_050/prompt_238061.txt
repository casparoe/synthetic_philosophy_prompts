So I've been reading Nāgārjuna and one of his ancient critics basically walked him into a corner, and I honestly can't see how he gets out. Here's the objection as I understand it, cleaned up a bit:

(1) Any meaningful assertion has to land somewhere in the four options of the tetralemma: it is so; it is not so; it is both; it is neither. There is no fifth place to stand.
(2) Nāgārjuna's whole method is to reject all four options for any thesis about what things ultimately are -- he does this with motion, causation, the self, the Buddha, even emptiness itself.
(3) But "all phenomena are empty of intinsic nature" is itself an assertion, so by his own lights it has to be rejected as true, as false, as both, and as neither.
(4) A claim you may not assert on any of the four options can't be believed, argued for, or taught. Argument only works if at least one option can be affirmed.
(5) Worse, if his arguments succeed in showing that every view collapses, that success is itself a result -- a truth the arguments establish. So either his arguments fail, or they succeed and thereby establish something, in which case they fail. Madhyamaka is meaningless or self-refuting.

The critic concludes that the middle way proves too much: it doesn't just empty tables and selves, it empties itself, and an empty argument can't refute anything.

Here's where I'm stuck. Nāgārjuna obviously thought he had a reply -- he wrote a whole short work, the Vigrahavyāvartanī, answering pretty much exactly this charge, and people have been arguing about what the reply even is ever since. As far as I can tell there are four main readings. (a) He has no thesis of his own at all, so there's nothing for the objection to hit. (b) He distinguishes conventional truth from ultimate truth: emptiness is asserted conventionally, and the tetralemma only bites at the ultimate level, where nothing -- including emptiness -- is asserted. (c) Some modern readers take him to accept the contradictions head-on, i.e. some contradictions are true (dialetheism). (d) The therapeutic or ladder reading: his arguments are tools for releasing you from views, to be abandoned once they've worked, so their own emptiness doesn't matter.

What I want: first tell me which step of (1)-(5) you think actually fails, if any. Then the harder part -- for each of the four readings, say which step it denies and what it has to give up to deny it. I suspect at least one of these readings doesn't really answer the critic so much as change the subject, but I can't decide which one. If you think more than one of them collapses into another once you push, say that too and show where.
