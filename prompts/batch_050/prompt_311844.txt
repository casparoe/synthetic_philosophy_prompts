A colleague of mine has been ruling on persistence cases for years, and I have a running disagreement with her about what principle she's actually applying. Her verdicts so far:

A bicycle that, over a decade of repairs, has every single component replaced, one part at a time, while remaining in continuous use: still the same bicycle throughout. The discarded parts, later collected and reassembled into a functioning bike: a new bicycle, not the original one. A lump of clay shaped into a statue and then squashed flat: the lump of clay survives, but the statue does not. An amoeba that divides in two: the original amoeba ceases to exist, and two new amoebae come into being; neither daughter is identical to the parent. A person whose neurons are gradually replaced, one by one, by functionally identical synthetic substitutes, with memory, personality, and behavior preserved throughout: the same person at the end. A river whose water is completely different from one year to the next: the same river.

Here is the new case. A planarian flatworm is cut cleanly in half. Each half regenerates the missing portion and becomes a complete, fully functioning worm, and both resulting worms behave in every testable respect as continuations of the original -- same conditioned responses, same temperament, so far as these things can be assessed. There is no asymmetry between the two: neither has a better claim than the other to be the original. Did the original flatworm survive, and if so, as which worm, or as both?

I'd like three things. First, your prediction of my colleague's verdict on the flatworm case, with your reasoning about which principle best explains her earlier rulings. Second, a map of the plausible candidate principles -- for instance, an endurantist view on which objects are wholly present at each moment and persist through immanent-causal continuity under a dominant sortal, a perdurantist worm view on which persisting things are four-dimensional wholes and fission reveals two worms that overlapped all along, or a stage view on which persistence is a matter of counterpart relations rather than strict identity -- showing which of them fit all six earlier verdicts equally well and exactly where they come apart on the flatworm. Note that I care about what her verdicts commit her to, not about what the correct metaphysics of persistence is. Third, the single additional case -- real or hypothetical -- that would most efficiently discriminate among the candidate principles that survive, and what each principle would have her say about it.
