I work on the comparative economic history of early modern Europe and Asia, and I have gradually come around to a position often associated with the "California school" in the Great Divergence debate: that the economic divergence between north-western Europe and the most developed parts of China came late, was relatively sudden, and depended on contingent features of geography and empire -- in particular, conveniently located coal and access to New World land and resources -- rather than on deep institutional, cultural, or scientific differences reaching back centuries. On this view, the Yangzi Delta around 1750 was roughly comparable to England in living standards, market development, and ecological constraints, so that the question "why Britain and not China?" has a genuinely contingent answer rather than a long-brewing structural one.

What I would like is a map of this position's exposure: what would count against it, and how badly. Please don't just give me the standard objections rehearsed in the literature. I want to know which kinds of evidence, argument, or result would actually threaten it, and to what degree. For instance:

- Empirical findings. Would revised reconstructions of real wages, agricultural productivity, or GDP per head showing a substantial English (or Dutch) lead well before 1700 be fatal, or could a defender absorb them by redescribing what "divergence" means? Is there a principled, non-ad-hoc way the position specifies which regions count as the relevant comparators, or does the choice of units (England versus Europe; the Yangzi Delta versus China) do too much of the work?
- Conceptual pressure. Does the view depend on a particular answer to what makes a cause a cause -- on contrastive versus difference-making versus INUS-style accounts of causal explanation in history -- such that someone who rejected that view of historical explanation could reject the position without touching any of the data?
- Internal tensions. Does the emphasis on coal and colonies quietly reintroduce the deep-structural explanation it claims to avoid, given that exploiting coal required particular scientific and engineering traditions and that colonies were acquired through institutions and military capacity that were themselves not contingent windfalls?
- Predictions. What does the position predict that its rivals (institutionalist accounts, accounts stressing European science, or "great divergence as long process" accounts) do not, and have those predictions been tested or merely asserted?

Where you can, distinguish what the position's own defenders have conceded as genuine costs from what only its critics count as costs, and rank the candidate defeaters from those that would sink the view outright to those that would merely be awkward. If you think the position has been insulated against falsification by qualifications and definitional flexibility, say so plainly, and say whether you think that is a vice or just a feature of any historiographical thesis of this scale. And if you think the whole framing is confused -- for instance, if you think "the divergence was late and contingent" and "the divergence had deep causes" are not genuine rivals at all, or if you think the question "why Britain and not China?" is ill-posed in a way that makes my position unstateable -- then push back on that directly rather than answering as if the framework were sound.

Please write in British English.
