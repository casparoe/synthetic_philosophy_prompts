I am a PhD student in philosophy working on normative ethics, and I am preparing a seminar presentation on the cluelessness objection to consequentialism — roughly, the worry that because our actions ramify into the indefinite future in ways we cannot foresee, expected-value reasoning leaves us unable to rank almost any pair of options. In discussions with my cohort, I have become convinced that the objection is really two problems wearing one coat. Sometimes our ignorance about distant consequences seems symmetric: for all we know, the unforeseeable downstream effects of option A and option B cancel out in expectation, so the foreseeable near-term difference still decides. But sometimes the ignorance seems structured: we have some reason to think the unknown effects are not symmetrically distributed, yet we cannot assign stable probabilities to them. My supervisor suggested I build a single illustrative case that separates these two situations cleanly, and I have found this surprisingly hard to do without cheating. So I would like you, acting as a moral philosopher with expertise in consequentialism and formal epistemology, to construct it for me. Think carefully, and before committing to a scenario, briefly brainstorm (four or five sentences at most) which structural features a case would need in order to toggle between the two kinds of ignorance.

Here are the specifications. First, the scenario must involve a single realistic decision that an ordinary person could face — no aliens, no demons, no perfect predictors, no exotic physics — describable in one paragraph, where the near-term expected consequences of two options are known and favor option A, but where the agent recognizes that both options have long-run consequences she cannot estimate. Second, the scenario must contain one explicitly identified structural feature — call it the dial — such that with the dial in one position, the agent's ignorance about the distant future is symmetric and the standard cancellation reply delivers a clear verdict for A, while with the dial flipped, the ignorance becomes asymmetric in a way the agent can describe in words but cannot quantify, so that the cancellation reply fails and straightforward expected-value reasoning goes silent. Third, show the reasoning rather than stipulating it: for each dial position, state what verdict (if any) is delivered by (i) simple expected-value consequentialism, (ii) a consequentialism that discounts consequences by causal proximity, and (iii) a view that models cluelessness with imprecise or indeterminate credences — and explain briefly why each theory lands where it does. Fourth, identify precisely which feature of the case does the work, and say what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed. Fifth, give the strongest attack a defender of the cancellation reply would mount against the dial's asymmetric position, and assess whether it succeeds.

Please close by being explicit about your own epistemic position: state which argument or technical result (for instance, about how imprecise credences behave under sequential choice) would lead you to a different verdict, and identify the point in your construction where you are most likely to have smuggled in an assumption that does the real work. After the prose analysis, provide your deliverable as a JSON object with the following fields: "scenario" (a string containing the one-paragraph case), "dial" (a string naming and describing the structural feature), "verdicts" (an array of objects, each with fields "theory", "dial_symmetric_verdict", "dial_asymmetric_verdict", and "reasoning_summary"), "load_bearing_feature" (a string), "best_objection" (a string), "confidence" (one of "high", "medium", or "low"), and "what_would_change_my_mind" (a string).
