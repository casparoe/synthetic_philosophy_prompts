I run an informal reading group on decision theory, and we keep circling back to Death in Damascus: the case where Death, an excellent predictor of your choices, has already scheduled its appointment in whichever city you will choose to be in, so that fleeing to Aleppo is rational only if staying in Damascus was the predicted move, and vice versa. My group members disagree about which decision theory handles the case least badly, and I realized our arguments were sloppy because everyone was picturing a slightly different version of the setup. So I built a small battery of variants and I'd like you to score the theories against it. The variants: (1) the classic, where Death predicts you perfectly; (2) a fallible Death that gets your choice right about 90% of the time, and both of you know the error rate; (3) a variant where you have access to a genuine randomizer -- say, a quantum coin -- and can delegate your choice to it, while Death must publish its appointment before the coin lands; (4) a variant where Death predicts not your action but your decision procedure, and you know this, so any rule you adopt is in effect transparent to it; and (5) a variant where you can pay a small fee to learn where the appointment is, but Death has already predicted whether you'd pay.

Here is the task. Take causal decision theory, evidential decision theory, and functional/timeless-style decision theory (treat the last as one entry, noting internal differences only where they matter for a score). For each of the five variants, give each theory a score from 0 to 10 for how badly it embarrasses itself -- 0 meaning it gives clearly correct advice and knows it, 10 meaning it is dutch-booked, paralyzed, or recommends the choice it can foresee losing with. Then aggregate into a tier ranking of the three theories for overall robustness across the battery, and name the single variant that most cleanly separates the theories from one another, explaining why the separation is clean there and muddy elsewhere. If you think the scoring rubric itself conflates things that should be kept apart -- say, recommending a foreseeably losing act versus failing to converge on any recommendation -- push back on the rubric rather than quietly complying with it, and propose the fix.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: the final tier ranking, stated plainly, before any of the per-variant scoring or reasoning. No preamble, no restating the setup. After the verdict, give the score table, then the reasoning, and flag any variant you think is underspecified in a way that changes the scores.
