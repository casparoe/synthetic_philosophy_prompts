I'm a journalist working on a long piece about decision theory, and an argument I heard at a workshop has been nagging at me because its conclusion seems to license a kind of universal gullibility. I'll set it out as carefully as I can. First the background case, known as the counterfactual mugging: a superintelligent predictor, Omega, flips a fair coin. If it comes up heads, Omega gives you $10,000 on the condition that its model of you indicates you would have paid $100 had the coin come up tails. If it comes up tails, Omega simply asks you for $100 and offers nothing in return. The coin comes up tails, and Omega asks.

Now the argument, as my source gave it to me. (1) When the coin comes up tails, you should pay, because the policy of paying has far higher ex ante expected value -- roughly $4,900 rather than $0 -- and rationality consists in following through on the policy you would have wanted to commit to before the flip. (2) Notice that paying buys you nothing actual: the coin has already landed. What justifies the payment is therefore purely counterfactual -- it is what Omega would have done in the heads-branch, given the sort of person you are. (3) But then the justification cannot depend on whether a coin was actually flipped, or on anything else about the causal history. What matters is the structure of the conditional: a large payoff attached, in some branch, to your being a payer. (4) So consider a stranger who approaches you and says: had you been the sort of person who pays me $100 on request, I would have left $10,000 in your mailbox last week. By (2) and (3), you should pay him too. (5) Worse, the reasoning extends to benefactors who do not exist at all: you should act so as to do well in every merely possible world where someone rewards people with your dispositions. (6) That is absurd -- it makes you exploitable by anyone who can formulate a counterfactual, and it obligates you to ghosts. Therefore (1) is false, and you should not pay in the counterfactual mugging.

My own inclination is to keep (1) -- the ex ante case for paying strikes me as sound, and the piece I'm writing treats it as one of the more defensible verdicts in this literature. But I cannot locate the exact step where the argument jumps the rails. Which premise or inference do you reject, why did it look acceptable, and what does rejecting it commit you to -- in particular, what principled line separates counterfactuals that can rationally move you from ones that cannot?
