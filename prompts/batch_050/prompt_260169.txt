Two of my officemates got into it over Goodman's grue at lunch last week and it's still going over email, so I want a verdict.

Dan's position: the riddle shows induction has no rational basis, period. The same emerald evidence 'confirms' both all emeralds are green and all emeralds are grue, so which you project just depends on which language you happen to speak. And the symmetry is perfect, he says -- take grue and bleen as basic and you can define green as grue-if-examined-before-t-and-bleen-otherwise, which makes green the gerrymandered predicate, not grue.

Marta says that's way stronger than anything Goodman claimed. The riddle targets naive confirmation theories, not induction itself. Goodman grants the definitional symmetry but says it's beside the point: projectibility isn't definitional simplicity. Grue-predicates can't appear in genuine laws because whether something is grue depends on a date, so a grue-law isn't time-invariant the way real laws are.

Dan's reply: 'lawlike' and 'projectible' are just labels for predicates we already trust, and Goodman's actual answer -- entrenchment, the history of our linguistic habits -- is sociology, not justification. Which was exactly Dan's original point.

Marta's latest: entrenchment answers a descriptive question, and the normative asymmetry shows up in counterfactuals. An examined emerald would still have been green if t had been different, but nothing like that holds for grue -- the grueness of the examined emeralds is entirely explained by their being examined before t, so it says nothing at all about unexamined ones.
