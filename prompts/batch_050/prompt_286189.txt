I recently joined my university's animal care and use committee, and the pattern of what we regulate has started to puzzle me in a way I can't articulate cleanly. In our protocols, a mouse requires justified analgesia, humane endpoints, and an approved method of euthanasia; a zebrafish counts only once it reaches the independently feeding larval stage; octopuses and squid are covered in Europe under Directive 2010/63/EU despite being invertebrates, while fruit flies and nematodes, which we kill in enormous numbers, require nothing at all. The official rationale, whenever anyone states one, is always sentience: the capacity to feel pain and suffer. The UK's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 made this explicit in its name, extending legal recognition to cephalopods and decapod crustaceans after a commissioned review of the evidence. So the practice seems straightforwardly to presuppose that moral status tracks sentience. But then: the very same mouse that my committee protects in the lab can be caught on a glue trap in the building's basement by the facilities department with no oversight whatsoever; recreational anglers impale live bait and practice catch-and-release in ways no committee would ever approve; and within our own regulated sphere, coverage is binary and drawn along taxonomic lines, even though the scientific literature the committee circulates treats sentience as graded, uncertain, and probabilistic. On top of that, we routinely approve protocols in which healthy animals are painlessly killed at the end, which seems to presuppose that painless death is no harm to them at all.

What I want is a reconstruction of the implicit theory of moral status this whole practice presupposes. Does it strictly presuppose that sentience is the sole ground of moral status, or would several rival views -- cognitive sophistication, species membership, the value of life itself -- license the same lines, leaving the practice underdetermined? Is the binary vertebrate-plus-cephalopods line best read as a threshold view with precautionary inclusion, and if so, how does that sit with the graded evidence it cites? Does the painless-euthanasia convention commit the practice to a purely experiential account of harm, and what would that rule out? Most importantly, does the lab-versus-basement asymmetry show that the practice quietly presupposes that human purpose or institutional context modulates an animal's moral status -- and is that coherent, or is it the kind of thing a practice can get away with but a theory cannot?

Please don't give me a neutral survey of possible interpretations. Commit to the single best reconstruction, say which of these apparent inconsistencies are genuine contradictions and which can be dissolved, and identify where the practice is truly philosophically underdetermined. Then, as a stress test, tell me concretely what would have to change if the committee adopted a strict expected-moral-weight view instead -- protection proportional to the probability of sentience multiplied by capacity and intensity of suffering. Would we have to regulate the glue traps, review the fruit fly facility, or abandon the vertebrate line altogether? I want a definite position with reasons, including on whether the practice as it stands is ultimately defensible.
