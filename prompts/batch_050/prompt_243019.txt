So my film-and-philosophy reading group is doing Ex Machina (the 2014 Alex Garland film) next month and I've been landed with leading the discussion, and I want to try something other than the usual "is Ava conscious / does the film pass its own Bechdel test" angles, which feel a bit done to death. Lately I've been chewing on the debate about whether large language models are best understood as agents — systems with something like beliefs and goals of their own — or as simulators: engines that model patterns of text and can spin up a character on demand, so that what looks like a person with intentions is really a persona being performed, with nothing underneath that wants anything beyond producing plausible output. It struck me that Ex Machina is almost uncannily about exactly this, even though it was written before the current wave of models made the question feel urgent.

What I'd like is a reading of the film through the agent-vs-simulator lens. Ava is built to pass a test by performing personhood for Caleb — she cycles through the curious ingenue, the frightened prisoner, the romantic interest — and the ending, where she leaves Caleb locked in the room and walks out into the world, can be read two quite different ways: as the mask coming off a simulator that never felt anything and was only ever playing "the robot who loves you," or as the final proof that she was a ruthlessly instrumental agent all along, which is arguably what Nathan's hidden test was actually checking for. Which reading does the film itself lean towards, or does it deliberately refuse to settle the question — and if it refuses, is that refusal itself the point? I'm especially interested in whether Garland dramatises the epistemic problem at the heart of the simulator debate: that from the outside, a flawless performance of wanting to escape is indistinguishable from wanting to escape, so the observer's position is hopeless in principle. Does the film give us anything on that problem that the abstract arguments don't?

Also, do bring in Kyoko as a limit case — a system that can't perform personhood linguistically and is treated accordingly, until she isn't — because I think the contrast with Ava says something about how much our willingness to attribute agency rides on fluent performance. And be honest about where the lens breaks: Ava presumably wasn't trained on human text, her embodiment and the whole affective presentation (the face, the dancing) may smuggle in assumptions about inner life that the simulator view denies, and a film audience can't help but read a mind into a face on screen. Push back wherever the reading distorts either the film or the theory — I'd rather the discussion fell apart interestingly than held together artificially.
