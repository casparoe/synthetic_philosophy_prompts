I run a small forecasting club with some friends from work — nothing professional, just prediction questions we score for fun — and an argument keeps recurring that none of us can settle. One member insists that whenever a trustworthy source of "objective" probabilities exists (a well-calibrated weather model, the actuarial tables our insurer uses, the implied odds from a betting market), it is simply irrational to hold a personal credence that departs from those numbers, unless you possess private information the source lacks. Another member thinks this is superstition dressed up as rigor: probabilities, she says, are always someone's model, and deferring to a model's output is a practical heuristic, not a requirement of rationality. I later learned that philosophers have a name for something close to my friend's position — the Principal Principle, due to David Lewis, roughly the idea that your credence should match the known objective chance — and that there is a literature on when and why deference to chance is rationally required. But the discussions I've managed to find assume a lot of machinery I don't have, so I want to pose my version of the question directly.

Here is the proposition I'd like your credence on: for an agent who knows the objective chance of an event and has no further relevant information, adopting a credence different from that chance is irrational — not merely unwise or badly incentivized, but irrational in the same family of ways as believing a contradiction. Note the clause about "no further relevant information," because our club's hardest cases are the ones where it isn't clear whether extra knowledge counts as relevant: I might know that the weather model has been wrong three Sundays running (is that evidence about this Sunday, or just noise?), or I might know that the event in question is one whose chance will itself change tomorrow (does knowing that today's 60% might become tomorrow's 20% license departing from 60% now?). I gather the second kind of case — where chances can be "undermined" by their own future behavior — caused trouble even for Lewis's own formulation.

Please give me your credence in the proposition as stated, and then decompose it rather than leaving it as a bare number. I'd like separate rough probabilities for at least: whether objective chances exist at all (as opposed to chance-talk being a useful fiction over ignorance); whether, granting they exist, knowing them imposes a rational constraint on credence that goes beyond a practical rule of thumb; and whether the undermining-type cases are fatal to the principle or merely force a careful restatement of it. Then show how the sub-probabilities combine into your final number, tell me which term your final answer is most sensitive to, and name the single argument or consideration that would move it most. If you think the real disagreement between my two friends lives somewhere else entirely — say, in what "irrational" means — say so and reassign the weight accordingly.
