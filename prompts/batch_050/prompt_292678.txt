In debates over AI assurance, one often hears the following defense of behavioral evaluation: we certify cars by crash-testing a sample of vehicles and tracking accident statistics, not by proving that no crash is possible; so demanding worst-case guarantees from AI systems, rather than average-case performance on evaluations, holds AI to a standard we impose on no other technology. Audit this analogy as an argument for the sufficiency of average-case evaluation. Give the explicit mapping (test tracks and dummies to eval suites, fleet accident rates to deployment failure rates, regulatory certification to deployment approval), and then identify where it breaks: which features of automotive safety — fixed physics, no adversary selecting inputs, failure rates that stay stable between testing and deployment, units that do not change after certification — have no counterpart in learned systems, and which of these disanalogies actually bear on the conclusion rather than being incidental. Say what the analogy quietly assumes about the relation between the test distribution and the deployment distribution, whether a better source domain exists (for instance, software security, where adversaries adapt and average-case certification is generally rejected), and what the analogy can still legitimately support once its limits are granted. Finally, state which empirical or theoretical result — about distributional shift, adversarial optimization, or correlated failure across copies of a model — would most change your verdict.
