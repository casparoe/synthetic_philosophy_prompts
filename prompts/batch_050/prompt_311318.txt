I'm a stats PhD student and i keep getting lost in the philosophy of biology debate over the tautology objection to 'survival of the fittest'. Can you restate the whole thing in vocabulary I actually use -- estimands, identifiability, latent parameters, prediction vs. explanation -- and then give me an honest report of what doesn't survive the translation?

My rough picture: critics say fitness gets defined by actual reproductive success, so 'the fittest survive' reduces to 'survivors survive' -- empty. The propensity interpretation replies that fitness is a disposition an organism has *before* it reproduces: expected reproductive output given its traits and environment, which can misfire in any particular case, so the claim is substantive. In my language that's just the gap between a realized outcome and an expected value, i.e. between the data and a parameter of the generating process. But then fitness becomes a latent variable you can only ever estimate from realized reproductive success, and now I'm stuck: is the parameter even identifiable? Does the propensity move just push the tautology worry one level up (the parameter is only ever pinned down by its manifestations), or is it exactly like positing p for a biased coin -- p = 0.3 is a real claim even though any finite run of flips is consistent with lots of values of p?

The part I most want translated carefully: philosophers fight over whether fitness *causes* reproductive success or merely *summarizes* it, and over whether selection explanations add anything once you already condition on all the physical facts about the organisms and their environment. I can render 'summary vs. cause' roughly as 'sufficient statistic vs. causal variable in a DAG', but I suspect something is getting lost there. What's the residue, and is it a genuine defect of the statisitical frame or a sign that the philosophers are partly arguing about bookkeeping conventions?
