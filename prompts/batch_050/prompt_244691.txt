Alright, so I spent thirty-five years doing molecular enzymology before I retired, and one thing that always struck me as philosophically weird (long before I knew that word for it) was how we decide what counts as "the same mechanism" when something goes wrong in the lab. You get a strange result, and the whole troubleshooting process is basically an argument: is this the same old enzyme doing something unexpected, or has something broken so fundamentally that you're not even looking at the original mechanism anymore? Since retiring I've been sitting in on philosophy of science seminars, and I've found the mechanisms literature (Machamer, Darden, Craver, Glennan, that crowd) talks endlessly about what mechanisms are but seems oddly quiet on individuation -- when are two token systems the same mechanism, and when is one a broken version versus a different thing entirely? Meanwhile, anomaly detection, which is what half of actual lab life consists of, seems to me to presuppose an answer to exactly that question every single time.

So here's my ask. I want to write this up as a one-year research project, maybe as a basis for applying to one of those post-retirement independent scholar fellowships, and I need a proper proposal document, roughly two pages. The half-formed idea I want developed: instead of trying to define mechanism identity top-down from metaphysical principles, work backward from how scientists actually make these judgments in troubleshooting episodes -- what cues (which entities, which activities, what spatial organization, which invariances under intervention) actually drive the call between "artifact of a broken same mechanism" and "genuinely different mechanism at work." The proposal should state the question precisely enough that it could be answered, explain why the existing accounts of mechanisms leave it open (my sense is they assume individuation is either obvious or someone else's problem, but check me on that), lay out the method month by month, and be honest about what would count as the project failing -- e.g., if it turns out troubleshooting judgments are just pragmatic and track nothing structural about mechanisms at all.

Two more things. First, don't include any bibliography or citations to specific papers; I'll dig up the references myself, since I know roughly where the bodies are buried in this literature. Second, since this is a proposal and not a verdict, do the equivalent of leading with the bottom line: start the document itself with the one-sentence statement of the project, no throat-clearing, and at the end include a short section on the proposal's own weakest point -- which objection a skeptical reviewer could raise that would make you abandon or radically restructure the project, and what finding early on would tell you the whole approach was a dead end.
