I’m helping organize a small exhibit for our university’s Black student union, and we keep circling around the same arguement. Some people think every piece should clearly advance an anti-racist message; others say that treating art mainly as messaging flattens it into activism with frames around it. Du Bois seems like the obvious person to bring in here, since he defended art with a social purpose and rejected the idea that propaganda is automatically unartistic, but he also cared deeply about beauty, craft, and the freedom of Black artists not to perform a role for white audiences.

Imagine Du Bois were alive today and had to judge three cases: (1) a streaming service hires lots of Black writers, but its recommendation algorithm promotes comforting aspirational stories while burying angrier, stranger, or more experimental work; (2) a generative-AI tool trained largely on the work and archives of Black artists lets students instantly make polished protest art, while the living artists whose styles it imitates receive nothing; and (3) a wealthy donor offers to fund our exhibit only if we select works that can be shown through audience surveys to improve viewers’ racial attitudes. What would Du Bois probably say about each case? Where would he draw the line between good and bad propaganda, and where might he surprise both the message-first students and the art-for-art’s-sake students?

Please reason from his actual commitments rather than just repeating that he supported politically engaged art. I’m especially interested in tensions: art as a weapon versus art as a search for truth, collective advancement versus individual artistic freedom, and reaching a broad public versus refusing to make oneself legible to the market. End with your best prediction of the short curatorial note he would write for the exhibit catalogue, without inventing any quotations or citations.
