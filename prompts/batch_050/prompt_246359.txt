My historiography professor's verdicts so far, from her seminar on what counts as a historical explanation: a passage explaining a region's early industrialization by appeal to a cross-national regularity — regions with coal deposits near navigable rivers industrialized early — while offering no mechanism, counts as an explanation; a passage reconstructing a prime minister's decisions during a crisis, all contingency and misjudgment, with no general law and no explicit comparison class, counts as an explanation; a fluent, internally coherent narrative attributing a general's retreat to personal cowardice does not count, because the documentary record shows the order issued from a supply collapse; a passage tracing how mobilization timetables and railway schedules narrowed statesmen's options, with neither a general law nor much storytelling, counts as an explanation; a passage subsuming a war's end under the true generalization that all wars eventually end does not count; and a passage asking why the northern provinces revolted while the southern provinces, taxed just as heavily, did not, and answering with the north's tradition of representative assemblies, counts as an explanation. Next week's passage explains the fall of a fortress city entirely by the sappers' mining of one stretch of wall: every claim in it is true, the mining genuinely hastened the surrender, but the passage says nothing about the garrison's starvation or the relief column's failure, which the professor's own lectures treat as decisive, and it asks only why the city fell, not why it fell when it did or why it fell rather than holding out. Predict her verdict; say which candidate rules — truth plus causal relevance, proportionality to the weightiest causes, contrastive adequacy, or something else — are each consistent with her entire record and what each predicts about the new passage; give your best guess with reasons; and name the further case that would settle which rule she is running.
