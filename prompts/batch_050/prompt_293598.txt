You're a moral philosopher who specialises in twentieth-century ethics, and in particular the debates around Bernard Williams and impartial moral theory. I need your help with something, and I'd like you to think carefully rather than just recite the standard moves.

Some background, so we're on the same page. In Persons, Character and Morality (collected in Moral Luck, 1981), Williams imagines a man who can rescue only one of two people in equal peril, and one of them is his wife. He saves her, of course. Williams's complaint is that on the moral theories he's attacking, the man's motivation is only acceptable if it carries a moral authorisation along with it — roughly, it's my wife, plus the thought that morality permits him to save her. Williams says this gives the man one thought too many: the wife might have hoped that his motivating thought, fully spelled out, would simply be that it's her. The charge is one of alienation — the theory inserts a moral checkpoint between the agent and the people he loves.

Here are the responses to the one thought too many charge that I already know and don't need rehearsed back at me:

1. The sophisticated consequentialist response (Railton and others): consequentialism is a criterion of rightness, not a decision procedure you run in your head mid-rescue. The person whose character is shaped in the way consequentialism recommends is precisely the person who acts from love without a second thought. The theory lives in how the character was formed, not in the deliberation.

2. The reconstruction response: Williams is psychologising what was only ever a logical point. The permissibility clause is the theorist's representation of what justifies the action, not a claim about what passes through anyone's mind. Spelled out by whom? Not by the husband.

3. The background-check response: the permissibility clause does real work. Sometimes partiality goes too far — saving your wife is one thing, saving her by pushing three strangers out of the lifeboat is another, and nepotism in hiring is another still. A theory that never had the second thought available couldn't mark the difference between permissible partiality and wrongful favouritism. Having the checkpoint there, even if it rarely fires, is a feature.

4. The mature-love response: Williams's ideal is actually a bit creepy on inspection. A spouse who would do absolutely anything for you, who would never so much as wonder whether their devotion had limits, is not obviously the ideal partner. Mature intimacy involves knowing where love stops; the capacity for the second thought is part of what makes the first one worth having.

What I want from you: a fifth response to the one thought too many charge that is genuinely different from these four. Not a rewording, not a special case of one of them, and not a hybrid cooked up by combining two of them. Before you commit, brainstorm three or four candidates, briefly say why each rejected one collapses into a response already on my list, and then develop your strongest candidate properly — state it, explain why it counts as new, and tell me honestly whether you think it's any good and where its weak point is. If the honest answer is that the well has run dry and everything new is really one of the four in disguise, say that instead, but I'd be suprised.
