I moderate a monthly public-affairs podcast, and our upcoming guest is a political theorist who argues that national legislatures should be replaced by chambers whose members are drawn by lottery from the adult citizenry, serving fixed, well-paid terms with expert support staff — the position sometimes called lottocracy. He is fluent and thoroughly prepared, and he has a rehearsed three-part defense: elections are aristocratic in effect because they select for wealth, ambition, and telegenicity; descriptive representation by lot tracks the population's actual interests; and structured deliberation neutralizes the competence problem. I want to get him off that script.

Please draft nine or ten interview questions, ordered so that each answer he gives makes the next question harder to deflect. The set should include the following, and for each question, write a follow-up that presupposes his most likely reply rather than merely repeating the question:

- An opening question that forces him to say what he takes elections to be for — aggregating preferences, securing accountability, or producing good policy — since which disjunct he chooses determines which of the later objections are even objections to his view.
- A question putting the competence objection in its strongest form: not whether randomly chosen citizens deliberate well over a weekend with curated briefing materials, but whether those results survive when the body wields sovereign power for years and lobbyists, donors, and partisan media redirect their efforts toward its members. His likely reply cites evidence from deliberative polling; the follow-up should press the inference from invited, powerless minipublics to a legislature nobody is performing for an organizer.
- A question about agenda control: if civil servants and issue specialists decide what the chamber considers, in what order, and under what framing, has political power been democratized or merely relocated? Anticipate his reply (agenda committees, themselves chosen by lot and rotated) and draft the follow-up asking where that regress terminates and who, at the end of it, is answerable for what never reaches the floor.
- A question about self-selection within the sample: in practice only some of those drawn by lot accept the seat, and the willing differ systematically from the unwilling. He will answer with salary and prestige; the follow-up should ask whether any design keeps service attractive without making it attractive to exactly the careerist types the lottery was meant to exclude.
- A question on accountability: no one can vote a randomly selected chamber out of office, so failure carries no electoral cost. He will respond that accountability to the median voter is precisely what produces short-termism and pandering; the follow-up should ask him to name a concrete disciplining mechanism — not a hope about civic virtue — that operates on a body no one can remove.
- A question about legitimacy among losers: whether citizens who oppose a decision will accept it on the grounds that it came from people like them, and whether that acceptance survives the first genuinely unpopular ruling.
- A closing question asking him to specify some observable outcome — in the record of existing citizens' assemblies, or in any feasible pilot — that would count as evidence against his position, and, if he maintains that none could, what that implies about the status of the view.
