ok so I need help getting ready for a talk. I'm a third-year undergrad in a cross-listed course called "Mind, Brain and Free Will" (about half philosophy students, half neuroscience/psych majors) and for the end-of-term student conference I'm doing a 15-minute presentation defending this thesis: the Libet-style experiments — the readiness potential appearing before the conscious urge, the later studies claiming to predict choices seconds ahead of awareness, the veto experiments — don't actually show that conscious will is an illusion, because they only ever test arbitrary, meaningless motor decisions where it doesn't matter what you pick, and the stochastic accumulator interpretation of the readiness potential suggests there may not even be a genuine "decision" sitting there before awareness to be detected. If neuroscience threatens free will at all, it's through a different route, like post-hoc confabulation of reasons, not through timing experiments.

The audience is about 15 people. Mostly neuro/psych majors who learned in intro classes that "Libet disproved free will" and will be pretty skeptical that a philosophy argument undoes hard data. Our prof is a compatibilist who keeps saying the whole Libet literature changes the subject. There's also one hardcore incompatibilist in the class who'll think I'm selling out by only defending arbitrary decisions.

Your role: you're a philosopher of neuroscience who's TA'd this exact kind of course and watched this exact kind of talk go badly. Think carefully about who is actually in the room. Before writing the prep sheet, brainstorm privately the full list of objections each person could raise, then cut it down to the ones that will actually come up.

Format the prep sheet with these sections in this order:

1. **The five objections I'll actually get** (~150 words each) — ordered by likelihood x damage. Phrase each the way a psych major who half-remembers the "brain predicts your choice 7 seconds early" study would phrase it, not the way a philosophy paper would. For each: what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my reply is soft.
2. **The prof's angle** (~100 words) — what a compatibilist will push on, which probably isn't what the students push on.
3. **The question I hope nobody asks** (~80 words) — the one that hits the seam between my two moves (the "arbitrary decisions don't count" move and the "accumulator model" move), which I'm worried don't fit together as well as I've been pretending.
4. **My weakest link** (~100 words) — honest call on which premise I'd drop first if the Q&A goes south.
