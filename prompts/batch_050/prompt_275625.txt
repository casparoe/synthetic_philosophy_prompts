Write me a dialogue about Zeno's paradoxes of motion (stick mainly to the Dichotomy and Achilles; the Arrow only if it comes up naturally). The two characters:

1. A mathematician who thinks the paradoxes are fully and finally dissolved by the standard toolkit -- convergent geometric series, the continuity of the real line, and the fact that an infinite union of intervals of decreasing measure can have finite total measure. On her view there's nothing left to explain once the math is on the table, and people who still feel puzzled are confusing "infinitely many parts" with "infinitely much distance/time."

2. A philosopher who completely concedes the math but denies it touches the real puzzle. His line: summing an infinite series is one thing, *completing* an infinite sequence of distinct tasks is another, and the paradox isn't "how can the intervals add to a finite length?" but "how can the runner have gotten through all of the sub-intervals, given there was no last one she crossed?" He leans on the staccato version of the run (each sub-task done faster and faster) and worries in the style of Thomson's lamp about what grounds the runner's state at the finish line, given that no converging sequence of positions settles it.

Constraints on the dialogue:
- Both characters have to be genuinely sharp. Neither gets to be a strawman: the mathematician must understand the summing/completing distinction rather than just repeating "the series converges," and the philosopher must actually grant that no finite distance is ever at issue.
- Real back-and-forth. Each speech should respond to the previous one's actual content, and each character should concede at least one point where they're plainly forced to.
- At some point they should try to pin down the precise crux -- i.e., is the disagreement about whether "the runner passed every point of division" is itself in need of explanation, or about whether a supertask with no final member is coherent -- and they should agree on at least that much by the end, even if they don't settle who's right.
- No narrated winner, no "and so they saw both had a point" cop-out ending. It can end mid-disagreement but not mid-air.

If you think this framing is muddled -- e.g., if you think the mathematician's position as I've described it already answers the philosopher, or vice versa, so the debate can't be run fairly -- say so and tell me why instead of writing a rigged dialogue.

Start straight into the dialogue itself, no preface, no "here's your dialogue" note, and no afterword explaining it.
