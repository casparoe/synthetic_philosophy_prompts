Next month I am presenting a paper to a reading group on nineteenth-century British moral philosophy, and I would rather be properly prepared for the discussion than merely hopeful. The paper argues that Henry Sidgwick's decades of involvement with the Society for Psychical Research — which he helped to found and whose first presidency he held — was not an eccentric hobby to be bracketed off from his serious philosophy, but the empirical arm of it. My three claims: first, that the epistemological test The Methods of Ethics applies to common-sense morality — winnowing beliefs down to what survives the scrutiny of competent judges reasoning carefully and agreeing with one another — is structurally the same procedure the Society applied to reports of telepathy, apparitions and survival of death; secondly, that Sidgwick needed this because the dualism of practical reason, the apparent stand-off between rational egoism and universal benevolence, could only be dissolved if there were evidence of a moral order built into the universe, such as personal survival; thirdly, that the Society's failure to produce such evidence is therefore not a biographical footnote but the quiet catastrophe of his system, and explains the pessimism of his later years.

The audience matters. The group consists mostly of historians of ethics who admire Sidgwick and treat the psychical research as an embarrassment best left unmentioned; there is one philosopher of science with firm views on demarcation who thinks testimony-based investigation of the paranormal was never science and never could have been; and there is one Sidgwick specialist who has argued in print that the connection I am drawing is overblown. About fifteen people, ninety minutes, roughly half of it discussion.

I would like you to act as a historian of nineteenth-century philosophy who has chaired many such sessions and knows how reading groups actually behave — who interrupts, who stays silent until the fatal moment, which objections get nods around the table. Think carefully about this, and begin by brainstorming every pressure point in the argument before writing anything polished. Then prepare me:

1. The objections I will actually get from this particular audience — not generic objections to psychical research — ordered by how likely they are and how much damage each would do to the paper's survival.
2. Each objection phrased in the vocabulary of the person raising it: the Sidgwick specialist presses texts and chronology; the philosopher of science presses demarcation and method; the historians press biography, institutions and intellectual context.
3. For each objection, a reply I can deliver in roughly ninety seconds, with an honest note on where that reply is weakest and whether I should hold the line or concede gracefully.
4. What I should concede up front, unprompted, to buy credibility before the questioning starts.
5. The question I am hoping nobody asks, and the best answer available to me if it comes.

Finally, a note on your own confidence: which consideration, if I brought it back to you afterwards, would lead you to re-rank the most dangerous objection, and where is your preparation most likely to let me down on the day?
