In On Lying (De Mendacio, c. 395), Augustine defends a strikingly speaker-centered account of what a lie is. Early in the treatise he writes:

"Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind. ... Whence it comes to pass, that he may say a false thing and yet not lie, if he thinks it to be so as he says although it be not so; and, that he may say a true thing, and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, although in reality it be so as he utters it. For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie."

I find this definition attractive: it explains why an honest mistake is not a lie, and why a lucky guess made with intent to deceive still is one. Augustine pairs it with an absolute prohibition -- no lie is permissible, whatever the good at stake -- and a friend of mine has used the combination to build an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept but whose flaw I cannot locate. Here is the case he sets up: your friend is hiding in your house from men who intend to kill him; they knock and ask whether he is inside, and he is.

(1) If every lie is sinful, then in this situation you may not utter, in words or signs, what you take to be false.
(2) You must either answer the question or not answer it.
(3) If you answer, then by (1) you must say what you take to be true; and what you take to be true is that your friend is inside.
(4) So if you answer, you must tell the murderers where your friend is.
(5) If instead you refuse to answer, your refusal tells the murderers exactly what they asked to know -- an innocent man with nothing to hide would simply have said no.
(6) So on Augustine's view every option open to you delivers your friend to the murderers, whether by your words or by your silence.
(7) But no acceptable moral theory requires you to hand an innocent person over to his killers.
(8) Therefore some lies are permissible after all, and Augustine's absolute prohibition is false.

My friend says this is a reductio of pairing the speaker-centered definition with absolutism: the definition tells you what you may not do, and the case shows that what remains is morally intolerable. I would like to keep two things if I can: that lying is wrong in a way that merely misleading someone is not, and that morality cannot require me to betray my friend. Which step of the argument should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If Augustine's own position has the resources to escape (6) -- say, because his definition appears to permit true utterances and meaningful silences that mislead, since neither utters against the mind -- I want to know whether that escape route is principled or whether it reduces his absolutism to an arbitrary rule about which words we use to deceive. And if it is arbitrary, I would rather know that too.
