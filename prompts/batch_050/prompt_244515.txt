Last month a disagreement I have been managing for years finally became unavoidable, and I would like your help thinking it through before a meeting. You are a philosopher of history with particular expertise in the concept of societal collapse and in historiographical disputes over how we should describe large-scale social change. Please reason carefully, and please begin by brainstorming informally -- sketch the candidate principles at play before you commit to any conclusion. The final product should be written in American English.

Here is my situation. I am the commissioning editor of a popular-history book series. Our press's senior history editor, a historian of societal change, has final approval over chapter titles, and for nearly a decade she has applied an informal but consistent standard for when the word "collapse" may appear in a title, as opposed to "transformation," "decline," or "transition." I have a chapter manuscript due next month on a highland empire, I titled it using the word "collapse," and she has flagged it. Before our meeting, I want to predict how she will rule and to understand what each possible ruling would commit the series to going forward.

Her past rulings, as I have recorded them over the years, are these:

1. Classic Maya cities. She permitted "collapse." Monumental construction and dated inscriptions cease, the population of the southern lowlands fell drastically, and although descendants of the Maya persisted -- and persist -- no successor community maintained or claimed the city-state institutions. Her comment: the people continued; the society did not.

2. The Western Roman Empire. She refused "collapse" and required "transformation." The eastern half of the empire persisted for another millennium, Latin and the church carried real institutional continuity, and the successor kingdoms ruled explicitly in the empire's name. Her comment: the loss was slow, partial, and absorbed by claimants.

3. The Norse settlements in Greenland. She permitted "collapse." The settlements ended entirely -- no surviving population, no institution, no claimant. She remarked that the cause mattered less to her than the completeness of the ending.

4. Rapa Nui. She refused "collapse" for a chapter framed around a society's self-inflicted failure, on the ground that the demographic catastrophe was substantially driven by outside contact rather than internal dynamics, and that the islanders had already reorganized their own institutions beforehand. Her comment: a title should not blame a society for its own destruction when the destruction was substantially imported.

5. The Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean. She permitted "collapse," even though Egypt and Assyria survived and eventually recovered, because within a few decades the palace economies and literacy vanished from Greece and much of the region, and recovery to a comparable level of organization took centuries. Her comment: speed, and the loss of an entire level of social organization, were decisive -- even though the people, and later self-identification with that world, continued.

Now the disputed chapter. The highland empire I describe looks like this: over roughly 150 years, its population fell by about two-thirds; the cities emptied into villages, but the villagers kept using the imperial calendar, royal titles, and origin myths, and insisted throughout that they remained the empire; writing was lost for about two centuries and was later reintroduced from outside; the principal driver appears to have been a prolonged drought, compounded by the empire's own political overextension; and population and urbanism returned to their former levels only after about 350 years, with the modern nation-state in the region claiming direct continuity and using the old royal symbols.

What I would like from you:

First, reconstruct the plausible principles that could be generating her five rulings. I suspect the record is consistent with more than one rule -- something based on the scale of demographic loss, something based on the loss of an organizational level such as literacy or cities, something based on speed, something based on whether any community continuously maintains and claims the institutions, and something based on whether the drivers were internal or external. Identify which of these principles diverge on my case and what each predicts.

Second, give me your best prediction of how she will rule, with reasons. Which of the five precedents is the closest analog to my case, and why? If her record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, say so honestly rather than forcing a verdict.

Third, tell me what principle the series would effectively be adopting under each possible ruling -- I want to know what precedent we set for future volumes either way.

Finally, describe a further hypothetical case -- one that has not come up in her rulings -- whose outcome would discriminate between the two strongest candidate principles, so that I can test which one she actually holds.
