I co-host a little podcast on moral philosophy and the guest in two weeks is a philosopher whose new book defends tranquilism – roughly the view that nothing is good in itself except the absence of craving, and that pleasure only matters because it relieves or prevents craving, so a perfectly content state with no desires left unsatisfied is merely neutral rather than good. She has clearly done the rounds on the basic objections and bats them away pretty smoothly, so I want to push past the obvious stuff.

Pretend you're a professional philosopher who knows the suffering-focused ethics literature inside out and has done a fair bit of interviewing yourself. Think carefully about where her view is actually most exposed before you draft anything – do a quick brainstorm of pressure points first, then pick the best ones.

Write me eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up question keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so I'm not left stranded when she gives the standard reply. I want at least one question on each of the following:

- The case of an intensely pleasurable experience that leaves you wanting more (a great meal, a first kiss) – on her view that's apparently bad overall because of the unfulfilled craving, which most listeners will find mad. If she bites the bullet, press on what she's asking us to prefer.
- The asymmetry implication: if satisfied craving adds nothing, then a blissed-out meditator contributes no more value than a rock, and creating a happy, untroubled child does nothing to make the world better. How does she avoid this collapsing into the claim that the best possible world is just the emptiest painless one?
- What's actually new here. Listeners who know a bit of Epicurus or Buddhism will say they've heard this before – what does tranquilism say that those views don't already say, and where does she think they got it wrong?
- Aggregation: can one person's unfulfilled craving be compensated for by someone else's pleasure, or even by the same person's later pleasure? If she distinguishes the intra-personal case from the interpersonal one, ask what justifies the difference.
- Whether the view counsels a kind of quietism: should we stop pursuing ambitions and projects, since they just manufacture cravings? If she says cravings can be instrumentally fine, ask how she draws the line.

Also end with one question about what evidence or argument could actually change her mind, since she's very practiced at treating every objection as a misunderstanding of the definition.

No generic questions (nothing about how she got into philosophy), and dont front-load all the hard stuff – sequence them so each question makes her usual escape routes harder to use. The whole thing in British English please, and write the follow-ups as actual questions I can read out, not as stage directions.
