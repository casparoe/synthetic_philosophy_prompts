I am assembling a handout for a graduate seminar on naturalizing intentionality, and I want a classification instrument rather than an overview. Please write a decision tree that takes a student from the starting commitment — mental content is a natural fact, and so misrepresentation must be explicable without smuggling in an interpreter who already has content — to a named position on what fixes the content of a simple internal signal.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from a reading list: first devise the diagnostic questions, then locate positions at the leaves, and label any combination of answers that no one defends, with one sentence diagnosing why (incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored). Every branch node must be phrased as a question the student can answer by reflecting on a concrete case, without knowing which position the answer leads to. Use only these cases at the nodes: the frog whose tongue-flick is triggered by flies, BB pellets, and the occasional small dark moving speck; magnetotactic bacteria whose internal compass mechanism pulls them toward oxygen-poor water in the northern hemisphere but toward oxygen-rich water if you carry them south; a thermostat; and a self-replicating machine lineage in which a light-sensitive component has been shaped purely by differential replication across generations, with no designer. (The machine case is there to test whether a position's notion of function secretly depends on Darwinian selection rather than selection as such.) Where a node needs it, you may add a malfunction detail — for instance, a bacterium whose compass mechanism is damaged — but introduce no other cases.

Among the leaves the tree must reach: pure informational-covariation semantics without functions; asymmetric-dependence semantics without functions; learning-history teleosemantics; selection-history producer-side teleosemantics; selection-history consumer-side teleosemantics; a selection-history view on which the content is fixed by the response the mechanism was selected to produce, so that the frog's signal means something proximal rather than anything about flies; a success-based view on which content is fixed by what has to be the case for the consuming system's behavior to succeed; and an eliminativist or indeterminacy-embracing exit for a student who concludes that no natural relation can draw the misrepresentation line and that we should stop asking. If the tree cannot separate two of these without an extra node you deliberately omit, say so explicitly and name the pair; do not paper over it.

Keep the tree to between eight and twelve branch nodes. For each leaf, give the position's name (or "unnamed, apparently unoccupied"), one sentence stating its most awkward commitment as it surfaces in the frog or bacteria case, and one sentence on what the machine-lineage case does to it. End with a short scoring note: two or three lines on which node does the most sorting work, and which node is most likely to produce unstable answers because students equivocate between "what the mechanism indicates" and "what the mechanism is for." The handout's readers are philosophy graduate students who know the disjunction and distal/proximal problems in outline but have not committed to a solution, so the tree should force commitments rather than recite them.
