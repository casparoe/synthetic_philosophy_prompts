Write a dialogue for my philosophy of time seminar between two colleagues, a presentist and an eternalist, who get stuck on a case I want my students to chew on: a dying woman asks her friend to promise that he'll read her favorite poem at her grave every year on her birthday. Fifty years later the friend is still doing it, and a mutual acquaintance says he's being silly, since there's no longer anyone there who could be let down if he stopped.

The presentist should argue that promises to the dead are genuine but somehow still binding even though the promisee doesn't exist anymore, and the eternalist should press the point that this only makes sense if the dead woman still exists (tenselessly) as the current bearer of the obligation. But don't let the eternalist have it easy either -- the presentist should push back on whether a tenselessly existing person can actually be wronged *now*, given that nothing that happens now can change anything about her. Have them actually engage with each other's moves: if one suggests an account (say, that the obligation is really to the past person, or to oneself, or grounded in the friendship rather than the promisee), the other should respond to that specific proposal, not just restate their opening position.

Both should be sharp and neither should win decisively. Around 600-900 words. No narrator, no stage directions beyond names, just the two of them talking like actual philosophers who know each other and have had versions of this argument before.
