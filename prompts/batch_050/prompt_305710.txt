So my grandfather died in March and left behind a decent pile of money, and my family has been at war over it ever since. Not even over who gets what — over the structure. My uncle hired this estate-planning firm and they've set up a "dynasty trust," which aparently means the fortune skips a generation for tax purposes, all the gains get a "stepped-up basis" at death so decades of appreciation basically never get taxed, and the trust can just sit there compounding while my cousins borrow against the portfolio instead of ever selling anything, so no taxable event ever happens. The family group chat is now 80% the words "legacy," "stewardship," and "what grandpa would have wanted."

I'm losing my mind a little, and since nobody responds to my earnest 1am emails about equality of opportunity, I've decided to go with satire instead. Write me the firm's own glossy client brochure — the actual marketing PDF they'd send to prospective families. Give the firm a plausible fake name (not a real company). Sections: an About Us page on the firm's founding values, a Our Services page describing the stepped-up basis, the generation-skipping trust, and a concierge lending program where heirs live off loans collateralized by the portfolio so they never realize a gain, three short testimonials from satisfied families, and an FAQ with questions like "Is this fair?", "Will my grandchildren still be motivated to work?", and "What if the tax laws change?"

The brochure should be completely sincere on its own terms — steeped in the language of hard work, family values, sacrifice, and protecting what grandpa built — while the mechanics make it obvious to anyone reading closely that the product being sold is one family's permanent exemption from the rules everyone else plays by. The FAQ answers should accidentally concede the standard objections about desert, luck, and equal opportunity without ever naming them. The satire has to come from real features of how dynastic wealth actually works, not invented absurdities: no cartoon villainy, no winking at the reader, no narrator breaking character. Someone who knows the actual debates about inheritance and equality of opportunity should be able to tell the writer gets it; someone who doesn't should still find it funny. Around 650 words.
