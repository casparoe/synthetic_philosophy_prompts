I'm in the middle of a philosophy of science course where we've just spent two weeks on Friedman's essay on positive economics and the so-called F-twist — the claim that the realism of a theory's assumptions is irrelevant to assessing it, and that a theory should be judged by its predictions alone. We've read the usual replies: that false assumptions can carry explanatory weight, that science progresses by gradually replacing idealizations with more realistic descriptions, and so on. In the course of all this I noticed a recurring pattern that none of the taxonomies we've covered seems to capture, and I want to know whether it has an established name or literature of its own.

The pattern is this: a model contains two or more known-false assumptions, each of which would be indefensible on its own, but which are retained together because their errors run in opposite directions and roughly cancel within the model's intended domain — so that if you corrected either one while leaving the other in place, the model would get strictly *worse* at the very predictions it was built for. Two instances. In an epidemiology elective I'm taking, the basic compartmental model we used assumes that everyone in the population mixes with everyone else equally (which, given how real contact networks are structured, overstates how quickly a disease moves between subpopulations), while simultaneously assuming that every infected person is detectably sick (which understates the spread); when someone asked why we didn't fix one or the other, the TA's answer was essentially that the two distortions pull against each other and the model is more accurate with both than with either corrected alone. And a friend writing an economics thesis showed me her tax-incidence model, which assumes consumers react to price changes instantly (overstating the short-run behavioral response) while ignoring the portion of the tax that firms absorb through compressed markups (understating the effective price change consumers face) — and again, her supervisor told her to leave both assumptions in, because they compensate.

What strikes me is that this configuration doesn't seem to be any of the things our readings distinguished. It's not the standard defense that the falsehood is approximately true where it matters, because here each falsehood matters enormously — taken singly, each badly skews the results. It's not Galilean-style idealization, where you deliberately distort one factor and later correct it stepwise, because here deidealization is blocked: correcting one factor without the other degrades performance. And it doesn't seem to be what I've seen called robustness analysis, where you trust a result because it survives across many models built on *different* false assumptions, because here the result depends on one particular *pairing* of falsehoods and might collapse if either partner is removed. So my question is threefold. First, does this configuration — mutually compensating falsehoods that are jointly load-bearing — have a name of its own, or is it simply folded into one of these neighboring notions, and if folded in, does it actually fit? Second, where are its edges: is a single calibrated assumption that is false but fitted to data an instance of the same phenomenon or a different one, and does it matter whether the cancellation was deliberately engineered or discovered only after the fact? Third, is the configuration epistemically benign in some contexts (say, measurement, where offsetting systematic errors seem unproblematic) but pathological in others (say, explanation, where the model arguably gets things right for the wrong reasons)? I'd rather have the carving than the label — what exactly this phenomenon is, what it isn't, and how it bears on the Friedman debate — so please don't just hand me a term.
