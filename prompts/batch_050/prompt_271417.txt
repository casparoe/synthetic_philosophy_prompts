For the past year I have run a small philosophy reading group, and for the last several sessions we have been stuck on free will. The argument keeps returning to a stock example from the philosophy of mind literature: the digger wasp that paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the entrance of its burrow, leaves it there while it goes inside to inspect the chamber, and — if an experimenter moves the cricket a few inches — drags it back to the entrance and inspects the burrow again, repeating the loop apparently without limit rather than simply taking the cricket inside. The incompatibilists in the group say that humans in a deterministic universe are just very large wasps: our apparent flexibility is a longer and more elaborate fixed program, and adding subroutines to a mechanism never makes it free. The compatibilists reply that the loop is exactly what we are not: we respond to reasons, revise plans mid-course, and would have acted differently had things been slightly different, and that this is the only sense of "could have done otherwise" worth wanting. Every session dies at exactly this spot, with both sides agreeing on the facts about the wasp and about determinism.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about determinism at all but about what the ordinary phrase "could have done otherwise" has to mean in order to matter — a counterfactual-behavioral reading versus a reading on which the agent must be the ultimate source of the action — and that neither side can argue for its own reading without assuming it. As a way of forcing the issue, I have proposed what I call the sphex test: an agent could have done otherwise just in case some suitable small change to its inputs would have led it to do otherwise through genuine re-planning, rather than through blind repetition of a subroutine or mere random breakdown. The compatibilists in my group mostly accept the test; the incompatibilists say it defines freedom as sophisticated flexibility and thereby builds the compatibilist conclusion into the criterion, since nothing about re-planning touches the question of whether the planner is the source of its plans.

Is my diagnosis right, and does the sphex test actually smuggle in everything at issue, or does it expose that the incompatibilist demand for sourcehood cannot be stated without circularity? I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, substantive, or two questions pursued under one heading — grounded in what each side actually says, not a survey of positions. Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that dissolves the impasse, an empirical finding (say, about how rigidly even adult humans repeat interrupted routines), a formal result about what deterministic systems can and cannot do, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the dispute rests on bedrock intuitions neither side can defend. Please write your answer in American English.
