So I'm writing a paper for a continental ethics seminar and I keep going back and forth on whether my framing actually works, so let me just run it by you. I want to analyze the Milgram obedience experiments — the Yale shock studies from the early 60s — through Levinas's ethics of the face. Here's what I know, and what you can take as given: subjects were told to administer escalating shocks to a learner for wrong answers, the shocks weren't real, and obedience was extremely high in the canonical setup where the learner was in another room and could only be heard, not seen. But Milgram ran a bunch of variations, and the one that matters for my paper is proximity: when the learner was seated in the same room as the subject, obedience dropped substantially, and when subjects had to physically force the learner's hand onto the shock plate, it dropped further still. Meanwhile the experimenter — the guy in the lab coat calmly insisting that the experiment requires that you continue — was in the room with the subject the whole time.

The mapping I have in mind: Levinas holds that the face of the Other is where ethics begins, that the face issues a command along the lines of do not kill me, and that this command is prior to any contract, reciprocity, or calculation — ethics as first philosophy, before ontology. On that picture the proximity variations look almost like an experimental manipulation of the face-to-face: reduce the victim to a voice or a bang on the wall and the "killing" (so to speak) is easy, put him literally face to face with you and it becomes hard. What I want you to do is work this mapping out carefully, not just gesture at it. What exactly counts as the face in the Milgram setup? Does the experimenter's presence also count as a face — and if so, does Levinas have the resources to explain why one face wins out over the other, maybe via the stuff about the third party introducing justice and comparison, or does the fact that the experimenter's "face" usually won show that the face was never the right unit of analysis here to begin with?

And then run it in the other direction too: is Milgram actually evidence for Levinas, or does it cut against him? Because if the ethical force of the face rises and falls with a few meters of physical distance, doesn't that make it look less like an absolute pre-rational command and more like ordinary empathy, which is just psychology rather than first philosophy? Finally, tell me where Levinas's account simply doesn't speak at all — I suspect it's not meant to be a predictive theory of behavior, but I'm not sure how much I can concede on that front before the whole analysis becomes trivial. Don't just tell me Levinas is profound; tell me where the mapping strains.
