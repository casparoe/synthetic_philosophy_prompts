A friend and I spent last night arguing about why some nations are rich, and we ended up stuck. Her position: geography does most of the explanatory work — disease burden, agricultural endowment, access to trade routes — and good institutions are largely a downstream product of favorable conditions rather than a cause of wealth. Mine: institutions do most of the work, as shown by paired cases that share geography and culture but diverge sharply, like the two Koreas or the two Nogaleses. Give me your credence that, among the seriously debated candidates — geography, institutions, culture — institutional quality is the single largest contributor to present-day cross-country income differences. But decompose it: the probability that the question is even well-posed given how entangled these factors are over centuries; the weight you place on natural experiments and historical reversals of fortune versus the objection that they exploit rare, unrepresentative variation; whether paired-border cases like mine actually generalize; and whatever other terms you think belong in the calculation — each with a rough number and one line of justification, then the combination. Finally, tell me which term you are least confident in and what evidence would move it most, and given your decomposition, which of the two of us is more likely mistaken about which sub-question.
