I'm giving a talk next month at an energy policy seminar defending a slightly unfashionable position: that Germany's Atomausstieg was defensible in 2011 but that completing it in 2023 -- shutting the last three plants in the middle of a gas crisis while coal plants were being restarted -- was a serious mistake, and that the real contrast with France isn't nuclear vs. no nuclear but *fleet standardization vs. bespoke plants*. My core claim is that France's advantage came mostly from building essentially the same reactor design dozens of times, so learning effects compounded, while Germany (and the US, and the UK) treated each plant as a one-off megaproject. So the lesson of the Energiewende isn't "nuclear is too slow and expensive" but "nuclear is only slow and expensive if you never build the same thing twice."

The audience: mostly energy economists and political scientists, plus a handful of engineers. The economists are renewables cost-curve people, some of them pretty committed to the LCOE way of seeing everything. The political scientists care about coal-state politics and the EU state-aid angle. The engineers have strong opinions about ramping nuclear plants to follow load, which the German fleet was actually doing more of toward the end. Nobody in the room is a pro-nuclear activist; the median attendee thinks the exit was roughly fine but is open to the standardization argument becuase it also applies to wind (serial production of turbines vs. bespoke grid connections).

Prep me for the Q&A. I want:

- The objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, each phrased the way this specific audience would phrase it (the economists will say "capacity factor" and "learning rate," not "baseload"; the political scientists will talk about veto players, not atoms)
- For each one: what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and a two-minute reply
- A flag on which objections have real force against the *standardization* thesis specifically rather than against nuclear power in general -- I don't want to get dragged into defending all of nuclear when my claim is narrower than that
- The weakest point in my own argument. I suspect it's that Flamanville and Olkiluoto are counterexamples to my own learning-effects story (both were first-of-a-kind EPR builds that went catastrophically over budget, which either proves my point or disproves it depending how you slice it), but tell me if there's something worse I'm not seeing
- The question I'm hoping nobody asks

One constraint: don't build any replies on specific papers or numbers I can't back up from memory. Frame everything so it stands on logic and genuinely well-known facts -- the broad shape of the French buildout, the German phase-out timeline, the fact that recent first-of-a-kind builds in Europe went badly. If a reply only works with a citation attached, give me the citation-free version instead and note where it's softer as a result.
