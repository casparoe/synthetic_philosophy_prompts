So my dad got diagnosed with early-stage Alzheimer's about eight months ago, and for now he's still fully competent, sharp most days honestly. He's a retired engineer, so of course he immediately started treating his own decline like a project, which means our whole family is now deep in advance directive territory. The one he's drafting says roughly: once I can no longer recognize my kids or hold a real conversation, no artificial feeding, no antibiotics for infections, comfort care only. My sister thinks this is fine but only as a default his future self can override. My dad wants the opposite -- he wants the document to bind even against a future him who seems content and verbally says he wants to stay alive. His line, which I find weirdly compelling, is that the decision should belong to whichever version of him has the most information and the most at stake, and right now that's clearly current-dad, not some future version who's lost the thread of his own life.

Here's where I got stuck. I read somewhere (probably just a pop-science piece, I cant find it again) that people with serious disabilities routinely report way higher quality of life than healthy people predict, and that dementia patients often seem less miserable than their families expect from the outside. So there's a real chance future-dad is fine, maybe even happy, in a way current-dad can't imagine from here. Which makes me wonder whether the earlier self's authority should decay as the psychological connection between the two selves thins out -- like, is the demented patient the same person the directive was written by, in the sense that matters morally? A friend of mine who's really into Parfit says identity comes in degrees and so directive-authority should too, which sounds clever but I genuinely can't tell if it's doing real work here or just sounds sophisticated.

So what I want: give me your own credence, as a number, that a clear directive written by a competent person should override the apparent present wishes of their later, moderately demented self, in roughly the case I described (no longer recognizes family, seems content day to day, verbally resists comfort-only care). But don't just hand me the number -- decompose it. Break the question into the sub-questions it's riding on (personal identity/sameness, whether you can wrong someone by ignoring instructions they no longer understand, whether the adaptation evidence undercuts the earlier self's judgment, whether present preferences carry independent moral weight at low competence, whatever you think the load-bearing pieces are), put a rough probability on each, show how they combine, and tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to. For calibration: my own credence that the directive should govern is maybe 0.65, my sister's is like 0.15. Tell me which sub-question we most likely actually disagree about, because after six months of arguing about this I honestly cannot locate it. And say what new evidence or argument would move your number the most.
