The semantics of generics has been worked on seriously for roughly five decades, and as far as I can tell the debate has made almost no net progress. Every theory on offer handles its motivating examples and fails somewhere nearby. A default-quantification account gets "ducks lay eggs" right (true, even though only females do it) but then has to work hard to explain why "ducks are female" is false when the same proportion of ducks is involved. Normality-based accounts handle characteristic properties nicely, but "mosquitoes carry the West Nile virus" is accepted as true even though only a tiny fraction of mosquitoes carry it, while "books are paperbacks" is false even though most books are. Prevalence-based or probabilistic-threshold accounts explain the last pair but not the first two. And every camp responds to counterexamples the same way: by adding a parameter (context-sensitivity, alternative kinds of predication, a distinction between the characteristic and the merely statistical) that saves the theory for that case and that the other camps regard as ad hoc. My reading group has been through this literature for a semester and the pattern repeats each week: a paper presents cases its rivals can't handle, the rivals could in principle handle them with a tweak, and nobody considers the tweak mandatory.

I have a hypothesis about what is going on, but I'd like you to test it rather than accept it. The hypothesis: this is not one disagreement but at least three, conducted under the single heading "the semantics of generics." Some authors are asking what truth conditions generic sentences have, some are asking what psychological mechanism lets humans reason so fluently with generics, and some are asking what logical form to assign them for purposes of systematic compositional semantics. These questions can come apart — the best psychological model might not be a compositional semantics, and a compositional semantics might be deliberately silent on processing — and the apparent impasse might be an artifact of demanding one answer to all three. Alternatively, the impasse might reflect something deeper: that generic sentences don't have a uniform semantic analysis at all, so the field's shared presupposition that there is one theory to find is itself the problem.

So: diagnose the disagreement. Is it substantive or partly verbal? Are the sides disagreeing about which data a theory must explain (truth-value intuitions? acquisition patterns? inference behavior?), about the standards a solution must meet, or about starting assumptions neither side can argue for? Where exactly does my three-questions hypothesis succeed, and where does it fail? And given your diagnosis, tell me concretely what would count as progress in this debate — a new kind of data, a formal result, a distinction the field lacks, or a dissolution of the question — or else argue that no progress should be expected and say why.
