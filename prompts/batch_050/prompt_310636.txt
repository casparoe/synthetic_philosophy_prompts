Our school's philosophy and politics society is student-run, and next term it's my turn to lead the weekly session. We meet for one hour after school; attendance is usually somewhere between twelve and sixteen students, aged fifteen to eighteen, with no assumed knowledge beyond general curiosity. I want to do a session on conservatism as a philosophical position, and I've been reading Edmund Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France to prepare.

One passage stopped me. Burke argues that anyone should be infinitely cautious about demolishing an institution that has served the ordinary purposes of society tolerably well for a long time, or about rebuilding it, without having proven models of workable alternatives in front of them. What struck me is the epistemology underneath: his caution doesn't rest on claiming the institution is good, or even on knowing what it's for. It rests on the thought that its survival is itself evidence of a function the reformer may simply not be able to see. I've since learned that a version of this circulates online as "Chesterton's fence," usually flattened into "never remove anything until you personally understand it," which I suspect misses Burke's stronger point that some functions may not be discoverable by any individual at all.

I'd like you to design an activity for this session in which the other students feel the force of that passage before anyone names it. Concretely, I'm imagining something like a simulation or structured game in which participants inherit a system full of strange, apparently pointless rules and are given real power to abolish them under time pressure and some kind of scoring or stakes (not money — points, privileges, or winning the session would all work), so that they discover through consequences, not through my telling them, why a rule whose purpose nobody in the room can state might still be load-bearing. Please specify: the setup and materials; the exact rules of play, including whatever mechanism creates the asymmetric risk Burke is pointing at (it's cheap to demolish, expensive to be wrong); how to stop the trivial strategy of refusing to change anything, since Burke plainly thinks some things should be reformed and the game should too; a realistic timeline for fifty minutes of play plus debrief; and what will predictably go wrong the first time a student (me) runs it — for instance, participants guessing the trick in advance, or treating it as a puzzle with a designer behind it — and how I should handle each.

Then the debrief. I want a mapping from specific moments of the activity onto specific parts of the passage: where the game reproduces the claim about age and tolerable service as evidence of function, where it reproduces the asymmetry of demolition and error, and where it must fall short. In particular, please be frank about the feature of Burke's view the game cannot reproduce — my own worry is that in any game the hidden functions were put there deliberately by a designer and can in principle all be known, whereas Burke's point is about institutions whose functions emerged over generations without any designer and may not be statable by anyone living — and about how I should present that gap rather than paper over it.

Finally, one interpretive question to fold into the debrief plan. The passage ties caution to institutions that have answered society's purposes tolerably well for ages. That seems to let genuinely oppressive or useless traditions slip through under the cover of longevity, and the flattened fence slogan online has the same problem. Does the activity, as you've designed it, give participants any grip on how a Burkean can distinguish a load-bearing fence from a merely old one — and if it can't, what should I say about that in the debrief?
