An essay I saw circulating on a forum I read — the kind of place where people take the map-territory distinction seriously and treat revising logic itself with deep suspicion — offered the following summary of dialetheism and paraconsistent logic, and I'd like to know how much of it to trust:

"Classical logic contains a structural flaw known as explosion: from a single contradiction, absolutely anything follows, which is why an inconsistent theory can 'prove' every sentence and is therefore worthless. Paraconsistent logics fix this by removing the law of non-contradiction from the logic, so that a contradiction no longer licenses arbitrary conclusions and inconsistent theories can still be reasoned about usefully. Building on this foundation, dialetheism — the view associated with the philosopher Graham Priest — goes further and holds that some contradictions are literally true: the Liar sentence ('this sentence is false') is both true and false, and similar 'true contradictions' arise for vague predicates at the borderline of a sorites series, for bodies of law that both require and forbid the same act, and at the limits of thought more generally. Because the law of non-contradiction has been rejected, dialetheists claim to have thereby proved that true contradictions exist, dissolving the paradoxes of self-reference once and for all and establishing that reality itself is inconsistent at the edges."

My instinct — from half-remembered discussions in these communities — is that the first paragraph is roughly right but that something goes wrong in the transition to the second, and that the final sentence oversells things badly. But I can't articulate where. Could you give me an accuracy audit? Specifically: (1) which claims a specialist would accept as stated, including the basic account of explosion; (2) whether it's right to describe paraconsistent logics as working by "removing the law of non-contradiction," or whether that conflates two things the literature keeps apart; (3) whether dialetheism is accurately characterized, and what the strongest version of the view actually claims versus what the essay attributes to it; (4) whether the concluding sentence follows from anything true earlier in the summary — in particular, whether accepting the Liar as both true and false amounts to a proof of true contradictions, whether it "dissolves" the paradoxes, and whether it commits one to claims about the physical world. Finally: if the two or three most dubious claims were corrected, what would a fair one-paragraph summary look like, and would it be more or less exciting than the original?
