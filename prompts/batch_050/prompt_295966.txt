I am a novelist in the early planning stages of a long-form project: a five-volume saga following one family across roughly sixty years, with the narrative told out of chronological order and with the same characters appearing at wildly different ages, sometimes within the same chapter. A philosopher friend, upon hearing the premise, told me that serial fiction of this kind is "applied persistence theory" and that I had better decide where I stand on endurantism versus perdurantism before I write another word. I suspect this is overstated, but it has lodged in my mind, and I would like your help sorting out what, if anything, in that debate should actually change how I work.

For context, here is my lay understanding. The endurantist holds that a person is wholly present at each moment of her existence; the perdurantist holds that a person is a four-dimensional whole extended through time, with temporal parts the way a road has spatial parts, so that "Elena at twenty" and "Elena at seventy" are, strictly, different parts of one worm-like entity rather than the same thing twice. There are variants — stage theories, counterpart-theoretic treatments, views on which persistence is a matter of degree or convention — and a great deal of the debate seems to concern material objects, lumps of clay, and ships, with persons brought in mainly as test cases.

My specific questions about craft are these. First, when I write a scene in which an elderly character remembers her own childhood, the grammar of my sentences presupposes that the rememberer and the child are identical. Is that presupposition doing metaphysical work I should care about, or is it a harmless fiction every theory can paraphrase away? Second, I plan a volume in which a character undergoes a conversion so radical that other characters say he is no longer the same man. Whether that sentence is literal or metaphorical seems to matter enormously to how the volume's moral bookkeeping works — whether he inherits his earlier self's debts, loves, and crimes. Third, late in the saga I intend a speculative chapter in which a character is duplicated by a fantastical device, and both copies live on for years. I gather this resembles fission cases in the personal identity literature, but I do not know which resolutions of those cases produce the most interesting rather than the most confusing narrative. Fourth, there is the sheer practical fact that I will write prequel material after the sequel material, retrofitting youth onto characters whose old age I have already fixed in print.

What I want from you is a defended prioritization, not a survey of the persistence debate. Of the open or contested questions in this area — the coherence of temporal parts for persons, whether identity across time is strict or a matter of degree, the treatment of fission, the relation between metaphysical persistence and narrative or practical identity — which two or three should a working novelist actually settle, at least provisionally, before structuring a project like mine? For each question you rank highly, explain: why it is upstream of concrete decisions I will have to make on the page; how tractable it is for someone who is a reader of philosophy rather than a philosopher, within a few months of background study; and what would make it drop down the list. Equally, tell me which parts of the debate are overrated for my purposes — questions that feel foundational but whose resolution would leave my actual sentences unchanged. If the honest answer is that my philosopher friend is wrong and none of this should alter my practice, defend that instead, but then tell me what the debate is good for, if anything, from where I sit.
