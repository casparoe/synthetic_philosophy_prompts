I am finishing the normative chapter of a book on the allocation of scarce medical resources, and before the manuscript goes to the press's referees I want a consistency audit of the framework I defend. My commitments are these:

1. Prioritarianism. The moral value of an outcome is the sum of each person's lifetime well-being run through a strictly increasing, strictly concave function. How much a benefit matters depends on how badly off the recipient is in absolute terms, not on their position relative to others.
2. The leveling-down objection is decisive against telic egalitarianism. Inequality between people is never bad in itself; making the better-off worse off while benefiting no one is not good in any respect, not even slightly.
3. My main reason for preferring prioritarianism to utilitarianism is that the weighting honors the separateness of persons: it refuses to treat a gain to one person as straightforward compensation for a loss to another.
4. Under uncertainty, the concave weighting should be applied to people's prospects at the time of choice — that is, to their expected well-being — rather than to how their lives actually turn out.
5. Final value resides only in how lives actually go. An unrealized possibility, however good it was ex ante, leaves nothing of value behind once it fails to materialize.
6. Where two patients have equal claims on a single cure, allocating it by lottery is morally required, not merely permitted — and this requirement is fully compatible with commitment 2.

Please tell me which of these are in genuine tension and which only appear to be, ordered by severity. For each genuine tension, state what would have to give and what that revision costs; for each apparent tension, show why it dissolves. Conclude with the least costly overall repair. I have no preferred revision in mind.
