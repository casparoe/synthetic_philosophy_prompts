I cover criminal justice and the politics of policing for a national magazine, and I'm preparing a long-form essay on the recurring dispute over whether police departments should be reformed, defunded, or abolished. In the course of reporting I've noticed that a single formula does heavy lifting on both sides of that dispute: the state is "the monopoly on the legitimate use of violence." Abolitionist writers invoke it to argue that every act of policing is state violence by definition, so that reform merely launders violence rather than reducing it. Defenders of policing invoke the same formula to argue that concentrating force in one publicly accountable institution is exactly what distinguishes order from vendetta, so that abolition would not eliminate violence but privatize it. I want the essay to take the slogan apart rather than let either side use it as a conversation-ender.

Please lay out the distinct precise claims this formula could be making. I can already see that it might be (a) a definition of what a state is, (b) a descriptive claim about what modern states in fact achieve, (c) a normative claim that only state violence can be legitimate, or (d) a normative claim that the state ought actively to suppress private violence — but I suspect the menu is longer and the distinctions subtler, especially around the words "legitimate," "monopoly," and "use" as opposed to threat or capacity. For each reading: state it precisely; say whether it is a truism, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false; and identify what, if anything, follows from it for the reform-defund-abolish debate. In particular, tell me which readings the abolitionist argument actually requires and which readings the defender's argument actually requires, and flag any case where a side's rhetoric depends on sliding between two readings that cannot both be true. Finally, if there are positions in the debate that survive even once the slogan is disambiguated — arguments about police that don't lean on it at all — say briefly what those are, so the essay doesn't leave readers thinking the slogan was ever the real issue. I'd like the result organized as a set of numbered readings with verdicts, which I can use as the skeleton of the piece.
