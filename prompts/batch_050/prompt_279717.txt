So here's something that's been bugging me. When a big company does something awful -- say a carmaker gets caught rigging its emissions tests, or a bank opens fake accounts for years -- the response follows a pretty standard script. Regulators fine the company some eye-watering sum. Prosecutors let the corporation itself plead guilty (not just employees, the actual legal person). The company puts out a public apology, promises a culture change, sometimes agrees to an outside monitor who sits in for a few years checking it has reformed. Occasionally a prosecutor will talk about wanting to revoke its license to operate entirely, the so-called corporate death penalty, though they almost never pull the trigger.

What does this whole ritual presupose about what a corporation is? Some parts seem to treat the firm as a genuine agent -- you don't extract guilty pleas and apologies and promises of moral reform from a vending machine, and a monitor checking whether it's "reformed" only makes sense if there's a character there to reform. But the fine, which is the part with real teeth, gets paid out of money that would otherwise go to shareholders, workers, and customers, mostly people who did nothing wrong. That part looks less like punishing an agent and more like yanking a lever on an incentive machine, which a pure deterrence theorist could justify even if corporations have no agency whatsoever. Meanwhile actual executives often walk away without individual charges, which suggests the system thinks the blame lands somewhere besides the individual humans.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory of corporate moral agency baked into this practice. Which parts strictly require the firm to be a blameworthy agent in its own right, and which parts would work just as well on a deflationary picture where the firm is just a structure for channeling incentives at individuals? Is the script coherent as a whole, or does it presuppose one theory in the courtroom and a different one at the sentencing hearing? And then tell me concretely what would have to change if we committed to each rival view: if only individuals can be agents, does the apology become nonsense and the guilty plea get scrapped? And if the firm really is an agent distinct from its members, does fining it become as weird as punishing one person for another's crime, and should we be doing something to the company itself instead? I'd rather you reason it through than survey the literature -- I mostly want to know whether the practice I described has a consistent philosophy in it or not.
