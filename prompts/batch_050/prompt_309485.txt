I'm a teaching assistant for a graduate seminar in philosophy of economics, and next week I'm running a session on the revealed preference tradition in choice theory. The students have read the standard textbook material — completeness, transitivity, the weak and strong axioms, utility as a numerical representation of choice — but I keep finding that they conflate several genuinely different positions under the heading "economists think preferences are revealed by choices." I'd like you to construct a decision tree that takes a student from the neutral starting commitment "an agent's choices are systematic enough to be described by the utility framework" to a named position on what utility actually is.

The tree should have seven to nine branch nodes, and each node must be phrased as a question the student can answer from a concrete case or thought experiment, without already knowing which position the answer commits them to. The cases I most want used as diagnostics: framing effects and decoy options (where adding an irrelevant third alternative flips choice between two others); the phenomenon of an addict choosing against what she herself calls her own good; and the difference between using choice data to *infer* a pre-existing preference versus *stipulating* that whatever was chosen was preferred. Where the positions come apart is precisely on these: whether choice is evidence for something psychological, whether it constitutes the preference itself, whether utility is merely a mathematical bookkeeping device with no psychological or normative content whatsoever, and whether satisfying revealed preferences has any claim to track welfare.

The leaves should include at minimum: the strict behaviorist reading associated with Samuelson's original program, the deflationary formalism of the sort Ken Binmore defends, mentalist or hedonic interpretations on which utility names a real psychological magnitude that choice merely measures imperfectly, and idealized preference-satisfaction welfarism of the Harsanyi sort. For each leaf, give the position's name (or "unnamed" if the combination is unoccupied) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Please also do two things that taxonomies usually skip: flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate by the answers given, and note any answer combination that lands on no defended position, with a one-line diagnosis of whether that square of the logical space is empty because the view there is incoherent, merely unpopular, or simply unexplored.
