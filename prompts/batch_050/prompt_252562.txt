Analyze the 2013 Edward Snowden disclosures as a test case for theories of political obligation and civil disobedience. For the purposes of the analysis, stipulate that Snowden, while working as an intelligence contractor, knowingly violated legal and professional secrecy obligations by releasing classified material about government surveillance; that the material concerned programs of genuine public interest; that he publicly identified himself as the source; and that he left the United States rather than remain available for prosecution. Do not let the analysis turn on disputed details about the precise contents of the disclosures or Snowden’s private motives.

Please proceed in both directions: use the theories to illuminate the episode, and the episode to test the theories.

First, distinguish legal obligation, moral political obligation, and all-things-considered justification, without assuming that one settles the others. Then apply a familiar Rawlsian template on which civil disobedience is a public, nonviolent, conscientious breach of law intended to protest a serious injustice within a broadly just constitutional order, ordinarily accompanied by willingness to accept legal consequences. Which elements does Snowden’s conduct clearly satisfy, which does it clearly fail, and which resist classification? In particular, distinguish publicity of the disclosure, publicity of the dissenter’s identity, and submission to punishment.

Second, assess whether Snowden had a prima facie obligation to obey the relevant secrecy laws under consent or promise-based, fair-play, associative, and natural-duty accounts of political obligation. Should a security-clearance undertaking receive special weight as an explicit promise, or can such a promise be defeated by the content it requires one to conceal? Avoid treating the justice of his cause as sufficient by itself to settle either the existence or the weight of that obligation.

Third, use two brief counterfactuals to isolate the contested variables: one in which Snowden releases the same material anonymously, and another in which he acts identically except that he remains in the United States and contests the charges in court. Does either variant make the conduct more or less clearly an instance of civil disobedience, and does that classification bear on its justification?

Conclude by saying what this episode shows about whether accepting punishment, exhausting internal channels, and remaining within the jurisdiction should be understood as defining features of civil disobedience, evidence of conscientiousness, or merely strategically relevant considerations. Throughout, separate points that are relatively settled among political philosophers from those that remain genuinely contested, and do not present Rawls’s account—or any competing account—as the consensus view.
