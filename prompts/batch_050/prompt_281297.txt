I am the methods director of a university psychophysics laboratory that studies apparent motion and other postdictive perceptual phenomena — effects in which a stimulus presented at one time appears to determine what subjects say they experienced at an earlier time. Our staple is the color phi phenomenon: two brief, differently colored dots flashed in succession at different locations produce the report of a single dot that moves and changes color partway along its path, including, apparently, at positions and moments before the second dot was ever presented. We run roughly fifteen to twenty experiments a year, and we keep colliding with the same problem from three directions: journal reviewers demand to know whether subjects actually saw the interpolated motion or merely constructed the report afterward; an industry partner wants to cite our studies in claims about what display users experience; and my own postdocs, none of whom have philosophical training, use verbs like see, perceive, report, and remember almost interchangeably in drafts.

I want a standing decision procedure for the laboratory that translates the philosophical debate about the timing of experience into laboratory policy. The debate I have in mind is the one standardly framed as Orwellian versus Stalinesque interpretation (terminology associated with Dennett): on the Orwellian reading, subjects veridically experience two stationary dots and their memory of the episode is revised afterward; on the Stalinesque reading, the brain fabricates the interpolated motion and color change before the episode enters experience at all. A widely discussed critique holds that below some timescale — roughly the temporal grain at which experience can be reported, rehearsed, or probed — the two readings make no divergent predictions, and may state no genuine difference at all. The procedure's job is not to settle that debate; it is to govern what our papers may claim while it remains unsettled.

Concretely, the procedure will be run by a postdoc, in under an hour, before any results section is drafted, and I will sign off on its output. It should take as inputs an experiment's design, timing parameters, response modalities, and data, and terminate in one of three rulings per candidate claim: licensed (the claim may be stated in experience-language), report-language only (results must be stated in terms of what subjects said, judged, or remembered), or blocked (no version of the claim is supported; the claim is dropped or the study redesigned). Please provide:

1. The ordered sequence of tests or questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step and where the sequence stops with each ruling.
2. A quantitative rule for the step I care most about: deciding, for a given experiment, whether the interval within which a later stimulus could have shaped the report is small enough that the Orwellian/Stalinesque question should be declared undecidable for that study — call this the revision window — including how to set its length when the data give you nothing to go on.
3. A mapping from design features and response modalities to permitted vocabulary: keypresses during stimulation, immediate verbal report, delayed forced choice, next-day recognition, confidence ratings, and EEG-derived timing estimates, taken singly and in combination. In particular, state whether neural timing measures alone can ever license experience-language, and under what conditions.
4. An explicit list of the steps where judgment cannot be eliminated, so reviewers can see which parts of the procedure are policy rather than logic.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show its workings:

(i) A standard color-phi display, about 250 ms between dots, immediate verbal report, and a draft claiming that subjects saw the dot change color mid-trajectory.
(ii) Our interrupt paradigm, in which on unpredictable trials the second dot is never presented and subjects are immediately asked what they saw; specify what each possible pattern of interrupt-trial reports would license or block, rather than assuming any particular outcome.
(iii) A study whose only timing evidence consists of reaction times and an EEG component, where the principal investigator wants to write that subjects experienced the change before their behavior could reflect it.

At least one of these should be a case where the procedure blocks a claim the laboratory badly wants to make; if none is, identify which case you think most deserves that treatment and why. Close with an honest statement of what the procedure cannot do: which questions about the timing of experience it leaves untouched by design, and where it will misfire if applied mechanically.
