Ok so I've been going back and forth about sweatshops with my econ reading group and I wrote down roughly where I've landed. Before I embarass myself in front of them I want a consistency audit:

1. Sweatshop jobs are usually better than the workers' actual alternatives — subsistence farming, scavenging, worse local factories — so consumer boycotts and import bans that shut them down make the world's poor worse off, and are wrong for that reason.
2. A deal can be mutually beneficial, voluntary on both sides, and still exploitative: if I use someone's desperation to grab nearly all the gains from our transaction, I'm wronging them even though the deal leaves them better off. Sweatshop wages are exploitative in this sense.
3. Price gouging after a hurricane — $20 cases of water, $800 generators — is fine, or at least nothing to condemn. The price spike is just the system calling in supply, and the people paying it are still better off than they'd be staring at empty shelves.
4. What makes a deal between competent adults morally legitimate is that both parties actually consent to it. If the worker takes the job knowing the wage and the conditions, the deal is clean, and third parties who will never have to live her life don't get to second-guess it.
5. A company that opens a sweatshop in a poor country is doing something morally better than a company that could, but refuses to operate there at all — at least the first one shows up and interacts.

Which of these genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do? Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each give me the cheapest way out — the smallest edit that dissolves it and what that edit costs me.
