Write the minutes of a fictional meeting of the Standing Committee on the Shape of the Past, convened sometime in the mid-nineteenth century, at which a handful of historians finalize the division of European history into Antiquity, the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the modern age. The comedy should come from the decisions being made for recognizably bad reasons that nonetheless produce the real, familiar scheme: the middle period defined entirely by what it sits between, its name settled on because no one in the room can say what it was for; a Swiss delegate, fresh from an Italian sabbatical, successfully insisting that the rebirth be located in Florence and dated to suit his lecture course; a junior member's objection that the allegedly dark centuries contain universities, eyeglasses, and sophisticated optics tabled for lack of time; a motion on whether the Scientific Revolution was an event or three hundred years of bookkeeping deferred to a subcommittee that never reports back. Roughly 600 words, in deadpan bureaucratic prose, complete with the secretary's footnotes. The minutes should let a historian feel every standard charge against periodization -- that the labels were coined by partisans and inherited uncritically, that the boundaries saw through real continuities, that the schema flatters whoever drew it -- without anyone in the room ever noticing that anything questionable has occurred.
