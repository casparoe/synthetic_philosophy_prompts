I got into a long argument with my coworker last week about AI and it's been bugging me ever since, mostly because I think we were both just pattern-matching off stuff we read on substacks. His position: AI is basically like nuclear weapons. A handful of labs are racing toward something world-changing, nobody fully controls what they're building, and the lesson of the 20th century is that when a technology like that appears you need treaties, test bans, inspectors, non-proliferation regimes — or you get catastrophe. So frontier AI should be handled like fissile material: tracked, licensed, locked down. My position (sort of, I was winging it): AI is more like the printing press. Gutenberg's thing also terrified the authorities, destabilized institutions, flooded the world with what the establishment considered dangerous misinformation, arguably helped kick off a century of religious wars — and the places that tried to license and restrict presses mostly just entrenched whoever got to hold the licenses. The societies that let the technology spread are the ones that benefited. So regulating AI like a weapon would just hand control to governments and incumbents and we'd lose the good parts.

The thing I realized afterwards is that both analogies were doing a huge amount of the arguing and neither of us ever examined them. Can you audit both? What I'd like is the explicit mapping for each: in the nuke one, what plays the role of the fissile material, the warhead, the missile-gap panic, the test ban, the weapons inspectors? In the press one, what plays the role of the press itself, the pamphlets, the church's licensing regime, the newly literate public? Then the breakdowns. I noticed a couple myself — nukes don't improve themselves or copy themselves through software updates, and printing presses never wrote the pamphlets — but I assume there are more important disanalogies I'm not seeing, and I'd like to know which ones actually matter for the argument versus which are just trivia. Also, what is each analogy quietly smuggling in? My hunch is the nuke framing smuggles in the assumption that the danger is concentrated state-scale power, and the press framing smuggles in the assumption that the real danger is gatekeeping, but spell that out properly.

And then the actual question. My coworker thinks the nuke analogy supports strict licensing of frontier labs; I argued the press analogy supports keeping things open and distributed. Once the disanalogies are on the table, do either of those conclusions survive, or were the analogies just decorative — carrying conclusions we each already wanted to reach? If you think there's a better historical analogy (electricity, cars, aviation, whatever), say which and why, but mostly I want to know whether either of these two earns its keep.
