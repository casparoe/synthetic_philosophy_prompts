Some background: I'm a self-taught ML engineer (no degree, I just write code for a living) and recently I fell down a rabbit hole reading about how psychologists think people store concepts, because it maps wierdly well onto stuff I do at work. As far as I can tell there are three rival stories:

1. Prototype theory: the mind keeps one summary per category, roughly an average or best-case member, and you categorize new things by closeness to the summary. Explains why a robin feels like a better bird than a penguin.

2. Exemplar theory: there is no summary. You just remember a heap of actual past instances and categorize new things by similarity to the heap.

3. Theory theory (terrible name btw): concepts aren't about similarity at all, they're chunks of an informal causal theory of the world. You know what a bird is through what you believe about how birds work -- growth, reproduction, insides -- not through resemblance to anything.

Here's why I care: in my vocabulary all three of these are just engineering options. Prototype = class centroid in embedding space. Exemplar = k-NN / retrieval over stored examples. Theory theory = a learned generative or causal model, like a little simulator. So I want the whole debate restated in the vocabulary I actually use -- embeddings, centroids, nearest-neighbor retrieval, fine-tuning vs in-context learning, generative models, training distributions, loss functions. As faithfully as you can, term by term where that works.

Then the part I actually want: the residue report. What translates cleanly, what only translates with distortion, and what can't be said in ML-speak at all? Three things I've half-noticed but can't sort out myself:

- The psychologists treat these as rival hypotheses about what's literally inside one human head, and fight it out with reaction times and typicality ratings. But a single model can have a centroid head AND a retrieval head AND a generative model and just use whichever per task. Does "which one does the brain run" even survive translation, or does my vocabulary dissolve their disagreement?
- Apparently prototypes choke on combinations -- like "pet fish": your prototype pet is dog-ish, your prototype fish is trout-ish, but a pet fish is a goldfish, which resembles neither. Is there an analog of this problem for composing embeddings, or is the problem just an artifact of how prototype theory is defined?
- In ML, two systems can behave identically on every benchmark while being built completely differently inside, and we shrug and say functionally equivalent, open the weights if you care. Psychologists can't open the head. So maybe their whole fight is what my field would call underdetermined by behavioral data. Is that a genuine residue, or does it eat the entire debate?

And the meta question: where the translation fails, is that my vocabulary missing something true about minds, or is the psychology less substantive than it looks? Please actually pick a side instead of staying balanced.
