Second-year PhD student in history here, and I need help before I embarrass myself in front of my advisor. I wrote up an argument for our methods seminar that's supposed to defend a strong presentism about historical practice, and now I'm worried it proves way more than I want. Laying it out:

(1) Which historical questions get asked at all depends on present concerns. Nobody studies what nobody currently cares about; the archive gets mined where living interests point the flashlight.

(2) If the questions are selected by present concerns, then the standards for what counts as a good *answer* are also set by present concerns, since you can only evaluate an answer relative to what the questioner wanted to know.

(3) The only evidence available to us is whatever happens to have survived into the present -- documents, ruins, DNA, whatever. Evidence that didn't survive cannot constrain our claims at all, by definition.

(4) So what counts as a justified historical claim is fixed entirely by present interests plus present survivals. There's no third ingredient.

(5) Interests and survivals both vary across times and communities, and the past itself can't step in to correct us, because any "correction" would have to arrive through present evidence selected under present concerns.

(6) Therefore there's no standpoint-independent sense in which one account of, say, the causes of a revolution is better than another. "Getting the past right" just means satisfying the standards of whoever happens to be asking now.

(7) But then historiography has no more claim on truth than historical fiction, and the honest thing would be to admit we're writing novels with footnotes.

I cannot accept (7). My entire dissertation assumes otherwise, and also it just seems false -- Gibbon and a guy on a forum are not doing the same thing, even if both are "of their time". But every step looked innocent when I wrote it down. Seperately, I notice (5) has the same shape as skeptical arguments I'd reject in other domains (the evidence for claims about the deep past is present, but so is the evidence for claims about the far side of the moon, and I don't conclude lunar geology is fiction), so maybe the problem is there? Though I can't tell if that's the actual flaw or just an uncomfortable consequence I should learn to live with.

If forced to choose, I'd most like to keep (1) and (3) -- they seem almost definitional. Which step do I actually give up, and what does giving it up cost me? And if the argument is in fact valid, which premise is the cheapest to drop, and what does my seminar paper look like afterwards?
