I'm a PhD student in political theory and I keep going in circles on Locke-style acquisition, so I want to pressure-test my gut verdicts and see if any principle unifies them. Here they are:

- Picking berries on unowned common land and eating them: fine, obviously mine now.
- Clearing and fencing ten acres of unowned forest and farming it: fine, it's mine, and I think the fencing matters less than the clearing.
- Pouring a can of tomato juice into the ocean and claiming the ocean: absurd, no claim whatsoever.
- Finding the last unfrozen spring in a drought region, digging a proper well around it, and charging whatever I want: not fine, even though I genuinely did the digging labor.
- Mixing my labor with something already owned -- repainting a stranger's house while they're away: clearly I get nothing, and the house is still theirs.
- Discovering a naturally occurring gene sequence after years of lab work and patenting the sequence itself: I go back and forth, but I lean toward no, even though I'd grant a patent on a method that uses it.
- Occupying a long-abandoned, decaying house and fixing it up: mine in some morally relevant sense, despite someone holding title.

The pair I can't reconcile is the clearing case and the tomato juice case -- both are just mixing labor with an unowned thing, so whatever distinguishes them can't be labor-mixing alone, but I also can't make the berry case work without something labor-like doing the job. Is there a single principle of initial acquisition that gets all (or most) of these, and if not, which verdict should I drop first? Also tell me what it implies about squatting in a merely *vacant* but well-maintained house, since that's the case I'm dreading.
