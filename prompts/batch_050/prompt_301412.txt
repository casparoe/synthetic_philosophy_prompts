Question for my social choice seminar, we're two weeks into Sen's liberal paradox and I genuinely can't tell what the debate is about anymore. Every response to the theorem picks a different premise to blame: Nozick-style people say rights aren't the sort of thing a social ranking should be optimising over, so minimal liberalism was misstated from the start; other people say the real culprit is unrestricted domain and you should just exclude meddlesome or other-regarding preferences; and then there's the game-form crowd who say Sen modelled rights incorrectly in the first place, so the theorem doesn't even apply to actual rights as people exercise them. What strikes me is that none of these camps argue with each other head-on — they each just present their fix and remark in passing that the other fixes miss the point. So: is this one substantive disagreement about what rights are, or three groups answering different questions under a single heading (roughly: what rights are, how to model collective choice, which preferences are legit)? My working hypothesis is that the theorem itself is actually benign — it just says you can't have unrestricted domain + Pareto + minimal liberalism, full stop — and that the decades of debate are really philosophers and economists disagreeing about what a formal impossibility result can even tell us about politics. But maybe that's too dismissive. Diagnose the impasse using what the positions actually say, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a result, a changed question — or argue that none is coming.
