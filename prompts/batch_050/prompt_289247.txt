I'm an economist by training (standard micro plus monetary econ, the usual Menger-style story of money emerging from barter) and I'm in a reading group working through Aristotle's Politics. In Book I ch. 9 (1257a, Rackham translation) he writes:

"For the natural necessaries are not in every case readily portable; hence for the purpose of barter men made a mutual compact to give and accept some substance of such a sort as being itself a useful commodity was easy to handle in use for general life, iron for instance, silver and other metals, at the first stage defined merely by size and weight, but finally also by impressing on it a stamp in order that this might relieve them of having to measure it; for the stamp was put on as a token of the amount."

Restate this passage, plus the claim just before it that barter exists only "for the replenishment of natural self-sufficiency," in the vocabulary I'd reach for: double coincidence of wants, medium of exchange, commodity vs. token money, seignorage, transaction costs, spontaneous order vs. contractarian or chartalist accounts of money. Be precise, and don't just tell me Aristotle basically anticipated Menger, because I'm suspicious that he didn't. Then give me the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which you have to distort to fit (I suspect "mutual compact" is one — is that a genuinely chartalist commitment or just loose talk?), and which parts of his picture have no counterpart in the standard framework at all, like the normative ceiling on exchange baked into the self-sufficiency bit. For whatever ends up in the residue, say whether you think it's a real insight modern econ lost or just metaphysical baggage. Also say how confident you are in each piece of the translation, and flag the single mapping you're least sure about.
