In my graduate seminar on philosophy of mind we have just finished a unit on what is sometimes called the meta-problem of consciousness: not the question of why physical processing is accompanied by experience, but the question of why creatures like us say, judge, and confidently intuit that it is. I had hoped this reframing would be a way around the usual impasse, since explaining our reports and intuitions looks at first like a tractable, almost empirical project. Instead the seminar reproduced the original standoff almost exactly, and I would like your diagnosis of why.

The two camps, as I read them, are these. The illusionists hold that introspection delivers a compressed, schematic self-model, that our sense of a further explanandum is an artifact of that compression, and that once the mechanisms generating our phenomenal reports are explained, nothing remains. The phenomenal realists reply that the intuitions themselves -- the seeming obviousness that there is something it is like, the felt irresistibility of the explanatory gap -- are data, and that a purely functional story about why we produce such reports leaves the datum untouched, so that the meta-problem simply reinstates the hard problem one level up. Each side regards the other's central move as question-begging, and neither has moved in roughly a decade.

My working hypothesis is that the dispute is not really about consciousness at all but about the evidential status of intuitions: whether the seeming of phenomenality is a datum to be explained or an output to be explained away, and that this choice functions as a starting premise for which neither side can offer non-circular argument. A student pushed back with a point I could not settle: she noted that we can, in principle, test the relevant mechanisms -- there is real empirical work on introspective error, on dissociations between verbal report and judgment, on how self-models are constructed -- and that if, say, phenomenal reports turned out to dissociate cleanly from the intuitions they are supposed to express, one camp's position would become untenable on purely empirical grounds. On her view the impasse is temporary and conceals an ordinary empirical question.

Which diagnosis is right? Is this a disagreement about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for, a verbal dispute in which the parties use phrases like the explanandum in different senses, or a genuine empirical disagreement currently masked by a shortage of the right experiments? Please support the diagnosis with what each side actually says rather than a survey of positions, and tell me what would count as progress given your answer -- a conceptual distinction, a class of experiments, a formal model of introspective self-representation -- or, if you think no such progress should be expected, argue for that instead.
