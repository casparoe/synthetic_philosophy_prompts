My father had a stroke last spring and has since insisted that my mother has been replaced by an impostor — he grieves for her while she stands beside him. In trying to make sense of this, I've been reading about the two-factor theory of delusion and the dispute over whether delusions are beliefs at all, and I'd like a thought experiment that lets me probe both questions with a single case.

Construct a scenario to these specifications: (1) realistic and clinical, modeled on an acquired monothematic delusion like Capgras — no fictional drugs, implants, or other science-fiction devices; (2) it must contain two patients with the same anomalous experience, as near as neurology allows, only one of whom forms the delusion, so that whatever explains the difference has to be located in the second factor; (3) the deluded patient must sincerely assert the delusional content and reason from it when asked, while their actions, habits, and emotional responses conflict with it — they do not search for the "real" person, alert the authorities, or otherwise behave as someone who believed such a thing would behave; (4) the scenario itself should be one paragraph.

Then demonstrate that the case works: state what the two-factor theory predicts for each patient and why; say what a one-factor, experience-first account must claim and where it strains; and adjudicate, on the best available functional criteria, whether the deluded patient's state counts as a belief, an imagining, or something in between, giving the strongest defense of each verdict. Finally, identify which single feature of the case does the work of separating the theories, what happens if that feature is removed, and the most damaging objection a one-factor theorist could press against the construction.
