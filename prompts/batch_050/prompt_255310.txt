I'm designing a unit on free will and cognition for an undergraduate philosophy of mind course (about 20 students, one 75-minute session), and I want to build it around sphexishness -- Dennett's term for the kind of rigid, mechanical behavior the digger wasp exhibits when it keeps dragging its prey to the burrow entrance and re-performing its inspection ritual no matter how many times the prey is moved a few inches away. The standard treatment makes students feel superior to the wasp and then vaguely uneasy, but I've never found a way to make them feel it from the inside. I want an activity or game in which participants adopt some strategy that works well under the initial rules, the rules then shift quietly or the environment is perturbed in a structured way, and at least some participants keep executing their strategy past the point where it has stopped making sense -- so that the class discovers, mid-activity, that they cannot easily tell whether their own continued behavior is intelligent policy-following or sphexish looping. Ideally the design should let different participants fall into rigidity to different degrees, so the debrief has real variation to work with rather than a uniform demonstration.

There's one twist I specifically want built in, because it's the part of the idea I find most philosophically loaded: the worry that flexibility is always only relative. Dennett's point, as I understand it, is that the wasp is rigid at one level while we're flexible at that level but presumably mechanical at some higher level -- every rule for when to abandon a rule is itself a rule, so a genuinely non-sphexish agent may be impossible in principle, not just in practice. I'd like the activity to engineer a second-order version of the trap: participants who successfully notice the first perturbation and adapt should then face a second change that punishes the very meta-strategy they used to adapt (for instance, adapting too quickly, or trusting the pattern of changes), so that the 'smart' students get caught at a higher level. The debrief should then be able to ask whether anyone in the room actually escaped sphexishness or merely exhibited it at a different order, and connect that to the free will question: if flexibility always bottoms out in some fixed policy, does the feeling of deliberative freedom track anything real?

Please give me a complete, runnable design: the game structure with concrete rules, materials, and roles (including any information asymmetries between participants or between participants and facilitator); a minute-by-minute timeline; exactly how and when the perturbations are introduced so the rigidity is revealed without the facilitator announcing it; and a debrief plan that maps specific moments in the activity onto the wasp case, the levels-of-flexibility point, and the connection to free will. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong -- participants who guess the trick immediately, participants who feel tricked and disengage, a room where everyone adapts instantly -- and how to handle each. Finally, be honest about which feature of sphexishness the activity cannot reproduce: the wasp presumably has no experience of looping at all, whereas my students will feel frustration or doubt, and I want the debrief to acknowledge rather than paper over what that difference means for the analogy.
