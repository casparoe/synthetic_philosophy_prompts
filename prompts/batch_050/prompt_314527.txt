I'm leading a graduate reading group in philosophy of time, and I've tried to reconstruct, in numbered steps, the line of thought Gödel developed out of his rotating-universe solutions of general relativity — universes in which matter has a net rotation, timelike curves loop back on themselves so that an event can be reached from itself by a future-directed path, and there is no global time function: no assignment of times to events that increases along every future-directed curve, and hence no objective succession of worldwide "nows." Gödel concluded that in such universes there is no objective lapse of time, and then argued that since these universes satisfy the same field equations as ours, differing only in how matter happens to be arranged, lapse is no objective feature of our universe either — it belongs to appearance, not to reality.

Here is my reconstruction:

(1) In rotating-universe solutions there is no global time function and therefore no objective lapse; any carving of those universes into successive "nows" is conventional or perspectival. I take this to follow directly from the geometry.

(2) Our universe is governed by the same fundamental laws as those solutions. Whether a universe is of the rotating kind turns on contingent facts — the distribution and state of motion of its matter — not on the laws.

(3) A feature a universe has or lacks depending only on contingent facts about its matter, with the laws held fixed, is not a feature of what time is in that universe; at most it is a local, contingent phenomenon, on a par with the direction of "down," which depends on where the nearby mass happens to sit.

(4) What time has only locally and contingently, it does not have objectively. An objective feature of time must belong to time as such; a feature that can be switched on or off by rearranging matter while the laws stay fixed belongs instead to the circumstances observers find themselves in — to how time appears to them, not to time itself.

(5) So in our universe, too, the lapse of time is not objective; time as we experience it is ideal, a feature of appearance rather than reality.

The conclusion unsettles me, but what really makes me suspect the argument proves too much is that the schema seems to generalize. Whether our universe has a thermodynamic arrow depends on the contingent fact that it began in an extremely low-entropy state; whether there are galaxies depends on contingent initial conditions; so by parallel reasoning neither the arrow nor the galaxies are objective or real. Applied to everything not fixed by the laws alone, the schema yields that only the laws are real and all else is appearance, which I cannot accept.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Please name the step you reject and explain why it looked compelling — especially why it looks compelling given that in the rotating universes the failure of lapse is genuine. Then price the alternatives. If I instead hold that lapse is real but contingent, like the arrow presumably is, what exactly remains of Gödel's conclusion, and does anything of philosophical interest survive? If I keep the step that forces the idealist conclusion and bite the bullet elsewhere, what am I committed to saying about the arrow and the galaxies? If forced to choose, I'd rather keep the reality of the thermodynamic arrow than keep the demand that the real be underwritten by the laws alone — but I want to know what that choice costs. One thing I can't settle myself: does the argument require that rotating universes be physically possible under our actual laws and constants, or does the mere mathematical existence of the solutions do the work — and does the answer change where the argument breaks?
