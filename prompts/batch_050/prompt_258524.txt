I’ve been chewing over a Parfit-style teletransporter variant that seems to isolate something weird. A scanner records every psychologically relevant detail of your brain and then destroys your body. The file is sent to Mars and Titan, and both stations assemble a successor right up to the point just before consciousness. Neither successor has any experience yet. A quantum randomiser then selects one station; that successor is activated, while the other is destroyed one second before the selected person first becomes conscious. So there is never a moment with two conscious successors, and perhaps the unactivated one was never a person at all.

The activated successor has your memories, personality, intentions, attachments, phobias, sense of humour, and so on, and the whole process is causally dependent on the scan of you. My question is whether that person preserves what matters in your survival: roughly, whether you have the same basic reason to anticipate their experiences and care about their future as you would after ordinary sleep, even if they are not strictly numerically identical to you.

My credence is about 0.55. A philosopher friend’s is about 0.9, because she thinks the never-conscious spare is irrelevant. Give me your own credence for the exact proposition above, but decompose it rather than just giving a number. Include at least: the probability that numerical identity and what matters in survival can come apart; the probability that reliable psychological continuity is enough in an ordinary one-successor teletransporter; the probability that a merely pre-conscious duplicate changes the result; and the probability that the case has no determinate answer because our concept of a person breaks down. Say roughly how these combine, without pretending they are independent. Then identify which part of the decomposition your final number is most sensitive to, where my friend and I most likely disagree, and what argument or thought experiment would move your number most. Please answer in British English.
