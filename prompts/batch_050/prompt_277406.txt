I am a high school student writing an essay on so-called 'transformative experiences': experiences that change what you value and whose character you cannot imagine in advance. The stock examples are having a first child, losing one's sight, or emigrating permanently. A classmate presented me with the following argument, which I suspect proves too much, and I would like a proper diagnosis of it.

(1) A decision is rational only if you can weigh the value of its possible outcomes in advance. (2) You can weigh an outcome only if you can at least roughly imagine what it would be like and how you would respond to it. (3) Transformative experiences alter your preferences and your perspective in ways that cannot be imagined beforehand. (4) Therefore, decisions that hinge on transformative experiences cannot be made rationally. (5) Nearly all consequential life decisions — choosing a career, marrying, moving abroad, having children — are transformative in this sense. (6) Therefore, nearly all consequential life decisions cannot be made rationally.

The conclusion strikes me as absurd: people plainly make these decisions well or badly, and we criticise some choices as rash and praise others as thoughtful. Yet each premise looks defensible on its own, and I cannot locate the step to reject. If I must choose, I would rather retain (1) than accept that such decisions are simply arational. Please identify where the argument goes wrong, if it does; explain why the faulty step appeared plausible; and price the alternatives — that is, say what we would have to give up if we rejected each candidate premise instead. Please write your answer in British English.
