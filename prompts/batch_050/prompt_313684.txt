At a workshop on interpretability, I listened to two researchers argue past each other for nearly an hour, and I would like an outside verdict. The exchange, reconstructed as faithfully as I can manage, went roughly as follows.

Maya opened by arguing that recent interpretability work licenses straightforward realism about representations in neural networks: sparse autoencoders recover directions in activation space that correspond to human-recognizable concepts, and ablating or clamping those directions changes model behavior in predictable, specific ways. Something that is causally efficacious, stably recoverable across training runs, and even shared across differently trained models, she said, is not something we invented — it is something we found.

Dan replied that causal efficacy is doing less work than she thinks. Any invertible transformation of the activation space preserves all downstream causal structure, so the fact that a direction can be intervened on does not privilege it over infinitely many alternatives. The choice to decompose activations into sparse, human-interpretable directions reflects our prior commitment to our own conceptual scheme; a different decomposition would be equally compatible with everything the network does. The network computes a function, he said, but calling the components of our favored description of that computation "representations" is projecting our ontology onto linear algebra.

Maya's response was that this underestimates the theory-ladenness of the discovery itself. The decomposition is not arbitrary: the superposition hypothesis predicted in advance that features would be stored non-orthogonally and approximately sparsely, and that prediction was borne out. Polysemantic neurons were a surprising empirical finding, not something anyone imposed. Moreover, the network itself is the primary consumer of these directions — downstream weights read and transform them regardless of whether any human has labeled them — so the "we are the only interpreters" objection mislocates where the interpretation happens.

Dan conceded that the network uses directions in activation space but denied that this closes the gap. Using a direction is not representing with it; a representation, as opposed to a causal relay, requires that something treat it as standing for something, and the only agents doing that in this picture are the researchers. He added that the claimed universality of features across models is evidence for his side, not hers: models trained on similar data with similar architectures will compress similar statistics, so convergence shows only that the compression targets are shared, not that any particular carving of activation space is the network's own.

Maya then pressed on falsifiability: if his deflationism is an empirical thesis rather than a semantic preference, it should predict differently from realism somewhere, and she doubted it did.

Dan said it does: realism about features predicts that as dictionaries are scaled up, the decomposition should stabilize on a canonical set — the network's actual vocabulary — whereas his view predicts indefinite fragmentation, with features splitting into ever finer, ever less interpretable sub-features with no natural stopping point, which he took to be what the scaling results so far actually show. Maya replied that fragmentation is exactly what realists should expect if features are compositional, so the observation doesn't discriminate between the views after all, and there they stalled.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six or so steps above, say who has the better of it and why, flagging any point where one of them concedes too much, overclaims, or quietly shifts the question. Then address the final standoff directly: does feature-splitting-under-scaling actually discriminate between realism and deflationism, or can both sides absorb it equally well? Finally, say who is more right overall, and identify the load-bearing premise — about what a representation is, or about what would count as discovering rather than imposing structure — on which the whole dispute turns.
