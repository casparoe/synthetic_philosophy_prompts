I'm giving a 20-minute talk next month at our university's economic history seminar and I want help prepping for the Q&A. My thesis: the classic Great Divergence question -- "why did industrialization happen in Britain rather than China?" (the Needham question, basically) -- is badly posed, and the field should stop organizing itself around it.

My argument, roughly:

1. The question presupposes that China was on the same developmental track as Europe and just stalled or got stuck, i.e. it treats the European path as the default trajectory and China as the deviation that needs explaining. That's teleology dressed up as a neutral why-question.
2. The unit of analysis is rigged. Comparing "Britain" (a small island) with "China" (a continent-sized empire) guarantees an apples-to-oranges comparison, and when people fix this by comparing Britain to the Yangzi Delta instead, the divergence shrinks to the late 18th century and largely comes down to contingent factors like where the coal happened to be and access to New World land and resources -- at which point there's not much of a deep question left to ask.
3. It conflates "why did an industrial breakthrough happen somewhere" (a genuine question about the world) with "why did it happen here specifically" (which may be mostly chance -- like asking why the lottery winner was that particular guy).

My positive claim: replace the one big why-Britain question with a set of smaller comparative questions (factor prices, energy costs, state capacity, scientific institutions, etc.) that don't presuppose a single race with a single finish line.

The audience, about 15 people: most importantly, a senior economic historian who is broadly California School -- Pomeranz's work is basically her bible, she's published on the Yangzi Delta comparison herself, and she'll hear point 2 as attacking her life's work even though I'm partly borrowing from her. Then an institutionalist in the Acemoglu-Robinson mold who thinks inclusive vs. extractive institutions explain the divergence and will say my contingency framing throws away the one variable that actually generalizes. And a cliometrician postdoc who works on real-wage series and the high-wage-economy literature, who'll press me on whether the data even lets me say the divergence was late and shallow. Plus some grad students who mostly want to sound smart.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll come up, phrased the way each of these three would actually phrase them (the California School person will frame things in terms of reciprocal comparison and avoiding eurocentrism, the institutionalist in terms of what travels across cases, and so on). For each objection: how damaging is it really, what should I concede, what should I hold, and a 2-3 sentence answer I can deliver on the spot. And at the end, tell me the objection I'm secretly hoping nobody raises -- the weakest joint in my own argument -- and what my least-bad answer to it is.
