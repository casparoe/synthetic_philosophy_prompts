ok here's one i've been chewing on. say epicurus somehow ends up in the present day and learns about cryonics — people paying to have their heads or whole bodies frozen at death on the off chance future tech can revive them. he only gets the basic pitch: frozen at death, maybe revived centuries later, probably not. dont give him modern neuroscience, just the pitch.

what do you think his actual position would be? and don't just trot out his old line that death is nothing to us and call it a day, because cryonics pokes at real tension points in his view:

1. his whole argument is that death can't be bad for you because once you're dead there's no subject left to be harmed. but a frozen brain that later gets revived means the dead person exists again down the line. does that turn death into a mere gap, like sleep? and if the gap could last forever (revival fails), is the cryonicist then someone it *can* go badly for — which would seem to resurrect exactly the fear his philosophy was built to cure?

2. the lucretian symmetry move: we don't grieve the eternity before our birth, so why grieve the one after death. does cryonics break the symmetry? post-death time is now possibly *yours* in a way pre-birth time never was. or would he say the symmetry holds, since being frozen is just as nothing-to-you as the void before you existed?

3. the desire angle. he sorted desires into natural-and-necessary, natural-but-not-necessary, and vain. which box does the wish to be revived go in? does signing up for cryonics automatically out you as someone who failed his therapy and still fears death, or could a perfectly tranquil person buy it the way they'd buy insurance — zero anxiety, just a preference?

one more thing i'm curious about: epicureans are strict materialists, soul included, and identity for them is a matter of atoms and their arrangement. if freezing preserves the brain's structure but the actual matter degrades or gets swapped out over the centuries, does his metaphysics even let him count the revived person as *you*? does he have the resources to care about a pattern rather than the same stuff?

structure the answer like this:

- Bottom line: his verdict in 2-3 sentences, no hedging yet
- The main argument: ~250-300 words, the prediction built from his actual commitments
- The strongest pull the other way: ~150 words, steelman the case that cryonics could fit his philosophy fine
- What would flip it: ~100 words on which single consideration would most plausibly change your prediction, or where your reasoning feels shakiest

dont name-drop scholars or papers, just argue from his own ideas as best you understand them. if you're unsure whether some doctrine is really his versus a later epicurean spin, flag that in the last section.
