I'm a high school senior taking an elective on the philosophy of law, and for our final project each of us has to predict how our teacher would rule on a new hypothetical case, based on verdicts she has given on earlier hypotheticals throughout the term. The theme of the unit was when purely statistical evidence can justify a verdict. I wrote down her rulings carefully, and here they are. First, a pedestrian was sideswiped at night by a bus; 80 percent of the buses using that route belonged to one company, and there was no eyewitness, no camera footage, and nothing else tying that company to the accident. She ruled for the defendant. Second, a warehouse fire was caused by a forklift; 92 percent of the forklifts in the building belonged to one logistics firm; the firm had also shredded its shift logs after the fire, logs that would have shown exactly which of its drivers was on duty. She ruled for the plaintiff. Third, a cyclist was injured by a delivery van; only 60 percent of the vans in the area belonged to the defendant, but a credible eyewitness picked out its distinctive livery. She ruled for the plaintiff. Fourth, a criminal prosecution of a randomly selected concertgoer on the ground that 97 percent of the audience had entered without paying; the defendant had done nothing to conceal anything, and the organizers' records were simply lost in an ordinary IT failure. She ruled to acquit.

The new case we must decide is this: a jogger is knocked down on a street with no cameras (none were ever installed there) by an electric scooter that fled the scene. In that district, one rental company operates 19 of every 20 scooters, so there is a 95 percent chance its scooter was involved. The company cooperated fully and turned over everything it had; the relevant GPS data had been wiped by a citywide server outage that hit every company equally, so no one is at fault for the missing evidence. There is no eyewitness and no physical trace linking this particular company to the accident. The jogger sues the company in a civil action.

Please predict how our teacher would rule and defend the prediction as the one most consistent with her track record. In doing so, I'd like you to spell out at least two general principles that each fit all four of her earlier verdicts, show that they deliver opposite answers on the scooter case, and explain which principle better captures her overall pattern and why. Please also identify which of the four precedents is the closest analog to the scooter case, describe the further hypothetical case whose verdict would decisively distinguish your two candidate principles from each other, and state honestly what consideration or argument would change your mind about the prediction -- where your reading of her record is most likely to go wrong. Finally, please write your answer as continuous prose, the way you'd write a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
