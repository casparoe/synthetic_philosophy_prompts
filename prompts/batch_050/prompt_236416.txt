I am drafting an essay on whether karma can work as a theory of moral desert, and I keep encountering the same picture on both sides of the debate. Defenders reach for a bookkeeping analogy: karma is a ledger in which every deed is entered as credit or debit, good fortune and suffering are payments settling old entries, and the books always balance eventually. This, they say, shows that karma delivers perfect justice without a judge -- and, on a harsher reading, that the unfortunate are collecting on their own debts. A friend of mine turns the same ledger against the view: if suffering is repayment of karmic debt, then relieving someone's suffering merely postpones what the universe owes them, so compassion-driven aid is at best futile and at worst an interference with justice. Someone in my reading group has even defended the accrual of karmic interest, arguing that a debt left unpaid across lifetimes grows, which would explain why present suffering can vastly outrun anything the sufferer did in this life.

Audit this analogy for me. Begin with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the creditor, the debtor, the currency, the interest, the bookkeeper, and the final settlement. Then map the breakdowns. Three stress points interest me especially. First, the ledger presupposes that debts are fungible and quantifiable, which seems to import the claim that all wrongs and sufferings are commensurable -- can a theory of desert accept that? Second, financial debts attach to a persisting debtor, but classical karma is often paired with a doctrine of no-self and rebirth, so the party presented with the bill may be a fresh person; does the analogy survive that, or does it quietly assume the very continuing self it was meant to do without? Third, a ledger seems to require an accountant, whereas karma is usually advertised as an impersonal mechanism. Note also where the tradition itself complicates the audit -- the practice of transferring merit, for instance, seems to concede the transferability the ledger assumes.

For each disanalogy, say whether it defeats the conclusions the analogy is made to carry or is merely cosmetic. In particular, does my friend's non-intervention argument survive once the disanalogies are on the table -- and does the analogy in fact cut the other way, given that in finance a third party can simply pay a debt off? If the ledger is the wrong metaphor, propose the best replacement you can and say what it buys and what it costs. Finally, if you think my framing misdescribes how the analogy is actually used, or that one of my stress points rests on a confusion, say so plainly rather than auditing a picture nobody holds.
