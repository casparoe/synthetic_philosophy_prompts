I run a weekly discussion group for undergraduates who've taken one ethics course — 8 to 14 regulars, 75 minutes, no math background assumed. Design an activity that makes participants feel both horns of expected value fanaticism from the inside: first, that refusing to multiply tiny probabilities by enormous payoffs seems to require an indefensible discontinuity in one's values, and second, that actually following the multiplication through makes one exploitable by any sufficiently large promise. The session should work in two halves: an opening sequence of small-stakes choices (points or tokens, not money) in which participants individually trade away a modest certain gain for ever-longer-shot ever-larger prizes, calibrated so that nearly everyone keeps trading well past the point where they'd endorse their own behavior; then a group phase in which one participant is handed the power to offer others arbitrary hypothetical payoffs in exchange for their tokens — a mugging-by-numbers where the group discovers that anyone who kept trading in phase one has no principled way to refuse. The rules and payoff schedule need to be fully specified, including how to keep the probabilities concrete and checkable (dice, cards, a spinner) rather than verbal, since stated tiny probabilities invite disbelief. Then give me a debrief that maps specific moments onto the philosophical structure — where the continuity pressure came from, what it costs to impose a probability cutoff or discount extreme outcomes, and which responses (bounded utility, risk weighting, ignoring sufficiently small chances) the activity makes look attractive versus merely ad hoc. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I expect at least one participant to refuse all trades on principle and one to go bankrupt mugging themselves — and say which feature of the real debate the activity cannot reproduce and I have to supply in discussion.
