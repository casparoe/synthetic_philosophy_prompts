Before I commit to a position in the debate over norms of assertion, I want the specification first. Write the desiderata that any adequate account of the norm (or norms) governing assertion must meet: the phenomena it must explain or explain away, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the theoretical constraints it must respect. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether you take it to be non-negotiable (an account failing it is thereby inadequate) or negotiable (a cost a contender might rationally accept for compensating gains elsewhere).

The phenomena I have in mind include at least the following, though you should not treat the list as exhaustive: the felt impropriety of asserting lottery propositions one knows to be highly probable but not certain; the infelicity of Moore-paradoxical assertions of the form "p, but I do not know that p"; the fact that "How do you know?" functions as a standing challenge to an assertion in a way that "Why do you believe that?" arguably does not; unlucky assertions, where what is said is true and even believed on good evidence, yet the belief is knowledge-underminingly lucky; excused assertions, where what is said is false but responsibly believed; assertions made wholly on testimony the speaker has no independent way to check; and the norms of retraction — what an asserter owes her audience upon learning that her assertion was false or unsupported. Among the distinctions the account must respect: between the norm of assertion and whatever norm governs belief; between an assertion's being improper and the speaker's being blameworthy (in particular, whether the account may appeal to excuse, and what that move commits it to regarding primary versus derivative norms); and between a norm that partly constitutes the speech act and a rule that merely regulates it.

In addition, work through two constructed cases and derive a candidate desideratum from each, then judge whether it earns a place on the final list. First, a press secretary asserts only statements vetted by a research office whose vetting she knows to be excellent, without being in any position to vouch for the underlying evidence herself: is there a norm violation here, and if not, does the norm of assertion attach to the individual speaker or to the epistemic system standing behind her? Second, a speaker asserts something that complies with any plausible norm, but counterfactually she would have asserted the negation with equal confidence had the office vetted the negation instead — a kind of meta-level epistemic luck invisible at the level of the particular assertion. Can a norm of assertion even register this defect, and should an adequate account be required to?

Conclude with three things: (i) which single desideratum you judge excludes the most contenders, and why; (ii) any pair or triple of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, with the tension made explicit rather than merely gestured at; and (iii) a brief scorecard of the knowledge norm, the truth norm, and the (justified) belief norm against the final list — one or two sentences per contender, since the criteria, not the verdicts, are the deliverable.
