Got into it with my cousin over the holidays about whether my university is right to have formal rules against slurs and "demeaning speech" in the student code. His whole rebuttal was basically one line: sticks and stones may break my bones but words will never hurt me. Like, that's it, that's the argument -- if a slur can't break your arm, the administration has no business policing it, and people who run to the dean over an insult are doing something vaguely shameful.

The thing is, I feel like that proverb is smuggling in about four different claims at once and I want them pulled apart. What are the distinct precise theses it could be making? Some candidate directions I can half-see: something empirical about whether words can hurt at all; something about the hurt from words being under the hearer's control in a way a broken bone isn't; something about what a self-respecting person does with an insult (shrug it off or handle it yourself vs. escalate to authorities); something about what rules a community ought to have even granting that words do hurt. Lay the readings out as a menu, from the trivially true to the obviously false, and say for each one whether it's a platitude, defensible, or just wrong.

Then the part I actually care about: which of those readings, if any, would support his conclusion that the rules shouldn't exist? My suspicion is that the reading that makes the proverb clearly true doesn't get him anywhere, and the reading that gets him his conclusion is clearly false, but I want to see this done carefully rather than just assert it.

If it helps, I've seen the sociological framing where cultures are sorted into honor cultures (insults must be answered personally or you lose face), dignity cultures (your worth isn't up for grabs, so you ignore slights or settle them one-on-one), and victimhood cultures (you publicize the slight and appeal to third parties). The proverb seems to be pure dignity-culture advice, and my cousin was using it to sneer at victimhood culture. Feel free to use that frame to say which readings of the proverb each culture would endorse, and whether "dignity culture is just correct" follows from anything in the proverb itself.

One request on calibration: please flag which parts of your answer are settled (like, whether words can cause real psychological harm is presumably an empirical question with some actual evidence) and which parts are genuinely contested among people who study this stuff -- e.g. whether the three-cultures typology even carves reality well, or whether one culture type is morally better. Don't present a contested take as the consensus.
