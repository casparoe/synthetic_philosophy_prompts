Two friends of mine got into a surprisingly heated argument over dinner about the Newton–Leibniz priority dispute, and I need a referee.

(1) Maya said the whole fight was pointless: calculus was obviously going to be invented by *someone* within a decade or two either way, so the fact that it was invented twice just proves the individuals were interchangeable. Credit for discoveries like this is basically a lottery over who got there first by a few months, and we shouldn't build our picture of history around winners of that lottery.

(2) Dan pushed back: even if *that* calculus would be discovered was inevitable, *how* it was discovered wasn't. Leibniz's notation is the one we still use precisely because it was better, so the individuals shaped the discovery even if they didn't determine whether it happened. And being first still takes something -- someone has to actually do the work, inevitability doesn't write the proofs.

(3) Maya replied that he was conceding her point. The notation is packaging; the content is one and the same, which is why the two systems turned out to be equivalent. Her test: if Newton and Leibniz had both died in infancy we'd still have calculus today, but if Shakespeare had died in infancy there'd be no Hamlet. Science discovers things that are already there, which is why it keeps getting discovered twice.

(4) Dan's last move was to call her inevitability claim rigged. We only ever hear about the discoveries that happened *multiple times* -- nobody writes books about the brilliant idea that only one person ever had, so of course the famous cases look inevitable. Plus 'inevitable eventually' is cheap: if no Newton means a 60-year delay, that delay matters, and the person who prevents it deserves real credit.

Who's right about which point? Please go through the four moves and say where each one lands, and also tell me if there's something both of them are missing.
