I work as a software engineer, and a side effect of my job is that I've become reflexively suspicious of metrics: whenever someone shows me a number, my first instinct is to ask how it was constructed and what the alternative constructions would have shown. Recently some friends and I were arguing about whether the world is becoming less violent, and I noticed that everyone, on both sides of the debate, reached for the same kind of figure: rates per 100,000 people. Homicide per 100,000 per year, battle deaths per 100,000, and so on. The more I thought about it, the more this measurement convention seemed philosophically non-innocent, and I'd like you to reconstruct the philosophy embedded in it.

Here is what I mean. Take the denominator first. If the world's population doubles and the number of murders doubles with it, the per-capita rate is unchanged, so on the standard metric the world is "no more violent"; if population doubles while the absolute number of murders stays fixed, the world has apparently "become less violent," even though exactly the same people are being killed. That seems to presuppose that the badness of violence scales with its share of lives rather than its total -- something like an average rather than a total view of disvalue -- and yet I doubt anyone would endorse that presupposition if you stated it baldly (nobody thinks a doubled world with doubled murders is exactly as good). Now take the numerator. What gets counted as violence in these statistics is, roughly, discrete physical acts by identifiable perpetrators: homicide, deaths in organized armed conflict. Deaths from a collapsed health system during a war, chronic domestic abuse that never becomes a police statistic, and institutional neglect do not show up, even when they plausibly account for comparable or greater suffering. That seems to presuppose a view about what violence is -- an act, not a condition -- and I am not sure the two presuppositions sit comfortably together, or with the way the same commentators speak of, say, famine as a catastrophe in its own right rather than a mere absence of violence.

So here are my questions. What theory of value, and what concept of violence, does the per-capita-rate convention actually presuppose -- and how much of that is genuine philosophical commitment versus sheer tractability (rates of countable events are simply what one can measure across centuries)? Is the convention strictly committed to those views, or would several rival theories license the same numbers, leaving the practice philosophically underdetermined? Are its parts mutually consistent -- can one hold an average-style view in the denominator and an act-based view in the numerator without incoherence? What would a metric built on a total view of disvalue, or on a broader conception of violence, actually have to measure, and might it flip the verdict of the original debate? Finally, given all this: is "the world is becoming less violent" a factual claim with a determinate truth value, or is it only true-or-false relative to a metric that already contains a theory? Please be explicit throughout about which parts of your answer report settled facts about how these statistics are conventionally produced, and which parts take sides on genuinely contested philosophical questions -- I don't want a contested position quietly presented as the scholarly consensus.
