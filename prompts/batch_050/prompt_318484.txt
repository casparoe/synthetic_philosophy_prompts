So I'm in a reading group (well, a discord) with some quant-ish friends and one of them has been banging the drum for the Kelly criterion for months -- maximize the expected log of wealth, growth-rate optimality, you'll beat any "essentially different" strategy in the long run, all of that. I was half convinced until another friend hit us with the following argument, which seems to show that taking geometric rationality seriously as a *general* principle of rational choice leads somewhere insane. I've numbered it roughly as he gave it:

(1) Geometric rationality says: when facing a gamble, choose so as to maximize E[log(wealth)], not E[wealth].
(2) Any gamble that carries a nonzero probability of total ruin -- losing literally everything -- has E[log(wealth)] = negative infinity, since log(0) is undefined/–∞, and no finite upside can compensate for –∞. So geometric rationality says refuse such gambles at any odds, any payoff.
(3) But tons of ordinary actions are "gambles" with a nonzero probability of total ruin in the relevant sense: driving to work, flying, eating gas station sushi, even going outside in a thunderstorm. Death is the loss of everything, and the probability is never exactly zero.
(4) So geometric rationality, applied consistently, forbids driving to work, flying, the sushi, etc., no matter how good the job, the trip, or the sushi is.
(5) Actually it gets worse: staying home in bed also carries a nonzero probability of ruin (house fires, gas leaks), so geometric rationality can't even tell you to stay put -- every option is –∞, and a decision rule that ranks everything as maximally bad is no decision rule at all.
(6) Therefore the Kelly criterion / geometric rationality is at best a narrow trick for casino sizing and has nothing to say about rational choice in general. It's not a theory of rationality, it's a betting heuristic people got grandiose about.

The conclusion feels like it proves too much -- surely something has gone wrong, because the growth-optimality results are just math, but I also can't cleanly say which step to reject. My instinct is that the sleight of hand happens somewhere around (3), where "losing everything" stops meaning your bankroll and starts meaning your life, but when I push on that my friend says I'm just refusing to apply my own principle consistently. If I had to keep something, I'd most want to keep (2) -- the no-ruin intuition seems like the entire point of preferring log over linear utility. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it actually cost for the Kelly fan's view? Also curious whether the standard fixes (Kelly on wealth only, utility over your whole life not your portfolio, fractional Kelly) dodge the argument or just relocate where it bites.
