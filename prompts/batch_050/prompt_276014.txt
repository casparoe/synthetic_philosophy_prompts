So I've been in an epistemology reading group for about two years now and the testimony debate has basically frozen solid. The reductionists keep saying you need positive, non-testimonial reasons to trust a speaker -- you've got to have some inductive basis, like a track record of reliability that you verified yourself. The anti-reductionists keep saying no, testimony is a basic source of justification on par with perception or memory, and you're entitled to believe what you're told unless you have specific defeaters. Everyone knows the standard moves by heart at this point. Reductionists wheel out the gullibility worry and the obvious fact that some testimony is worthless. Anti-reductionists wheel out children, who learn language and basically everything else through testimony long before they could possibly run any inductive check, plus the point that if you demand independent verification for everything you heard you end up in a skeptical hole almost as deep as Cartesian doubt. Then both sides say their case handles the other's example fine, and nothing happens.

My hypothesis, which I'm putting forward tentativly: the two sides aren't actually disagreeing about the same thing. The reductionists seem to be asking what a maximally careful, ideally self-sufficient knower would need, while the anti-reductionists are describing how knowledge actually works in a social species where cognition is distributed and no one can check anything themselves. If that's right the debate is really about what question epistemology is supposed to answer, and the surface fight about justification is a proxy war.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a genuine first-order dispute about the structure of epistemic justification? Is it a verbal dispute where 'justified' is doing different work for the two camps? Is there a buried empirical question -- about how much checking people really do, or whether testimonial belief-forming is reliable in the actual world -- that neither side can settle from the armchair? Or is it bedrock intuitions about autonomy vs. trust that just can't be argued for? I want a diagnosis that engages with what each side actually argues, not a textbook survey, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis -- a distinction that dissolves it, some result from developmental psych or social epistemology that would force movement, or an argument that we should expect it to stay stuck forever.
