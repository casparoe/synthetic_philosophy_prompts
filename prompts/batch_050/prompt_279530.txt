So I have zero philosophy training, I work in logistics, but I've gotten sucked into free will arguments mostly because my brother in law is a determinist and won't shut up about it at family dinners. I read a bunch online about Frankfurt cases (the setup where a guy decides to shoot someone on his own, but there's a neuroscientist lurking who would have forced him to do it if he'd shown any sign of deciding otherwise, and never actually has to step in). Everyone says this demolishes the idea that you're only morally responsible if you could have done otherwise, and then they just argue endlessly about whether the case actually works. But I realized I don't even know what I should be asking of a theory here. People pick one case, declare victory for their side, and move on.

So before I read anything else, write me the desiderata: what does a theory of moral responsiblity actually have to deliver to count as an adequate response to Frankfurt-style cases? For each requirement give me the case or intuition motivating it. Things I want the list to cover, from my own confused attempts to think about this: it has to say whether the counterfactual intervener matters at all, or whether a mindless machine -- or just the laws of physics in a deterministic universe -- does the exact same job as the neuroscientist, because if determinism is secretly a Frankfurt case then the example proves way too much. It has to deal with the objection I saw somewhere that in an indeterministic world there's always some tiny chance the agent does otherwise, and whether a 0.001% flicker of an alternative is enough to ground blame or whether that's a joke. It has to explain the difference between being *caused* to act and being *compelled* to act without just asserting the difference. And it has to handle a variant I came up with and haven't seen anywhere: the device only steps in if the shooter hesitates for more than three seconds. The decisive, impulsive shooter gets no alternative possibility whatsoever, not even a flicker, because he never hesitates. Is he suddenly off the hook just because he's decisive? That seems insane.

Then tell me which of these desiderata are genuine requirements and which are just one camp's prejudices, whether some subset of them looks jointly unsatisfiable (if the list itself is impossible that's the most interesting possible outcome to me), and which single requirement knocks out the most positions people actually hold. If you want, briefly run the main camps against the list at the end -- compatibilists who just drop the could-have-done-otherwise principle, the people who say what matters is the action coming from the agent's own character, and whoever's left -- but keep the scoring short, the requirements list is the actual deliverable. A table is fine for the scoring part.
