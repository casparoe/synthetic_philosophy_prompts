I've been roped into presenting at our department's grad-student reading group on population ethics, and instead of giving a talk I said I'd just write down where I've landed and let people attack it. Before I do that, I want to know if my position even hangs together. Here's the set: (1) The repugnant conclusion is false — for any population of people with excellent lives, there's no population of lives barely worth living, however enormous, that would be better. (2) Mere addition never makes things worse: if you add people with lives well worth living and nobody already existing is made worse off, the resulting world is at least as good. (3) "Better than, all things considered" is transitive — I'm not interested in views that give that up. (4) Inequality is bad in itself, so shifting welfare around to make everyone equally well off, without losing any welfare overall, doesn't make an outcome worse. (5) I'm broadly person-affecting about procreation: there's no duty to create happy people, but a couple who chooses to have a child they know will have a significantly worse life, when they could have had a different child with a much better one at no extra cost to anyone, acts wrongly — even though the worse-off child isn't harmed, since they wouldn't have existed otherwise. (6) Any acceptable axiology has to avoid the sadistic conclusion too: it can't turn out better to add a bunch of miserable lives than to add some number of flourishing ones.

When I ran this past a friend she said "that's basically the standard package, you're fine," but she couldn't tell me *why* it's fine, and I've read enough to know there are impossibility results floating around this area, so I'm suspicious. What I want is an actual audit, not a survey of the literature: go through my commitments and tell me which subsets are in genuine tension, which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection (I suspect at least one does — I think my friend was half-right), and rank the real ones by how deep the problem goes. For each genuine tension, say exactly what follows from what, and what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs — including whether (6) blocks any of the easy ways out. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most of the set overall. Don't tell me which commitment you think I *should* drop first based on plausibility; I want the structural picture before I decide what I care about least. Please write in American English.
