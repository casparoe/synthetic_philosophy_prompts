I've recently been drawn into a reading group on the ethics of individual consumption, and I keep running into what the philosophers call the inefficacy objection: the worry that one person's choice (skipping a chicken dinner, buying an electric car, voting) makes no causal difference because markets and electorates are too coarse-grained to register a single unit, so a consequentialist can't condemn the choice. I'm fluent enough in the philosophical material to follow it, but I keep silently translating it into my own vocabulary, and I'd like you to do that translation out loud, systematically, so I can check whether my silent version is faithful.

Here's what I'd like. Take the main moves in this debate -- the basic objection that a single purchase triggers no additional supply; the expected-value reply (one purchase has a tiny probability of crossing a supply threshold, with a huge payoff conditional on crossing, so the expected harm is nonzero); the cases in the style of Parfit's harmless torturers, where many agents each make an imperceptible contribution to a collectively terrible outcome; the attempts to handle it with threshold or cooperative consequentialism; and the non-consequentialist responses that appeal to complicity or to what happens if everyone does likewise -- and restate each of them in the vocabulary I actually work in: payoff structures, Nash equilibria, mixed strategies over threshold events, marginal expected contribution, coordination and assurance games, ex ante versus ex post evaluation. My suspicion is that the complicity talk and the what-if-everyone talk don't survive translation -- that they're either covert claims about payoff structure or else not really claims about outcomes at all -- and I want to know whether that suspicion is right, or whether it just reveals that the game-theoretic frame can't express a kind of normativity that isn't about equilibria or expectations. Conversely, if the expected-value reply turns out to be trivially correct in my framework (it's just a point about probability mass at a discontinuity), tell me whether the philosophers are belaboring something simple or whether my framework is quietly assuming something they're entitled to contest.
