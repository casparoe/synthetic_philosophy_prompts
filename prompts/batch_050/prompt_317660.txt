Write the client-facing prospectus of a fictional boutique consultancy, "Concordia Partners," whose entire business model rests on the Harsanyi doctrine. The firm holds that all disagreement among rational people traces to differing priors, and it sells "prior alignment" engagements to executive teams on the promise that once a leadership group shares a common prior — and knows that it does — disputes about strategy, forecasts, and fault become literally impossible to sustain.

Constraints: a deadpan, glossy corporate voice throughout — earnest, bullet-pointed where a real prospectus would be, with no winking or overt jokes. The humor must come entirely from the firm taking the common prior assumption and its well-known consequences seriously and committing to whatever a business would actually have to do and claim if it believed them. To that end, include at least the following, woven into the prospectus rather than listed: a founding myth about the senior partner who proved, one afternoon, that the firm could discontinue all disagreement-resolution meetings; the firm's policy of dissolving clients' internal prediction markets and prohibiting side bets between "aligned" employees, on the grounds that no wager can be mutually expected to profit; the handling of a client executive who keeps dissenting from her aligned CEO, treated as a case of concealment or irrationality requiring further alignment sessions rather than as evidence of a legitimate difference; a testimonial from a client whose board meetings now run four minutes; an FAQ entry explaining why new hires must disclose their priors during onboarding; and a troubleshooting appendix on "posterior drift."

The satire should land the standard objections to the assumption — that the common prior does the explanatory work it is invoked to avoid, that persistent real-world disagreement seems untouched by it, and that common knowledge of everyone's rationality and posteriors is an extraordinary premise on which to build anything — without ever naming, quoting, or stating those objections. A specialist in game theory or formal epistemology should be able to recognize every commitment as a faithful consequence of the doctrine, drawn out with a straight face. No more than 700 words.
