I have no academic background, but I read a fair amount about climate policy, and after a debate with my brother-in-law I tried to write down the "moral hazard" objection to solar geoengineering as a tidy argument. It seems to prove too much, and I cannot locate the error.

(1) A protective measure ought to be opposed if making it available weakens people's motivation to prevent the harm it protects against.
(2) A ready-to-deploy solar geoengineering capability would weaken governments' motivation to cut emissions, since it would offer a cheaper-looking fallback.
(3) Therefore, developing that capability ought to be opposed.
(4) Premise (1) is perfectly general, though. Seatbelts weaken the motivation to drive carefully; smoke alarms weaken the motivation to prevent fires; flood insurance weakens the motivation not to build on floodplains.
(5) So by (1), we ought to oppose seatbelts, smoke alarms, and flood insurance.
(6) That is absurd, so (1) must be false, and moral-hazard reasoning is never valid.
(7) But (6) also seems wrong: surely it is sometimes a genuine objection that a safety net encourages recklessness -- people say exactly this about bank bailouts.

So my argument appears to prove too much twice over: first it condemns seatbelts, and then it condemns the very idea of moral hazard. If forced to choose, I would rather keep the objection to geoengineering in (3) than keep the blanket principle in (1), but I suspect the real flaw lies in the move from (3) to (5). Which step should I reject, why did it look plausible, and what does rejecting it cost me? In particular, is there a principled difference between geoengineering and the other cases -- I keep hearing about termination shock, and about the fact that a single country could deploy this alone and expose everyone to the risk -- or am I just special-pleading against a technology I happen to dislike?
