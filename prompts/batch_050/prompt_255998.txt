I run a graduate reading group on AI alignment (8–10 participants, one 90-minute session) and we've just finished a unit on iterated amplification and distillation: the proposal to train a capable, aligned system by having a human overseer recursively delegate questions to faster, shallower copies of itself (amplification), then train a streamlined model to imitate the amplified system's input-output behavior (distillation), and iterate. My group understands the scheme abstractly but treats the two central worries -- that decomposition might destroy competence on hard tasks, and that distillation might silently drop whatever made the amplified system aligned -- as purely theoretical. I'd like an activity that makes them feel both worries in their own performance before we name them.

Design a runnable in-room simulation with the following structure and constraints:

- Participants themselves play the roles of overseer, sub-agents (copies with restricted context, restricted time, and no memory between calls), and later a distilled successor that may only learn from transcripts of question-answer pairs, never from the overseer's reasoning. Write out the actual rules: what each role can see, say, and write down; turn limits; how queries get passed; what counts as an answer.
- Stage two tasks in sequence. First, a task with an objectively checkable answer that is hard to solve directly but decomposable (so the group can measure whether decomposition preserved or destroyed competence, and can see error compounding across delegation levels when it happens). Second, a task that is evaluative rather than checkable -- for instance, judging whether some plan or argument is acceptable -- designed so that value judgments must be smuggled into the decomposition choices themselves, so participants can watch the overseer's intent drift or leak as it passes through sub-agents who never see the whole question.
- Include a distillation round for each task: a fresh participant (or small team) trains only on the logged input-output pairs from the amplified runs and must then answer new, slightly-shifted queries. The design should make it likely -- not guaranteed -- that the distilled agent reproduces surface behavior while missing the reasoning that made the original answers acceptable.
- Give a timeline for the 90 minutes, materials needed (paper, envelopes, timer, etc.), and a scoring or comparison scheme that lets the group quantify the competence gap and the intent gap without real stakes.
- Provide a debrief guide that maps specific predictable moments in the activity (a badly decomposed subquestion, a sub-agent answering a different question than intended, the distilled agent confidently extrapolating) onto the concepts of amplification fidelity, error compounding, and distributional shift. The debrief should name the concepts only after participants have experienced them.
- Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first two times I run this (e.g., participants cheating by smuggling extra context into queries, tasks that turn out too easy to need decomposition, the distilled agent doing too well because the training set was hand-picked) and how to adjust the rules to prevent or exploit each failure.
- Finally, be frank about which features of real iterated amplification the simulation cannot reproduce -- for instance, that real sub-agents are literally identical copies rather than different people with different priors, and that real distillation is gradient descent rather than a human inferring patterns from transcripts -- and say what the facilitator must supply by hand to keep the analogy honest.

End your response with a final line beginning "Summary:" followed by one sentence stating the core pedagogical bet of the activity.
