At the policy research institute where I work, a senior economist defends our housing-market model with an analogy she presents as settling the matter. Maps, she writes, are useful precisely because they are unrealistic: a map reproducing every contour, building, and pothole at full fidelity would be useless, and every projection deliberately distorts something. Economic models are maps in this sense, she argues; assumptions like perfect information, rational expectations, and frictionless adjustment are a projection's distortions, and so criticizing a model's assumptions as unrealistic simply misses what a model is. What matters, on the standard instrumentalist position in economics associated with Milton Friedman, is predictive success. I am drafting a reply, and I want the analogy audited rather than dismissed, because I suspect it is doing real argumentative work for her and I want to know exactly how much it can carry.

Please do the following. (1) Lay out the analogy's mapping explicitly -- a table of correspondences is fine: territory to target system, scale and resolution to level of abstraction, projection to mathematical formalism, deliberate omission to idealizing assumption, navigational usefulness to predictive adequacy, and whatever else belongs on the list. (2) Separate the disanalogies that matter for her conclusion from the ones that are merely incidental. In particular, consider at least these candidates and say whether each cuts against her use: maps are checked against an independently surveyable territory, whereas the model's target is largely accessible only through data the model itself helps select; cartographers generally know what they omitted and why, whereas the live question about as-if assumptions is whether the omitted factor stays quiet when conditions change; maps guide movement through a fixed terrain, whereas the model is meant to guide interventions that alter the terrain; and the uselessness of a one-to-one map follows from the map's purpose, which may show only that some idealization is necessary, not that any particular unrealistic assumption is thereby licensed. (3) Distinguish a weak reading of the instrumentalist claim -- unrealism of assumptions is not in itself a defect -- from a strong reading -- predictive success is the only relevant standard -- and say which of the two the map analogy could support even if fully granted. Test the strong reading against its best case, Friedman's expert billiards player, who plays as if solving the relevant equations without doing so, and say whether the map analogy covers that case too. (4) Distinguish three kinds of unrealistic assumption -- those that remove a factor known to be small and reintroducible, like friction in elementary mechanics; those that fix the domain of application; and those known to be false and never relaxed -- and ask whether the map analogy treats them alike and whether it should. (5) If the map analogy survives only in weakened form, propose a better analogy for what idealized models actually do -- a caricature, a wind-tunnel scale model, and a laboratory idealization are candidates, but choose your own if you prefer -- and state what norm for model assessment your preferred analogy would support.
