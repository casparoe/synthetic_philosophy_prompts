I sit on the historical commission of a mid-sized town, and our recurring headache is that every dispute over commemoration collapses into the same shouting match: one side wants to celebrate the past, one side wants to preserve it untouched, and one side wants to put up a plaque explaining why it was terrible. In a seminar years ago I encountered Nietzsche's three modes of history from the second Untimely Meditation -- monumental history, which keeps great exemplars alive to inspire action; antiquarian history, which preserves and honors the past simply because it is ours and we are its product; and critical history, which drags the past before a tribunal and condemns parts of it so the present can breathe. I have become convinced these are not three attitudes to be reconciled but three distinct functions with distinct pathologies, and that our commission's failures come from never deciding which function a given case calls for. Please turn Nietzsche's taxonomy into a decision procedure my six-member commission could actually run on any commemoration proposal that comes before us: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, how to detect when a mode is being deployed past its proper limit (Nietzsche himself warns that each mode becomes destructive when it dominates -- monumental history falsifies the past into myth, antiquarian history mummifies everything equally, critical history severs the roots a community needs to live), and where the procedure must stop and hand the question to a vote because it is a matter of what the town wants to become rather than anything a framework can settle.

Some details to calibrate the procedure against: we handle roughly ten cases a year, our meetings are two hours, only two of us have any academic background in history, and our decisions can be overruled by the town council, so the procedure's outputs need to be defensible in a public meeting in plain language. The cases I have in mind for you to run it on as worked examples: (1) a proposal to restore and re-dedicate a dignified 1910 statue of a locally born industrialist who funded the library and also broke a miners' strike with hired armed guards; (2) a request to preserve a row of ugly, structurally sound 1960s workers' cottages that younger residents want demolished for parking; (3) a proposal for an annual civic festival celebrating the town's founding, which newer immigrant residents experience as exclusionary but which is many families' main source of civic pride. Please include at least one worked case where the procedure yields an outcome a reasonable commissioner would dislike, and be explicit about which distinctions in Nietzsche's account you had to flatten to make this usable, and what the procedure cannot do.
