Here was the exam question:

"Explain the multiple realizability argument against the mind-brain identity theory. What alternative view is it usually taken to motivate, and why? Does the argument show that mental states are not physical states at all? Defend your answer briefly."

And here's the student's answer:

"The identity theory, associated with Place and Smart, holds that mental states are identical to brain states -- for instance, that pain just is C-fibers firing. Putnam's multiple realizability argument points out that the same mental state can be realized by very different physical setups: humans feel pain via C-fibers, octopuses have a completely different kind of nervous system, and a silicon-based AI could in principle feel pain too. If pain can be realized by many different physical states, then pain cannot be identical to any one particular physical state, so the identity theory fails.

What the argument motivates instead is functionalism: mental states are defined by their causal role, not their material. Pain is whatever state is typically caused by tissue damage and in turn causes wincing, groaning, avoidance behavior, and so on. This is essentially a more sophisticated version of behaviorism, since both views define the mind in terms of inputs and outputs rather than what's going on inside.

The argument also shows that mental states are not physical states at all. If a mental state were a physical state, it would have to be some one specific physical state, and multiple realizability rules exactly that out.

One worry for functionalism, though, is liberalism: on a purely functional definition, something like the population of China simulating the functional organization of a brain (Block's example) would count as feeling pain, which seems absurd. So functionalism handles multiple realizability well, but maybe too well."
