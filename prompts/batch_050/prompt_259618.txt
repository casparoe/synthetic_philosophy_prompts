I'm a software engineer at a company that builds clinical decision-support tools, and I've been reading about machine ethics without any formal philosophy training. I keep running into the standard taxonomy: top-down systems that follow an explicitly encoded ethical theory or rule set, bottom-up systems that learn from human judgments (imitation learning over historical decisions, RLHF-style feedback), and hybrids of the two. My team has proposed treating agreement between a rules-based policy engine and a model trained on clinician behavior as a safety signal: if both recommend the same action, we ship the recommendation without human review. I suspect this double-counts correlated errors, and I want a thought experiment that demonstrates the failure mode crisply.

Construct a scenario in a clinical setting (triage, ICU bed allocation, organ allocation, a surge protocol -- your choice) meeting these specifications:

1. A top-down system following an explicitly written allocation policy and a bottom-up system trained on historical clinician decisions issue the same recommendation.
2. A top-down system that simply maximizes expected lives saved (or quality-adjusted life-years) issues the opposite recommendation.
3. The agreement in (1) must be overdetermined by two genuinely independent sources: the authors of the written policy must not have derived it from the very practice recorded in the training data, and the clinicians whose decisions form the training data must not have been applying the written policy (say, the data predates it or comes from a different institution). If one channel was simply copied from the other, the case is rigged by construction and proves nothing.
4. Everything must be realistic: no futuristic technology, no perfectly informed prognosticators, only the rough outcome probabilities hospitals actually work with. State the scenario itself in a single paragraph.

After the scenario, do the following: (a) walk through the reasoning each of the three systems would execute and state its verdict; (b) identify the single structural feature of the case that makes the utility-maximizer peel off from the other two; (c) show that removing that feature collapses the case back into unanimous agreement across all three systems; (d) give the strongest objection a defender of the utility-maximizer would raise against treating the case as evidence for the majority recommendation -- I expect it will be something about the two agreeing systems laundering the same human bias through two channels, but develop the argument properly rather than gesturing at it.

The point of the exercise is to determine whether "rules engine and learned model agree" can ever be evidence of correctness when both ultimately inherit human judgment, so the case only counts if the three verdicts fall out of how each system actually computes its answer, not from being stipulated.

End your response with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing a short phrase naming the structural feature identified in (b).
