I am a novelist, and I have stumbled into a dispute entirely of my own making, on which I would like serious advice.

Three books ago I ended a novel with its central figure, an aging sea captain, walking alone into a storm at night. The passage was written to be ambiguous about whether he came back, and I never settled the matter privately: there is no answer in my head or in my notes. My publisher, without consulting me, then ran a reader competition — guess the captain's fate, with a substantial prize for a correct answer. The entries split nearly evenly, and the publisher now wants me to say which entrants guessed right so the prize can be awarded. A further complication: while sorting my late mother's papers (she typed my early manuscripts), I found a notebook in her hand containing what looks like an outline I dictated to her around that time, in which the captain plainly drowns. I have no memory of dictating it, and nothing in the published text entails it.

I would like my questions treated as genuine rather than waved aside. Is there any fact of the matter about the captain's fate at all, or is the question like asking the maiden name of a character's mother when the book is silent on the subject? If there is no fact now, would my publicly announcing an answer create one — or would the announcement merely be one more claim, so that even I, the author, could turn out to be wrong about my own story? Does the notebook change anything? What exactly fixes what is the case in a fiction: the published words alone, the author's intentions at the time of writing, whatever the author says later, the conventions of the genre, what attentive readers are entitled to infer — or some mixture, and in what proportions? Finally, the practical question: what do I tell the publisher? If I declare both answers equally correct, I honor the ambiguity but seem to cheat the contestants; if I pick one, I seem to retroactively falsify half the entries for no reason beyond ending an awkward week.

I am not asking for a general essay on made-up people. I want the odd metaphysics of fictional truth — what it could even mean for something to be the case in a story, where such facts come from, and what authority, if any, an author keeps over them after publication — brought to bear on my actual predicament, with a concrete recommendation at the end.
