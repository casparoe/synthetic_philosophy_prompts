You are a philosophy instructor with fifteen years of experience designing discussion-based activities for undergraduate ethics courses, known for exercises that make students encounter a philosophical problem in their own choices before they ever hear it named.

I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on normative ethics (about 24 students, one 80-minute session). Midway through the term, I want to spend a class on John Taurek's argument that in rescue cases we should not simply save the greater number — that the five do not, merely by being five, acquire a claim against being left to die that outweighs the one person's claim, and that a fair coin flip may be as respectful as an automatic rescue of the many. Students will not have read Taurek before this session; that's deliberate. I want them to feel the pull of aggregation first and then be genuinely destabilized, not to watch a lecturer present the argument.

Design an in-class activity that does this. It should have two phases. In the first phase, students make repeated rescue-type decisions under rules that quietly reward counting heads, so that aggregation feels natural and unreflective. In the second phase, the structure should force them into a position where aggregating harms someone in a way they personally flinch at — ideally by making each student vulnerable to being the overridden minority, so the separateness of persons stops being an abstraction. Include at least one decision point that is not about life and death (small stakes, tokens or privileges) so that the debate about whether numbers count can be rerun at lower temperature afterward.

Think carefully before settling on a design: first brainstorm two or three candidate activity structures, note the main weakness of each, and then develop the strongest one in full detail. For the final design, give me:

1. Complete rules, written so I could hand them to students, including materials, the token/point scheme (no real money), how groups are formed, and exactly what choices are forced and in what order.
2. Timing for an 80-minute session, including how much slack to leave for the argument that will inevitably break out mid-game and whether to let it run or cut it off.
3. A debrief plan that maps specific moments in the activity onto the components of Taurek's position (the rejection of aggregation, the appeal to what each person may claim, the coin flip as equal respect), plus one or two questions that push students who emerge as confident aggregators and one or two for students who emerge as confident anti-aggregators — I want both camps argued with.
4. Predictable failure modes the first time this is run: what students will do to game the point scheme, where the analogy to a real rescue case will break down, and how to respond without lecturing.
5. One feature of Taurek's challenge that this activity structurally cannot reproduce, and how I should supply it in discussion instead.

End by telling me how confident you are that the second phase will actually produce the flinch I'm after rather than a room of students coolly applying expected-value reasoning, and single out the single design choice you are least sure about and what you'd watch for to tell whether it failed.
