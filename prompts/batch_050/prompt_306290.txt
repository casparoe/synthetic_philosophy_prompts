I did a module on international relations last year and the one idea that properly lodged in my head is the offence–defence balance: roughly, the thought that how jumpy and conflict-prone a system is depends on whether the technology of the day favours attackers or defenders. Offence-dominant worlds breed arms races and first strikes because striking first looks cheap and decisive; defence-dominant worlds are stable because everyone can sit tight behind their walls. And there's a subtler clause, which is that the perceived balance matters as much as the real one, because it's the perception that drives the racing. I was arguing about AI in the pub with some friends from the course and it occured to me this might map onto the open-weights debate, so I want you to try it properly.

The episode I have in mind is Meta and the Llama models in 2023. As I remember the sequence — flag it if I've garbled something — Meta first gave out the original LLaMA's weights under a restricted research licence, the weights leaked onto the open internet within a few weeks and were obviously un-recallable, and later that year Meta pivoted to openly releasing Llama 2, commercially usable and all, publicly defending this on the grounds that openness democratises capability and lets a global community find the flaws, while critics (including a couple of US senators, I think) argued that handing out downloadable near-frontier models is handing out the capability itself. So: run the theory over the case. Is open-sourcing frontier models offence-enhancing — uplift for malicious actors, misuse that outpaces any defence — or defence-enhancing — distributed scrutiny, safety work happening everywhere instead of in three labs, no single point of failure? Does the perception clause have an analogue, e.g. does open release change what rivals and adversaries believe they must race to build? And be honest about where the mapping snaps, because it must somewhere: the theory was built for states and armies, and here the 'weapon' is dual-use, copies itself at zero marginal cost, and the leak means the balance arguably can't be restored by any decision Meta took after February. Is the leak-then-release sequence exactly what the theory predicts, or an anomaly it has no vocabulary for?

End by running it the other way: what does this episode do to offence–defence theory itself — confirmation, an awkward case, or evidence it was never really about anything except great-power war? And since I don't want false confidence either way, finish with a short bit saying what would change your mind: which feature of the episode, if it turned out I'd misremembered it, or which empirical fact about actual misuse of open models, would most shift your overall verdict on where the balance lies.
