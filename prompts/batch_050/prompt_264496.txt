I'm a philosophy grad student drafting a paper on causation and I keep tripping over a configuration I can't find a proper name for. Please take on the role of a metaphysician of causation who's also fluent in the moral responsibility literature, and think carefully here -- maybe brainstorm the nearest candidate concepts first before commiting to a verdict, because I suspect the obvious ones don't quite fit.

Quick background so we're on the same page. Plenty of people accept causation by omission: the gardener who forgets to water the plants while you're away causes their death. And there's the well-known wrinkle that a total stranger across town also failed to water your plants but doesn't count as a cause, which pushes people toward norm-based accounts, where an omission only causes when it violates some duty or reasonable expectation. All fine, that's not my problem.

My problem is what happens when omissions get into the structures we normally discuss for actions. Two cases:

Case 1: Two lifeguards are on duty at the same pool, either one could have saved the drowning swimmer just by blowing a whistle and jumping in, neither does anything. Each one, asked later, shrugs and says the other was right there. With actions we'd call this kind of thing overdetermination -- two sufficient causes stepping on each other. But here the "causes" are two absences, and absences don't obviously crowd each other out of anything, since there was never going to be a causal process from either of them in the first place.

Case 2: The gardener fails to water the plants and they die. But it turns out a spiteful neighbour was watching, and if the gardener *had* watered them, the neighbour would have snuck over that night and poisoned them. So the gardener's failure makes no counterfactual difference to the outcome -- plants die either way -- and yet she's the one we blame, and in the ordinary sense her neglect killed the plants. With actions we'd call this preemption. But can one absence preempt another absence, or an absence be preempted by a merely possible action? Preemption is usually cashed out in terms of actual causal processes cutting off backup processes, and there are no processes here, just things that didn't happen.

So what I want to know: (1) Is there an established name for preemption- and overdetermination-style structures where the relata are absences rather than events? (2) Does the metaphysics of omissions matter to the answer -- i.e., if omissions aren't genuine events, do these structures even arise, or do they collapse into something else? (3) In Case 2, is the blame verdict actually tracking causation, or is it tracking duty-violation and just wearing causation's clothes -- and does the analogy with ordinary action-preemption break down at some specific point?

Please don't just toss me a label and stop. I want the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, which nearby concepts it isn't (ordinary preemption, ordinary overdetermination, collective inaction, norm-based omission accounts), and if nothing established fits, coin a term, define it, and give me boundary cases. Also, tell me what would change your mind -- which argument or case would push you to a different verdict on whether these cases involve genuine causation, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fall apart.
