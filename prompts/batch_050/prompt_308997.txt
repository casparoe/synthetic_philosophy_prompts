I have agreed to run a single session of an interdisciplinary undergraduate seminar (students mostly from literature, political theory, and design; none has read Gödel, Escher, Bach) on Hofstadter's notion of a tangled hierarchy and the inviolate level, using Escher's lithograph Drawing Hands as the central exhibit. They have all seen the image, and most have encountered feedback loops in some discipline, but none has a worked-out notion of levels of description.

Plan the explanatory arc rather than delivering the explanation itself. Specifically: what must be in place before the lithograph can do its work — I assume some usable distinction between a level and a hierarchy of levels, stated in the students' own terms — and why the ordering matters, i.e., which later moves depend on which earlier ones. What the carrying example must make vivid and what it must not smuggle in: the picture seems to me to work only if students see that two hands drawing one another is impossible in life yet coherent in the drawing, and that Escher's own hand, which drew both, stands outside the tangle — but I want this assessed, not assumed. The misconceptions I should expect, in the order they will surface (my guesses: that the image depicts a contradiction rather than a level-crossing within a representation; that any feedback loop — a thermostat, a market — is already a tangled hierarchy; that the inviolate level is just one more level which could itself be tangled, turtles all the way up; or the opposite error, that every tangle evaporates once its inviolate level is named), with the point in the sequence where each is best headed off before it hardens rather than corrected afterward. A check for understanding that would show the idea has landed — perhaps a fresh case, such as a legislature whose rules govern the changing of its rules, where students must locate the tangle and argue for a candidate inviolate level. And what to leave out at this level (Gödel numbering and the consciousness argument, presumably), stated as deferrals rather than secrets.

If any of my framing rests on a misreading of Hofstadter — for instance, if I have placed the inviolate level in the wrong relation to the tangle, or if Drawing Hands is the wrong exhibit for the point I want made — say so plainly and rebuild the plan on the corrected reading instead of accommodating my mistake politely.
