I'm a journalist, I cover science and big ideas for a general-audience magazine, and right now I'm working on a piece about how mathematicians decide what to believe *before* anyone has a proof. Last month I was at a small workshop on heuristic methods in number theory, and during a coffee break two people I'd interviewed got into an argument that went on for almost an hour. I reconstructed it from my notes and both of them ok'd my writeup (more or less). Now I want an adjudication for my fact-check column.

Quick background so the dispute makes sense: number theorists often guess at conjectures by pretending arithmetic events are independent random events -- e.g., treating divisibility by different primes like independent coin flips, or modeling the primes as a random sequence where a number near x is prime with probability about 1/log x. This "presume independence" move has a strong track record but some famous failures. Here's the argument. T is a senior number theorist, P is a philosopher of math who was visiting:

T: Look at the Mertens story. The running sum of the Möbius function was conjectured to stay within the square root of x. It survived every computation anyone could do for decades, and it fit a seductive random-walk picture of the Möbius values as coin flips. Then in the eighties it was disproved -- the real behavior is driven by the zeros of the zeta function, which an independence model structurally cannot see. That's my whole problem with these heuristics: they're most confident exactly where they're blind, because the interesting arithmetic is always in the correlations.

P: But notice what the field actually concluded. Nobody said "stop using heuristics." The model got *refined* to build in the zeta information, and the refined version makes sharp, checkable predictions. Same thing with primes in short intervals: a theorem in the eighties showed they don't distribute the way the naive random model predicts, and the response was to correct the model, not to abandon it -- and the corrected model fit the data. That's self-correction, which is exactly what you want a method to do.

T: Or it's unfalsifiability. Every failure gets absorbed: "we just hadn't noticed that constraint." Your rule -- presume independence except where there's a known obstruction -- can never lose, because any miss gets relabeled as an obstruction nobody knew about yet. And there's no principled way to say which constraints you were obligated to condition on *before* running the heuristic.

P: Sure there is: condition on everything locally computable -- all the congruence information. The corrections aren't ad hoc, they fall out of the same probability theory once you condition properly. And the parity problem in sieve theory is a case where the method flags its *own* blind spot: the heuristic tells you where heuristics fail. That's the opposite of unfalsifiable.

T: Funny, I'd run the history the other way. The celebrated successes -- the twin-prime constant, random-matrix models of the zeta zeros -- are successes about quantities we can't yet reach rigorously, so "the heuristic matched" and "the heuristic was right" are propping each other up. We tally hits where we can check and retrofit stories where we can't.

Okay, here's where you come in. Act as my expert fact-checker: a philosopher of mathematics who genuinely knows the number theory, not just the folklore. Think carefully and don't rush the verdict. First, spend a short section brainstorming the strongest version (steelman) of each side -- I want to see T and P at their best, not their worst. Then adjudicate point by point: (1) does the Mertens disproof discredit independence heuristics in general, or only that one independence assumption? (2) is "condition on all local information" a principled stopping rule or a patch? (3) does the self-correction after the short-intervals result count for the method or against it? (4) is T's survivorship-bias charge fair, and how would you even tally the track record honestly? Finally, tell me what BOTH of them are missing -- e.g., whether they're wrongly treating trust as all-or-nothing when the real question is how much a heuristic should move your credence, or whether there's a difference between heuristics as conjecture-generators and heuristics as evidence.

Write it so I could paraphrase you directly in the column -- plain language, no reading list or citations needed, and flag anything where the honest answer is "this one is genuinely unresolved."
