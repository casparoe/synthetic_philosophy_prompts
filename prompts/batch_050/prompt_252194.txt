I am a PhD student in philosophy working on the ethics of deception, and I am trying to anticipate how my dissertation supervisor will assess a case that has arisen in our department. Her verdicts on earlier cases, expressed in seminars and informal discussion, are as follows.

First case: a colleague she dislikes asks whether she can attend his farewell dinner. She plans to stay home, but replies that she already has a commitment that evening. She holds that this was mildly wrong but easily excusable — a routine social lie.

Second case: an applicant for a grant she administers states in the proposal that preliminary experiments have already been conducted, knowing they have not. She holds that this was seriously wrong.

Third case: asked by a funding officer whether the preliminary results support the hypothesis, a researcher replies, truthfully, that every experiment run so far has come out positive — having in fact run only the single experiment he was confident would succeed, and knowing the officer will take his answer as evidence of a robust research program. She holds that this was seriously wrong.

Fourth case: a student asks how she found a conference she has just returned from. She thought it was tedious and poorly organized but answers that the panels were very well attended, expecting the student to conclude that she enjoyed it. She holds that this was entirely permissible.

The new case is this. A colleague on a hiring committee asks her, in confidence, whether she ever had reason to doubt the integrity of a candidate's data during their past collaboration. She has in fact harbored well-founded doubts. She wants the candidate to be hired, and she is deciding between two answers: (a) a flat denial, or (b) the literally true statement that the collaboration was productive and the published work has held up well, crafted so that the colleague will conclude she has no concerns. Assume she is equally confident that either answer will produce the same false belief, and that the stakes are identical either way.

I have four questions. First, identify at least two general principles, each consistent with all four earlier verdicts, that she might implicitly be applying: for example, one on which the wrongness of a deceptive utterance is exhausted by the intent to deceive and the stakes for the deceived, so that lying and merely misleading are morally on a par when those are equal; and one on which a false assertion breaches a commitment to truthfulness that a literally true but misleading utterance does not, so that lying is worse even when the deception produced and the stakes are identical. Second, state what each principle predicts about the new case — in particular, whether (a) and (b) are equally wrong or whether (a) is worse than (b) — and identify which of the earlier verdicts is the closest analog under each reading. Third, give your best prediction of her actual comparative verdict, with reasons drawn from the pattern of her earlier judgments, including any verdict you take to be most diagnostic. Fourth, if you think the earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine the answer, say so, and describe the further case — ideally one differing from the new case in only one respect — whose verdict would discriminate between the two principles.
