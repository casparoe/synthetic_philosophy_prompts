Two people in my epistemology reading group have been going at it all year: one defends the KK principle (roughly, if you know that p then you're in a position to know that you know that p), the other is a Williamson-style anti-luminosity type who holds that no non-trivial mental condition is such that being in it guarantees you're in a position to know you're in it. They've finally agreed to write up the outcome as an adversarial collaboration and I said I'd spec the document. Draft it: a numbered section of claims both sides can actually sign (make these non-trivial — e.g. both should be able to accept that knowledge requires some margin for error in borderline cases, the fight is over what follows from that), then the exact residual disagreements, each worded so its own side would accept the wording, then the cruxes, and for each crux a case or argument both agree bares on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Two things they have to confront. First, the objection that the margin-for-error argument proves too much — that a parallel argument with slightly tweaked premises would show nobody knows anything gained through gradual discrimination at all, which would make the anti-luminosity result a lot less interesting. Second, a test case I'm handing them: a subject watches a light dim imperceptibly slowly and is asked "do you know you can see it?" every thirty seconds, and keeps answering yes the whole way down. Each side must commit, beforehand, to what that answer pattern shows and what it doesn't show. As a preliminary I also want them to settle whether KK is meant as a norm on knowers or a descriptive constraint on knowledge, since I suspect half their fight is just that.
