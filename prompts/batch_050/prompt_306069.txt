So I help run this informal poetry-and-philosophy night we do at the end of each term, where someone reads something short tied to whatever the grad reading group just finished. This semester the group did a unit on measurement and metrics, and we spent basically two whole sessions on Goodhart's law and specification gaming -- teaching to the test, citation counts, the nail factory that hits its tonnage quota by making one giant nail, chatbots optimizing for thumbs-up. I got voluntold to write this term's poem.

I want a villanelle, proper 19 lines, two refrains alternating and meeting at the end. But here's the constraint I actually care about: in a normal villanelle the refrains come back word for word, and I want mine to *drift*. The first refrain should return each time slightly altered -- a word swapped for a more impressive-sounding synonym, a qualifier dropped, the line getting punchier and less true -- because each time it comes back it's been revised in response to being *evaluated*. Maybe the poem's speaker is someone whose work keeps getting scored on some rubric, and the refrain is the thing they keep polishing to score well, so by the final quatrain it's become hollow or even means the opposite of what it started as. The second refrain can stay fixed, as the thing that was actually being measured all along, quietly unchanged while its proxy gets gamed. Someone who knows Goodhart should clock immediately why the refrains behave differently; someone who doesn't should just feel the uncanny wrongness of watching a line decay.

A few more asks: don't name Goodhart or say "metric" or "target" anywhere in the poem, the idea should be carried by the form and the imagery, not by name-dropping. Concrete imagery over abstraction (workshop floor, gradebook, whatever you like). And don't let the ending get cute or resolve into a tidy moral -- the last two lines, where the fixed refrain and the fully-corrupted refrain finally stand side by side, should just let the gap between them sit there. It's a room full of philosophers, they'll do the resolving themselves.
