Write a deadpan parody of a technical certification standard issued by the fictional Institute for Higher-Level Causal Efficacy. The standard, titled Standard 7.4: Certification of Genuine Symbolic Causation in Careenium-Based Systems, certifies whether a mind’s careenium—its internal arena of active symbols—contains “simmballs” that genuinely push the neural substrate around, rather than merely redescribing neural processes that would have occurred anyway.

For the purposes of the standard, a simmball is a stable, reusable symbol-token implemented in the brain: it can be activated, combined with other simmballs, inhibited, reinterpreted, and used to guide subsequent processing. The careenium is not an extra organ but the organized symbolic level at which these tokens appear to interact. The Institute’s inspectors must determine whether an agent’s thought that, say, “this argument begs the question” causes a neural and bodily trajectory in a way not fully capturable by the lower-level description, or whether the apparent symbolic agency is only a useful bookkeeping fiction.

The piece should be about 800 words and formatted as a formal standard with numbered sections, definitions, testing procedures, compliance grades, and an appendix of failure modes. Its style should imitate regulatory prose: precise, bureaucratic, cautiously qualified, and entirely serious. Possible provisions might include an intervention test requiring inspectors to alter a simmball without altering its neural realization, an exclusion-review panel for claims that higher-level causation competes with neuronal causation, a multiple-realizability waiver, and penalties for “unlicensed semantic clamping.”

The satire should target the discourse surrounding downward causation, causal exclusion, levels of explanation, and the temptation to treat a successful higher-level description as evidence of an additional causal force. Do not simply ridicule the idea that symbols are causally relevant: the strongest satire should acknowledge that symbolic states can be robust, explanatory, and indispensable while making comic trouble for the claim that they push the substrate in some extra, substrate-independent way. A specialist should recognize the real conceptual tensions; a general reader should still enjoy the absurdity of inspectors trying to issue a certificate to a thought.
