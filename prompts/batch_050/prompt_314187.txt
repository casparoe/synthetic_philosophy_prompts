I am taking a graduate seminar on the epistemology of testimony, and for my final paper I need to reconstruct the reductionism/anti-reductionism debate in a form that neither side could dismiss as a caricature. Please act as a senior philosopher of testimony who has spent a career mediating between these two camps, and think carefully through the positions before drafting. If it helps, begin by brainstorming -- privately or explicitly -- the strongest version of each view and the points where each tends to strawman the other, and only then write the deliverable.

The task: draft the joint statement that a committed reductionist about testimonial justification (in the spirit of Hume: testimony reduces to perception, memory, and inductive inference from observed speaker reliability) and a committed anti-reductionist (in the spirit of Reid: testimony is a basic source of justification, on a par with perception, requiring no independent positive reasons) could both sign after an extended adversarial collaboration.

Structure the document as follows:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. I want the genuine common ground, not platitudes -- for instance, what both sides accept about the empirical facts of how hearers actually vet speakers (monitoring for signs of incompetence or insincerity), about cases where testimony obviously fails to transmit justification, and about what an adequate theory must explain (children's acquisition of knowledge from testimony should be among the test cases, since each side typically accuses the other of mishandling it).

2. A disagreements section giving the precise statements of residual disagreement, each formulated so that the side whose view it describes would accept the wording. At minimum, state the disagreement over whether a hearer needs positive, non-testimonially-grounded reasons for trusting a speaker, and the disagreement over whether the justification testimonial belief enjoys is fundamentally transmitted from the speaker or generated in the hearer.

3. A cruxes section. For each crux, identify (a) the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one camp, and (b) a concrete case, empirical finding, or argument that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that case written down in advance -- the way an adversarial collaboration preregisters diverging predictions.

4. Finally, add a short section applying the joint framework to a case the classic literature did not anticipate: testimony-like outputs of large language models, which have no beliefs, intentions, or sincerity to assess. Have each camp state, in its own signed wording, whether and how such outputs can justify belief, and identify which crux this case bears on most directly. If the two camps converge here despite their disagreement elsewhere, say so explicitly and explain what that convergence reveals about the debate.

Throughout, flag any disagreement you conclude is merely verbal, and say why. Do not adjudicate the overall winner; the document's success condition is that both parties could put their names to it.
