Quick question growing out of a seminar I'm teaching on moral disagreement. One of my sharper students has latched onto the idea that moral compromise is basically just trade: when two sides disagree, each gives ground on the issue they care less about in exchange for ground on the one they care more about, and both walk away better off by their own lights -- gains from trade, same as swapping apples for oranges. She uses it to argue that being uncompromising in politics isn't principled, it's just irrational, like turning down a deal that would leave you better off. It's come up constantly this term, and honestly half the room nods along whenever she wheels it out. I've also seen colleagues use a version of it to justify working with people whose views they find genuinely appalling -- "think of it as a trade, not an endorsement."

I want to stress-test the analogy properly before I either adopt it or push back. Lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the goods, the price, the currency, the mutual gain, and (importantly) the option to just not trade? Then tell me where it breaks. A few things nagging at me: in a market, walking away is always on the table, but parties to a moral dispute are usually stuck inside one shared policy or institution, so there's no "no deal" baseline; selling something you own feels structurally different from doing something you believe is wrong -- when I concede on an issue, am I trading a good, or becoming complicit in something?; there's no common currency across moral values the way money lets you compare apples and oranges; and ordinary trades mostly affect the two traders, while moral "trades" often impose the costs on third parties (the people the policy actually harms) who were never at the table. Which of these are fatal to the analogy and which are just frictions a market could have too?

And then the payoff question: does the "compromise is a free lunch, refusing it is irrational" conclusion actually survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was it being smuggled in by the market picture the whole time? If the analogy works in some cases but not others, sort the cases for me -- and if there's a better comparison for the cases where it fails (a treaty between enemies? a hostage exchange? co-authorship?), sketch what that better analogy would be and what conclusion it would support instead.
