So I retired from the chemistry department three years ago and have since fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole, and now I've somehow agreed to host our town's little "big ideas" lecture series. Next month's guest is a political theorist whose new book argues that we should abolish elected legislators entirely and replace them with a sortition chamber -- a few hundred randomly selected citizens serving one short non-renewable term, with professional staff, and that's it, no elections for that body ever again.

Here's my problem: as an experimentalist, random sampling is my native language, and I'm worried I'll either fawn over the idea (finally, politics done like proper science!) or grill him about statistical power while missing the actual philosophy. I've been reading around and I can see the obvious objections -- that randomly picked amateurs will get captured by lobbyists or by the permanent staff who draft the agenda, that one term means no learning curve, that nobody can be voted out so there's no accountability, and that voters will never accept laws made by people nobody chose. But I assume he's heard all of these a thousand times and has canned answers.

Write me eight to ten interview questions that don't let him coast on the canned answers. Specifically I want:

- at least one question that grants him the sampling analogy and then presses on where it breaks down (a sample can be representative of opinions without being representative of judgment, or however that should go -- you tell me)
- one on what the point of elections actually is if it's not picking the best people -- consent? accountability? -- and whether sortition loses something real there
- one on the permanent staff problem, since in my lab the "rotating" postdocs basically did what the senior techs told them
- and each question should come with the follow-up I'll need when he gives his most likely reply, because he's smooth and I am not

Keep the tone friendly, it's a public event, not an inquistion.
