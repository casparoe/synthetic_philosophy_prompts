I am a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and I have been circulating an argument among my cohort that I now suspect proves too much. (1) A legitimate scientific explanation must be built from the bottom up out of mindless, algorithmic processes — cranes, in Dennett's sense — rather than positing ready-made intelligence or foresight, which would be a skyhook. (2) A posit counts as a skyhook whenever the theory employing it does not itself derive that posit's capacities from a lower-level mechanism. (3) Cognitive science explains behaviour by appeal to rational agents with beliefs, desires and goals, without deriving those capacities from neurochemistry; economics, and ordinary folk psychology, do likewise. (4) So all of these fields traffic in skyhooks. (5) Anything trafficking in skyhooks must either be reduced to mechanistic detail or eliminated as unscientific. (6) But (5) would abolish most of the special sciences, which is absurd. (7) Hence (1) is false: science is entitled to skyhooks after all. A colleague with intelligent-design sympathies is delighted by (7), which is precisely why I now distrust the whole chain, yet each step looked innocent when I wrote it down. Which premise or step should I reject, why did it look compelling, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, is there a way to fault the argument without conceding anything to the design theorist, or is his satisfaction a price I simply have to pay? Please answer in British English.
