Eight months ago my mother was diagnosed with early-stage dementia, and watching her personality shift as her brain changes has forced me to take seriously a claim I used to dismiss: that minds are simply what brains do, and that everything about us -- character, love, moral conviction -- is the accumulated product of mindless processes, with no spark or special ingredient added from above. Next month I am recording an episode of my philosophy podcast with a philosopher who defends exactly this view in its strongest form. Her position is that minds, morality, meaning, and consciousness are all adequately explained by what Dennett called cranes -- mindless, purpose-free, bottom-up mechanisms such as natural selection and cultural evolution -- and that every appeal to a skyhook, some miraculous top-down principle injected from outside nature, is either confusion or intellectual laziness. She also holds that accusations of greedy reductionism leveled at this program are almost always misplaced.

I do not want the episode to be her restating the view. Draft eight to ten interview questions that genuinely press on the architecture of her position, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. Specifically, I want the set to cover at least the following pressure points:

First, the base of the crane: natural selection presupposes reliable replication, so the standard account of the origin of life seems to require either an unexplained stroke of luck or something suspiciously like a skyhook at the foundation. If she answers that abiogenesis is ordinary chemistry, press her on whether mindless mechanism plus astronomical improbability is explanatorily different from a design hypothesis in any way that does not merely restate her own framework's commitments.

Second, the criterion problem: she claims there is a principled line between legitimate reduction and greedy reductionism. I want a question forcing her to state that criterion in advance, and a follow-up for the likely reply that greedy reductionism is reduction that skips explanatory levels or assumes tractability -- namely, whether that verdict is ever available before the reduction succeeds, making the criterion purely retrospective and therefore unfalsifiable in practice.

Third, normativity: if moral norms are crane-built products of cultural evolution, ask whether her view is committed to an error theory about moral truth, and prepare a follow-up for the probable response that moral norms are real in the same way institutions or money are real.

Fourth, consciousness: I want one question on whether the demand to explain why experience feels like anything at all is, on her view, itself a confused request for a skyhook, and a follow-up on why the intuition that something has been left out tends to survive even after every functional and evolutionary story is accepted.

Fifth, the case I actually care about: if selves are crane-built and continuously maintained by neural machinery, ask what, if anything, of normative significance persists when the machinery degrades -- whether the person my mother was exists anywhere now, and whether her view can say more than that this is a matter of pragmatic classification.

Finally, end with a question about what evidence could in principle count against the whole program -- what observation or result would force her to concede that some phenomenon genuinely requires a skyhook, and if she answers that nothing could, whether that concession damages the view's claim to be an empirical research program rather than a metaphilosophical stance.
