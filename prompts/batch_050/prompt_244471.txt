Some months ago I ended up in a long exchange in the comments under an article about whether a sufficiently advanced computer could, in principle, reproduce everything a human mind does when it does mathematics. I had argued that it could. Someone replied with a line I have since seen repeated in several places: "Gödel proved that there are truths that can never be proven — so mathematical reasoning will always outrun any machine that works by fixed rules." The conversation died there, because nobody, including me, could say exactly what was being claimed. I have no academic training, but I have thought about this on and off since, and I would like help turning that slogan into something precise.

What I want is a menu of the distinct claims the sentence could be making, with a verdict on each. Here are some of the readings I have half-formed on my own, to give a sense of the granularity I'm after:

First, there seems to be a modest reading: for any formal system of a certain kind (consistent, and strong enough to do basic arithmetic), there is a sentence that the system can neither prove nor disprove. Second, a stronger reading: some such sentences are not merely undecided by the system but actually true, so that truth outruns provability in that system. Third, a much stronger reading: there are truths that are unprovable absolutely — not just unprovable in this or that system, but unestablishable by any method whatsoever, including human reasoning. Fourth, something like: human mathematicians can see the truth of the very sentences the machine cannot prove, and therefore the human mind is not such a machine. There may also be a fifth, looser reading that people seem to gesture at: no body of knowledge can ever be complete, so science can never be finished.

For each of these (and for any readings I have missed), could you say: is it a theorem, a plausible philosophical claim, a confusion, or something in between? In particular I would like to understand where the words do the heavy lifting. "True" — true in what sense, and does that notion come with assumptions a skeptic about mathematics might refuse? "Provable" — provable in a fixed system, provable in any system, or knowable at all? And when someone says the human mind "can see" the truth the machine misses, does that observation itself rely on the human assuming something (like the system's consistency) that the machine could in principle assume too?

Finally, and this matters most to me: which of these readings, if any, would actually support the use the slogan was put to in my argument — namely, the conclusion that no rule-following computer could replicate human mathematical thought? If only the weak readings are theorems and only the strong readings support that conclusion, I would like to see that gap stated plainly, because I suspect the person I was arguing with was trading on it.
