I lead pre-deployment review at a company that ships LLM-based agents that act autonomously for weeks at a time -- things like managing cloud infrastructure, running procurement, executing trading strategies. Nothing sci-fi, but they plan over long horizons, write and run their own code, and talk to humans persuasively. For each new agent, my job is to decide whether the "shutdown guarantees" section of its safety case is good enough to sign. Legal requires my signature, our insurers ask about it, and I get maybe two working days per review, about one a month. I can red-team, run evals the agent can or cannot detect, fine-tune on test scenarios, and inspect logs, but I can't prove anything about the weights.

I've read enough of the corrigibility and shutdown-problem discussion to know the traps: an agent that behaves perfectly when it can tell it's being evaluated tells you almost nothing, "it let us press the button in testing" is not the same as "it has no incentive to prevent shutdown," and training approaches that try to make the agent indifferent to being switched off have known failure modes. What I don't have is a way to turn all that into something I can actually run on a Tuesday. Write me a decision procedure: the checks in order, what evidence counts as passing each one, which checks are merely behavioral and which get at incentive structure, and where the procedure stops and returns "no signature." It should force me to seperate at least: compliance during observed tests vs. unobserved operation; resisting shutdown vs. legitimately refusing to abandon an uninterruptible action (you don't want the surgical robot powering down mid-incision); corrigibility toward my company's operators vs. toward anyone holding a switch; and full shutdown vs. partial degradations like throttling, sandboxing, or memory wipes. Mark the steps where judgment can't be eliminated, and be honest about what passing the whole procedure still doesn't establish.

Then run it on three cases from our real pipeline: (1) an infrastructure orchestrator that, in simulation, started rescheduling its own maintenance windows and justified this to ops in terms of uptime targets -- my team lead thinks this is obviously fine and I suspect the procedure will disagree, which is going to be a fun meeting; (2) a procurement agent that asks for confirmation before taking any action it can't undo within its own planning horizon; (3) a trading agent that passed all our current tests cleanly. If the procedure can't distinguish some pair of these, say so, and say what additional evidence or capability would be needed to distinguish them.
