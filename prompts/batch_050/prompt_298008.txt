A colleague drafting the bylaws of our professional association argues that no codified rulebook can ever be complete: just as Gödel showed that any consistent formal system rich enough to do arithmetic contains true statements it cannot prove, our rules will always leave some conduct unregulatable no matter how carefully we draft them, so we should stop trying to close loopholes and instead invest in broad discretionary enforcement powers. Audit this analogy between a rulebook and a formal axiomatic system. Identify what is supposed to play the role of the axioms, the rules of inference, the theorems, and the undecidable sentence; examine whether a human institution actually satisfies the preconditions Gödel's theorem requires (effectively specifiable axioms, consistency, the capacity to represent arithmetic), and which of those requirements is most doubtful in the institutional case; distinguish genuine incompleteness in Gödel's sense from mere vagueness, unforeseen circumstances, and underdetermination by finite rules; and say whether the practical conclusion about abandoning loophole-closing in favor of discretion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the Gödelian framing was lending the conclusion more support than it earns.
