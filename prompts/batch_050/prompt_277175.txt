I am preparing a graduate seminar on the Scientific Revolution, and a conversation with a colleague in the history of science left me genuinely puzzled about how to frame a unit on Kepler for the philosophers in the room. The puzzle is not about whether Kepler "believed in" astrology — that framing strikes me as too coarse — but about what his actual practice presupposed. Here is the practice as I understand it: Kepler spent much of his career issuing the annual calendar prognostications expected of a provincial and later imperial mathematician, cast nativities for paying patrons including, late in life, Wallenstein, and in his 1610 Tertius Interveniens positioned himself between a court physician who wanted astrology abolished outright and a physician-astrologer who practiced it wholesale. His middle position, as I read it, is that most of received astrology — the twelve zodiacal signs, the houses, the detailed rules of judicial astrology — is worthless superstition fit for the rubbish heap, yet that something survives: roughly, that the angular relations between planets (the aspects) are geometrical harmonies which affect earthly processes, weather above all, and which resonate in human souls disposed by nature to perceive harmony. He also, in a famous image, treats astrology as an unruly daughter of astronomy whose earnings nonetheless keep the honest mother alive. So the same man mocked the trade, reformed it on his own mathematical lights, and kept practicing it for money and on demand.

My question for the seminar is: what does this practice presuppose, philosophically? One reading is that it presupposes a naturalistic causal theory — celestial configurations exert physical influence via light, heat, or harmonic structure, and the astrologer is just a premature scientist working with bad data and no statistics. A second reading is that it presupposes a metaphysics of correspondence: the cosmos is built on harmonic archetypes, and prediction works not because planets push things around but because microcosm and macrocosm participate in the same formal order — a view that sits uneasily with the mechanical "celestial physics" Kepler himself was inventing. A third, more deflationary reading is that the practice presupposes almost nothing theoretical at all: it is what a mathematician had to do to eat, and the philosophical scaffolding in Tertius Interveniens is rationalization after the fact. I would like you to adjudicate among these, but with some discipline about what "presuppose" means: which parts of the practice (the prognostications? the nativities? the reform program?) strictly require which theory, and which parts are merely consistent with several theories and so underdetermine the answer? And if the answer differs across those components — if the weather prognostics presuppose the causal theory while the nativities presuppose the correspondence metaphysics — does that make the practice internally inconsistent, or is there a single epistemology of evidence (his insistence that astrological claims be tested against records the way astronomical claims are tested against observation) that holds it together?

Finally, I would like the response to be epistemically self-aware. Please indicate how confident you are in your reconstruction overall, distinguish what the surviving texts settle from what they leave open, and single out explicitly the one point in your answer where you are least sure — whether because the historical evidence is thin, because the theoretical options are closer than they look, or because the question itself may rest on an anachronistic assumption about how a thinker of that period would have divided science from superstition.
