I am preparing a graduate metaphysics seminar on material constitution, and before we assess any of the standard positions I want the students to work from an explicit specification of what a theory of the statue and the clay must accomplish. Please write that specification.

Concretely, produce a list of desiderata that any adequate account of the relation between a statue and the lump of clay constituting it must satisfy. For each desideratum, state the requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and indicate whether it is a genuine constraint on any view whatsoever or instead a commitment of one camp that rival views may reasonably decline. The list should cover at least the following terrain, though you need not organize it this way:

- The modal and temporal divergence between statue and lump: the lump, it seems, survives being rolled into a ball while the statue does not, and the pressure this verdict exerts on identity via the indiscernibility of identicals.
- The counting problem: how many material objects occupy the pedestal, and which answer ordinary thought is entitled to.
- The grounding problem: if statue and lump share all their parts and matter, what could possibly ground their difference in properties.
- The asymmetry of constitution: the lump constitutes the statue and not vice versa, and a theory should explain why rather than merely assert it.
- Generality: the account should extend to the other puzzle cases in the neighborhood (persons and their bodies, ships and their planks, coins and their metal) rather than being engineered for statuary alone.
- Mereological discipline: the view should state explicitly which principles about parthood and composition it retains and which it abandons, and price the abandonment honestly.
- The arbitrariness worry: whatever principle admits the statue and the lump as distinct should not thereby admit an uncontrolled proliferation of coincident objects, one for every imaginable set of persistence conditions.

After the list, do two further things. First, identify any subset of the desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied. I suspect the list contains at least one such tension, and I want it stated as an explicit inconsistent triad (or n-ad), with a note on which horn each of the main positions in the literature — constitutionalism, perdurantism, relative identity, and eliminativism about the ordinary objects — elects to reject. Second, say which single desideratum excludes the most otherwise-live candidates, and why.

Keep the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases. A brief closing scorecard of the main positions against the list is welcome, but I will grade the positions in class myself; what I cannot get from the textbooks is a well-argued statement of the standard itself, including which demands are negotiable, which are not, and what follows if the best theory still fails a requirement we had no right to impose.
