In their 1979 critique of the adaptationist programme, Gould and Lewontin introduce the spandrels of St Mark's Basilica in Venice: the tapering triangular surfaces left over where rounded arches meet at right angles beneath a dome, each bearing a mosaic so apt that the space seems designed for the image. Their claim is that many biological traits are like this — necessary byproducts of other structures, later decorated with a use, and that adaptationist reasoning systematically mistakes the decoration for the reason the structure exists. The metaphor is now cited well beyond that paper to license a general skepticism toward adaptive explanations. I want an audit of the analogy itself, not a survey of the debate.

Begin with a correspondence table, one row per element, with these columns: architectural element; proposed biological counterpart; whether the mapping holds (holds / strained / fails); and a one-line reason. Then address, in prose: (i) the disanalogies that matter — at minimum, that the spandrel's shape is geometrically necessitated by the arch-and-dome scheme whereas biological byproducts are typically contingent and variable, and that the mosaics were added later by intentional agents whereas co-optation (exaptation) is not; (ii) which disanalogies are idle for the critique's actual target; (iii) whether the conclusion the metaphor is standardly used to support — that byproduct hypotheses deserve equal standing and that adaptation must be demonstrated rather than presumed — still stands once the metaphor is withdrawn, or whether the metaphor was carrying it. Note too whether the architectural facts themselves, independent of biology, fully support the byproduct reading. Close with a one-sentence verdict and, if you think a better comparison exists for non-adaptive traits, name it and say in one line why it is better.
