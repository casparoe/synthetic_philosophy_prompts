A few weeks ago my neighbour's sixteen-year-old son passed his driving test, and over Sunday dinner my father-in-law, a retired industrial safety officer, remarked that every time the boy pulls out of the driveway he imposes a small probability of death on everyone else on the road, none of whom ever consented to bearing it. The remark has lodged in my head. Nearly everything we do imposes risks on non-consenting strangers: driving, hosting a dinner party during flu season, emitting carbon, even ordering deliveries that put more vans on the street. Yet we treat some non-consensual risk impositions as obviously fine, some as actionable wrongs (second-hand smoke, reckless pollution), and some as wrong only when the harm actually materializes. I co-run a small philosophy reading group, and I would like to open our session on this with a handout that sorts the possible positions rather than arguing for one of them.

Please write a decision tree for the question: when is it permissible to impose a risk of harm on someone who has not consented? Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names — start from the questions on which views genuinely diverge, then locate the actual positions at the leaves. The branching questions should include, at minimum: whether the wrongness of an imposition turns on whether harm actually results or lies in the risk itself; whether actual consent, hypothetical consent, or the bearer's acceptance of the activity's benefits makes a difference; whether a risk borne reciprocally within a shared scheme (as in traffic) stands on a different moral footing from a one-way imposition; whether imposing a risk differs from merely adding to a background risk the bearer already faces; and whether the imposer's reasons, or the size of the risk relative to the benefit, can settle the matter on their own. Each node should be phrased as a concrete case someone can answer without knowing any labels — the ambulance that must speed through a residential street, the unvaccinated guest at a gathering of elderly relatives, the factory whose emissions slightly raise an existing cancer rate, the reckless motorist who luckily kills no one — so the tree sorts people by what they believe, not by what they call themselves.

Aim for six to eight branching questions and five to seven leaves. The leaves must include: a view on which only realized harm can wrong the victim, a consent-based rights view, a reciprocity or mutual-benefit view, a contractualist view keyed to what each affected individual could reasonably reject, and a conventionalist view on which the ordinary activities of life are exempt from scrutiny. Where a combination of answers has no defender, still show it as a leaf, mark it unoccupied, and give a one-sentence diagnosis of whether that is because the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. If two of the required positions cannot be separated by any case in the tree, flag that explicitly rather than papering over it. Close the handout with one sentence per leaf on the commitment its holders find most awkward, since that is what I want the group to argue about once everyone has found where they land.
