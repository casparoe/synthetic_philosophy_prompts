You are a scholar of comparative metaphysics with genuine depth in both Kant's critical philosophy and Śaṅkara's Advaita Vedanta, and with a reputation for refusing facile East-meets-West harmonizing. Think carefully about what follows. I would like you to begin by brainstorming a wide list of candidate correspondences between the two systems before committing to any of them, and to be at least as interested in the failures of translation as in the successes.

Some background. Last night I spent four hours arguing with a close friend. She is a committed Advaitin; I am not, but I know Kant well — I have taught the Critique of Pure Reason for years, and the vocabulary of phenomena and noumena, the forms of intuition, the categories, and the transcendental unity of apperception is second nature to me. My Advaita, by contrast, comes almost entirely from her, so treat the five commitments listed below as all you may assume I know of the system. At some point she claimed that Śaṅkara's framework says something Kant's simply cannot — not merely something Kant did not happen to say, but something his philosophy lacks the expressive resources to state. I replied that anything genuinely stateable in her vocabulary should be stateable, perhaps at some cost, in mine, and that whatever resists translation is likely less clear than it looks. We left it there. I want to be better prepared when the argument resumes.

Here is the task. Take the following Advaita commitments one at a time:

1. The world of distinct objects, persons, and events, as ordinarily experienced, is māyā: not sheer nothing, but a misprojection — the stock example being the rope seen as a snake — sustained by beginningless ignorance, and liable to cancellation the way the snake is canceled when the rope is finally seen.
2. What I truly am, ātman, is not the empirical ego, not the stream of mental states, not the agent of actions, but pure witnessing awareness.
3. This ātman is numerically identical with Brahman, the sole reality; the felt separation between self and absolute is itself part of the error.
4. Liberation is not an event that befalls a person but the removal of an error by knowledge, after which nothing about the world need change.
5. The method of neti neti is not skepticism but the only honest route to the truth, since anything assertible already belongs to the side of the misprojection.

For each commitment, do three things. First, say whether Kant's vocabulary can express it outright, and give the best such rendering. Second, if it can be rendered only with distortion, give the rendering anyway and name precisely what had to change. Third, if no rendering is possible, say so plainly rather than forcing a fit — and take care not to smuggle a Kantian assumption into the translation and then call the result faithful.

Then the residue report. Collect everything that failed to survive and adjudicate. Does the untranslatable remainder show that Kant's framework lacks expressive resources — and if so, which resources, exactly? Or does it vindicate my suspicion that the residue was never fully clear in the first place? My friend will push back hard on either verdict, so anticipate the strongest version of her rejoinder.

Close with a compact table: one row per commitment, with columns for clean translation, distorted translation, and untranslatable residue.
