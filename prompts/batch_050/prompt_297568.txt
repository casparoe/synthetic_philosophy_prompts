Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about whether believing what people tell you is ever rationally basic. Her view is that you always need independent evidence that the speaker is reliable. Mine is that sometimes being told something by another person can give you at least defeasible justification without your first checking their track record. We kept saying reductionism and anti-reductionism at each other, but it became clear that these labels were hiding several different distinctions.

Please make a decision tree that sorts someone’s actual position on testimonial justification, aimed at philosophy undergrads who know ordinary epistemology but haven’t studied testimony. Start from this neutral case: an apparently competent adult tells you, in an ordinary face-to-face conversation, that the museum closes at six. Use 10 to 12 diagnostic questions, each answerable without knowing the name of any position.

The branching questions should separate views along at least these dimensions:

- whether testimony is ever a basic source of justification, or only transmits justification derived from perception, memory, induction, etc.
- whether the hearer needs positive reasons for trusting the speaker, or merely an absence of undefeated defeaters
- whether the required evidence concerns testimony generally, a kind of speaker, or this particular speaker
- whether testimony can support its own reliability without vicious circularity
- whether a speaker’s act of taking responsibility for an assertion creates a distinctive interpersonal reason to believe them
- whether practical stakes alter the epistemic threshold rather than merely making caution prudent
- whether social identity, expertise, or institutional role can affect the default amount of credibility owed
- whether a long chain of reports preserves testimony’s status or gradually reduces it to statistical evidence

Use concrete mini-cases as some of the questions, not only abstract formulations. Please include: a sincere speaker with an excellent record who is, unknown to everyone, guessing on this occasion; an isolated person with no track-record evidence about anyone; a high-stakes medical report; a fourth-hand rumor; and a case where prejudice would normally cause the speaker to be assigned too little credibility.

The leaves should include strict reductionism, local reductionism, global reductionism, default-entitlement anti-reductionism, an assurance-based interpersonal view, testimonial skepticism, and at least one principled hybrid. Build the tree from possible answer combinations first, then locate those positions in it. If any plausible combination has no commonly recognized position attached, leave it unoccupied and say in one sentence whether that is because it seems incoherent, unstable, unattractive, or just underexplored. Flag any named positions your tree cannot really distinguish, rather than inventing an artificial difference.

Make the quiz feel like something my friend and I could actually take after dinner, but don’t include citations, a bibliography, or a history-of-philosophy section. We definately don’t need another list of famous names.
