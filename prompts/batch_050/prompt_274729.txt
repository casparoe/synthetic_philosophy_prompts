I'm in the second year of a philosophy degree and my reading group has spent all term bickering about whether it ever makes sense to blame a company rather than the individual people inside it. It kicked off when one of us said the water company ought to be ashamed of itself over the sewage spills and our resident sceptic replied that being angry at a firm is like being angry at the weather. So I went away and built an argument to settle it in the affirmative, and I was quite pleased with it until a friend pointed out that, as written, it also proves an ant colony can be morally responsible for stripping a farmer's field. Which is mad. But I genuinely cannot find where the rot gets in. The argument goes: (1) Something is a moral agent -- the kind of thing that can be blamed for what it does, in its own right -- if it forms representations of how the world is, holds goals, revises both in response to new information and considerations, and acts on the result in a reasonably consistent way over time. (2) Any collective that meets those conditions is a moral agent in its own right, and not merely as shorthand for its members. (3) A large company meets all of them: it builds and maintains models of its market and its customers, it has official objectives, its boards and review procedures revise objectives and models when the evidence shifts, and its policies persist coherently even through complete staff turnover -- more coherently than many individual humans manage, frankly. (4) So a company can be blamed in its own right. (5) But an ant colony tracks its environment, "aims" at food and defence, "revises" its foraging pattern within minutes of conditions changing, and behaves with dreary consistency for years. (6) So by (1)–(3), an ant colony is a moral agent and can be blamed for what it does. (7) That conclusion is absurd, so at least one of the earlier steps has to go.

Where does it go wrong? I'd really like to keep (4) if at all possible, because my coursework this term argues that it can be right to hold a bank responsible for a mis-selling scandal even in the case where no individual employee saw the whole picture or did anything clearly sackable, and the whole essay collapses if blame can only ever land on persons. If I have to sacrifice something I'd rather it be (1), since that's the bit I lifted most directly from the functionalist vibe of the readings, but every weakening of it I've tried either lets the ants back in through a side door or kicks the company out along with them. So: tell me which step you think I should reject, explain why it looked perfectly fine in the first place (it must have done, it fooled me for weeks), and be honest about what rejecting it costs -- including whether the right move is to bolt an extra condition onto (1), and if so which one, or whether the actual mistake is treating (3) and (5) as true in the same sense when they aren't. And if the honest answer is that (4) has to go and my essay is in trouble, I'd rather know that than not.
