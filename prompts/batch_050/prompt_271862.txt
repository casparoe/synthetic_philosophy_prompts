So last night I got into this stupidly long arguement with a friend about whether agriculture was a mistake — like, the whole shift from foraging to farming — and we went around in circles for two hours and I want an outside verdict. I won't say which of us took which side, because I want a straight answer, so here's the exchange with the sides anonymized.

Side 1 opened with the standard move: the skeletons of early farmers are shorter, more anemic, worse teeth than the foragers right before them, work hours went up (the original affluent society thing — foragers often get subsistence done in maybe 15-20 hours a week), and once you have stored surplus you get taxation, hierarchy, patriarchy, states. So measured by the lives of the people who actually lived through it, farming was a catastrophe we never really climbed out of.

Side 2 said the mistake framing is incoherent because nobody chose it. Each step — camp near the good grain stands, burn a bit, plant a bit, stay a little longer each year — was locally rational, and by the time population had grown you couldn't go back. A trap isn't a mistake, the same way evolution isn't a mistake. Plus the forager work-hours numbers are cooked: they leave out processing time back at camp, and forager "affluence" is mostly just having few wants, which is a weird thing to call wealth.

Side 1 replied that traps can still be mistakes — the question is evaluative (was the outcome worse?) not agential (did someone choose badly?). And the inequality bit isn't hand-waving: grain specifically is visible, measurable, storable and taxable in a way wild food isn't, which is plausibly why the first states were built on it, whereas forager egalitarianism had to be actively maintained by ganging up on would-be big men.

Side 2 said the skeletons are confounded: early farmers were packed into dense settlements with disease pools and each other's sewage, so you're measuring crowding, not farming per se, and stature recovered later anyway. And the counterfactual matters: no surplus, no writing, no science, no medicine. Average welfare dipping for a few millennia and then paying off enormously is, on balance, a decent trade.

Side 1 said that payoff defense is doing monstrous work — the people who paid aren't the people who cashed in, and we don't normally let later benefits launder earlier suffering. Also, farming didn't mechanically cause inequality anyway: there were sedentary foragers with slaves and chiefdoms (Pacific Northwest coast, the Calusa) and farming societies without states, so the causal chain both sides keep assuming is just wrong.

Side 2 said: fine, but that point cuts against you harder — if agriculture didn't cause the bad stuff, your mistake verdict evaporates and all we're left with is "civilization has trade-offs," which nobody ever denied.

Please go through it step by step and tell me who's right at each point, who's more right overall, and what both sides are missing. I care less about the final verdict than about exactly where each move holds up or falls apart.
