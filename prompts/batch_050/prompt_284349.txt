So my moral philosophy seminar just did a unit on suicide and we read Hume's essay on it (the posthumous one, sometimes called Of Suicide, sometimes On Suicide), and I've been stuck ever since because my gut reactions to cases don't line up with his argument and I can't figure out if that's my problem or his. His reasoning, roughly, is that suicide can't be a crime against God because everything that happens is part of the order of nature and it would be arrogant to think my dying early disrupts anything important; it can't be a crime against society because when I die I merely stop doing good, and I can't be obligated to keep suffering just to keep conferring small benefits on others; and it can't be a crime against myself because by hypothesis the person who does it judges their life to be more burden than good. So suicide is at least sometimes permissible, whenever life has become genuinely worse than death for the person living it.

Here are my verdicts, which I mostly trust. A terminally ill woman in constant unmanaged pain who ends her life: clearly permissible, maybe even admirable. A twenty-two year old with severe depression, whose depression is treatable but who sincerely judges his life not worth living: it would be a catastrophe to just defer to his judgment, even though structurally his case looks identical to hers (person whose life seems unbearable to them, making a settled judgment). Cato killing himself rather than live under Caesar: feels noble, not tragic. A miserable father of three young kids who ends his life: feels plainly wrong, and the wrongness seems to be about the kids, but Hume would say he only ceases to do them good, and we don't normally think people owe their whole continued existence to others. A soldier diving on a grenade: I don't even want to call it suicide, it feels categorically different, though I can't say why. And a healthy seventy-year-old who is simply bored and tired of everything, no illness, no dependents: I'm genuinely torn, but I lean toward thinking something has gone wrong with him rather than that he's made a fine call.

Is there one principle behind these verdicts, or am I just a mess? I want you to try seriously to find a principle (or two competing candidates) that captures as many of them as possible, tell me which of my verdicts it has to throw out, and say what it would predict for cases I didn't list (say, someone facing life in prison, or a burdened elderly person whose care is ruining their child's life). Also test Hume's three-part argument against whatever you come up with, because my suspicion is his society clause is doing something sneaky. And please flag the weak point: what consideration or case would most plausibly overturn your principle, or where you think my intuitions are likeliest to be the unreliable ones.
