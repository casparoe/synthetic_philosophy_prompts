I sit on the five-person review panel of a small public-history institute. Members of the public send us manuscripts claiming to overturn some piece of accepted history, and twice a year we decide which submissions get a serious expert review and which are declined after a single meeting. Our budget allows us to review at most four submissions a year, so the triage decision matters. I want to predict how my fellow panelists will vote on the next submission, and I'd like your help reconstructing the rule we seem to be running.

Our track record so far:

1. A submission arguing that a famous ancient monument is several millennia younger than the accepted dating, based on a reinterpretation of tool marks. Declined at first meeting, unanimously. Accepting it would have required the radiocarbon results, the stratigraphy, and the textual evidence all to be wrong at once.

2. A submission arguing that a celebrated medieval manuscript is a nineteenth-century forgery. Sent for full review, despite a firm scholarly consensus behind its authenticity, because the claim turned on a small number of testable questions (chiefly ink composition and handwriting analysis).

3. A submission arguing that a well-documented atrocity never occurred, resting mainly on the absence of records the author believed should exist. Declined at first meeting.

4. A submission by a complete amateur arguing that the date of a minor battle should be shifted by a decade, based on a newly surfaced regimental diary. Sent for full review; the panelist most skeptical of amateurs voted with the rest of us.

5. A submission arguing that the conventional chronology of an entire era is inflated by several centuries, on the grounds that copyists across several independent chronicle traditions all made compounding errors. Declined at first meeting, over the objection of one panelist who wanted it reviewed.

The new submission argues that a major site's accepted dating is wrong by roughly two centuries, because the pottery seriation for the whole region was anchored to a layer that was itself misdated at the original excavation. The author alleges no fraud or cover-up, works entirely from published excavation reports, and concedes that if her claim is right, the dates of dozens of linked sites would all have to shift together — the error is systematic, not local. Nothing in the submission requires new fieldwork to evaluate; it is a reanalysis of existing data.

Here is what I want from you. First, articulate the plausible rules that fit our five verdicts — I suspect at least three do, such as a rule about how many independent lines of evidence a claim asks us to give up, a rule about whether a claim requires coordinated deception, and a rule about whether a claim reduces to a few independently checkable points — and show that each is consistent with the record. Second, state what each rule predicts for the new submission, and where they diverge. Third, give me your best prediction of how the panel will actually vote, with your reasons, and identify which of our past cases is the closest analog. Finally, describe a hypothetical future submission that would most cleanly discriminate among the candidate rules — that is, a case on which they give different verdicts — and explain why it would settle the matter.
