I keep noticing the same failure mode in arguments over extraordinary claims, and I want to know whether it has an established name. Each report of the event is weighed on its own against the event's prior improbability and discounted — reasonable in itself — but the next independent report is then weighed in exactly the same way, from scratch, so that no accumulation of testimony ever moves the sceptic. Twenty independent witnesses to a meteorite fall get processed as twenty individually incredible claims, never as one increasingly incredible scenario in which twenty independent people all erred or lied in the same way. The believer often mirrors this, treating every corroboration as purely additive even when the witnesses are plainly correlated (all read the same newspaper, say). In Part I of Section X, "Of Miracles", of his Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Hume writes:

"All probability, then, supposes an opposition of experiments and observations, where the one side is found to overbalance the other, and to produce a degree of evidence, proportioned to the superiority. A hundred instances or experiments on one side, and fifty on another, afford a doubtful expectation of any event; though a hundred uniform experiments, with only one that is contradictory, reasonably beget a pretty strong degree of assurance. In all cases, we must balance the opposite experiments, where they are opposite, and deduct the smaller number from the greater, in order to know the exact force of the superior evidence."

My question: is there an established concept in the epistemology of testimony or the formal literature on evidence for this pattern — a locally sound discounting rule whose repeated independent application globally blocks belief revision — and if not, can you name and define it, with boundary cases showing when per-report discounting is legitimate (correlated sources) and when it becomes the failure I've described? More specifically, does Hume's balancing model in this passage commit him to the failure mode (each miracle report facing the same uniform contrary experience afresh), or does his own arithmetic of experiments actually require the aggregation the failure mode omits — in which case the phenomenon is a misapplication of Hume rather than Hume's rule? Distinguish it from merely holding a low prior, from the slogan that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence, and from base-rate neglect, which looks similar but cuts the other way.
