The question I set was: Explain how lexical threshold negative utilitarianism differs from both classical utilitarianism and strict lexical negative utilitarianism, and discuss whether its treatment of the relation between pleasure and pain is stable -- that is, whether the view can consistently hold that the two are commensurable in some ranges but not in others. Use at least one concrete case (say, a life containing only trivial pains versus one containing a period of extreme agony) to illustrate.

Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"Negative utilitarianism holds that we should minimize suffering rather than maximize happiness. Strict lexical negative utilitarianism says no amount of pleasure can ever compensate for suffering, which leads directly to the pinprick problem: a world containing a single pinprick of pain is worse than a world of pure bliss, and arguably painless extinction of all life would be preferable to any world with even mild suffering. Lexical threshold negative utilitarianism avoids this by introducing a threshold: suffering below a certain intensity can be outweighed by sufficient pleasure, but suffering above the threshold, extreme agony for instance, is lexically prior, so no quantity of pleasure compensates for it. A life of mild aches amid great joy can therefore be net positive, while a life containing even a brief episode of torture cannot be redeemed by any amount of bliss.

This means the view rejects the commensurability of pleasure and pain altogether. On this account, trade-offs between pleasure and pain are impossible, beacuse the whole point of the theory is that pains are never offset by gains. That is also what sets it apart from classical utilitarianism, which aggregates pleasure and pain on a single continuous scale. The view is stable, since the threshold marks a qualitative difference in kind between mild and extreme suffering rather than a merely quantitative one, so there is no arbitrariness problem about where the line is drawn."
