Since retiring from a career in atmospheric research, I have served in an unpaid capacity on the advisory board of a national reforestation programme. Last year the ministry announced, on the basis of a model I helped construct, that the programme had restored 40,000 hectares. Re-examining the inputs, I am now confident the true figure is closer to 28,000, though the error bars are wide. When I proposed publishing a correction, the programme's director — a decent person, I believe — argued as follows: the error is modest relative to the good the programme does; the original announcement was made in good faith; no one lied, since the ministry merely reported the model's headline output; and a correction would hand a weapon to opponents of environmental spending generally, endangering programmes beyond this one. He added that a statistic is not a fact in the way a tree is a fact, and that I would be sacrificing a living institution to an abstraction.

I find myself unconvinced, yet unable to say exactly why. In my recent philosophical reading I have encountered the suggestion that factual truths occupy a peculiarly fragile position in political life — more fragile than the truths of mathematics or of logic — but I am unsure how this bears on a case where the "fact" in question was always a point estimate drawn from a distribution.

My questions are these. First, is the ministry's refusal to correct a lie at all, given the model's genuine uncertainty, and does the answer matter? Second, does the good-faith origin of the error change the moral character of declining to correct it now that the figure has been cited in parliament and taken on a public life of its own? Third, how should I weigh the director's claim that foreseeing the political consequences of a correction is part of what a responsible adviser must do — is that prudence, or is it precisely the confusion of the adviser's role with the politician's? I would be grateful for advice that takes the director's argument seriously rather than dismissing it.
