I am a senior at a public high school and co-president of our ethics club. Our faculty advisor, a philosophy teacher, has a well-developed instinct for when it is and isn't acceptable to alter people's minds or bodies for their own good or for society's. Over the past two years I've collected her snap verdicts on six cases, each with her one-line rationale as best I remember it:

1. A city adding fluoride to the water supply with no individual opt-out: acceptable. Her rationale: a trivial burden for a large benefit to children's health.
2. Requiring childhood vaccination as a condition of public school enrollment: acceptable. Her rationale: declining to vaccinate endangers other people's children, not just your own.
3. Offering convicted violent offenders an impulse-control drug as a voluntary alternative to a longer prison sentence: acceptable. Her rationale: the choice is theirs, and declining simply means the ordinary sentence.
4. Parents giving a healthy but shy ten-year-old a safe drug to make her more outgoing: unacceptable. Her rationale: you don't redesign a person's temperament without her consent for your own convenience.
5. Editing an embryo so the future child will be predisposed to obedience and agreeableness, as a service marketed to parents who want easier children: unacceptable. Her rationale: you don't get to engineer someone's character before they exist to object.
6. A mandatory empathy and perspective-taking curriculum in all public schools: acceptable. Her rationale: helping children become decent people through teaching is part of what school is for.

We have recently been discussing moral bioenhancement — the idea, associated with philosophers like Ingmar Persson and Julian Savulescu, that human moral psychology evolved for life in small groups and is poorly suited to large-scale collective problems, so biomedical means of improving it should at least be on the table. Our club wants to put the following case to her, and I'd like to predict her answer before we do.

A city, citing research that links rising political polarization and declining social trust to failures in pandemic response, infrastructure maintenance, and climate adaptation, proposes adding a compound to the public water supply that mildly increases empathy and willingness to cooperate. The compound is physically safe at the proposed dose, its effects are mild and wear off if a person stops drinking the water, and everyone — including the officials proposing it — would receive it. The city's public argument explicitly invokes the fluoridation precedent: the same delivery system, the same logic of a small, universal, low-burden intervention purchased for a large collective benefit.

Here is what I'd like from you:

(a) Identify at least two general rules or principles that each fit all six of her prior verdicts — rules that might turn on harm to third parties, on consent, on the distinction between biomedical and educational means, or on working through a person's reason rather than around it — and show where they diverge on the water-supply proposal.

(b) Give me your best prediction of her verdict, say which precedent you take to be the closest analog (the city says fluoridation; I suspect she'll say the embryo case), and explain why you weight the evidence as you do.

(c) Design a seventh hypothetical case — one more verdict from her — that would do the most to discriminate among the rules you've identified, and say what each rule would predict about it.

Finally, please be explicit about what would change your mind: which of her six verdicts, on a different but plausible reading of her one-line rationale, would most destabilize your prediction, and where your reconstruction of her reasoning is most likely to fail. If her record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say so rather than forcing a prediction.
