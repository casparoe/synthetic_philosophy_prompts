Buddhist monks in the Theravada tradition aren't vegetarians, but they can't just eat any meat either. The rule, roughly, is that a monk must refuse meat if he saw, heard, or suspects that the animal was killed specifically for him -- killed with that monk or that alms-round in mind. Meat from an animal killed for the market, or for somebody else's table, or that died on its own, is fine to accept. (In the traditional accounts this comes up when Devadatta pushes for compulsory vegetarianism for all monks and gets refused, if I'm remembering that story right.)

I've been chewing on what moral theory this rule presupposes, because from a modern consumer-ethics angle it's genuinly odd. Buying or eating meat from the market arguably contributes to killing just as much as asking for an animal to be killed for you, only in a diffuse statistical way: your demand nudges the butcher's calculations, some future animal dies that otherwise wouldn't. The vinaya rule seems completely uninterested in that channel. It cares about a specific, knowable link between you and one particular act of killing, and if that link is absent the meat is clean -- even if meat-eating monks in the aggregate keep the butcher in business.

So: what view of complicity does the practice presuppose? A few candidates: (a) karma tracks intention, and you can't intend a killing you don't even know about; (b) responsibility requires being the reason for a particular act, not a marginal contributor to a class of acts; (c) the rule isn't about the eater's culpability at all but about refusing to give killers a forward-looking motive. Reconstruct the implicit theory, tell me which of these the rule strictly presupposes versus which ones it merely fits well with, and which views of complicity (e.g. a demand-based, marginalist one) it quietly rules out. Does the practice sit comfortably with a consequentialist reading of Buddhist ethics at all? And what would monastic life actually have to look like if the rule had instead been built on the marginalist picture -- full vegetarianism, or something subtler?
