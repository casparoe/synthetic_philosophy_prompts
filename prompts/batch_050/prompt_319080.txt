I’m in a modern European history seminar and last week we got into an arguement that refused to die: if Hitler had somehow vanished from the scene, would Germany still have become something recognizably like Nazi Germany? We invented a crude diagnostic we called the replacement test. Imagine Hitler is removed at some point, while leaving in place the major background conditions: defeat in WWI, resentment over the peace settlement, Weimar’s institutional weaknesses, the Depression, antisemitic movements, the army, conservative elites, street violence, and so on. Then replace him with another historically plausible far-right leader. Call an outcome leader-elastic if several plausible replacements could probably have produced roughly the same outcome, and leader-specific if Hitler’s particular personality, decisions, timing, or coalition-building seem crucial.

Write a dialogue between two equally competent historians using this test: a structuralist who thinks Hitler was more replaceable than people assume, and a Great Man theorist who thinks the replacement test breaks down precisely where the case matters most. They should apply it separately to three different outcomes: Germany becoming some kind of authoritarian, revanchist, fascist-adjacent regime in the 1930s; the specifically Nazi movement gaining and consolidating power; and the scale and timing of WWII and the Holocaust. Don’t let either character get away with repeating a slogan like society made Hitler or Hitler made the Holocaust. They need to discuss mechanisms, distinguish causal necessity from salience and moral responsibility, and challenge each other over what counts as roughly the same outcome. Give them real back-and-forth, have each concede at least one genuine point, and end with them identifying whether their remaining disagreement is mainly historical, methodological, or just about how the original question should be defined.
