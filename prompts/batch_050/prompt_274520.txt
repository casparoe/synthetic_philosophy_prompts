I'm after a bit of help settling a row that kicked off in my philosophy of mind reading group last night. It was nominally about hedonism, but it turned into an argument about whether pleasures have anything in common at all, and neither of them would give an inch. I'd like you to play the part of a patient, sharp philosopher of mind and adjudicate it properly -- go through the exchange move by move, say who lands each point, and flag anything both of them are missing. Think it through carefully before committing to a verdict.

So: Maya's position was that all pleasures share a distinctive felt quality -- that there's such a thing as "pleasure itself", a single feeling that gets triggered by different things (food, music, warm baths, good news), and that this common phenomenology is what makes them all pleasures. Tom countered that if you actually introspect, the pleasure of eating chips, the pleasure of finally getting a cryptic crossword clue, and the pleasure of a hot shower have absolutely nothing in common as experiences; what unifies them, he said, is an attitude -- you're pleased that such-and-such, or you want the experience to continue -- not any shared feeling. Maya's first comeback was introspective: she insisted she could simply tell, by attending to any pleasure, whether something feels good, and that this wouldn't be possible unless there were a common mark. Tom said that test is rigged -- when you attend you're attending to the chips or the shower, not to some free-floating pleasure-feeling, and the sense of a common mark is an artifact of attending with the question "is this nice?" already in mind.

Round two went empirical. Maya brought up the brain: reward pathways, opioids, the idea that pleasure is one kind of neurochemical business whatever the source. Tom pushed back with the wanting/liking distinction -- he said the dopamine stuff is mostly about wanting and motivation, not pleasure, and that even if there are neural "liking" hotspots, finding a common mechanism doesn't show there's a common felt quality, which was the actual claim; he accused her of quietly switching from a phenomenological thesis to a physiological one. She said he was dodging, that if the same system fires for chips and crosswords that's evidence the experiences share something even if we struggle to describe it. Then Tom wheeled out his cases: background contentment while you're absorbed in a task, which you only notice afterwards and so arguably had no felt quality at the time; the pleasure of relief from pain, which he said isn't a positive feeling at all but just the removal of a bad one; and the puzzle of scratching an itch, where the pleasure seems inseparable from -- maybe even constituted by -- something unpleasant. Maya bit the bullet on the first (no noticing, no pleasure), said relief really does feel positively good and Tom was underdescribing it, and on the itch she said the pleasure is a component that happens to be mixed with discomfort, like salt in caramel, so mixture cases don't threaten a common ingredient. Tom's last move was to say that if pleasure is just "being pleased that p" then of course you could be pleased your team won without any particular feeling, and that's fine -- which Maya pounced on as a reductio: a feelingless "pleasure" isn't pleasure at all, it's just believing something good happened, so his attitudinal view can't capture sensory pleasures even if it fits the crossword case.

That's where it stalled, with both claiming the other had changed the subject. So: who's right about what? I'd like the verdict broken down point by point -- the introspection test, whether the neuroscience actually supports Maya or is beside the point, the background-contentment and relief cases, the itch, and the final exchange about feelingless pleasure. And beyond scoring it, is there a way of seeing what the real underlying disagreement is -- for instance whether they're both assuming "pleasure" names one thing when maybe it doesn't -- and what a genuinely good next move for each side would be?
