In a reading group I run on philosophy of mind and AI, we have spent nearly a year on the question of whether large language models have beliefs, and the discussion has not advanced in months. One faction takes its cue from Dennett's intentional stance: a system has the beliefs and desires it is predictively fruitful to attribute to it, full stop, and since attributing beliefs to a good language model is enormously fruitful -- it predicts what the model will say, how it will reason, what it will deny -- the models qualify. The opposing faction, which includes people who otherwise call themselves Dennettians, insists this misreads the stance: they argue that intentional ascription presupposes a holistic network of states that hang together under rational pressure, revise one another, and show up stably across contexts, and that a model which asserts a proposition in one conversational frame and contradicts it in the next, without registering any tension, displays at best local mimicry of belief rather than the real pattern. The first faction replies that this smuggles in an internal-mechanism requirement the stance was designed to abolish; the second replies that without such constraints the stance degenerates into ascribing beliefs to thermostats and databases. Notably, everyone accepts the same behavioral evidence, and no new model release changes anyone's verdict. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is really about what would count as defeating a belief ascription: for one side, only predictive failure defeats it; for the other, an ascription is defeated whenever the attributed state does no stable causal-explanatory work -- when it cannot be tracked across interventions on the system, cannot explain anything beyond the single output that prompted it, and would not be missed if dropped from the interpretation. Please assess whether this hypothesis correctly identifies the structure of the impasse. Is the dispute verbal -- two senses of 'belief' talking past each other -- or a substantive disagreement about the standards an interpretivist must impose, or a concealed empirical disagreement about what is going on inside these systems that better mechanistic evidence could settle? Support your diagnosis with what each side actually commits to, and state what, if anything, would count as progress: a sharper distinction between kinds of pattern, a test of cross-contextual stability, a finding about internal representations, or an argument that the question should simply be abandoned as ill-posed.
