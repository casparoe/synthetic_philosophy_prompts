I'm trying to pin down when social punishment is legitimate and when it isn't, so here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I'd like to know what principle (if any) captures them.

A company quietly deciding not to book a comedian anymore after a string of jokes targeting a vulnerable group: fine. A thousand strangers dogpiling a random teenager whose dumb joke went viral, until she gets fired and has to move: not fine. Customers boycotting a bakery because the owner donated to a cause they hate: fine, that's their money. The bakery firing an employee because some of those same customers dug up his old tweets: not fine, even though it's also their money in a way. A grad student being frozen out socially by peers -- no invites, cold shoulders, nobody citing him -- over views he published in good faith in a peer-reviewed venue: not fine. Those same peers declining to invite him to a small workshop because his presence would make a junior researcher from the group he writes about genuinely uncomfortable: fine, or at least defensible. A professor sanctioned by his university for quoting a slur in the course of analyzing a court case containing it: clearly not fine. The same professor saying the slur in a debate to provoke: sanction seems fine. Employers googling candidates and passing on someone with a swastika tattoo: fine. Employers doing the same for someone photographed at a lawful protest for an unpopular cause: not fine, and I'm less sure why.

Is there one principle that gets all of these? My tentative one is: social sanction is legitimate when it targets conduct people could reasonably foresee being sanctioned, proportional to the actual harm, and carried out by whoever bears the relevant cost of continued association -- but that clause structure already feels like it's straining to save verdicts I've committed to rather than flowing from something real. Test it against my cases, repair it or replace it, and tell me straight up which of my verdicts have to go if the resulting principle is going to stay simple. Also tell me what it says about two cases I didn't list: a university rescinding an honorary degree, and a private book club quietly dropping a member. Lead with your bottom line -- the best candidate principle -- in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble.
