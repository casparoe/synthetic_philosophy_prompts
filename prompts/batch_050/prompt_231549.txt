In a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind that I help run, we keep returning to the split-brain literature, and the discussion has calcified in a way I want help diagnosing. Everyone at the table knows the standard findings from the commissurotomy patients -- the left hand that retrieves the object matching the picture flashed to the right hemisphere while the patient verbally reports seeing nothing, the cases where the two hands appear to work at cross-purposes -- and everyone agrees about what the experiments show. Yet the group splits cleanly and immovably into a one-subject camp (the patient is a single person with fragmented access to information, since a unified agent emerges whenever behavior has to be coordinated) and a two-subject camp (at least during the experimental tasks there are two streams of experience, each with its own perspective). What strikes me is that new data never seems to move anyone: show the one-subject camp a striking case of hemispheric independence and they redescribe it as a deficit of integration; show the two-subject camp a striking case of everyday behavioral unity and they redescribe it as mere behavioral coordination that says nothing about experience. Fifty years of accumulated results seem to function as a Rorschach test rather than evidence.

My working hypothesis, which I offer only as a hypothesis, is that the camps are not disagreeing about consciousness at all but about what the unit of counting is supposed to be. The one-subject camp implicitly treats a subject as whatever can be the locus of deliberation and report, while the two-subject camp treats any informationally encapsulated stream with its own perceptual field and goals as a subject, full stop. A thought experiment I posed to the group seemed to support this and also to embarrass everyone: imagine a surgical technique that severs the corpus callosum one fiber at a time over the course of years, with careful testing after each step. Does the number of subjects present jump from one to two at some particular fiber? A continuous slide into two subjects seems bizarre, a jump at a single fiber seems arbitrary, and "it was indeterminate all along" seems to concede that subject-count was never a well-defined quantity. Both camps hated this case, which made me suspect it exposes something real.

So my question is not which side is right. It is what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a verbal dispute in which both parties agree on all the facts and differ only about where to draw the boundaries of the word subject? Is it a substantive dispute that is nonetheless empirically undecidable because the contested datum -- what it is like to be the patient during the tasks -- is in principle inaccessible to third-person methods? Or is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work, for instance about whether phenomenal unity is an all-or-nothing relation or comes in degrees, such that progress in the neuroscience of conscious integration could actually settle it? I would like a diagnosis with reasons, grounded in what each side actually says rather than a survey of positions, together with an answer to what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction, a new experimental paradigm, a theorem about information integration, or an argument that the question "how many subjects?" is simply ill-posed and should be replaced rather than answered.
