I've never studied philosophy, but over the past couple of years I've been keeping a kind of informal catalogue of the ways people deceive each other, because I became convinced that the simple rule "don't lie" misses most of what's morally interesting about dishonesty. So far my catalogue has five entries:

1. Saying something literally true that any reasonable listener will take to mean something false (the classic being "there's a gas station just up the road" when you know it's closed).
2. Answering a different question than the one asked, so the listener draws the conclusion the questioner was looking for ("Did you take the money?" — "I've worked here fifteen years").
3. Telling the truth but omitting the one fact that would change the listener's picture entirely.
4. Deceiving through actions and staging rather than words — leaving the lights on and a car in the driveway so the house looks occupied.
5. Presenting accurate numbers in a frame chosen to mislead, like quoting a survival rate instead of a mortality rate because the listener will hear it differently.

I'm fairly sure my catalogue is missing at least one whole category, because everything on it either works through spoken or written language or through staged evidence, and I suspect there are ways to mislead that fit neither mold. Please give me one more way of deceiving someone that is structurally different from all five entries — not a rewording, special case, or combination of them. Once you've described it, tell me: (a) whether it should count as lying or merely as misleading, or whether that distinction breaks down for it; (b) whether it seems morally better, worse, or on a par with outright lying, and why; and (c) which of my five entries it comes closest to, so I can be sure I've understood how it differs.
