For my jurisprudence seminar, I want the following tension pressed. Legal practice appears to presuppose two rival theories of concepts at once. Statutory interpretation is standardly taught on H. L. A. Hart's model: a term like "vehicle" in a rule forbidding vehicles in the park has a core of settled application (the sedan) and a penumbra of doubt (the bicycle, the skateboard, the ambulance), and judges exercise something like legislative discretion at the edges. Adjudication by precedent, by contrast, is openly analogical: the court asks whether the case at bar is relevantly like the decided cases, with no rule fixing in advance which similarities count. On the account of concepts developed in Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences, however, categorization is analogy-making all the way down: even the apparently settled core consists of analogies so rapid and entrenched that they no longer feel like judgments at all, and there is no essence for a penumbra to surround. Please (i) reconstruct what each department of legal practice presupposes about the structure of concepts, (ii) say whether the two presuppositions are genuinely inconsistent or merely pitched at different levels of description, and (iii) tell me whether, if Hofstadter and Sander are right, Hart's core-and-penumbra picture must be abandoned or can instead be recovered as a description of analogies that have gone silent. Where the practice is underdetermined -- where either theory would license what lawyers actually do -- say so explicitly.
