I spent my career as an experimental physicist, and since retiring I have been reading, with a mixture of fascination and professional suspicion, in the literature on cliodynamics — the attempt, associated above all with Peter Turchin, to model the rise and fall of states quantitatively. The structural-demographic theory at its core is elegantly mechanical: population growth against carrying capacity, falling real wages, state fiscal strain, and, most intriguingly, "elite overproduction" — too many credentialed aspirants competing for too few positions of power — together drive societies through secular cycles of integration and disintegration lasting, supposedly, a few centuries. As someone who has fit curves for forty years, though, I cannot shake two worries that I rarely see pressed together. The first is a degrees-of-freedom worry: the historical record of agrarian states offers perhaps a few dozen full secular cycles, noisy and partially codetermined, while the theory deploys several interacting variables and lag structures — enough, I suspect, to fit almost any shape of elephant. The second is a reflexivity worry: Turchin's models are now read by journalists and, one assumes, by some elites themselves, so if the theory is right, its own publication becomes a causal factor in the system it describes, unlike anything I ever faced in the laboratory.

Write an essay weighing whether cliodynamics — and quantitative macrohistory more generally — constitutes genuine predictive science or sophisticated curve-fitting dressed in differential equations. Engage seriously with both of my worries: under what conditions, if any, does a model fitted to a small number of long cycles earn the right to be called predictive rather than merely retrodictive, and does the reflexivity of social prediction (forecasts that alter the forecasted) genuinely distinguish history from physics, or can it be absorbed into the modeling the way feedback is absorbed in control theory? Consider also what the right unit of prediction would even be — perhaps cliodynamics should be judged like climatology rather than meteorology, forecasting regimes and probabilities of instability rather than events. Reach a definite conclusion: either that cliodynamics has earned provisional scientific standing (and state what evidence, over what time horizon, would confirm or disconfirm it), or that it fails some criterion you can state precisely (and explain whether the failure is remediable or intrinsic to the subject matter). I would rather read a wrong verdict with sharp criteria than a hedged survey of both sides.
