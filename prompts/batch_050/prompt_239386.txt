My coworker has been needling me with this argument for weeks and I can't find the hole, which is annoying because the conclusion is absurd. (1) If two diets cause different total amounts of animal harm per calorie, the one causing more harm is worse, all else equal. (2) Growing crops kills staggering numbers of feild animals -- mice, voles, rabbits, ground-nesting birds -- through plowing, harvesting, pesticides and grain storage. (3) A steer raised entirely on pasture eats grass, not crops, so producing pastured beef involves no plowing or harvesting at all. (4) One steer yields a few hundred thousand calories, so each pastured-beef calorie carries a tiny fraction of one death, while every calorie of wheat or soy carries a share of the deaths from cultivating the field it came from. (5) So per calorie, pastured beef kills far fewer animals than bread, beans or tofu. (6) He's consistent about it, too: by the same metric he refuses to eat chicken, since a chicken yields only a couple thousand calories and lives a miserable life besides, so chicken comes out worst of all on his scale. (7) Therefore, if my reason for being vegetarian is reducing animal harm, I should eat pastured beef and feel bad about tofu. (8) And since nobody can seriously hold that buying tofu is morally worse than buying a steak, harm-reduction arguments for vegetarianism must be broken in general.

He says my only ways out are saying field mice don't count morally, which is speciesist, or saying deaths don't matter, which defeats the whole point of being vegetarian for the animals. I suspect the slide is somewhere around (3)-(5) but I can't name it. Which step do you actually reject, why does it look fine at first glance, and what does rejecting it cost me? If I have to give something up I'd rather keep (1) than concede that deaths-per-calorie is the wrong metric entirely. And if the arguement fails, does ordinary vegetarianism survive intact, or does his (6) still force me off chicken even if I get to keep my tofu?
