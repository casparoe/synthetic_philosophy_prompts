Just got home from a 3 hour argument with a friend from my cohort (she does phil of math, I don't) and we need a tiebreaker. We were talking about structuralism — roughly the view that numbers are nothing over and above positions in a structure, which is supposed to explain why "is 2 an element of 4?" has no real answer: it comes out yes on the usual von Neumann ordinals and no on Zermelo's construction, so the question is just ill-posed. Fine so far. But then she ran the following on me, and it feels like it proves way too much, except neither of us could pin down were it breaks:

(1) If structuralism is true, then everything there is to being the number 2 is its position in the natural number structure — its relations to the other positions, and nothing more.
(2) If that's all there is to being 2, then nothing about what 2 is settles any facts connecting it to positions in other structures, since those connections are not part of the natural number structure.
(3) If nothing about 2 settles such facts, then statements identifying 2 with some position in another structure are not true — at best they're conventions we adopted and could just as well reverse.
(4) The naturals, the integers, the rationals, and the reals are different structures.
(5) So the natural number 2 is not identical to the integer 2, the rational 2, or the real 2.
(6) So there are indefinitely many different 2's, the reals do not literally contain the naturals, and when a calculus teacher tells first-years they're still working with the same numbers they met in grade school, she is saying something false.
(7) But that's absurd. Nobody thinks real analysis introduces a brand new number 2, and every textbook treats the reals as extending the naturals.
(8) So structuralism is false.

She calls this a reductio. I keep wanting to attack (1), but she says (1) is just what structuralism is, so rejecting it means conceding the view before the argument even starts — and honestly I do find the view attractive, it really does seem to dissolve the is-2-an-element-of-4 puzzle. Something in the middle of the chain feels slippery to me but I can't articulate it, and she says I'm hand-waving.

If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (7) than any premise — I refuse to hold a philosophy of math on which children and analysts are talking about different numbers. So: which step should I reject, why did that step look fine in the first place, and what does rejecting it actually cost? In particular I want to know if the cost is basically "become a platonist about numbers," because if so I want that on the table.

Lead with the bottom line — the step you'd reject, in one sentence — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the argument back at me.
