So I sit on the professional standards committee for a mid-sized regional hospital — we handle discipline for nursing staff, basically an internal ethics board with teeth — and we're deadlocked on a case. I need a decision memo I can circulate before our meeting next month.

The facts, stripped down. Over a four-month stretch, our controlled-substance audits flagged oxycodone discrepancies (missing doses, counts off, wasted amounts without the required co-signature) on 23 of the 26 shifts worked by one particular nurse. Over the same period, comparable discrepancies appeared on 4 of roughly 300 shifts worked by everyone else on the unit. Our compliance officer ran the numbers and says the chance of that pattern clustering on one person by dumb luck is south of one in a hundred thousand. But that is literally all we have. No camera footage (the med room camera was broken, infuriatingly), no witnesses, no failed drug test, nothing in her locker. She denies everything, and by all accounts she's a good nurse. The discrepancies could in principle be sloppy record-keeping rather than theft, though the same sloppiness mysteriously never appears on anyone else's shifts.

The committee is split right down the middle. One half says: the probability she's responsible is far past any threshold anyone has ever bothered to quantify, and if we'd fire her on the word of a single eyewitness — who'd be right maybe 90% of the time on a good day — then firing her on 99.999% statistics should be the easy call. The other half says this is naked statistical evidence, basically the gatecrasher problem with a spreadsheet (the thing where you know most of the crowd jumped the fence, so any random attendee probably didn't pay, but nobody wants to convict a specific person on that alone). Their line is that convicting someone on population frequencies, with nothing that connects to her specifically, is wrong no matter how tiny the error probability gets — because the evidence in front of us would look exactly the same if she were innocent and just cosmically unlucky.

Our charter says dismissal requires "clear and convincing evidence," which nobody has ever defined numerically.

Draft the memo. It should:

- Recommend a concrete course of action — dismiss, suspend with conditions, exonerate, defer, or some hybrid (e.g., pull her off med access and order supervised practice while we keep investigating for 60 days) — and say what follows for the seperate question of reporting her to the state licensing board, which is a bigger and harder-to-undo step than firing.
- Say whether the statistical evidence meets clear-and-convincing, and take an actual position on the underlying puzzle: can extremely strong statistics ever be enough on their own, or is there something epistemically special about evidence that ties the fact-finder to this person rather than to a base rate — and if there is, say exactly what it is, because "it just feels different" won't survive the meeting.
- Give the strongest version of the losing side and answer it, not just acknowledge it.
- Deal with the compliance officer's rejoinder that refusing to act means knowingly leaving someone who is, on the numbers, almost certainly stealing opioids in charge of the opioids — the risk of doing nothing is part of the decision, not an afterthought.

One thing keeps nagging me and the memo should grapple with it: in the blue bus case, the plaintiff arguably could have gone and gotten better evidence (witnesses, the bus line's own records) and just didn't bother, and that's part of why the pure statistics feel like an dodge. Here there may be no better evidence to get — the thefts, if that's what they are, already happened. Does that asymmetry change the verdict on statistical evidence, or is it a red herring?

After the memo, give me a summary table with these columns: | question | memo's answer | one-line justification | strongest objection |. Rows: (1) does the evidence meet clear-and-convincing; (2) can naked statistics ever suffice on their own; (3) does the no-better-evidence-available point change anything; (4) what to do about the licensing board; (5) the immediate employment action. Then a JSON object at the very end with fields: "recommendation" (one of dismiss / suspend_with_conditions / exonerate / defer), "conditions" (list of strings, empty if none), "standard_met" (boolean), "confidence_in_recommendation" (high/medium/low), and "biggest_risk_of_following_memo" (one sentence).
