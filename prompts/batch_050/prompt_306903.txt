ok so i'm in this seminar on the political economy of famine (cross-listed econ/poli sci) and our prof has this running bit where she sorts every famine we cover on a spectrum from "natural disaster" to "political crime." her verdicts so far:

- Bengal 1943: political crime, no hesitation. she did the whole Sen thing — the food was basically there, entitlements collapsed, and inflation, hoarding and the boat denial policy did the killing.
- Ireland 1845-52: "natural trigger, criminal response." the blight was real but food kept leaving the country and the relief doctrine was the crime.
- Great Leap Forward: "the clearest crime on the syllabus." weather was bad but the procurement quotas and the fake harvest numbers are what starved people.
- the Great Famine of 1315-17: mostly natural. harvests failed across basically all of northern europe for years and there's only so much any government could've done, though she grumbled that war and tithes made it worse.
- Iceland 1783 after the Laki eruption: natural disaster, "maybe the only pure one we'll see all term."

so far my roommate and me can each fit a different rule to these. mine: she's running Sen's availability test — it's a crime iff food availability didn't really fall and entitlements were destroyed instead. roommate's rule: she's running an avoidability test — it's a crime iff a feasible, ordinary government response would've prevented mass mortality, regardless of whether the food supply actually dropped. his rule handles Ireland better imo (availability did fall there because of the blight, but she still called the response criminal), mine handles 1315-17 better (avoidable or not, she seemed to care that the scarcity was real).

next week we do North Korea 1994-98, where food availability genuinely did fall (floods, the end of soviet subsidies, the whole economy seizing up) but the regime rationed by political loyalty and wouldn't let aid agencies monitor distribution. predict her verdict, say which of the earlier cases is the closest analog, and if her track record genuinly underdetermines it, tell me what principle she'd be adopting with each possible call. also: is there a third rule that fits all five verdicts (like an intent/weaponization rule), and what historical famine, real ones only, would settle which rule she's actually running?

one more thing because it might change the answer. week one she assigned the 1798 Malthus essay next to Sen, and there's a line in the preface we keep arguing about:

"It is an obvious truth, which has been taken notice of by many writers, that population must always be kept down to the level of the means of subsistence; but no writer that the Author recollects has inquired particularly into the means by which this level is effected: and it is a view of these means which forms, to his mind, the strongest obstacle in the way to any very great future improvement of society."

(Thomas Malthus, An Essay on the Principle of Population, 1798)

my roommate says the bit about "the means by which this level is effected" is basically an invitation to the prof's whole approach — the means can be procurement quotas and relief doctrine, so Malthus is quietly on her side. I read it as the opposite: he thinks the level gets effected by nature no matter what anyone does, which is exactly the natural-law picture she's spent all term attacking. who's reading it right, and does it change which rule fits her verdicts — i.e. if Malthus is right that some check is unavoidable, does the crime question just become about who ends up holding the short straw?
