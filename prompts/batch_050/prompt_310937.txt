I'm a third-year taking a course in social choice theory and we've just covered the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem (roughly: with three or more alternatives, any voting rule that's non-dictatorial and can actually produce any of the outcomes is manipulable, meaning there's some possible configuration of preferences where at least one voter does better by misreporting their true ranking). Down the pub afterwards me and a friend got into an argument about the 2000 US presidential election, and I'd like to settle it properly instead of just gesturing at the theorem.

The setup as I understand it: Bush won Florida by 537 votes, Nader (the Green candidate) took about 97,000 votes there, and most Nader voters, going by exit polls, preferred Gore to Bush as their second choice. My friend says the moral is simple: plurality rule is terrible, switch to ranked ballots and the spoiler effect is solved, strategic voting is a design flaw we could engineer away. My instinct is the theorem says something bleaker and more precise than that, but when I try to spell out exactly what, I get tangled. So walk me through the 2000 election as a case study for Gibbard–Satterthwaite, in both directions. Be careful with the mapping: who exactly is a 'player' here (individual voters? voting blocs? campaigns? Nader himself deciding to run?), what counts as 'misrepresenting your preferences' when the electorate is a hundred million people who can't coordinate, and does a theorem about the *existence* of some profile where manipulation pays even license claims about what real voters should or will do? Then my friend's fix: since GS covers every non-dictatorial rule, instant runoff can't be strategy-proof either — so what precisely would it have fixed about Florida 2000, and what would it leave untouched? Is there a real difference between a rule being manipulable in principle and manipulable in practice, given that exploiting a rule seems to require knowing everyone else's preferences, which nobody in a mass election does? And the thing that bugged me most: the theorem takes the set of alternatives as given, but the whole Florida story turns on Nader *choosing to be on the ballot* — is candidate entry a kind of manipulation GS is just silent about, or can it be folded in somehow? Finally, run it backwards: in 2000 tons of voters apparently did vote strategically (all the 'a vote for Nader is a vote for Bush' discourse) and the spoiler effect happened anyway. Does that confirm the theorem's practical bite, or does it suggest the theorem's notion of manipulation doesn't actually capture the failure mode of real elections?
