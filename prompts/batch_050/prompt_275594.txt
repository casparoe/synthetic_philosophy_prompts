I spent my career as an experimental scientist (molecular biology, mostly microscopy) and have turned to philosophy in retirement. Over the past year I have been reading in aesthetics, and after a long argument with a friend -- a retired music teacher who insists that all taste is ultimately subjective -- I tried to write down exactly what I believe about beauty and taste, so that the position could be examined on its merits. I would like you to audit the set: tell me which commitments are genuinely in tension with each other, which only appear to be, and what the least costly revision would be. Please rank any tensions by severity rather than treating them all alike, and for each one state precisely what would have to give way.

Here is the set. (1) The old saying about taste is wrong as usually understood: some tastes are genuinely better than others, and a person who prefers hack verse to Milton is making a mistake, not merely differing. (2) Hume's account in his essay on the standard of taste is roughly correct: the standard is fixed by the joint verdict of "true judges" -- those with delicacy of sentiment, practice, experience of comparisons, and freedom from prejudice. (3) Beauty is not a property residing in objects; all aesthetic value consists in, or supervenes on, human responses and sentiments. (4) The standard of taste is not itself a matter of taste: whether someone qualifies as a true judge is a factual question, in principle checkable, much as one can check whether a wine taster can genuinely distinguish two vintages blind. (5) The convergence of practiced judges across centuries and cultures is the main evidence that (1) is true. (6) I am suspicious of expert convergence as evidence, because experts are all trained on the same canon: their agreement may be an artifact of shared training rather than a discovery about the works, the way a model that has seen its own test set tells you nothing. (7) Training does not merely change how one judges; it changes what one perceives and feels. I know this from microscopy -- after years at the eyepiece I did not interpret the image better, I saw a different image. The refined judge's pleasure is a different pleasure from the novice's, not the same pleasure more accurately reported.

A few remarks on what would help. I suspect (5) and (6) sit awkwardly together, but I may be wrong about how badly; I am more interested in whether (1), (3), and (7) can all be maintained at once, since (1) seems to require that the novice and the expert are disagreeing about the same thing, and I am no longer sure (7) permits that. I also cannot tell whether (3) and (4) are in real conflict or only look that way. If the set is salvageable, tell me the single revision that cleans up the most; if it is not, tell me which commitment is doing the damage.
