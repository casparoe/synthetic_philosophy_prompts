I am a novelist at work on a book whose characters are defined by how they treat their past investments: one stays in a marriage for the twenty years already given to it, one abandons a manuscript at page three hundred, one prosecutes a family feud whose original grievance no one can remember. To keep them from blurring into a single type, I want a decision tree that sorts positions on the sunk cost fallacy -- the question whether honoring sunk costs is ever rational, and if so, on what grounds. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of named authors: each branch should be a concrete diagnostic question phrased so that someone could answer it without knowing the labels (the expensive meal one forces down, the war continued so the dead shall not have died in vain, the degree finished in a subject one no longer likes), and each leaf a distinct position, such as the strict view that sunk costs are simply irrelevant, the self-binding view that honoring them protects one's future resolve, the self-signaling view, the narrative-identity view, and the view that fidelity to one's past self is a virtue in its own right. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, say briefly whether the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; and flag any pair of positions your tree cannot separate rather than papering over the difference.
