I teach a survey course in philosophy of science for first-year undergraduates, and last week a student defended a claim I want to scrutinize properly before I respond to her. We had been discussing Galileo's falling-bodies case and Einstein's elevator, and she argued -- deploying an analogy she said she'd picked up from a popular science book -- that a thought experiment is simply an experiment run in the laboratory of the mind: the imagination is the apparatus, intuition is the measuring instrument, idealization plays the role of the controlled conditions, and the "result" is genuine new knowledge about the physical world, obtained without touching anything. On her view, this explains how thought experiments can teach us things we didn't already know, since a real experiment also just rearranges what the experimenter already had access to and yet produces surprises. One of my colleagues in the department takes the opposite line: he says a thought experiment is at most a dressed-up argument, since nothing new enters the premises, so any appearance of discovery is really just the unpacking of assumptions you already held, and the laboratory analogy launders that circularity.

I can see that both are leaning on the same comparison and drawing opposite morals from it, which makes me suspect the analogy itself is doing unexamined work. What I'd like is a careful audit of the mind-as-laboratory analogy: what maps onto what, which correspondences are genuinely apt and which are misleading, what the analogy quietly imports from real experimentation (fallibility, surprise, replication by others, resistance from nature) that the armchair version lacks or only simulates, and whether the student's conclusion -- that thought experiments yield knowledge beyond what's contained in their premises -- survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it collapses into my colleague's deflationary view or into some third position.

Please give your answer as exactly six numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion: the first two should lay out the correspondence table, the next two should identify the disanalogies that matter most for the knowledge claim (as opposed to cosmetic ones), the fifth should say whether the student's conclusion survives, and the sixth should propose a better analogy if one exists or explain why none is needed.
