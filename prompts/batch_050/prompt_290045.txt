I am taking an interdisciplinary seminar on the historiography of the First World War, and we have just finished the unit on Fritz Fischer's Germany's Aims in the First World War and the controversy it ignited. Early in the course we read Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles (1919), the so-called war guilt clause, which runs: "The Allied and Associated Governments affirm and Germany accepts the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage to which the Allied and Associated Governments and their nationals have been subjected as a consequence of the war imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies." The treaty's German critics received this as a moral verdict on their nation, while some Allied commentators insisted it was merely the juridical basis for reparations. In a study group afterward, a classmate who double-majors in philosophy argued that both readings miss the deeper problem: the clause, and indeed Fischer-style theses about national war guilt, are not false but conceptually confused. Her argument ran as follows. (1) Blame is in order only toward agents, beings that deliberate, choose, and could have done otherwise. (2) A nation is not an agent in that sense; it has no mind over and above the minds of its members, and strictly speaking only individuals deliberate and choose. (3) So if anyone is blameworthy for the First World War, it must be particular individuals -- a chancellor, a foreign minister, a chief of the general staff. (4) But the war issued from a long chain of decisions distributed across many individuals in several governments, each choice locally intelligible under radical uncertainty, and no one of them intended, let alone single-handedly caused, the catastrophe that actually occurred. (5) Therefore no individual is blameworthy for the war as such, and Article 231's attribution of responsibility to Germany is a legal fiction for billing purposes rather than a moral judgment. (6) Notice that this reasoning is perfectly general: the same considerations show that no mob, corporation, army, or state can ever be blamed for anything, and that any disaster whose causes are sufficiently distributed is nobody's fault. (7) But we blame such collectives constantly and apparently correctly -- firms for oil spills, mobs for lynchings, governments for famines -- and a principle that renders every collectively produced catastrophe blameless would dissolve moral responsibility across huge stretches of ordinary life. (8) So something in (1) through (5) has to go.

Here is where I am stuck. I find (1) and (2) almost non-negotiable: methodological individualism about agency strikes me as the sober view, and I would much rather locate the error somewhere between (3) and (7) than concede that nations are literal agents with minds. At the same time, every specific repair I attempt seems to cost me something I also want -- for instance, if I say individuals can be blamed for outcomes they did not intend, I worry I have made blame too easy to incur; if I say the clause trades on a different, non-moral notion of responsibility, I worry I have conceded my classmate's conclusion under a friendlier label. Please tell me which premise or inferential step you think should be rejected, explain why that step looked plausible in the first place, and price out the alternatives I might choose instead. I would also like to know whether your diagnosis interacts with the historical question: does it matter, for the conceptual analysis, whether Fischer was right that key German decision-makers deliberately sought or consciously risked a European war in July 1914, or are the conceptual and empirical questions fully independent of each other? Please give your answer in American English.
