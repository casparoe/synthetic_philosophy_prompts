I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, reading W. K. Clifford's essay The Ethics of Belief alongside William James's reply, The Will to Believe. The sentence I keep returning to comes from Clifford's shipowner case, at the point where he asks us to imagine the ship making its voyage safely after all:

"The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him."

Two colleagues of mine, a strict Cliffordian evidentialist and a committed Jamesian, have agreed to stop debating and write up an adversarial collaboration. Draft their joint statement. It should contain: a numbered list of claims both can sign, including what James plainly concedes to Clifford and a shared reading of the quoted sentence — in particular, whether the "right to believe" it invokes is epistemic, moral, or both; a table of their residual disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording — I expect these to include whether there are options evidence cannot settle where deciding on passional grounds is permissible, and whether self-fulfilling beliefs (the confidence that helps a team ship, the trust that sustains a collaboration) refute the quoted sentence or can be redescribed in Cliffordian terms; the cruxes, with a case or argument both agree bears on each and each side's prediction recorded in advance, one of which should be my working case: deploying a dependency on a respected colleague's say-so without auditing it; and, if they conclude some of the dispute is verbal — say, whether James is defending belief at all or something more like a working faith — which part, and what survives once it is cleaned up.
