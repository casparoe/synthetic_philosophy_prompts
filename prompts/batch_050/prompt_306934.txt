You're a philosopher who works on responsibility and technology. Think carefully here — brainstorm a few candidate theories before committing to one.

Here's the practice I'm puzzled by. When a self-driving car or some other autonomous system hurts someone, our institutions do something kind of incoherent-looking. Criminally, we hunt for a culpable human — the safety driver who wasn't watching the road, the engineer who cut corners — and if no human was negligent, we just don't charge anyone, even though someone is dead. Civilly we do almost the opposite: product liability can hit the manufacturer even when they did everything a reasonable company would do. And meanwhile nobody seriously proposes sanctioning the system itself; ideas like AI legal personhood have been floated for years and gone nowhere.

So: what theory of moral responsibility does this whole setup presuppose? Reconstruct it. Does criminal law's refusal to convict without a culpable human presuppose that responsibility requires capacities — awareness, reasons-responsiveness, something like that — which current systems lack? Or is it just a pragmatic stopgap that would collapse the moment systems get sophisticated enough? And is the strict-liability civil side quietly working from a different theory entirely, where "responsibility" is really just loss-spreading? Tell me which parts of the practice strictly need the theory you reconstruct, and which parts are compatible with several rival theories.

Then the harder question: if the "responsibility gap" people are right that some future harms will have no fitting bearer at all, what would a practice that took that seriously actually look like — robot defendants, mandatory victim-compensation funds, or is "some harms simply have no one responsible for them" a coherent verdict our current concepts can recieve?

One more thing: flag where your reconstruction is shakiest. What argument, or what fact about how these systems actually work, would push you toward a different reading of the practice?
