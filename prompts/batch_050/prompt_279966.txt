Okay so I just spent like three hours arguing with a buddy of mine about Slaughterhouse-Five and the philosophy of time and we got nowhere, so I want an outside opinion. His claim: the Tralfamadorians are basically eternalists. They see all moments at once, Billy becomes "unstuck in time," and every death is just one moment among infinitely many that are all equally real, which is why "so it goes" is supposed to be the right reaction -- grief is a kind of category error if the dead person is still perfectly alive in 1944, just not in the slice you're currently looking at. He thinks Vonnegut is dramatizing the block universe, full stop.

My pushback was that this flattens the book. For one, the Tralfamadorians don't just believe all moments exist, they also seem totally fatalistic in a way that eternalism by itself doesn't entail -- plenty of eternalists think the future is real AND that what you do matters in fixing what it contains. For another, the novel drops pretty heavy hints that Tralfamadore might be Billy's trauma coping mechanism rather than the actual metaphysics of Vonnegut's world, in which case the "reading" isn't eternalism vindicated, it's eternalism as the story a shattered man tells himself to survive Dresden.

So: read the novel through the presentism/eternalism debate. Where does the Tralfamadorian picture actually line up with eternalism as philosophers defend it, and where is it smuggling in extra commitments (fatalism, the irrelevance of emotion, moral quietism) that eternalism doesn't come with? Does "so it goes" follow from the block universe, or is it a non sequitur the book itself might be ambivalent about? And if Billy's time travel is delusion rather than insight, does that make the book an argument AGAINST eternalism-as-consolation -- like, is Vonnegut showing us what it costs a person to actually believe it?

Please don't just pick my side or his. If the text genuinely underdetermines some of this, say so. Also, be upfront about how confident you are in each of your claims, and at the end single out the one point in your reading where you're least sure you've got it right.
