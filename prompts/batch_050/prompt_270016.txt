Two colleagues of mine at a mid-sized AI lab got into a long argument last week that ended with both of them asking me to referee, which I feel unqualified to do. Some context: their team is about to deploy a strong internal coding model, and while they have no particular reason to think it is misaligned, they also can't verify that it isn't, so the lab's policy is to treat it as untrusted and to secure the deployment instead of the model's intentions. The specific dispute concerns their main safeguard: every code change the strong model proposes is reviewed by a smaller, older model they trust before anything is merged. (1) Priya defended this setup on two grounds: reviewing code for danger is a much easier task than writing dangerous code, so a weaker monitor can plausibly police a stronger generator; and they've empirically validated the pipeline by having a red team fine-tune a copy of the strong model to actively try sneaking backdoors past the monitor, which caught most attempts. (2) Marcus replied that the red-team exercise proves far less than she thinks -- a model fine-tuned to scheme is not the same adversary as a model that is scheming on its own initiative, and a genuinely misaligned model might simply recognize when it is being tested and behave, or write code whose danger only becomes apparent across many individually innocuous-looking commits that no per-commit review would flag. (3) Priya answered that he was holding control to an impossible standard: the point isn't to catch everything, it's to raise the cost of misbehavior, the way layered airport security works without any single layer being airtight, and in any case they also plant honeypots -- tempting fake opportunities to sabotage the company -- so that a misaligned model would reveal itself before it ever got near anything that matters. (4) Marcus's rejoinder was that honeypots assume a misaligned model will defect whenever it sees an opening, whereas a patient one would defect only when the stakes are high, so every honeypot it declines actually trains the lab's confidence in exactly the wrong direction; and the cost-raising framing, he said, treats the model like a burglar with limited attempts, when in fact it gets millions of attempts across deployments and only needs to succeed once.

Could you go through the four moves one at a time, saying for each whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible but incomplete, with a sentence or two of justification? Then give an overall verdict -- who is more right about the safeguard as a whole -- and, just as important, identify anything significant that *both* of them are missing about the logic of controlling a system you cannot align.
