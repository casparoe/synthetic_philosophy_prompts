I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation on rational choice in strategic contexts, and I've come to accept a position I'd like stress-tested before I commit another year to it. The position concerns the well-known desert case: you're stranded, a driver offers rescue if he'll be paid once you're in town, he is an extremely reliable reader of character, and he will only bother if he believes you'll actually pay. I hold that the rational agent pays on arrival, and more generally that rationality can require an act you know to be against your interests at the moment of acting, because having become the sort of person who performs it was the only route to the benefit. My dissertation's contribution, which I haven't seen discussed in the literature, is an extension: a variant where the driver's reliability responds to the structure of your motives rather than to a fixed intention. Suppose his scan detects how much money still moves you — the more the payment would sting, the less confident he is that you'll go through with it, and the less likely he is to stop. Then intending to pay while privately planning to defect is useless, and so is a frozen, white-knuckled intention to pay formed at the moment of the scan. What rationality demands is that you genuinely erode your concern for money in advance, so that by the time you're rescued, paying is easy — and the person you've become pays not out of commitment or self-binding but out of what is now ordinary indifference to the sum involved. I claim this shows the core position doesn't rest on any puzzling power to bind a reluctant future self: what rationality requires is a deliberatively induced change in one's own evaluative dispositions, after which the contested act is rational in the most ordinary sense for the person one has rationally become.

What would count against this position? I'd like a map of its exposure rather than a verdict alone. Which intuitions, possible cases, or formal results would defeat it outright, which would merely be awkward, and can you order them accordingly? Does the motivational-erosion variant actually escape the standard objections to resolute choice and to constrained maximization, or does it inherit them in a disguised form — for instance, does it just relocate the puzzle from "how can the later act be rational" to "how can the earlier self-manipulation be rational when undertaken for a benefit that, by the agent's own lights, accrues to a person with different values"? What would defenders of resolute choice themselves concede as genuine costs, as opposed to what critics say the costs are? And what does the position predict that its rivals — straightforward causal decision theory, or views that treat the case as one where rationality simply parts company with self-interest — do not? If you conclude the position is well insulated against counterexample, I'd like to know whether you think that insulation is a virtue or a warning sign. Finally, please be explicit about your own epistemic standing: which consideration, if it were established, would most readily change your verdict, and where is your reasoning in the answer most likely to be going wrong?
