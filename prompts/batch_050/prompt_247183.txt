After forty years as a research chemist, I have spent my retirement reading moral philosophy, and I have run into a pattern I cannot find named anywhere. Consider three rules of conduct. First: leave for the airport at dawn in order to beat the traffic. Second: take a little-known back road to avoid the congestion. Both work only because most people do not follow them; universal adoption cancels the very advantage they aim at. Third, for contrast: board the emptiest carriage of the train. If everyone followed that rule, the load would simply balance, and nobody's benefit would evaporate.

My difficulty is with the first two. They fail the "what if everyone did it?" check, yet nothing about them seems morally wrong: no one is deceived, no cooperative practice is exploited, and no one is made worse off by my taking the back road. Compare fare-dodging, where universal adoption would destroy the railway the dodger depends on -- that case does feel wrong. So either Kant's universal-law test condemns the morally innocent here, or there is a distinction among failures of the test that I lack the vocabulary for: between rules whose universal adoption destroys a practice the agent relies on, and rules whose universal adoption merely erases a relative advantage while harming no one.

Is there an established name for the second kind of failure, or for the distinction itself? I am not after a label alone. I want to know what the phenomenon is exactly, how it differs from free-riding and from the two versions of Kant's contradiction test, and where its edges are. If nothing established fits, please coin a term, define it, and test it against queue-jumping, choosing the shortest checkout line, and buying an asset precisely because it is unfashionable.
