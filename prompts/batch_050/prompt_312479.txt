I'm TAing a second-year philosophy of mind course (about 20 undergrads, one 75-minute session) and we're in the middle of two weeks on Dennett's intentional stance. It's going badly. Half the class has decided the framework means chatbots literally have beliefs and desires, the other half thinks the whole thing is a semantic trick for dodging the hard problem, and both groups are just talking past each other in section. The prof suggested I stop lecturing at them and make them *use* the stance instead, so I want a full in-class activity where students have to attribute (or refuse to attribute) beliefs to something in order to win at a game, before the vocabulary gets attached to what they're doing. What they should come away seeing: that belief-talk is doing real predictive work for them even in cases where they'd swear the system has no beliefs, and also that there are cases where anthropomorphizing actively hurts you — the stance isn't free.

Concretely, I'm imagining some kind of black-box prediction game: teams compete to predict the outputs of hidden systems that vary in how agent-like they really are (maybe a dumb rule-based thing, something more LLM-flavored, and ideally one perverse system that punishes you for treating it as rational, like an opponent that deliberately does the irrational move). But you should design the actual mechanics — rules, what materials I need (has to run on index cards and one laptop, no fancy tech or internet access in the room), team sizes, scoring, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, and crucially the debrief: which moments in the game map onto Dennett's three stances, which map onto the live "do LLMs have beliefs" debate, and how to connect the two without me just re-lecturing. Also tell me what's going to predictably go wrong the first time I run it — e.g. students reverse-engineering the hidden systems instead of playing, or a team that refuses on principle to anthropomorphize anything and then loses in a way that muddies the lesson (or wins, which would muddy it worse) — and how to handle each. And please be honest about which part of the philosophical dispute the game *can't* settle: my worry is it shows the stance is predictively useful without touching whether the patterns it tracks are "really there" or just observer-relative, and I'd rather flag that explicitly in the debrief than have some sharp student point it out and feel like the whole exercise was rigged.
