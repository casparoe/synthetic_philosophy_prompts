I'm a junior on my high school's student advisory committee, and this year we've been asked to review the student discipline code before it's revised. Reading it closely, I've noticed what seems like a strange split personality. Half the code is rigid and mechanical: plagiarism on any assignment means an automatic zero and a report on your record, no exceptions, and there's a chart matching each infraction to a fixed number of detention days. The other half is the opposite -- our new "restorative justice" program handles fights, bullying, and conflicts through mediated conversations where trained facilitators consider everyone's circumstances, intentions, home situations, and relationships, and outcomes can be completely different for superficially identical incidents. When I asked our assistant principal why plagiarism gets the flowchart while a shoving match gets a circle of chairs and an hour of discussion, she said some rules "just have to be absolute to mean anything," while people "deserve to be seen as whole people." Both halves were designed by thoughtful people, and honestly both feel somewhat right to me, but I can't figure out whether they can both be right at the same time.

For an ethics elective paper, I want to work out what theory of moral reasoning each half of this system presupposes. Does the zero-tolerance half assume that there are general moral rules whose authority doesn't depend on the particulars of any case -- and if so, why would it be legitimate for the cheating rule but not for the fighting rule? Does the restorative half assume that what's right in a given situation depends entirely on its specific features, so that no code could state in advance what a fair outcome is -- and if so, doesn't that undermine the whole idea of a written code with fixed penalties, including the ones for plagiarism? I'm also interested in whether the two halves secretly presuppose inconsistent things about, say, whether similar cases must be treated similarly, or whether an act's wrongness can depend on facts about the person who did it.

Please reconstruct the implicit philosophy of each half of the code as charitably as you can, tell me whether the split is principled or just an accident of which reforms happened when, and explain what would have to change in each half if the committee concluded that only one of the two underlying views is correct. It would also help to know whether the apparent tension could be dissolved some other way -- for instance, whether the two halves might rest on a single coherent distinction that neither my principal nor I have articulated. I'd like the paper to be useful as an actual submission to the committee, so concrete recommendations matter, but I mainly want to understand whether our code has a philosophy at all or just a history.
