For the past two years I've kept a daily journal — originally just to track my moods, though it has turned into something closer to an audit of my own self-knowledge. The trigger was noticing how often the explanations I gave myself didn't survive a week of hindsight: I'd write that I was short with a colleague because of a remark of hers, and then see from my own earlier entries that I'd been irritable for hours before she said a word. I've since sorted my journal entries into eight kinds of claims I make about my own mind, and I'd like a verdict on which kinds actually deserve my trust.

The eight kinds, each with a typical entry:

1. Present sensations. "There's a dull ache in my left shoulder right now."
2. Present emotions, stated plainly. "I am angry."
3. Emotions with their causes attached. "I am angry because of what my brother said this morning."
4. Reasons for past choices. "I took the apartment on Cedar Street because of the morning light."
5. Predictions about my future feelings. "I'd be happier living near the sea."
6. Standing traits. "I'm not a jealous person."
7. Comparisons with my past states. "My knee bothers me less than it did last winter."
8. Inner images. "I can picture my grandmother's kitchen clearly, down to the color of the curtains."

For what it's worth, here is my own ordering of the eight by how certain each kind feels at the moment I write it down, most certain first: 1, 4, 6, 3, 2, 8, 5, 7.

Here is what I'd like from you.

First, produce your own ranking of the eight by how much weight they deserve as evidence about my actual inner life — present, past, or future — most reliable first, with each kind given a score out of 10 and one or two sentences of justification. Commit to an order; where two kinds run close, break the tie and say what breaks it.

Second, compare your ranking with my felt-certainty ordering and single out the two or three largest gaps. For each gap, tell me which side you think is wrong — the feeling or the evidence — and what produces the mismatch. I have a suspicion that the kinds with a story attached feel more certain precisely because of the story, not because of anything underneath it, and I'd like to know whether you think that suspicion holds up.

Third, if any of my eight categories seems to you badly carved — if one of them quietly lumps together two sorts of claim with very different reliability — identify it, split it, and place the pieces separately in your ranking rather than forcing an average.

I'm not asking for citations or a survey of expert opinion. I want your own best reasoning, aimed at a practical question: which parts of my journal should I treat as a window, and which as guesswork dressed in the first person?
