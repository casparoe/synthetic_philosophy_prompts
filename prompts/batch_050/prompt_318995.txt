For my course on women philosophers of the early modern period, I'm writing a term paper on Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness, and I want to stress-test her account by translating it into a framework I know better. I also work as a research assistant in a lab that studies the psychological science of well-being, so I'm fluent in that vocabulary: hedonic versus eudaimonic conceptions, affective forecasting and its biases, hedonic adaptation and set-points, self-determination theory, the distinction between life satisfaction and momentary affect, and so on.

So that we're working from the same text, here is my summary of du Châtelet's position, which you should treat as the object of translation. She argues that happiness requires, first, a susceptibility to strong passions, since pleasure is proportional to the intensity of desire satisfied and a temperament incapable of strong feeling is condemned to tepid contentment at best. Second, and most strikingly, she defends the positive value of illusion: because the unvarnished truths of the human condition are mostly sources of sorrow, happiness requires a capacity for agreeable illusion, and one should not destroy an illusion without having something to replace it with. Third, she recommends the love of study as an inexhaustible source of pleasure that depends on no one else. Fourth, she insists on independence -- from fortune, from the opinions of others, and from romantic attachments one cannot control -- while acknowledging love as the strongest passion and hence a dangerous one. Fifth, she requires freedom from prejudice, by which she largely means religious superstition, plus virtue and health.

Please restate this position, component by component, in the vocabulary of contemporary well-being science, as faithfully as you can: which constructs does each claim map onto, and what would her predictions be about, say, adaptation, forecasting errors, or the sources of durable life satisfaction? Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care most about. Which of her claims translate cleanly? Which translate only with distortion -- I suspect the illusion thesis lands very differently once it becomes a hypothesis about self-deception and measured well-being -- and which have no expression in the framework at all, for instance because her account is openly prescriptive and aristocratic in a way descriptive psychology cannot reproduce? Finally, take a position on what the residue shows: does it expose a gap in the scientific framework (e.g., its inability to say which pleasures one ought to cultivate), or does it show that parts of du Châtelet's account were less precise than they appear once you demand operational definitions?
