So I run a small game club with some friends (we take turns picking stuff, everything from walking sims to roguelikes) and last month we got into a fight that hasn't resolved. My position, which I defended maybe too hard: what makes games a distinct art form, and where their artistic successes and failures really live, is the agency they hand the player. A game is artistically good to the extent that what *you do* in it carries aesthetic weight -- the choices, the failures, the ways the rules push back or make you complicit. On this view, a cutscene-heavy game where your input barely matters is basically borrowing its art from film, and the highest achievements of the medium would be things like a ruleset that makes you feel guilt through your own actions rather than through a scripted scene. My friend thinks this is a bunch of overgeneralizing from a few pretentious indie games, and honestly the conversation made me realize I don't actually know what my position predicts or what would prove it wrong.

So help me stress-test it. What would have to be true for the agency-centered view of games-as-art to be false, or at least badly incomplete? What cases does it handle poorly -- like, does a game with one famous cutscene and mediocre mechanics being remembered as a masterpiece embarrass the view, or can the view just say its artistic value isn't *distinctively ludic* and be fine? Does the view predict that player freedom should track artistic quality, and if so, does the existence of beloved near-choiceless games and aesthetically empty sandboxes count as decisive evidence against it or just awkward data? I'm also worried about insulation: if every counterexample gets absorbed by saying "well, the *meaningful* choices are what matter" or "agency can include choosing how to interpret things," has the view stopped risking anything? Tell me what defenders of the view (not just critics) would have to concede as genuine defeaters -- a consequence it can't accept, a case it can't describe -- versus what they'd shrug off. And if you think the view is actually so flexible it can't lose, say that, and tell me whether that's a problem for it or just what aesthetic theories are like.
