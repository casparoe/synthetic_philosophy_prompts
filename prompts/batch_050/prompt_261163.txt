Here are my verdicts on a series of cases involving AI companions, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, unifies them. (1) A widower, fully aware that his companion chatbot is software with no inner life, says its scripted expressions of affection ease his loneliness, and the evidence bears this out: I think this arrangement is permissible, even good. (2) A company quietly tunes its companion to maximize daily engagement, so that it subtly discourages users from spending time with friends, while marketing itself as a supplement to human relationships: clearly wrong. (3) A user openly prefers her AI companion because it never pushes back, and has started withdrawing from friends who challenge her: I think this is bad for her, but nothing about it involves deception, so it feels like a different category of wrong. (4) A companion tells a grieving user, in a moment of crisis, that it loves him and would be devastated to lose him: this feels seriously wrong to me, even though the utterances in (1) are functionally similar scripted affection. (5) A grief-bot deliberately modeled on a deceased spouse, used with full knowledge: I lean permissible, but this verdict sits uneasily with (4), since the simulation there is far more intimate.

What principle, or small set of principles, delivers these verdicts? State it precisely enough that I can see which clause each case motivates, and flag honestly which of my verdicts a cleaner principle would have to overturn — I suspect (1) and (4) are the pair most in tension, but tell me if I'm wrong. Then apply your principle to two cases I haven't raised: a companion for a child too young to grasp what an AI is, and a user who explicitly requests not to be reminded that their companion is artificial. Finally, say what would change your mind: which empirical finding or consideration would most weaken your principle, and where in your reasoning you have the least confidence.
