Our company — a mid-sized defense robotics firm, call it Vantage Dynamics for this — has an independent ethics advisory panel, and I'm unlucky enough to chair it. The board has asked us for a written opinion on whether the company should sign a contract to build targeting software for a loitering munition: basically a drone that circles an area and, under the proposed design, detects and ranks potential targets entirely on its own. A human operator watches the feed and gets an eight-second window after each flagged target to hit an abort button; if they don't, the strike proceeds. The marketing people call this "human-on-the-loop," and the engineers are quick to point out that technically no strike happens without passing through a human checkpoint. But the company's own field trials show operators actually press abort in under 3% of engagements, and management admits the window can't be meaningfully extended without losing the speed advantage that is the whole selling point of the weapon. Most of us suspect that 3% would shrink further in real use, under stress and fatigue.

The panel is genuinely split, and the disagreement is partly conceptual. One camp says meaningful human control is satisfied here in the only sense that matters: the human is the final node in the causal chain, every strike is counterfactually dependent on their choice not to intervene, and besides, we don't hold a fighter pilot to a higher standard when they release a bomb on a target selected for them by ground intelligence. The other camp says a veto exercised 3% of the time on an eight-second clock is a rubber stamp wearing a uniform — control is only "meaningful" if the human is in some real sense the author of the act, someone who understands and endorses the reasons for this particular strike, not a failsafe that statistically never fires. A third member keeps insisting the whole debate is confused because "meaningful human control" was never meant as a verifiable design spec at all, and so far nobody has convinced her otherwise.

Draft the opinion I'll circulate before the vote. It should recommend one of: sign the contract, decline it, or sign with specific conditions (and if conditions, ones the engineers could actually test for, not vibes). Take a position on which sense of "meaningful" is the right one — causal placement in the chain versus something like authorship — and show what follows for this design either way. And give the strongest version of the losing side: if you reccomend declining, take seriously the point about the fighter pilot and ground intelligence; if you recommend signing, take seriously the worry that the 3% veto rate tells us the "control" is decorative.
