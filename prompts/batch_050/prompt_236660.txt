I am a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy, working in philosophy of mind, and through a training arrangement between my department and the university hospital I serve as the graduate-student member of the hospital's clinical ethics committee. At next Thursday's meeting the committee must issue a written opinion on the case below, and the chair has asked me to produce the draft. I would like you to write it.

The patient is a 58-year-old man who, three years ago, underwent an anterior cingulotomy for intractable chronic pain after failed back surgeries. The operation worked, in a manner of speaking: he developed pain asymbolia. He says he still feels pain, locates it accurately, and rates its intensity much as before (he will report 8 out of 10 for a fresh burn), but it no longer bothers him — he compares it to noticing that a room is warm. Neurology and psychiatry have evaluated him twice: the change is stable and specific, he shows no general flattening of affect and no cognitive impairment, and he passes every standard capacity assessment without difficulty.

Two consequences bring him to us. First, because pain no longer alarms him, he has accumulated serious injuries — a fractured foot he walked on for six weeks, second-degree burns from a stove. He now inspects his own body every evening, in his words, the way other people check their email. Second, he needs repeated surgical debridement on that foot in order to keep the leg. He consents to the surgery but refuses anything beyond minimal local anesthesia, saying the pain gives him no reason to accept sedation he dislikes and that he would rather read through the procedure. Our pain-management policy, written for ordinary patients, says that pain is whatever the patient reports it to be and that self-reported pain above a threshold must be treated; the pain service is split over whether the policy obligates them to medicate a man who reports severe pain and asks them not to. Meanwhile his sister, who is his healthcare proxy only if he loses capacity, has asked the committee to find that a man whose pain does not move him lacks the insight to refuse either anesthesia or the supervised living arrangement she is proposing.

Please draft the opinion, roughly 1,000–1,300 words, in a form the committee could actually adopt: a clear bottom line on both the anesthesia question and the capacity question, a concrete course of action with a review timeframe, and the reasoning behind each. The committee will not accept a verdict that hides behind a definition, so the draft must take a position on the conceptual question the case forces — where the badness of pain resides, if not in its sensory intensity, and what follows for a duty to relieve pain that is felt but not minded — while engaging honestly with the strongest consideration on the losing side of each question. For the anesthesia question, that is the pain service's worry that something is owed to severe reported pain regardless of how it is experienced, and that a hospital which lets a patient be debrided awake has failed him even if he never complains. For the capacity question, it is the sister's point that his preferences are now formed without the very signal on which everyone else's prudential judgment relies, and that his impressive capacity scores may be testing the wrong thing. Close with a short implementation note (who does what before and after the first scheduled procedure) and flag any point where you think the committee is making a policy judgment rather than following a conceptual argument, so that members can see where the genuine discretion lies.
