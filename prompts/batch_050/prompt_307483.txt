Next week my philosophy of logic reading group is doing the Church-Turing thesis, and I want to spend the session on a question people in the room keep talking past each other on: not whether the thesis is true, but what kind of claim it even is. A definition? A theorem? An empirical hypothesis? Something in between? So I'd like a decision tree that takes someone from "sure, Turing machines capture exactly the effectively calculable functions" to a named position on the epistemic status of that identification.

Some ground rules for the tree:

- Build it from the logical space first, not from a list of famous names. Figure out which questions genuinely seperate the positions, then locate the actual positions in that space. Roughly 5-7 branch questions and 6-8 leaves, but adjust if the space demands otherwise.
- Every branch node should be phrased as a question a smart student could answer without knowing any of the literature. So not "do you take computability to be an explication in Carnap's sense?" but something like "if someone demonstrated an intuitively mechanical, step-by-step procedure for computing a function no Turing machine computes, would that refute the thesis, or would it just mean we should adjust how we use the word 'effective'?"
- One node has to pull apart the thesis about what an idealized human following explicit rules could compute from the stronger thesis about what any physical system whatsoever can compute. Students conflate these constantly and then get hopelessly confused about whether a hypothetical hypercomputer would "refute Church-Turing," so I want the tree to force that distinction early.
- The leaves should include at least: the stipulative-definition view; the explication view; the view that the thesis has essentially been proven by axiomatizing what a human computor does and deriving Turing computability; the quasi-empirical view (the thesis as a mathematical identification on par with others, supported by the confluence of independent formalisms and the absence of counterexamples); and the view that it's a straight-up empirical hypothesis about human cognitive capacities. Name actual defenders where you can. If some combination of answers is uninhabited, keep it as a leaf anyway with one sentence diagnosing why -- incoherent, merely unpopular, or genuinely unexplored?
- Flag any pairs of leaves your tree can't really separate. I suspect "it's a definition" and "it's an explication" are annoyingly close neighbors, and I'd rather you mark the seam honestly than pretend it's clean.
- End each leaf with one sentence on the view's most awkward commitment -- e.g., does the definition-view have to say that all the evidence-marshalling about confluent formalisms was somehow beside the point?
