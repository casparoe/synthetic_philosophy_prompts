I'm a software engineer on a team that does internal forecasting for project timelines, and lately we've been having arguments about how to handle persistent disagreements between people who see the same dashboards. In the middle of one of these, a coworker confidently cited "Aumann's agreement theorem" and sent around a summary he'd written up from memory of a couple of blog posts. His summary goes like this: "Aumann proved that two perfectly rational agents who share the same prior beliefs cannot honestly disagree forever. If two such agents each update on their private evidence and then tell each other their conclusions, the mere fact that they know each other's estimates — and know that the other knows, and so on — forces their estimates to coincide. So whenever two smart, honest people keep disagreeing after talking it over, at least one of them is being irrational, or they started from different priors, which is itself a kind of irrationality since there are objectively correct priors. The practical upshot is that debating the reasons for your beliefs is pointless; you should just exchange your bottom-line probabilities, because reasons can be spun but numbers can't, and iterating the exchange of numbers a few times will converge you to agreement."

Something in here smells off to me — in particular the leap from a theorem about idealized agents to a verdict about actual humans, and the claim about correct priors, which I don't remember being part of any theorem — but I can't pinpoint what's fair and what isn't. I also have a vague memory that the "tell each other and you'll converge" part is a separate result from Aumann's original theorem, not the theorem itself, but I'm not sure.
