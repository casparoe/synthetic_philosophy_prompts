I direct a graduate program in philosophy, and something that happened last month has followed me into my exam-writing. I had to formally sanction a student for missing a major grant-reporting deadline -- a deadline I myself blew, quietly and without sanction, two years ago. When I told her the lateness showed a failure of professionalism, she replied, evenly, that I of all people had no business making that judgment. The uncomfortable part is that I still think the judgment was true. Since then I have been reading the literature on standing to blame and hypocrisy, and I now want my upper-level seminar's final exam to force students through the problem I couldn't resolve in the hallway.

Please draft one essay question on standing to blame, suitable for a 75-minute slot in a final exam for advanced undergraduates who have already done the unit on hypocrisy and the "who are you to talk?" retort. The question must not be answerable by reciting that hypocrites "lose standing" and summarizing one or two accounts of why. I want it to put pressure on the precise spot my case exposed: the standing complaint and the truth of the blame seem to come apart, so a student has to say what exactly is defective about hypocritical blame if not its content. In particular, a good answer should have to take a position on at least one of the live accounts of what standing consists in -- for example, the view that blaming involves claiming an authority the hypocrite has forfeited by violating the very norm invoked, versus the view that the defect lies in the blamer's lacking genuine commitment to the values the blame expresses -- and it should have to handle at least one hard case that each account handles badly: the reformed hypocrite who has stopped doing the wrong but never answered for it, and the case where the person without standing directs the same criticism at a third party rather than at their fellow wrongdoer.

Deliverables: (1) the exam question as it would appear on the paper, including any short scenario it sets up and the marks and time allocation; (2) a model answer of roughly 600 words written at the level of a genuinely excellent student -- not a survey of positions, but an argued answer that commits to a resolution; (3) a marking scheme built around five must-hit points totaling 20 marks, where each point names the specific move a top answer must make (not a topic area but a move, e.g. an argument or a distinction, stated concretely), the marks it carries, and a one-line note on the characteristic way weaker students fumble that point; and (4) a short paragraph stating what separates an A answer from a B answer overall, framed in terms of what each can and cannot explain about cases like mine, where the blame is accurate and the blamer is compromised.
