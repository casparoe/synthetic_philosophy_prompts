I've been reading a popular book on the philosophy of mind, and its chapter on consciousness summarises epiphenomenalism as follows: 'Epiphenomenalism is the view that conscious experiences are generated by brain processes but themselves generate nothing — they are like the whistle of a steam locomotive, a by-product that contributes nothing to the machine's operation. This produces a devastating and obvious problem. If your feeling of pain causes nothing at all, then it cannot be what causes you to believe that you are in pain, nor what causes you to say so. The epiphenomenalist's own assertion that she is conscious must therefore be entirely the product of unconscious brain matter, with her experiences playing no role in producing her beliefs or reports about them. So the view is self-refuting: nobody could ever know, or even justifiably claim, that epiphenomenalism is true, since knowledge requires a causal connection between the knower and the thing known. Unsurprisingly, philosophers abandoned the position long ago, and it survives today only as a historical curiosity, much like élan vital in biology.' Something in this troubles me — I'm inclined to think the view is wrong, but I suspect the case made here is sloppier than the view deserves, and I don't know the literature well enough to locate the trouble. Please audit the summary: which parts accurately characterise epiphenomenalism and the genuine difficulty it faces (which I believe is sometimes called the paradox of phenomenal judgment), which parts conflate a serious challenge with a strict self-refutation, which parts misstate what the view's defenders actually say in response, and whether the closing historical claim holds up. Give your answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: the first on what the summary gets right about the view itself, the second on what it gets right about the problem, the third on whether 'self-refuting' follows, the fourth on the strongest reply available to an epiphenomenalist, and the fifth on the claim that the position is a dead historical curiosity.
