I recently became a parent through adoption, and at a meeting of our local adoptive-parent support group the conversation turned to a news item about the heritability of educational attainment. Half the room heard it as fatalism -- that what we do for our kids barely matters -- and the other half heard it as a license to stop worrying about their kids' schools entirely, since "it's mostly genes anyway." I volunteered to give a short talk next month, twenty minutes plus discussion, to an audience of smart adults with no statistics background, and I've realized I understand heritability well enough to be annoyed by both readings but not well enough to design an explanation that reliably dismantles them. Please plan the talk rather than give it.

What I need is the explanatory architecture: the order in which the pieces have to be introduced, where the order is forced by dependency (e.g., does the idea of variance decomposition have to precede any talk of causes, and if so, what is the minimal intuitive version of it that won't be mistaken for a pie chart of "nature versus nurture" for a single child?), and which pieces are actually independent and can be sequenced for emotional effect. Then the carrying example: I have in mind using variation in plant height across uniformly and non-uniformly watered garden plots, but please assess whether that example can bear the weight -- what it gets right, what it misleads about, and whether something closer to home (say, reading ability across households with different numbers of books) would work better despite being more fraught. Tell me what the example must contain and must scrupulously lack.

Finally, map the misconceptions in the order this particular audience will generate them. I expect at least: high heritability means fixed and unchangeable; heritability within a group tells us about differences between groups; heritability of a trait tells adoptive parents their influence is small; and a heritability estimate is a property of the trait rather than of the population-and-environment it was measured in. For each, tell me where in the sequence to head it off -- some presumably must be defused before the carrying example lands, others only after the formal idea is in place. Then give me one question I can pose at the end whose answers would show the room actually grasped the concept rather than merely absorbed a slogan, and name the parts of the full picture (G×E interaction, rGE, molecular versus twin-study estimates) that I should openly decline to cover and say why I'm declining.
