I am a PhD student in philosophy, and for a dissertation chapter on personal identity I have been tabulating my own intuitions about cases in the hope that some principle underlies them. I would like you to do the reflective-equilibrium work for me: find the principle, if there is one, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save.

Here are my verdicts. First, standard brain transplant: my brain is moved into a healthy donor body, and the resulting person wakes with all my memories and character. I survive, and I go where the brain goes. Second, a Dennett-style separation: my brain is removed and maintained in a vat, but it continues to run my body through a flawless high-bandwidth radio link, so that from the inside nothing changes; I see through the body's eyes and speak through its mouth. Here my gut says the opposite of the first case: so long as the link is live, I am where the body is, and if technicians told me the vat was being shipped to another city I would not take myself to be moving. Third, if the link is then permanently severed and the body dies while the vat brain keeps generating conscious experience, my gut flips back: whatever life continues in the vat is mine, diminished but mine. Fourth, teletransportation with destruction: I am scanned, the original is vaporized, and an atom-for-atom replica is assembled on Mars with all my memories. I survive, though I hold this less confidently than the others. Fifth, the branch-line variant: the scan does not destroy me, the replica is assembled on Mars anyway, and I am shown a video call with him. The man in the chair is me; the man on Mars is a very well-informed stranger. Sixth, gradual replacement: my neurons are swapped one at a time for functionally identical synthetic components over several years. I survive throughout, even at the end. Seventh, complete organic amnesia: brain damage erases all autobiographical memory permanently and changes my personality, but the same living organism carries on. Still me, tragically diminished.

I am aware that several of these sit badly together: the first and third cases say I go with the brain, the second says I stay with the embodied point of view; the fourth and fifth treat physically indistinguishable replicas asymmetrically; the sixth and seventh together suggest that neither psychological continuity nor original matter is doing the work. What I want is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough to be falsified by a case. For each clause of the principle, tell me which of my cases motivates it. Then give me the simplest principle that accommodates most of my verdicts, name explicitly which verdicts it forces me to abandon, and rank those abandoned verdicts by how reluctantly I should let them go. Finally, apply your favored principle to two cases I have not listed and tell me what verdicts it predicts: fission, in which each hemisphere of my brain is transplanted into its own body and both wake with my full memories; and the backup, in which an inert duplicate of my brain is stored and activated only on the day I die. I also have a standing worry case that you should address: if, in the third case above, the vat brain is later connected to a newly grown body, does the person who wakes up count as me returning, or as a successor arriving?
