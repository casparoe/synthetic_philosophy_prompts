I work on our hospital's advance care planning service, and about a couple times a month we land in the same bind: a patient wrote a detailed directive or signed something years ago authorizing treatment (or refusing it) under certain conditions, and now, in exactly those conditions, they're telling us the opposite. The classic version is the psychiatric "Ulysses contract" -- someone with bipolar disorder, stable and thoughtful, signs a directive authorizing involuntary admission and meds during a manic episode; two years later, floridly manic, they're screaming that they never want to see this hospital again. But it comes up elsewhere too: dialysis refusals, feeding in dementia, patients who pre-authorized surgery for a condition that now terrifies them.

Our current process is "the attending calls the ethics consult service and we all talk in a room," which produces different answers depending on who's in the room. I'd like an actual decision procedure we can run in a day or two, with sign-off by the attending plus one ethics consultant, that a competent resident could drive without a philosophy degree.

Please write me that procedure. Specifically:

- The questions to ask, in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each one (e.g., how do we assess whether the directive was made by someone who understood what they were signing, how do we assess the patient's *current* decision-making capacity, and what do we do when those two assessments point in different directions?)
- It needs to handle the core conceptual problem honestly: the person in front of us is arguably not in the same position, autonomy-wise, as the person who signed. There are views that privilege the earlier, clear-headed self's wishes, and views that privilege whatever the current self can still want, and the procedure should say where it takes a stand on that and where it doesn't need to.
- Mark the points where the procedure *stops* and hands it to a human -- the questions that are genuinely about values and not something a checklist can settle -- and say why those are the irreducible ones.
- Also tell me where this procedure will misfire: the situations it will handle badly, so we know its limits going in.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) the bipolar/mania case above, where the directive is specific and recent; (2) a patient with moderate dementia who stated at 60, in writing and repeatedly to family, that she'd never want to be kept alive in "a state like that," but who now seems genuinely content -- eats, smiles at visitors, enjoys music -- while also resisting being moved to the facility her directive demands; and (3) a patient with anorexia whose directive authorizes tube feeding if her weight drops below a threshold she picked, and who is now below it and refusing. I want at least one of these to come out somewhere the current clinical instinct would be uncomfortable, so if your procedure agrees with gut feeling on all three, check your work. Also flag any case where the procedure just can't give an answer and we shouldn't pretend otherwise.
