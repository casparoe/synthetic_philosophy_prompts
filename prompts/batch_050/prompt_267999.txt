I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on formal epistemology, about 16 students, and I have one 75-minute session for the topic of Bayesian convergence. Design an in-class simulation that makes students feel, in their own betting behavior, both halves of the result: that shared evidence washes out even very different priors, and that this washing-out is conditional -- it fails, confidently and visibly, for any agent whose hypothesis space excludes the truth. Here is the rough shape I have in mind, which you should refine or replace: teams receive the same sequential stream of draws from a rigged randomizing device (a bag of beads, a weighted coin generator, whatever is practical), but each team is assigned a different prior over the device's bias; each round, teams must post a public probabilistic forecast of the next draw and are paid according to a proper scoring rule with token stakes, so that honest reporting is the incentive-compatible move; and -- the crucial design element -- at least one team's permitted hypothesis space is quietly restricted so that the true bias is not in it, so that as the data accumulate, most teams' posteriors visibly merge near the truth while the restricted team converges with increasing confidence to the "nearest" wrong hypothesis and keeps losing tokens without being able to say why.

Give me: the complete rules and materials list; a minute-by-minute timeline; a scoring scheme simple enough to run on paper in real time; the exact point at which the restricted team's situation should be revealed; and a debrief script that maps specific moments of the game onto the relevant concepts -- likelihood ratios dominating priors, merging-of-opinions results in the spirit of Blackwell and Dubins, the role of the prior's support (Cromwell's rule), and convergence to the KL-closest available hypothesis under misspecification -- without me lecturing on any of these beforehand, and without students hearing the theorem names until after the reveal. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (for instance: sample sizes too small for the wash-out to be visible, teams copying each other's forecasts, the restricted team suspecting the trick early) and how to handle each. Finally, be candid about which features of the actual theorems the activity cannot reproduce -- the asymptotic limit, the absolute-continuity condition, the difference between almost-sure convergence and a classroom streak -- and what I must simply tell the students rather than show them.
