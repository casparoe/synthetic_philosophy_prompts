So in my comparative federalism seminar the prof has spent all term doing this thing where she gives us a policy area in a fictional federation and tells us which level of government should get to decide it, central or member states. Her calls so far:

- River pollution that flows across state borders: central, "obviously"
- School curricula: member states, even though she admitted a decent national curriculum would probably raise test scores and help workers move around
- Technical standards for trains and planes (rail gauges, air traffic rules): central
- Marriage law: member states, even when we pushed on the fact that people move between states and you get messy recognition problems
- Recreational drug policy: member states, even though everyone acknowledged a lot of the drugs would just get bought in the permissive states and carried across the border
- Policing: member states
- A tax on carbon emissions: central

Now she's teed up next week's case and I want to get ahead of her. The case is: a new generation of general-purpose AI models, trained by companies in three of the member states, used by people and businesses in all of them, with harms that mostly show up where the users are (bad medical advice, manipulated media, hiring discrimination) but also some that spill across borders. Some member states want strict licensing rules, one wants almost none and is trying to lure the companies with that, and a few want to ban certain applications outright for moral reasons.

How I'd like the answer structured, roughly:

**Best guess** (a short paragraph) — predict her actual verdict, central or member states, and say how confident you are.

**The rule she's running** (a couple paragraphs) — what's the most plausible single principle that generates all seven of her past verdicts? Note that it has to handle the marriage and drug cases, where she let states decide despite clear cross-border effects, so it's not just "centralize whatever has spillovers."

**Other rules that fit** (a couple paragraphs) — give me at least two alternative principles that also rationalize the whole list (e.g. one based on uniformity/economies of scale, one on whether the issue is morally identity-laden, one on democratic experimentation), and say what each of them predicts for the AI case and where they diverge.

**The tiebreaker case** (a short paragraph) — what's the next hypothetical after this one whose answer would best tell your favored rule apart from the alternatives? I want to have it ready to ask her lol.
