I'm taking an upper-level undergraduate seminar in epistemology, and this week we covered the dogmatism paradox. As our professor presented it: if I know that p, then I seem to be in a position to know, of any future evidence appearing to tell against p, that it must be misleading — since it points away from a truth. Knowledge therefore appears to license dismissing counterevidence in advance, without even examining it, which strikes most people as a reductio or at least as deeply irrational closed-mindedness.

I've been turning this over against a real episode we briefly discussed in my history-of-science elective, and I can no longer tell whether the paradox is even paradoxical in practice. In September 2011, the OPERA collaboration announced that muon neutrinos sent from CERN to a detector at Gran Sasso had apparently arrived about 60 nanoseconds sooner than light could have traveled the same distance — an apparent violation of special relativity, reported at very high statistical significance and later tightened by an internal replication. What interests me is the physics community's response. Almost no working physicist concluded that relativity might be false, or even suspended judgment on it. Theorists publicly said the result had to be an artifact; experimentalists went on using relativistic calculations without hesitation; the near-universal verdict, months before anyone located the fault, was that the experiment must contain an error to be diagnosed rather than evidence to be weighed. The community was eventually vindicated: the anomaly was traced to equipment faults, including a loose fiber-optic link in the timing chain. At no point did anyone seem epistemically obliged to stop relying on relativity pending the outcome.

My questions are these. First, what epistemology does this pattern of response presuppose? On its face it looks like exactly the inference the dogmatism paradox condemns: we know relativity is true, so the OPERA data are misleading, so we may discount them. Does the practice thereby presuppose that the dogmatist inference is sometimes rational — and if so, does that dissolve the paradox or merely relocate it? Or does the practice presuppose something weaker and more respectable, such as a probabilistic picture on which a sufficiently confirmed theory assigns so low a prior to its own refutation that one anomalous measurement cannot move it — and is that genuinely different from dogmatism, or dogmatism with extra machinery?

Second, is the practice consistent with science's official norms? The same community that shrugged off OPERA teaches that theories must remain open to falsification and that anomalies deserve serious scrutiny. Are these commitments — dismiss apparent counterevidence to what you know, yet never close yourself to counterevidence — jointly coherent, or does the practice quietly presuppose a division of epistemic labor (most researchers dismiss the anomaly while a designated few investigate it) that the official norms do not acknowledge?

Third, where is the practice philosophically underdetermined? I suspect several rival epistemologies would license exactly the same behavior, in which case it presupposes less than it appears to. If that's right, which views would each license it, and what observable feature of the practice would discriminate among them? Finally, what would concretely have to change in scientific practice if we rejected the dogmatist inference outright — would the rational response to OPERA have been suspending reliance on relativity, and if that seems absurd, what should we conclude about the paradox?

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
