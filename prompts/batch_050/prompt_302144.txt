Next term I'm running a 90-minute seminar for second-year undergraduates on a political theory survey course, and my session has to convey the core of the Hobsbawm–Anderson moment in the study of nationalism: Hobsbawm's notion of invented tradition and Anderson's account of nations as imagined communities, and with them the claim that nations are far younger and far more constructed than they present themselves as being. The students will have read neither text; they'll arrive, at best, with the vague sense that someone once said nations are modern.

Plan the session's explanatory sequence rather than delivering the content itself. Specifically:

1. The dependency order. What must be in place before either thesis can land? My instinct is to begin with what traditions and nations claim about themselves (antiquity, naturalness, continuity) before touching any theory, so the students see what the theories are pushing against — but tell me if something else has to come first, and justify the ordering step by step.

2. The carrying example. I want one case that can bear the weight of the whole session. I'm considering the Scottish Highland tradition — the kilt and clan tartans presented as immemorial when much of the apparatus was assembled in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries — because it seems to let me show invention, function, and survival-as-genuine-identity in a single case. Assess this choice: what must an example have, and what must it lack, to carry both Hobsbawm and Anderson without collapsing them into each other? If the kilt is wrong, say what you'd use instead.

3. The misconception map. Anticipate the errors in the order the students will actually commit them, and tell me at which point in the sequence each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact. My guess at the big three: that invented means fake, so the argument becomes an exposé of bogus customs; that imagined means imaginary, so Anderson is read as saying nations are delusions; and that Hobsbawm and Anderson are making the same argument, so the difference between a claim about practices and a claim about the community those practices help constitute gets flattened. Confirm, refine, or add to this list.

4. The check. One question or short exercise for the final fifteen minutes that would show the students have grasped the distinction between debunking a nation's self-image and explaining how nations work — not merely that they can repeat the slogans.

5. What to leave out. What belongs in this session, and what — primordialism debates, print capitalism's mechanics, the subsequent critical literature — should I explicitly defer, and how do I mark the deferral without making the session feel truncated?
