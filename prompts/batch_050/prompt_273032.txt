I'm a novelist (literary fiction, mostly) and I've been roped into a panel discussion on photographs and memory at a festival, so I want to sanity-check my working position before I embarass myself. Here's roughly what I believe:

(1) When I look at an old photo of my grandmother as a young woman, I'm literally seeing her — not a picture of her, her. The photograph is transparent, like a window with a delay on it.

(2) What makes this work is that cameras capture appearances mechanically, with nobody's beliefs or interpretations intervening — which is exactly why a painted portrait of her, however accurate, wouldn't give me the same thing. The painter's whole take on the scene sits between me and the sitter.

(3) I'm a big fan of staged photography — Cindy Sherman, Jeff Wall, that whole world — and I still think we genuinely see what's in front of the camera there: the real set, the real actors, the real bus stop Wall rebuilt. Staging doesn't break the transparency.

(4) But when I watch a docudrama, I don't take myself to be seeing the historical events at all — just actors reenacting them. Reenactment is representation, not a window.

(5) In my novels I treat a character's photographs as better evidence of what they were really like than anyone's testimony about them, including their own diary.

So: does this hang together? Tell me which commitments actually pull against each other and how badly (rank them, don't just flag everything), which apparent clashes dissolve once you look closer, and what's the cheapest single revision if something has to give. I suspect I'm smuggling an inconsistency in somewhere around the staged stuff but I can't put my finger on it.
