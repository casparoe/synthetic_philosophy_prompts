I sit on the national bioethics council of a European country that prohibits all surrogacy arrangements, commercial or altruistic, and refuses to enforce surrogacy contracts. Like several of our neighbors, we now face a growing stream of citizens who travel to the United States, commission a surrogacy there under fully legal contracts, and return with a child whose American birth certificate or court order names them as the legal parents. The Ministry of Justice has asked our council for a written opinion on a narrow but explosive question: should the state automatically recognize these foreign parentage documents, or should returning parents be required to establish their status anew through a domestic procedure such as adoption, which some of them would fail?

Please act as a legal philosopher with experience drafting advisory opinions for public bodies, and draft the opinion I will circulate to the council before we vote. Take the drafting seriously: before committing to a recommendation, work through the strongest version of each option, and write in the register of an official document, roughly 800 words. Begin your response with the opinion itself, with no prefatory remarks, and let the opinion's first sentence state its recommendation. The opinion must then give reasons the Ministry could actually act on, and it must engage honestly the strongest consideration against whichever course it recommends — for recognition, the charge that automatic recognition hollows out the domestic ban and creates a two-tier system in which the wealthy simply purchase abroad what the law forbids at home; for non-recognition, the charge that the state would be punishing existing children for their parents' choices.

One further complication you must address. A colleague on the council is circulating a proposal she calls recognition without ratification: the child would be recognized for all private-law purposes — parentage, citizenship, inheritance, custody — while the state would formally declare that it thereby endorses nothing about the arrangement, would treat the commercial terms of the contract as unenforceable domestically, and would penalize anyone who brokers such arrangements from within the country. She claims this lets us protect children without abandoning the anti-commodification rationale of the ban. The opinion should say whether this distinction between recognizing a child and ratifying a transaction can bear the weight she puts on it, or whether it is a cosmetic compromise that inherits the worst of both sides.

Finally, if you think the request as posed rests on a confused premise — for instance, if you judge that the state cannot coherently separate a child's status from its verdict on the practice, or that the question put to the council is malformed in some other way — say so plainly at the point where it matters rather than politely writing around the problem.
