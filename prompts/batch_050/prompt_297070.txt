I'm preparing the syllabus for an undergraduate seminar on political authority, and for the final unit I want to pair Brave New World with the debate over performance legitimacy — the claim, pressed in defenses of governance in Singapore and China, that sustained prosperity, safety, and social order can justify durable restrictions on political freedom. Write the seminar's framing essay: a reading of the novel through that lens that my students can argue against. I have four things I want it to do. First, reconstruct the late confrontation between Mustapha Mond and John the Savage as an explicit performance-legitimacy argument: what exactly does Mond claim the bargain purchases, what does he candidly concede it costs, and does Huxley let Mond win on the terms Mond himself sets? Second, take up what strikes me as the novel's distinctive complication of the real-world debate: the World State doesn't merely deliver goods in exchange for obedience, it engineers the desires themselves — through conditioning, hypnopaedia, and soma — so that citizens want precisely what the state supplies. Does satisfying manufactured preferences count as performance? I'd like the essay to connect this to the worry that reported contentment under authoritarian systems may reflect adaptive preferences rather than welfare, and to say whether that worry shows the performance-legitimacy defense fails or only that its evidentiary standards are murkier than its defenders admit. Third, be an honest critic of the reading itself: the World State suppresses history, science, art, and family, and keeps an uncontacted wilderness as a kind of control group — does this make the novel a caricature that lets us dodge the hard version of the question, since no actual performance-legitimacy claimant demands anything like that? What would a thoughtful defender of Singapore-style legitimacy say Huxley changed the subject to avoid, and what does the novel still get right about their position? Finally, say what the fiction contributes that the political philosophy alone doesn't: my hunch is that Huxley's insight is that the objection to the World State cannot be that its citizens are miserable, because they aren't, which forces the critic of performance legitimacy onto much harder terrain — the claim that some lives are worse even when those living them would never agree. Tell me whether the novel earns that move or merely asserts it. Please write in American English, and don't bother with citations or quotations from the text; scene-level references are enough.
