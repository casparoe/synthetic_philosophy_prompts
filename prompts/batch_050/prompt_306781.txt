So I just got off a four-hour argument with my friend Mara about whether you can ever *rationally* choose to do something that will change what you value, and we kept going in circles because we actually agree on a bunch of cases but can't find the rule. Here's where we landed. I want someone to tell me what principle (if any) is behind our shared verdicts, because right now it feels like we've just got a pile of intuitions held together with tape.

The cases and what we both think:

1. Having a kid. Neither of us has one, neither of us can know what it's like from the inside, and it famously rearranges your whole value system. Verdict: totally rational to choose anyway. People do it on testimony and vague hope and that's fine.
2. The vampire case from the transformative experience literature -- someone offers to turn you into a vampire, and every vampire you ask says it's amazing and they'd never go back, but you literally cannot imagine what it's like. Verdict: NOT rational to take the deal. Mara and I both balked, even though structurally it looks a lot like case 1. (This is the pair we fought about -- why does parenthood get a pass and vampirism doesn't?)
3. A new medication that cures debilitating chronic pain but, as a known side effect, permanently rewires your aesthetic taste -- you'll come out not caring at all about the music you currently love, and you'll love stuff you currently find grating. Verdict: rational to take it. The pain wins.
4. Joining an intense religious order where the whole *point* is that after five years you'll value completely different things (and the members all say it's the best thing that ever happened to them). Verdict: you can't rationally choose this, you'd be choosing to stop being you. But wait -- how is this different from case 3?? We both feel it is, neither of us could say why.
5. Same vampire offer, but now there's a reversible trial version: one week as a vampire, then you get to decide with full memory of the trial. Verdict: obviously rational to try the trial. But then Mara pointed out that if a week of firsthand experience makes the decision rational, then the problem was never the transformation itself, just lack of data -- which seems to undermine our verdict in case 2 entirely. Ugh.
6. Choosing for someone else: parents getting a cochlear implant for their deaf kid, which will shape what the kid grows up to value. Verdict: fine, even admirable -- even though we'd both say an adult deaf person who *declines* the implant is also making a fine choice.

So: is there one principle that delivers all six? My tentative guess was something like "a transformative choice is rational iff the values it installs are ones your current self can at least recognize as belonging to a life you'd endorse," but Mara demolished that by pointing out current-me *can't* recognize kid-haver values any more than vampire values, and that the order's values are arguably more recognizable, not less. Her counter-guess was that it's about reversibility and information, but that collapses cases 3 and 4 together in a way neither of us accepts.

What I want: state the best principle (or two candidates) precisely, clause by clause, and tell me which verdict motivates each clause. Then be honest about which of my six verdicts it can't save, and tell me which one *should* go if I'm being consistent. Then run it on two cases we didn't discuss: (a) a soldier deciding whether to take a combat posting they know from veterans' accounts will permanently change how they see violence, and (b) someone choosing to take a pill that makes them stop loving a specific person who treats them badly.

Format: give me the analysis in whatever prose you need, but end with a JSON object summarizing it, with this exact schema: {"principle": string, "clauses": [{"clause": string, "motivating_cases": [ints from 1-6]}], "predicted_verdicts": {"case_a": string, "case_b": string}, "verdict_to_abandon": int or null, "why_abandon_it": string, "biggest_remaining_problem": string}. Don't flatter the intuition pile -- if its a mess, say so.
