I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a feature about how evolutionary biology explains things -- why peacocks carry those absurd tails, why human backs hurt so much, that sort of thing. While researching, I kept running into references to Gould and Lewontin's famous spandrels paper, and from the way alot of the secondary sources describe it, I came away with the impression that it basically demolished adaptationism: that it showed adaptationist explanations are unfalsifiable just-so stories, and that serious biologists afterwards stopped treating natural selection as the default explanation for a trait.

Here's what's confusing me. When I actually interview working evolutionary biologists for the piece, they offer adaptationist hypotheses constantly -- this beak shape is for cracking these seeds, this mating display evolved because females favor it -- and reviewers and journals seem perfectly fine with that. If the spandrels critique really showed that this style of explanation is unfalsifiable, why is it still the everyday bread and butter of the field? Are these researchers just ignoring a result everyone in the discipline knows about, is the critique treated as something the field has moved past, or have I garbled what the paper actually argued? For the article I need a couple of sentences I could defend to a tough fact-checker about what the paper did and didn't establish, so I'd really like to know exactly where my summary goes wrong, and what Gould and Lewontin were actually taking aim at, if it wasn't adaptationist explanation as such.
