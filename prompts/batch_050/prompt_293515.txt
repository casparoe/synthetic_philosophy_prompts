Having spent my working life as an experimental physicist, I have only in retirement developed a serious appetite for philosophy, and I have lately found myself stuck on a question at the crossroads of metaphysics and literature. While rereading Dickens's A Christmas Carol, I was struck by the scene in which the Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come shows Scrooge the neglected corpse, the plundered estate, and the untended grave, and Scrooge demands to know whether these visions depict what must come to pass or only what may, if his present course continues. This seems to me a small thought experiment in the logic of possible worlds, and I would like it read as such.

As I understand it, the debate between Kripke-style transworld identity and Lewis's counterpart theory bears directly on the scene. On the former, the dead man is Scrooge himself, considered in an unactualized possible world; on the latter, no individual inhabits more than one world, and the dead man is at most a counterpart, someone sufficiently similar to Scrooge in the relevant respects. My question is whether the moral and emotional force of the vision, and hence the rationality of Scrooge's subsequent reform, depends on which view is correct. If the corpse is merely a counterpart, is Scrooge's terror a category error, akin to fearing a stranger's fate? Or does Lewis's similarity relation, especially when the counterpart diverges from Scrooge only through Scrooge's own future choices, suffice to make the dread and repentance rational?

I would also welcome your judgment on whether Dickens's implicit metaphysics favors either picture, and whether the story exposes something both accounts neglect: that what moves us about unrealized possibility may be neither bare numerical identity nor mere resemblance, but the causal continuity between present character and future end.
