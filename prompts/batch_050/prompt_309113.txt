I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy and I've been roped into helping my supervisor put together the exam for a second/third-year undergrad module on population ethics and the ethics of procreation. I need one essay question on the procreation asymmetry (roughly: it's wrong to create a child whose life would be awful, but it's not wrong to refrain from creating a child whose life would be wonderful). Students get 75 minutes, closed book.

The problem with the obvious questions is that anyone who's done the reading can just trot out Benatar's asymmetry, maybe gesture at Shiffrin's consent argument, and be done. I want something that seperates students who actually get what's philosophically tricky about the asymmetry from ones who've just memorised that it exists. Specifically I want the question to force them to confront at least one of these pressure points:

- whether the asymmetry can be stated as a purely deontic claim (about what's permitted/required) without committing to Benatar's stronger axiological version, and what hangs on the difference
- why a person-affecting view looks tailor-made to deliver the asymmetry but struggles elsewhere (the non-identity problem is the obvious one)
- the objection that if there's genuinely no reason to create happy lives, then adding wonderful lives to the world is value-neutral, which lots of people find hard to swallow — and whether the asymmetry's defender can soften this without collapsing into a duty to procreate

So: please write the essay question itself (a single question, but you can use a "critically assess this claim" style setup with a claim supplied in the question if that helps sharpen it), then a model answer of around 500-600 words written at the level of a genuinely strong undergrad, then a marking scheme. The marking scheme should be built around five things a top answer must do, with marks assigned to each (20 total), and for each one a line on how weaker students typically fumble it — e.g. treating the asymmetry and the non-identity problem as the same thing, or assuming the axiological and deontic versions stand or fall together.

Happy for you to pick whichever of those pressure points makes for the best question, or combine a couple, as long as the question can't be answered by reciting a summary of the asymmetry.
