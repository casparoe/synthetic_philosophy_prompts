I'm in the second year of a philosophy degree and currently taking a seminar on the ethics of emerging technologies, where we've just started a unit on digital remains and so-called griefbots -- chatbots trained on the messages, voice recordings, and writings of dead people so that the bereaved can keep "talking" to them. Our tutor keeps pressing us to move beyond vague unease, so I've been polling my own intuitions on cases, and I'd like help figuring out whether there's a principle underneath them, because at the moment they feel like a grab-bag.

Here are my verdicts. A widow who keeps her late husband's old voicemail greeting and listens to it when she misses him: clearly fine, no ethical issue at all. A company that offers to fabricate new voicemail messages in that same husband's voice -- "happy birthday, I'm proud of you" -- assembled from his audio data without his having known: not fine, even though the widow might treasure them. A terminally ill father who, knowing exactly what the technology does, spends his last months curating a chatbot of himself for his young children, choosing what goes in and recording a video explaining what the bot is and isn't: fine, maybe even admirable -- and I note that this bot, like the fabricated voicemails, produces utterances the father never actually made. A startup that trains a bot on a dead man's private texts and emails, without his having consented either way (he simply never thought about it), which his adult son then chats with daily for over a year and says it helps: this one sits badly with me, though I struggle to say who exactly is wronged. A museum that builds an interactive bot of a long-dead novelist from her published letters and lets visitors converse with it: fine. A woman who, while alive, told her family to delete everything when she died, whose family builds a bot from her data anyway because they miss her: clearly not fine, the worst of the lot.

When I try to extract a principle, I keep generating candidates that each fail somewhere. Consent of the deceased handles the last case but can't explain why the museum bot is permissible (she consented to nothing), and it seems to condemn the startup case for the wrong reason -- my discomfort there seems to be about the son and his grief, not just the father's data. An authenticity principle -- real recordings fine, generated utterances not -- handles the voicemail pair but condemns the dying father's bot, which I refuse to give up. And "it depends on whether the user is helped or harmed" feels both too permissive and too hard to apply in advance. So my questions: is there a single principle, or a small principled package, that captures all of these verdicts, and if not, which of my verdicts is the best candidate to abandon and why? What does your best candidate principle predict about two cases I haven't listed -- a novelist who licenses her personality for a posthumous fan-facing bot as a commercial venture, and a service that never generates anything new but only retrieves and surfaces the deceased's actual old messages in response to whatever the bereaved person types? And does the principle treat the dead as the relevant patients of harm at all, or does it end up locating all the moral action in the living?
