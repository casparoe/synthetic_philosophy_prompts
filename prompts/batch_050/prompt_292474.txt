I’m a retired field geologist who has recently fallen down the philosophy-of-science rabbit hole, so excuse me if this is a bit rough around the edges. In machine-learning discussions, I keep seeing the no-free-lunch theorem explained through a toolbox analogy: no wrench is best for every possible bolt, so no learning rule can be best for every possible environment. Some people then take this to support a much bigger conclusion: Hume was basically right, and induction has no rational foundation unless we already assume that nature is regular.

Please audit the toolbox analogy. Give me the correspondence explicitly — what are the wrenches, bolts, fit, and user supposed to be? Then say where the analogy breaks. I’m especially interested in the assumptions hidden by talk of every possible environment. Imagine a tournament in which three forecasters — one that always extends the latest trend, one that prefers conservation laws, and one very flexible curve-fitter — are scored against every mathematically possible continuation of the same finite stream of measurements, with all continuations counted equally. Does equal counting represent genuine neutrality about nature, or does it quietly build in a strangely hostile picture in which nearby observations carry no information about what comes next?

Also separate some conclusions that are too easily run together: no method is best across all logically possible worlds; no method can be justified without assumptions; no observed regularity makes its continuation more probable; and science gives us no reason to prefer one prediction over another. Which of these, if any, follows from the theorem? Scientists use prior physical knowledge, simplicity, invariance under changes of units or reference frame, and deliberately designed interventions rather than passive observation. Do those practices fall outside the analogy, or can they be translated into it?

If the wrench comparison mostly misleads, suggest a better one. I occassionally suspect it turns a result about treating all worlds alike into a result about what we should believe in the world we actually inhabit, but maybe I’m missing something.
