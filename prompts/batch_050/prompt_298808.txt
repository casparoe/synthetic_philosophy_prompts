At the philosophy reading group I help run, a debate broke out last week over the Pascal's mugging scenario — the one where a stranger on the street promises that, in exchange for your wallet, he will bring about some astronomically good outcome that only he can deliver. I deliberately stayed out of it, since the two participants asked me to referee afterward and I found I couldn't. Andrei opened by saying that paying up is the rational move, full stop: if the mugger promises to save 10^30 lives conditional on payment, then even at a credence of one in a quintillion the expected value of paying comes to 10^12 lives, and refusing means treating the cash in your pocket as worth more than that. Beatriz answered that this is a reductio, not a result: any decision theory that instructs you to pay a stranger on the strength of such a promise has failed at its job, and the natural diagnosis is that expected value maximization breaks down when probabilities are tiny and payoffs unbounded — so the right response is to bound or steeply discount utility somewhere.

Andrei replied that capping utility merely relocates the absurdity: with any finite cap you are forced into indifference between outcomes that plainly differ — a world where a trillion people are saved versus a world where a trillion and one are — and agents with bounded utility can be walked through sequences of individually acceptable trades into clearly worse positions. Beatriz then abandoned the cap and offered a different fix: rational credence cannot be held fixed while the promised payoff scales, because the larger the number the mugger names, the less credible the promise — and if credence falls faster than the promise grows, the expected value never in fact dominates. The paradox, she said, is an artifact of freezing the probability while inflating the stakes. Andrei's final move was that this simply rigs the priors to dodge the conclusion: a prior over what a stranger can deliver cannot be tuned after the fact to keep expected values comfortable, and anyone holding such priors would be equally forced to dismiss well-supported claims that genuinely carry astronomical stakes, so her cure makes her worse off precisely in the cases where the huge number is real.

Neither would concede, and I'd like an outside adjudication. Please go through the exchange move by move — there are five in total — and for each one say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible but incomplete, with a sentence or two of justification for the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Keep the entire adjudication under 600 words.
