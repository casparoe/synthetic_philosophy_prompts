So a buddy of mine in my political theory reading group put together this argument and it's been bugging me for weeks, because the premises all sound like stuff I basically already believe and the conclusion is nuts. Here it is:

(1) A law is only legitimate if it enacts the will of the people — that's just what it means for rule to be democratic rather than imposed.
(2) The will of the people is whatever the majority of people actually want, right now, as measured by what they'd say if you asked them.
(3) So a law is legitimate only if it matches current majority opinion.
(4) Elected representatives routinely vote against majority opinion on trade, criminal justice, foreign policy, whatever, and keep their seats anyway.
(5) Courts, constitutions, central banks, and administrative agencies exist precisely to frustrate majority opinion — that's the whole point of them, everyone admits this.
(6) So representative government plus counter-majoritarian institutions is illegitimate, and the only genuinely democratic system is direct and majoritarian. (This is basically the populist conclusion, and he says we should just accept it.)
(7) But wait: referendum questions are worded by somebody, campaigns are funded unequally, voters are misinformed, and the "majority" on any given question is an artifact of how you slice the population and sequence the votes — Arrow-type problems mean there's often no coherent majority preference there to enact in the first place.
(8) So even direct majoritarianism fails (1)–(3).
(9) Therefore no political order can be legitimate. Every law is imposed, and "the will of the people" is a fiction whoever's in power invokes.

Now obviously (9) proves too much — an argument that starts from "democracy is good" and ends at "no law can ever be legitimate" has eaten itself somewhere. But when I press him, every step feels defensible. (2) seems like the only non-mystical reading of "the will of the people" — anything fancier smells like an excuse for elites to say the people "really" want whatever the elites want, which is exactly the move the populists complain about, no?

Here's where I stand: I'd really like to keep (1), since some version of popular authorization seems non-negotiable for democracy. And I have a soft spot for (7) too, since the aggregation worries seem real. So I guess I'm hoping the kill shot lands somewhere in (2)–(3) or in the move from (6) to (9), but I can't articulate what actually breaks.

Tell me which step you reject and why it looked fine. Then walk me through the main ways out — the populist who just bites (6) and rejects (7), the deliberative-democracy move that says the people's will has to be formed rather than measured, the skeptic who says "the people" never has a single will at all, and maybe the quietist who just denies (1). For each one, what does it cost? Specifically: does fixing the argument require giving up the idea that ordinary people's actual preferences are the ultimate standard of legitimacy, and if so, is that price too high?
