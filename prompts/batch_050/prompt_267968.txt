You are a philosopher of history with expertise in theories of collective responsibility and in the historiography of the First World War. Take your time with this; brainstorm the logical space first before you commit to a structure.

I am designing the culminating unit of an undergraduate course on the First World War, and I want students to discover that "which nation was to blame for 1914?" is really several different questions wearing one coat. Please construct a taxonomy of the positions on national responsibility for the war, presented as a decision tree, where each branch point is a diagnostic question rather than a name.

Requirements for the tree:

1. Build it from the logical space, not from a list of historians. Each branch should be labeled by a claim on which the positions genuinely diverge. The questions I care most about separating are these, though you may add others if they do real work: (a) whether responsibility attaches primarily to pre-war intentions and aims or to the concrete decisions of July 1914; (b) whether wartime evidence -- e.g., expansive annexationist war aims drafted after the war began -- can legitimately be read back onto pre-war intent; (c) whether a nation's responsibility requires that its leaders could realistically have chosen otherwise, or whether structural pressure (alliance commitments, mobilization timetables, fear of encirclement) is an excuse, a mitigation, or irrelevant; (d) whether "responsibility" is even a coherent predicate of nations, as opposed to named statesmen; (e) whether blame is a fixed sum to be apportioned, so that raising one nation's share lowers the others', or whether multiple nations can each be fully responsible.

2. The leaves must include, at minimum: the orthodox post-Versailles German-war-guilt position; the Fritz Fischer school and its emphasis on continuity of German aims; revisionist positions that spread responsibility across the capitals (including the recent "sleepwalkers" style account in which the key actors failed to grasp what they were doing); a systemic/structural position that blames the alliance machinery itself; and an individualist position that refuses to blame nations at all and assigns responsibility only to particular persons. Where two of these cannot be separated by any diagnostic question, say so explicitly rather than inventing a distinction.

3. For each leaf, give one sentence stating the position's most awkward commitment -- the bullet its defender must bite.

4. For any combination of branch answers that corresponds to no position anyone has defended, mark the empty node and diagnose briefly whether the gap exists because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

5. Ground the diagnostic questions in the concrete historical materials students will actually have read: the blank cheque of July 1914, Russia's general mobilization, the invasion of Belgium, the Schlieffen Plan's logic, and Germany's wartime annexationist program. A student should be able to answer each branch question from these materials without knowing any historian's name.

After the tree, add a short note on what the taxonomy cannot do: which live disagreements (e.g., over the moral weight of the invasion of Belgium versus its causal weight) cut across every branch and so will not be sorted by the instrument.
