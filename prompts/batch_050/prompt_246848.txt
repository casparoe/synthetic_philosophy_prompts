So here's something that's been bugging me while prepping my bioethics seminar. Standard medical practice in most countries draws a hard line between withdrawing treatment and actively ending a life. A doctor can take a terminally ill patient off a ventilator, knowing full well the patient will die within minutes, and that's considered routine, even mandatory if the patient or family requests it. But that same doctor giving the same patient a lethal injection, with the same consent, same prognosis, same outcome, same timing, is a crime pretty much everywhere outside a handful of jurisdictions. Even within the withdrawal category there are fine-grained distinctions: stopping a treatment already running (extubating, unplugging the vent) vs. not starting one in the first place (withholding antibiotics, honoring a DNR) is sometimes treated as morally different, with withholding seen as the easy case and withdrawal as the psychologically harder one, even though the official position is that they're equivalent.

What I want to know is what this whole apparatus presupposes philosophically. Does it presuppose a genuine moral distinction between killing and letting die, and if so, which version -- a doing/allowing distinction, something about causation (the disease kills the patient, not the doctor), the intention/foresight distinction from double effect, or just a rule-of-thumb about which permissions are safe to hand out institutionally? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, then tell me whether the practice actually needs it: could a consequentialist who thinks the killing/letting-die distinction is noise still endorse exactly this institutional arrangement, maybe on slippery-slope or trust-preservation grounds? And if we dropped the distinction entirely in either direction -- either active euthanasia becomes as routine as withdrawal, or withdrawal gets treated with the same suspicion as killing -- what concretely has to change in the practice, and which parts of it survive on independent grounds?

One more wrinkle I'd like addressed: the official line says withholding and withdrawing are morally on a par, but everyone who works in hospitals knows clinicians find withdrawal much harder and sometimes delay it past the point where withholding would have been fine. Does that gap between the doctrine and the felt experience suggest the doctrine has the wrong theory underneath it, or just that moral psychology doesn't track moral principles?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- just state straight up what the practice presupposes, or that it's underdetermined -- then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my question back at me.
