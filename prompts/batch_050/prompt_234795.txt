So I just got done with this huge argument with my friend Marcus that started at dinner and literally went til 1am. He’s a behavioral econ guy and his position is that loss aversion and the endowment effect are basically just bugs -- people overvalue what they have, WTA exceeds WTP, the mug experiments prove it, and good policy should correct for it (auto-enrollment defaults, cooling-off periods, whatever). My pushback was that at least sometimes refusing to give something up for its “market price” is a perfectly legitimate preference, not a malfunction -- like my grandmother would never sell her wedding ring for its pawn value and there’s nothing irrational about that, and if a regulator “corrects” that preference they’re just steamrolling people. He kept saying fine, but how do you tell which is which in a given case, and honestly I didn’t have a great answer.

Here’s why I actually care: I do policy review for a mid-size pension regulator, and about once or twice a month I have to sign off (or not) on plan-design proposals from providers -- default contribution rates, auto-escalation, whether an old plan’s funds get mapped into a new default or people have to actively opt in, that kind of thing. Each review I get maybe half an hour on, and my director cosigns. The endowment question is basically the whole game: every proposal assumes something about whether members’ attachment to the status quo is something to respect or something to route around.

So: write me a decision procedure I can actually run in that half hour, grounded in whatever the debate over whether the endowment effect is a bias or a real preference has actually figured out. I want ordered steps, roughly:

1. how to tell whether a reluctance-to-switch is plausibly a genuine preference vs. a status-quo/loss-aversion artifact (e.g., tests like: does it survive full information and a neutral framing? would the person pay to acquire the thing they refuse to sell? does it depend on which option was arbitrarily made the default?)
2. what to do when those tests conflict or when I can’t get the info to run one, which will be often
3. which cases the procedure just can’t settle and I have to kick it up to my director as a judgment call

Be honest about which steps are real diagnostics and which are judgment calls wearing a lab coat, because that was exactly Marcus’s accusation and I want to be able to say which is which. Then apply the procedure to three cases: (a) auto-escalating contributions out of future raises, (b) mapping people from a discontinued fund into a similar default fund unless they object, and (c) one where the procedure spits out an answer I won’t like -- e.g., it says we have to treat some attachment as legitimate that I personally think is dumb, or vice versa. End with what this procedure can’t do at all.

700 words max, and keep the actual checklist short enough that I could print it on one page.
