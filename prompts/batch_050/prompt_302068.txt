Ok so there's this argument happening at my company and I keep going back and forth on it, curious what people think.

Some background: I'm a software engineer at a mid-size company. Our core data pipeline was built like 6 years ago by a team that's since all left. They wrote this really thorough design doc laying out exactly WHY they built it the way they did — the tradeoffs they considered, the alternatives they rejected and why, the constraints they were under. I've read the whole thing and honestly the reasoning seems solid.

Here's the problem. Our new tech lead wants to rip it out and rebuild, and when people push back and point at the design doc, her response is basically: that doc isn't an argument, it's just dead text. Reasons only count if there's an actual person willing to stand up in a meeting and defend them. Since nobody currently at the company actually holds those views — most people havent even read the doc, and the ones who have couldn't reconstruct the arguments under questioning — the original justification is void, and we're free to do whatever. She says being bound by reasoning nobody understands or endorses is like being governed by ghosts.

And like... I kind of see her point? It does feel weird that a decision made by people who are gone should bind us forever through a document. But it also feels like she's smuggling in something wrong. The reasons in that doc are either good reasons or they're not — why should it matter whether anyone's head currently contains them? If I found a proof of some math result in a drawer and the author was dead, the proof would still prove the thing, right?

So who's right here? And specifically:

1. If the doc's reasons still count as reasons, what does it take to override them? Do we have to actually refute them point by point, or is it enough to just... not care about them anymore?
2. Does it matter WHERE the reasons live? Like if one of the original engineers still worked here but had changed his mind and no longer believed his own old arguments, would that weaken the doc? That seems absurd but I can't articulate why.
3. Messier version of the same thing: apparently half that doc was drafted by some internal auto-documentation tool and then lightly edited. So parts of the justification were arguably never in anyone's head at all, even at the time. Does that change anything about whether the reasons count?

Genuinely stuck on this because whatever principle applies here probably also applies to stuff like regulatory compliance docs and old legal memos we rely on, not just code.
