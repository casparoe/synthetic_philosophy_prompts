For a reading group on population ethics I said I'd write up where I currently stand, and before I send it round I want to check I'm not accidentally contradicting myself. Here is roughly where I've landed:

1. Total utilitarianism is basically right: one outcome is better than another iff it contains more total welfare, summed over everyone who ever lives, no matter when or where they live.
2. Human extinction would be close to the worst thing that could happen, mainly because of the enormous future it would cut off. That's why reducing even quite small probabilities of extinction strikes me as some of the most important work anyone can do.
3. I take pretty seriously the possibility that the universe is literally infinite — spatially infinite, or eternal inflation churning out endless pocket universes — and I think a moral theory should still say sensible things conditional on that being true, rather than just falling silent or breaking.
4. I'm not an average utilitarian: adding extra happy lives to a world can't make it worse, at worst its neutral.
5. Expected value maximization is the right way to make decisions under uncertainty. Multiply and add, full stop.
6. Any decent decision theory has to be able to shrug off Pascal's mugger. There has to be some principled way that a tiny probability of an astronomically huge payoff doesn't get to hijack your whole life.

Which of these are genuinely in tension and which only look like they are? Please rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each one say what the cheapest fix would be and what that fix costs me. And if I had to drop or weaken exactly one commitment to make the rest hang together as well as possible, which one should it be?
