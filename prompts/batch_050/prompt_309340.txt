So I somehow got roped onto the committee rewriting our company's code of conduct, and at happy hour last night the whole thing blew up over the word "harassment." The draft defines it to include stuff like repeated "harmless" jokes that nobody's actually complained about, and two coworkers went at it. (1) Dan said you can't just redefine a word because it's convienient — harassment means something, and stuffing new cases into it is Humpty Dumpty talk; if we want to ban certain jokes, fine, ban them, but don't pretend it's harassment. (2) Maria said words are tools, not museum pieces — the point of a policy term isn't to report what English speakers happen to mean, it's to do a job, and if the everyday concept lets too much slide we should engineer a better one. (3) Dan said that's changing the subject: the original question "does this actually count as harassment?" still stands, and her new definition just answers a different question while borrowing the old word's sting. (4) Maria said there is no pristine original question — which concept we use was always a choice, and keeping the word while changing its content is exactly right becuase the word carries normative weight that some brand-new coinage wouldn't. (5) Dan replied that she just admitted the redefinition is a rhetorical trick: keeping the label precisely so it does work the new meaning hasn't earned. Then the bartender closed out our tab and nothing got resolved. Who's right at each step? And does it matter that this is a made-up policy term at a company, as opposed to a concept like knowledge or truth that we can't just vote on?
