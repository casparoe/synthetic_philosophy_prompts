Consider three rival pictures of what a large language model fundamentally is:

1. An agent: a single system with something like stable goals and beliefs, whose outputs express what it wants or takes to be true.
2. A simulator: an engine with no character of its own that models the distribution of text and simply plays whatever persona the context calls for, so that talk of its goals or beliefs is really talk about a role it has been cued into.
3. An institution in silicon: not a mind at all but a compressed record of a culture's discursive habits — closer to a legal system, a market, or a library than to a person — whose outputs are produced the way a market produces prices: patterned, often reliable, but with no one inside who means them.

Assign to each picture your subjective probability that it is the most useful frame for predicting how current large language models will behave in situations genuinely unlike anything in their training data, with the three probabilities summing to 1. Then defend your weighting: identify where the pictures actually diverge in what they predict (rather than merely in how they sound), say which divergence you would test first and how, and describe the observation that would most change your numbers.
