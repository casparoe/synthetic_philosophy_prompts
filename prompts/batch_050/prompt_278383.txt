You are a veteran game theory instructor who has taught backward induction many times and knows, from experience, exactly where students get stuck. I am preparing a single seventy-five minute session on the centipede game and the backward induction paradox for second-year undergraduates who have finished one prior course covering normal-form games, dominance, and Nash equilibrium, but who have never seen an extensive-form game or heard of subgame perfection. Think carefully before answering: what I want is not an explanation of the material, which I know, but a plan for the explanation.

Address the following, in whatever internal order you judge right:

First, the dependency structure. Give the sequence in which the pieces must be introduced and defend it: does backward induction as a technique come before or after the game that makes it troubling, and why? What must students already have securely in place (extensive-form representation, strategies as complete contingent plans rather than ongoing intentions, the difference between an equilibrium path and off-path reasoning) before the paradox can even be stated, and what is the earliest point in the session at which the word paradox can be earned rather than asserted?

Second, the carrying example. Choose the one game to build the entire session around. I am inclined toward a short centipede with a steadily growing pot, but I want you to state what its payoffs must have and must lack for the paradox to genuinely bite — for instance, why the game falls flat if continuing is dominated in a way students can spot immediately, and why the temptation to keep playing must be real and symmetric rather than one player's mistake. If you think a different game carries the point better, say so and say what the centipede would have sacrificed.

Third, the misconception map. List the misunderstandings this audience will predictably form, in the order they will form them, and mark exactly where in your sequence each should be headed off. I anticipate at least these, but add what I've missed: that backward induction proves real players are irrational; that the paradox evaporates once you remember players might make mistakes; that this is just the prisoner's dilemma stretched over time; that a longer centipede makes the conclusion weaker rather than stranger; and a persistent confusion of backward induction with iterated elimination of dominated strategies, since both feel like peeling.

Fourth, scope decisions. There is a deeper issue underneath the mechanics — that reaching a late node is itself evidence that the premise driving the backward induction has already failed — and a live dispute about whether that observation breaks the argument or is absorbed by it. Tell me how much of this to let in for this audience, where in the session it belongs if at all, and what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, including the experimental evidence on how people actually play.

Fifth, one closing question I can put to the room whose answers will distinguish a student who has grasped why the result is unsettling from a student who has merely learned to run the algorithm backward.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single sequencing decision everything else in the session depends on — then give the reasoning and the plan, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
