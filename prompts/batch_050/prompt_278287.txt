In my philosophy of language seminar we have spent the last few weeks on presupposition and conversational dynamics — accommodation, the common ground, and the idea of a conversational score that fixes standards as we go. I have been testing my own reactions against a series of cases, and I would like to know whether my verdicts add up to a coherent theory or whether I have to surrender some of them. Here they are, as they come to me:

1. At a party, someone I have just met says, "I should leave soon — I have to pick up my sister from the airport." I had no idea she had a sister, yet the remark is completely unobjectionable; I simply add "she has a sister" to the shared background and move on. Verdict: fine, and the adjustment is automatic.

2. Someone announces, out of nowhere, "The present king of France is bald." I do not react the way I react to an ordinary falsehood like "Paris is in Spain." My feeling is that something has gone wrong before the question of truth even comes up. Verdict: defective, not merely false.

3. But then: if someone asserts the very same sentence in the middle of an argument — say, a debate about what the French government should do — I find myself wanting to call it false, flat out, without any quibbling about presuppositions. Verdict: false. I can see that 2 and 3 are in tension; I report them as I find them.

4. A stranger at a bus stop says, "Sorry I'm flustered — my chauffeur took a wrong turn." I know nothing about him, so by the pattern of case 1 I should simply accommodate that he has a chauffeur. But it grates in a way the sister remark did not; it feels presumptuous rather than smoothly informative. Verdict: the presupposition gets through, yet something is still off — and I cannot say what.

5. Someone asks me, of a man who has never smoked, "Has he stopped smoking?" I feel I cannot answer yes or no without being complicit in a mistake; the only proper response is to correct the question. Verdict: the question is illegitimate until its presupposition is discharged.

6. On a road trip, someone says, "Good, the road is flat," and I take it to be true. The next day, in a conversation about surveying and the curvature of the Earth, someone says of the same road, "It isn't really flat," and I take that to be true as well. Verdict: both true, in their respective contexts, and neither speaker contradicts the other.

7. And yet: if a student wrote "the road is flat" on a physics exam where precision is the point, I would not hesitate to call it wrong; and if someone taught a child that France is shaped like a perfect hexagon, I would call that false even by the loose standards of case 6. Verdict: conversational standards are flexible, but not infinitely flexible.

The principle I tentatively extract is this: a presupposition is acceptable if and only if the hearer can quietly add it to the common ground — provided it is uncontroversial, not the point of the utterance, and costs the hearer nothing to accept — and an assertion is true if and only if it meets whatever standard of precision the conversation has currently set. Testing this against my cases, it handles 1, 5, and 6 well; it struggles with 4, since nothing about the chauffeur is controversial or costly, yet the remark is still fishy; it forces a choice between 2 and 3; and 7 seems to demand a constraint on standard-setting that I have not stated.

My questions, then: Is there a single, reasonably precise account of presupposition and accommodation that captures all or most of these verdicts, and what extra ingredient does case 4 require? Between verdicts 2 and 3, which should a good theory keep, and can it explain why the other one felt right? On the scorekeeping side, what governs when standards may rise and fall — and does the theory really predict that once the surveyors have spoken, "the road is flat" cannot be truly said again in that conversation? If some of my verdicts must be sacrificed, tell me which, and why those rather than others. Finally, please test the resulting account against cases I have not listed: does "I don't regret that I lied" still commit me to having lied, and what should we say about a speaker who utters the sentence from case 2 knowingly, in full awareness that there is no king of France, purely in order to mock the conversation he is in?
