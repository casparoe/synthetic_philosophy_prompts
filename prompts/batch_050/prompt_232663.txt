A friend who teaches evidence law walked me into the following argument at dinner, and I suspect it proves too much, but I can't locate the slip. (1) A piece of evidence supports a claim only to the extent that the evidence would be less likely to exist if the claim were false. (2) As deepfake generation becomes cheap and indistinguishable to any available scrutiny, a video purporting to show an event will be just as likely to exist whether or not the event occurred -- anyone with a motive can produce it either way. (3) Therefore video footage will soon cease to support any claim about what happened, and should carry no more weight in court than a bare assertion. (4) But this reasoning cannot be essentially about neural networks: doctored photographs, forged documents, and coached testimony have always been producible by anyone with sufficient motive, so the same argument, applied retroactively, shows that photographs, contracts, and letters never had evidential force either. (5) Since they plainly did, something in the argument must be wrong; and since (4) seems to be just (1)-(3) generalized, the rot is already present in the deepfake case. He concludes that (1) -- the likelihood-ratio conception of evidence -- is the premise to abandon, and that we need an account of evidence on which a recording can count even when its false existence was fully possible. I would rather keep (1) than accept (3), and I suspect the trouble is a slide somewhere in (2), but I can't articulate what it is or why (4)'s generalization doesn't expose it. Which step should I reject, why did it look compelling, and what does rejecting it commit me to about how provenance, chain of custody, and corroborating testimony actually do their epistemic work?
