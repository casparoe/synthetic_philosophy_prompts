Analyze the Snowden/NSA disclosures as a case study in privacy, but not as the usual security versus liberty opinion piece. I’ve read too many rationalist-blog arguments that reduce the issue to a cost-benefit estimate of terrorist plots stopped versus people embarrassed, and I think that misses most of what was philosophically interesting.

Use four lenses: privacy as secrecy, privacy as control over personal information, contextual integrity (whether information is flowing in ways appropriate to its social context), and privacy as protected mental slack—the room people need to explore thoughts before they have to defend or optimize them. Apply each lens to the bulk collection of metadata, where no human analyst necessarily reads any particular message. Be precise about what the privacy harm is supposed to be if the data mostly sits in a database: exposure, loss of control, changed incentives, susceptibility to future abuse, the inferential power of aggregation, or something else.

Also make the argument run in the other direction. What does the episode reveal about the weaknesses of each theory? For example, does contextual integrity become too conservative if existing institutional practice itself defines the context? Does control become impossible once data is generated by interacting with infrastructure you can’t realistically avoid? Does the mental-slack view prove too much by treating almost any observation as corrosive?

Finally, steelman the government position without resorting to a cartoon version of it, then say what institutional design would come closest to preserving both legitimate investigation and privacy: warrants, minimization, audits, technical access limits, sunset clauses, adversarial oversight, or some combination. I want a case analysis, not a verdict on Snowden himself.
