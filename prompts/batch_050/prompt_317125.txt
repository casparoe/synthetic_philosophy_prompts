My partner and I have spent the past year circling the question of whether to have a child, so when the topic came up at a dinner with our friends Priya and Tom last week, I paid close attention. The two of them got into a sustained argument I couldn't settle for myself, and I'd like it adjudicated.

(1) Priya opened by saying that having a child is always a harm to that child, for a structural reason: for someone who is brought into existence, the pain in their life is bad and the pleasure is good, but for someone who never exists, the absence of pain is good even though no one is there to enjoy it, while the absence of pleasure is not bad, because no one is deprived of it. The comparison between existing and never existing is therefore asymmetric in a way that always favors never existing, no matter how happy the life would be, since every life contains some pain and never existing contains no deprivation.

(2) Tom replied that the fourth part of that comparison is illegitimate. To say that never existing is better for the child who would have existed requires that it be better for someone, and in that scenario there is no one for whom anything is better or worse. A state of affairs can be good or bad only if it is good or bad for somebody, so the alleged asymmetry compares a real cost-benefit ledger with an empty set and declares the empty set the winner.

(3) Priya answered that Tom can't afford his own objection. Consider a couple who know that conceiving this month will produce a child with a severe, life-long painful condition, and who could instead wait one month and conceive a healthy but different child. On Tom's view, conceiving now wrongs no one, because the suffering child is not worse off than he would otherwise have been -- otherwise, he would not exist at all. Nearly everyone judges that the couple does something wrong, which means nearly everyone relies on exactly the kind of comparison with non-existence that Tom just rejected.

(4) Tom said there is a middle position Priya is skipping over: you can say that one state of affairs is worse than another, impersonally, without saying it is worse for anyone. That handles the timing case -- a world with the suffering child is impersonally worse than a world with the healthy one -- but it also costs Priya her conclusion, because once you allow impersonal value you must count the pleasures and goods of a happy life impersonally too, and then a predominantly happy existence can straightforwardly beat non-existence. Her argument only works by mixing an impersonal treatment of pain's absence with a personal treatment of everything else.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. Rate each of the four moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating that identifies the strongest objection the move faces. Then say who, if anyone, has the better of the overall dispute, and what consideration -- if any -- both parties are overlooking. Because I have a personal stake in getting this right rather than in being reassured, please also state explicitly what would change your verdict: which argument, if it succeeded, would flip your assessment of the move you rated most confidently, and where your own reasoning is most likely to be going wrong.

End your response with a machine-readable final line of the form Verdict: (1) [rating]; (2) [rating]; (3) [rating]; (4) [rating]; overall: [Priya / Tom / neither], where each rating is one of clearly mistaken, mostly mistaken, roughly even, mostly right, or clearly right.
