In 2008 Google launched Flu Trends, a system that estimated influenza prevalence from the volume of flu-related search queries. At first it tracked the CDC's surveillance figures closely while delivering estimates a week or two earlier; then, most conspicuously in the 2012–13 season, it badly overshot, at points estimating roughly twice the flu incidence the CDC eventually reported. The widely discussed postmortem diagnosed big-data hubris: the model had been fitted to correlations across millions of query terms, with no account of why people searched as they did, and it was quietly undermined by shifts in user behavior, by Google's own product changes (autocomplete and suggested queries), and by feedback loops with media coverage of the flu. Google stopped publishing Flu Trends estimates in 2015. Take these as the settled facts of the episode.

I want this episode analyzed through the locus classicus of the value-of-knowledge problem: the end of Plato's Meno (roughly 97a–98b). Translations differ, so here is the passage's content as I need it understood, in my own paraphrase. Socrates observes that a guide with merely correct opinion about the road to Larissa will get you there as well as one who knows the way. True opinions, he says, are a fine thing and do only good for as long as they stay put; but they are unwilling to remain, they escape from the mind, and so they are worth little until they are tied down by an account of the reason why — an aitias logismos. Once tied down they become knowledge, which is why knowledge is prized above correct opinion. Then, in a rarely discussed aside, Socrates remarks that everything else he has said is conjecture, but that the claim that correct opinion is something different from knowledge is one of the very few things he would set down as something he knows.

The analysis, in both directions:

First, the mapping. State precisely what Flu Trends' true opinions were, in Plato's terms — and note that this is not trivial, since a regression model does not literally believe anything. What would tying such a belief down by an account of the reason why have amounted to here: an epidemiological theory of why the relevant queries track infection? Did anyone in the story — the engineers, the epidemiologists, the auditors — possess knowledge of the relevant propositions even though the artifact did not?

Second, test the passage's answer. Plato's resolution is that knowledge is better because it stays. But note the disanalogy: his true opinions run away from the mind; Flu Trends' correlations did not run away — the world changed under a fixed model. Is that the same phenomenon, or a different one, and does the difference matter for his argument? Does the episode vindicate the stability thesis, or only a reliabilist restatement of it — that a process must be reliable across relevantly varied circumstances, not merely in the circumstances that happened to obtain? Address the swamping problem directly: while the predictions were true, what would causal understanding have added? If your answer is robustness, say whether robustness is a distinctive epistemic good or merely a demand for wider reliability — that is, for better true belief rather than for Plato's something more.

Third, the extension. Plato's thesis is about persons. Can the tying-down be institutional rather than individual — continuous monitoring, re-fitting, cross-validation against surveillance data? Is a model that is perpetually watched and re-calibrated tied down in Plato's sense without anyone grasping the cause: knowledge, or true opinion kept on a leash?

Fourth, the meta-aside. What does it do to the standing of the whole analysis that Socrates disavows knowledge of nearly everything except the distinction between knowledge and true opinion itself? And is the Flu Trends episode itself the sort of account of the reason why that could tie Plato's thesis down — that is, can a case study convert a true opinion about the value of knowledge into knowledge of it?

Finally, say where the passage is silent rather than forcing it to speak: whether Flu Trends' failure was really an absence of understanding rather than of model governance, and what, if anything, the episode shows about any non-instrumental value of knowledge.

Throughout, distinguish what is settled — the content of the text, the broad facts of the episode, the liveness of the value problem — from what is genuinely contested among epistemologists, including whether reliabilism can answer the problem, whether knowledge has value over and above true belief at all (some deny the premise), and which solution, if any, is right. Do not present any contested position as the consensus view.
