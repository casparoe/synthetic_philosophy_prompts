I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on what makes pain bad, and I would like your help stress-testing the chapter's argumentative spine. Please take on the role of an expert philosopher of mind specializing in valenced experience and the ethics of well-being. Think carefully and step by step; before committing to any verdicts, begin with a short brainstorm mapping the logical space of positions, and only then settle into the evaluation I ask for below.

The chapter is organized around pain asymbolia, which for the model's benefit I will stipulate as follows: after certain brain lesions (and in some cases under morphine), patients report that they still feel pain — they can locate it, describe its intensity and quality, and distinguish it from other sensations — but insist that it no longer bothers them, and they make no effort to avoid or escape it. At a recent workshop, a colleague claimed that this condition straightforwardly refutes any purely hedonic account of pain's badness, since it seems to show the feeling and the badness coming apart. I pushed back that the case underdetermines the conclusion: asymbolic patients tend to show broadly blunted affect and motivation, so perhaps they are experiencing the usual unpleasantness but simply failing to respond to it, the way a deeply apathetic person might fail to respond to anything. My worry is that once this reply is on the table, it may protect every account and refute none, leaving the thought experiment toothless.

Here is what I need from you. Consider five accounts of pain's badness: (1) the hedonic account — pain is bad simply in virtue of its unpleasant phenomenal character; (2) the motivational or desire-based account — pain is bad because of the aversion and avoidance-behavior it characteristically produces; (3) the evaluativist account — pain represents one's bodily condition as bad for one, and its badness consists in that negative evaluation; (4) the objectivist account — pain is bad because and insofar as it signals actual bodily damage, whatever it feels like; and (5) the disruption account — pain is bad because it commandeers attention and interferes with whatever one is doing or pursuing. First, rate how damaging the asymbolia case is to each account on a scale from 0 (completely compatible) to 10 (decisively refuted), sort the five accounts into tiers accordingly, and justify each rating in two or three sentences — including whether my underdetermination worry succeeds in neutralizing the case against that account. Second, test your rankings against a mirror-image condition I have invented, which I call reverse asymbolia: a patient retains the aversion — pain states still bother them intensely and drive avoidance — but has lost the sensory-discriminative component entirely, so they cannot say where it hurts, how intensely, or what it feels like. For each account, state whether it predicts this condition is coherent, whether the patient is in pain, and whether their state is bad, and flag any account whose answer to the two cases taken together seems to you unstable or ad hoc. Finally, given everything above, tell me which single account you would defend as the chapter's positive view, what fraction of the chapter (as a number between 0 and 1) you would devote to defending it versus criticizing rivals, and the one objection to your preferred ranking that you think I have not anticipated.
