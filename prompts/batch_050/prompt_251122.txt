I've never studied philosophy, but I got interested in the money pump argument after a friend explained it to me: if I prefer apples to bananas, bananas to cherries, and cherries to apples, someone could keep offering me tiny favorable swaps until I've paid money just to walk in a circle. I've been trying to figure out what, exactly, is supposed to be irrational about me in that story, and the more I read informal discussions, the more it seems like there are several different answers hiding under the same label.

I'd like you to build me a decision tree that sorts out the positions on what the money pump actually shows. The audience is people like me: smart, no formal training, allergic to jargon. Five to seven yes/no or either/or questions, each phrased as a concrete claim someone could react to without knowing any names. For example, a question might ask whether it's possible to evaluate a single choice on its own, or only the whole sequence of choices someone makes across their life; another might ask whether the irrationality is in the preferences themselves or only in how someone acts on them over time; another might turn on whether a person at one moment and that same person a week later should be treated like one agent or like two agents negotiating with each other.

Here is what I want from the tree:

1. The leaves must include at least these outcomes: (a) the view that the cyclic preferences themselves are already irrational, before anyone is offered a single swap; (b) the view that the preferences are fine but a rational person must bind themselves in advance and refuse to reconsider, even when reconsidering looks profitable at the moment; (c) the view that the preferences are fine and each choice can be rational on its own, and the real mistake is only in letting the pattern repeat; (d) the view that each moment's choice is judged purely by the preferences held at that moment, so the pump is exploitable but not strictly irrational at any step. If the logic of the questions produces a leaf that maps to a position I've missed, include it.

2. Build the tree from the questions outward, not from the labels inward. Show me the full space of answer combinations your questions generate, and for any combination that lands on no known position, say briefly whether it's empty because it's incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

3. Include at least one question that separates positions the standard money pump story can't separate on its own, using a variation of the case if needed -- for instance, one where the person sees the whole pump coming in advance, or one where the swaps are free and only time is lost.

4. Flag any pair of positions your tree genuinely cannot distinguish, and explain what further question or case would be needed to split them.

5. End with a short worked example: take a fictional character with stated attitudes, walk them down the tree question by question, and show which leaf they land on and whether that verdict seems right.
