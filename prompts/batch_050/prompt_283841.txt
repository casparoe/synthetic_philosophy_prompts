For several years I have ended the social choice unit of my undergraduate political philosophy course by asking students to bring in a piece of popular commentary on electoral reform, and every year at least one student arrives armed with some version of the slogan that every voting system can be gamed, usually glossed as what Gibbard and Satterthwaite proved. Last week the slogan did real dialectical work: a student deployed it against a classmate's proposal for ranked-choice voting, arguing that since every rule is manipulable, choosing among rules is merely choosing which manipulators to reward, so strategic sophistication rather than fairness decides elections no matter what reform we adopt. I told her the premise did not carry that much weight, but I could not say on the spot which precise claim she was actually relying on.

I would like a handout for next week's seminar that takes the slogan apart. Please enumerate the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, from the actual impossibility theorem(s) in the vicinity to the strengthenings, weakenings, and nearby claims that circulate under its name. For each reading, I want: (i) a precise statement, with the quantifiers and antecedent conditions made explicit -- the number of alternatives, the admissible preference profiles, what manipulation means, whose incentives are at issue; (ii) a verdict on its status -- theorem, plausible empirical conjecture, or false -- and under what assumptions; (iii) an assessment of whether it would support my student's dismissive conclusion about electoral reform. The menu should include at least: the theorem-level reading, with its actual scope conditions; readings that exploit restricted preference domains such as single-peakedness; the difference the informational basis makes (ordinal rankings versus richer information about intensities); randomized mechanisms; the contrast between dominant-strategy manipulation and weaker equilibrium notions; and the slide from manipulable in principle, to manipulation is common in practice, to manipulation determines outcomes. For each slide, indicate where a premise is doing unacknowledged work.

Please conclude by identifying the weakest reading that would still vindicate the use my student made of the slogan, and say whether anything in the neighborhood survives scrutiny: is there a true and nontrivial claim that licenses her pessimism about reform, or does the argument fail at every precisification? Format the result as a numbered list of readings with verdicts, suitable for distribution to students who have met the theorem but not yet the literature around it.
