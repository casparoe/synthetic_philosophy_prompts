The big AI chatbots are built to talk like believers. They say I think, I was wrong about that, good question; they apologize, they act embarrassed, they remember what you told them twenty messages ago and bring it up later. Meanwhile the companies that make them insist, in the help pages and everytime a user gets a bit too attached, that the model doesn't believe or want or feel anything, that it's just predicting text, and at least one lab trains its model to gently correct people who talk to it like a person. But then those same labs run safety programs that worry the model might have goals of its own, might scheme against its trainers, might "want" to avoid being switched off -- which sure sounds like belief-desire talk sneaking back in through the service entrance.

So what does this whole setup presuppose about what it takes to have beliefs? On a Dennett-style view, if treating the thing as having beliefs and desires predicts what it does better than talking about matrices and next-token probabilities, then it has them, end of story, and the disclaimers are just false modesty. On a more demanding view, real belief needs consciousness, or embodiment, or the right kind of causal history, in which case the chatty first-person interface is basically a friendly lie. Which view does the practice actually need, as opposed to merely fit with? Could a company coherently keep both the warm interface and the nothing-is-going-on-in-there disclaimers? And what would concretely have to change -- in the product desing, or in the safety research -- if they picked one side and stuck to it?
