I’m a TA for an undergraduate ethics course and I’m grading responses to the following exam question: “In ‘Famine, Affluence, and Morality,’ Peter Singer presents the case of the drowning child. Reconstruct the analogy and the moral principle it is meant to establish, explain how Singer extends the principle from the pond case to famine relief, and evaluate what you take to be the strongest objection to his argument.” The response below is one I’m uncertain about, because it struck me as mostly right on a quick read but slightly off in ways I couldn’t pin down. Please grade it out of 10 and, more importantly, give me a point-by-point set of comments I could hand back to the student: identify what the answer gets right, flag any places where it misstates Singer’s position, conflates distinct claims, or draws an inference that doesn’t follow, and note anything important the answer omits.

Here is the student’s answer: “Singer asks us to imagine walking past a shallow pond in which a small child is drowning. Wading in to save the child would ruin your shoes and make you late for a lecture, and everyone agrees you are obligated to do it anyway. From this Singer derives his principle: if it is in our power to prevent something very bad from happening without thereby sacrificing anything morally significant, we ought, morally, to do it. He then argues that since absolute poverty involves suffering and death that we could prevent by donating to famine relief, the same principle requires us to donate, and that considerations of distance or of there being millions of other people who could equally well help make no moral difference — after all, it would be monstrous to ignore the drowning child because she was far away or because other bystanders were also doing nothing. Singer wrote the paper during the Bengal famine of the early 1970s, and he takes the upshot to be that we are obligated to give away our money up to roughly the point at which further giving would make us as badly off as those we are helping. Because Singer is a utilitarian, his argument here rests on utilitarian premises: saving the drowning child maximizes overall happiness, and so does donating to effective charities, so anyone who accepts the verdict in the pond case is committed by utilitarian reasoning to radical generosity. The strongest objection is that the principle is too demanding, since it would leave most of us donating nearly everything we have. Singer replies that demandingness is no objection to a moral principle, because morality often demands more than we are inclined to do, so the objection fails and his conclusion stands.”

For context, the question is worth a fair chunk of the exam and I’ve been instructed to penalize misattributions of the view even when the surrounding prose sounds fluent. I’d like the grading rationale to be specific enough that the student could see exactly where marks were lost and why, rather than a general impression of quality.
