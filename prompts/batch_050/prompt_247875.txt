For an advanced undergraduate seminar on choice under radical uncertainty, design a decision tree that sorts a participant from a single starting commitment — the evidence available does not justify assigning probabilities to the states of the world — to one of the standard decision rules for choice under ignorance. The required leaves are: maximin, minimax regret, the Laplacean rule (treat all states as equally probable and maximize expected utility), and the Hurwicz optimism–pessimism criterion. You may add further leaves if the logical space of your questions demands them, but those four must appear.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the names. Use six to eight branch nodes, each a concrete choice problem or claim that a participant can answer without knowing any of the labels — for instance, a case in which adding a new option to the menu, one that is worse in every state, changes which of the original options looks best; a case in which one option is better than another in some states and worse in none; or a case in which the states are described in a way that makes them seem interchangeable. The diagnostic work should be done by questions like these, not by asking whether the participant is cautious, regrets missed opportunities, or trusts symmetry. The point is to sort people by what they would actually choose, and your scoring commentary should make explicit which formal property each question probes (menu-dependence, statewise dominance, sensitivity to intermediate outcomes, and so on).

Your tree should be able to separate the four required leaves behaviorally, but it will encounter two pairs it cannot cleanly split, and you should handle these honestly rather than papering over them. First, nothing in a person's pattern of choices distinguishes maximin understood as a rule of individual prudence from maximin understood in Rawls's way, as the output of deliberation behind a veil of ignorance about the design of society, since the disagreement is about justification and scope rather than about what to choose; indicate how your tree should treat this — for example, by adding a final node about the rule's intended role, or by marking a single leaf as shared. Second, the Laplacean rule and Harsanyi's claim that rationality under ignorance requires Bayesian reasoning with a uniform prior likewise coincide in every choice while differing in what justifies them; flag this pair too.

Finally, include at least one combination of answers that your questions allow but no standard position occupies, and briefly diagnose why it is empty: is it incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? At least one such combination should be a rule that, like Hurwicz, attends to more than the worst outcome but, unlike Hurwicz, refuses to assign fixed weights to the best and worst cases — say, a rule that cares about the median outcome across states, or one that lexicographically checks the worst, then the second-worst, and so on. State whether such a rule would be a genuine rival to the four required leaves or collapses into one of them.
