So I teach a second-year ethics course (about 16 students, one 75-minute slot) and I want to do something on lying vs. misleading before we get into the literature. Here's the specific thing I want them to feel: most students walk in assuming "misleading without technically lying" is obviously just as bad as lying, or obviously a loophole, and neither intuition survives contact with actually having to do it. I want them to run into the distinction with their own mouths, so to speak.

Design me a game or activity for this. A few requirements and constraints:

- There should be real in-game incentives to deceive (points, not money), and the rules should naturally split deceptive moves into two categories: outright saying something false vs. saying something literally true that implies something false. I don't want to announce these categories up front, they should just fall out of how the game works.
- Ideally there's a mechanic where the deceived player gets to push back afterwards ("you said X!") and the deceiver gets to respond, because I have a hunch the morally relevant thing isn't truth vs. falsity of the sentence but how much the speaker puts themselves behind the claim -- a lie exposes you to a kind of protest that a misleading implicature lets you wriggle out of ("I never *said* that"). I want to see whether students spontaneously exploit that asymmetry, and whether it feels like a defense or like a slimeball move when they do.
- Tell me how to stop the degenerate strategy where everyone just speaks in hyper-literal implicatures the whole time and the game turns into a lawyering contest (or maybe that's a feature? tell me if so).
- Include: setup and rules precise enough that I could run it cold, the scoring, a rough timeline for the 75 mins, and a debrief that maps specific moments from the game onto the lying/misleading debate (e.g. which round should make the "no moral difference" people squirm, which moment isolates the assertion vs. implicature question).
- Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and be honest about what the activity *can't* capture -- e.g. in a game everyone knows deception is on the table, which is nothing like real conversation, so say how you'd handle that in the debrief rather than pretending the simulation covers it.
