I spent forty years as an experimental physicist before turning to philosophy in retirement, and I have become preoccupied with the problem of consequentialist cluelessness: if every action has consequences rippling indefinitely into the future, and we cannot even estimate their net value, then consequentialist comparisons between actions seem impossible to draw. I am applying for a small one-year research grant in moral philosophy and need a proposal of roughly two pages. My half-formed idea is this. In my old field we routinely confronted effects we knew existed but could not model -- systematic uncertainties to which no honest error bar could be attached -- and our practice was to bracket them rather than average over them, treating a conclusion as trustworthy only if it survived every plausible assignment of the unknown quantity. I suspect that the distinction philosophers draw between simple cluelessness, where symmetric ignorance arguably washes out, and complex cluelessness, where it does not, mirrors the scientific distinction between statistical uncertainty and model misspecification, and that decision tools developed for the latter (imprecise credences, robustness criteria, worst-case reasoning) might tell us which far-future effects a consequentialist may legitimately set aside without pretending to know them. Turn this into the proposal: state the research question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; explain why existing responses to cluelessness leave it open; lay out what I would actually do, month by month; describe what a positive result and a negative result would each look like; and address the strongest objection -- that bracketing unknown effects is merely evasion by fiat -- with an account of how the project would meet it.
