I convene the final exam for our third-year Philosophy of Action module, and this year I want to set a question on Kavka's toxin puzzle that can't be answered by students simply retelling the setup (eccentric billionaire, million pounds for intending at midnight to drink a toxin the next afternoon, no obligation to actually drink it, etc). Every year I get stacks of answers that summarise the puzzle nicely and then conclude, vaguely, that it shows something interesting about intention, which earns them very little. So draft me one essay question, answerable in about 75 minutes, that forces candidates to take a stand on where exactly the difficulty lies: is the problem that you cannot form the intention once you know you'll have no reason to drink, or that you could form it but only via some self-manipulation that seems beside the point? I want them to grapple with Kavka's thought that you can't intend to do what you're confident you won't do, and with the gap between having reason to intend and having reason to act -- the money is a reason for the former, not the latter.

Concretely: please write the question itself, then a model answer of roughly 600-700 words written at the level of a strong but not flawless finalist, and then a marking scheme out of 25 marks built around five must-hit points. For each must-hit point, give the marks its worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically fumble it (e.g. conflating "I intend to drink" with "I'll make myself drink", or treating the self-manipulation routes like side-bets and hiring a henchman as straightforward solutions rather than as moves that arguably change the subject). The scheme should also make clear what seperates a first-class answer from a 2:1 -- ideally by naming one specific move a first must contain, such as explaining why the puzzle would (or wouldn't) still arise if drinking the toxin were made physically unavoidable after midnight.
