Okay so I'm a novelist and I talked myself into a corner last night and I need someone to find the exit. I was drafting a book about a guy writing a book, and I produced this argument that seems to prove something nuts:

(1) Whatever I can describe in complete detail, I can put into a novel -- that's basically the whole job.
(2) I can describe in complete detail a novelist sitting at his desk choosing every word of a novel, including his hesitations, his deletions, his reasons for each choice. I know exactly what that's like; I'm living it.
(3) So I can write a novel whose protagonist is a novelist who writes, word for word, the very novel he's a character in. Nothing in (1) or (2) rules this out.
(4) In that novel, every single word of the text is chosen by the protagonist. The book attributes each word to him, his deliberation is the whole story, there is no word in the book that isn't his doing within the book.
(5) If every word of a text is attributed to one chooser, then that chooser is the author of the text. Authorship is nothing over and above being the one who chose the words.
(6) So the author of my novel is my character, not me.
(7) But he's a fiction! He can't choose anything -- fictions don't cause ink to hit pages.
(8) So (1) has to go: there are things I can describe completely that I nevertheless cannot put into a novel.

That last step is what kills me, because (1) really does seem like the whole job, and if it's false I want someone to tell me which describable things are unwritable and why. But (7) also seems obviously right, and (6) seems obviously wrong. My guess is something slipped between (3) and (6) -- the book attributes every word to the character, sure, but attributing isn't the same as... something. I cant quite cash it out though, and every time I try I end up saying "but really I wrote it," which is exactly what's in dispute.

Which step do I actually reject? I'd most like to keep (1) and (7). What does rejecting the guilty step cost me -- like, does it mean no novel can ever be genuinely self-including, and if so, does that tell me something general about stories that contain their own tellers, or is it just a verbal trick I've fallen for?
