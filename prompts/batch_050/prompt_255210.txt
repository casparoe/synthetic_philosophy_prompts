I'm putting together materials for my grad seminar on distributive justice and I want to spend a session on the clash between Harsanyi's aggregation theorem and Diamond-style worries about ex post fairness. Rather than reuse the tired textbook coin-flip case, I want a thought experiment built to order. So write me one that meets these specs:

1. Realistic setting — an allocation decision a normal person could actually face: hospital triage, a grant panel, splitting a scarce bonus at work, that kind of thing. No trolleys, no perfect predictors, no exotic physics.
2. Small numbers. At most three affected people, at most two states of nature, and every outcome gets an explicit utility number, so the arithmetic is fully checkable by a student with a napkin.
3. The theorem has to strictly prefer the unfair option. In the standard version of Diamond's objection, Harsanyi's criterion merely calls the fair lottery and the unfair certainty *equal*, and the egalitarian grumbles about the indifference. I want the harder version: engineer the utilities so that social expected utility ranks the ex post unfair option strictly above the ex post fair one, so that following the theorem means actively choosing unfairness rather than just tolerating it.
4. The unfairness must be visible in the structure of the options before any uncertainty is resolved — it can't depend on the lottery happening to turn out badly after the fact.

Then do the demonstration:

- State each premise the theorem needs (each individual's preferences satisfy expected utility theory, the social ranking satisfies expected utility theory, ex ante Pareto) and show how they jointly force the utilitarian sum and hence deliver the verdict in your case.
- Pinpoint which premise the fairness intuition is actually pressuring. Is the complaint really aimed at ex ante Pareto, or at the requirement that the social ranking obey the expected utility axioms (independence / sure-thing)? Show the other premises can't absorb the pressure instead.
- Say what happens to your case if you drop that premise: does the fairness intuition get vindicated, or does the aggregation theorem just fall silent?
- Finish with an honest note on which feature of your case is doing all the work, and how a defender of Harsanyi — someone who thinks ex ante reasoning exhausts what matters, or who wants to split the "goodness" of an outcome from its "fairness" — would try to resist.

One warning from experience: don't cheat by stipulating the verdicts, and watch that the disadvantaged party really is disadvantaged. If everyone ex ante prefers the supposedly "unfair" option, half my seminar will simply deny it's unfair and you'll have built a different (much easier) case than the one I asked for. Aim for something where reasonable people genuinly disagree about what to do.
