I'm taking a philosophy of technology course, and our discussion-section leader put this on an unofficial study sheet about coherent extrapolated volition:

'CEV means constructing an ideally rational future self for each person: someone who knows all the relevant nonmoral facts, has considered every argument, and is free from bias. We then imagine these ideal selves bargaining until their choices agree, and the AI implements that compromise. It is called volition rather than preference because it concerns what you would endorse on reflection, not what you currently happen to want. Since the extrapolated person is simply you under better conditions, their judgment has authority over yours in the same way your sober judgment has authority over a promise you made while drunk. If the idealized people still disagree, then there is no coherent volition for the AI to follow. So CEV is neither utilitarianism nor paternalism: it follows each person's deepest values rather than an external welfare function.'

She also proposed what she called a laundering test: an extrapolation is illegitimate if the only reason present-you would accept its conclusion is that the extrapolation process was designed to alter your evaluative standards until you accepted it. She said this catches the difference between learning something that changes your mind and being re-engineered into someone with a different mind.

How fair is all this as a summary? Please seperate what is basically accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what distorts CEV. In particular, I'm unsure about: whether the ideal self is supposed to be an actual future person or a hypothetical deliberative procedure; whether full factual knowledge and freedom from bias are required or just useful stand-ins; whether CEV requires all extrapolated people to converge; whether the sober/drunk analogy is fair or hides a problem about identity and changing values; and whether calling it neither utilitarian nor paternalistic follows.

Also assess the laundering test. Does it capture a real worry about extrapolation being path-dependent or manipulable, or does it just assume that present preferences have the final say? If the inaccurate parts are corrected, does the rest of the summary still support her conclusion that current disagreement is only a technical obstacle?
