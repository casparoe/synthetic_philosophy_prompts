For a twelfth-grade philosophy elective final project, I'm building a "what kind of bad faith is this?" sorting quiz on Sartre, and I need the instrument itself. Here's the setup I want: eight to ten short vignettes, each showing an ordinary teenager or adult doing something recognizable in school, work, family, or social life (for example, someone insisting they're "just not the kind of person who speaks up," or someone excusing a cruel joke by saying they had no choice about how the evening went). None of the vignettes should reuse Sartre's own famous examples; everyday analogues only.

Each vignette gets three answer options, and the scoring key maps responses onto these categories: collapsing oneself into pure facticity (denying one's freedom), collapsing into pure transcendence (denying one's situation, past, or body), treating values as if they were objective facts of the world, the version of bad faith that hides inside total sincerity about "what one is," and -- as a distractor so the quiz doesn't just diagnose everyone -- a genuinely authentic response to the situation. The key should explain for each vignette why the best answer wins and what specifically each distractor misreads, since I want at least some vignettes where two forms of bad faith are plausibly both present and the grader has to pick the dominant one. Please flag any vignette where you think two categories genuinely can't be separated, rather than forcing a clean answer.

The point of the project is that readers get sorted by how they judge cases, not by whether they recognize terminology, so the vignettes and options must avoid words like facticity, transcendence, and bad faith itself. Aim for vignettes of three to five sentences each.
