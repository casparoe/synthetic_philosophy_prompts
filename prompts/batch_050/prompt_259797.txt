I was recently promoted to head a forty-person department at a mid-sized company, and I've discovered -- mostly through HR complaints landing on my desk -- that my casual remarks keep becoming binding. An employee told HR I had "promised" her a salary review because I said in a one-on-one that we should be able to revisit compensation after the merger. A vendor treats a sentence in one of my emails as a formal commitment to renew. When I mused in a team meeting that remote work "might not be sustainable," three people updated their resumes. Meanwhile, when I actually try to do things with words -- apologize on behalf of the department for a botched client deliverable, delegate signing authority to a deputy -- I'm told the apology "doesn't count" unless the CEO signs off, and the delegation isn't valid because I announced it in chat rather than in writing.

I make perhaps a dozen consequential utterances a day, in meetings, email, and chat, often with minutes to think and no lawyer in the room. I'd like a pre-speech and pre-send procedure I can run in under two minutes, grounded in whatever the philosophy of language actually knows about the difference between describing something and doing something -- the conditions under which a remark becomes a promise, an offer, an apology, an appointment, or an official announcement, and what authority, form, and audience uptake each of those requires. It should be a sequence of questions in order: what to ask about my own standing to perform the act, what words or context trigger it even without formulas like "I promise," what makes it misfire or come off insincere, and what I should add (or delete) when I intend only to speculate, vent, or think aloud. Mark clearly where the procedure hands off to judgment -- places where whether something counts as a commitment depends on norms of my workplace or the expectations I have myself created -- rather than pretending those can be settled mechanically.

Please run the procedure on three cases: the salary-review remark above; an apology for the client error, drafted by me, when the affected client believes only our CEO speaks for the company; and a chat message to my deputy reading "you can sign off on invoices from now on." Include at least one case where the procedure delivers an answer I will not like -- where something I experienced as harmless musing counts as an act with real normative consequences, or something I experienced as decisive counts as nothing at all -- and say plainly what the procedure cannot do, i.e., which of my problems are actually legal, cultural, or interpersonal rather than conceptual.
