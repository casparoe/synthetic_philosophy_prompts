Got into an argument at the pub last week that I'm still chewing on. We were talking about mind uploading (as you do) and whether a sufficiently detailed simulation of my brain running on a server would actually be me, or just a very fancy recording. My mate Dan shut the whole thing down by saying, look, the brain is a computer, so if you reproduce the computation you reproduce the mind, end of story. Everyone sort of nodded like that settled it and moved on to talking about football.

But the more I think about it the less I know what he was even claiming. 'The brain is a computer' gets said constantly -- by neuroscientists, AI people, philosophers, random tech bros -- and I'm fairly sure they're not all saying the same thing. Sometimes it seems to mean something almost empty (sure, the brain takes inputs and produces outputs, so does a toaster?), sometimes it seems to mean something very specific about neurons doing digital symbol-crunching, and sometimes it seems to be a straight-up metaphysical claim that mind just IS computation and the meat doesn't matter.

So here's what I want: lay out the different precise claims that slogan could be making. For each one, say whether it's a platitude nobody would deny, whether it's a substantive empirical claim (and if so, how's the evidence looking), or whether it's actually false or confused. I'm especially interested in:

1. The reading where anything that maps inputs to outputs 'computes' -- is that claim even false-able, and does it say anything interesting about brains in particular?
2. The reading where the brain is specifically a *digital* computer, i.e. discrete states plus rules -- I've heard people say the brain is more analog than that, is that a real distinction or pedantry?
3. The reading that's just about the brain processing information and having representations -- is that the one working neuroscientists actually mean when they say it?
4. The strong reading Dan needed for his uploading argument, where computation is all there is to mind. Which of the weaker readings does it follow from, if any, and which of them is he just assuming?

And then the punchline: given Dan used it to *end* an argument, which reading does his conclusion actually require, and is that reading one of the defensible ones or one of the dodgy ones? I have a suspicion the slogan is doing a bait-and-switch -- starting from a reading everyone accepts and quietly sliding to the one he needs -- but I can't quite put my finger on where the slide happens.
