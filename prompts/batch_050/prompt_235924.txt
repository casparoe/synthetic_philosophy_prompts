Okay so my roommate and I just spent like three hours going in circles about Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument from Anarchy, State, and Utopia, and honestly we got nowhere. His take: the argument basically shows that any tax-and-redistribute scheme is unjust, period, because the unequal outcome arose from a zillion choices nobody forced. My take: the argument is sneaky — it makes each tiny transfer look obviously harmless and then pretends the aggregate result inherits all that harmlessnes. We agreed to settle it the mature way: poetry.

You're a political philosopher who moonlights as a formal poet, and I'd like you to think carefully about this before drafting. Write me a villanelle about the Wilt Chamberlain argument. Some requirements, because villanelles live and die on their refrains:

- The two refrains should each voice one side of the argument — one from the perspective of the just starting distribution everyone supposedly agreed to, one from the perspective of the fans each happily handing over their quarter to watch Wilt play. Roughly: a "we all started even, and even was fair" line versus a "nobody made me, I paid it myself" line. Refine these, don't just use my phrasing.
- The form has to do argumentative work: the refrains coming back again and again should feel like the two premises Nozick keeps reasserting no matter how you squirm, and in the closing quatrain they should finally stand side by side in a way that dramatizes the real crux — that you can keep the pattern or you can keep the free transfers, but apparently not both without someone interfering.
- Wilt and the quarter need to actually appear. The argument depends on each step being small and voluntary, so keep the amounts and the choices concrete, no vague abstractions about "wealth" or "capital."
- One fairness condition: my roommate must not be able to say the poem is just propaganda for my side. The ending should let the tension stand rather than declaring a winner.

Before the poem itself, do a short expert brainstorm out loud: what each refrain should be, how the rhyme scheme maps onto the dialectic, and why the villanelle fits better than, say, a sonnet. Then give the poem. Someone who knows the argument should be able to tell which refrain is which and wince at the ending; someone who doesn't should still get a good poem about a tall man, a ball, and a million small choices.
