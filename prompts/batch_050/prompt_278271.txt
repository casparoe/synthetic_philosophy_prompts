Two years ago my mother was diagnosed with early-stage Alzheimer's. She was an archivist by profession and a methodical person all her life, and her response to the diagnosis was to build what she calls her external memory: a large notebook divided into color-coded, cross-referenced sections, which she updates every evening without fail. It records her medications and her reasons for taking them, her friends and what she last discussed with each of them, the promises she has made, and the decisions she has reached together with her reasons for reaching them. Every entry is in her own hand, written at a time when she still held the relevant facts in her head. She carries the notebook everywhere, consults it constantly and unthinkingly, and trusts what it says over whatever occurs to her unaided — she has told me as much herself. With the notebook in hand she functions remarkably well: ask her anything about her own affairs and she will give you a confident, correct answer within half a minute.

Last month my sister, who now lives with her, decided the notebook was feeding the problem. Her view is that it lets our mother disguise how impaired she really is and postpones an honest reckoning with the diagnosis. She replaced it with a single laminated page of daily essentials. Our mother consults the page, but she can no longer navigate her life: she does not know what she promised me, why she chose her current doctor, or what she had decided about selling the house. My sister insists nothing of hers was lost, since the knowledge was never in our mother to begin with — it was in a notebook, and a person is what is in her head. I feel my sister removed a part of our mother, not a prop.

The dispute has acquired practical teeth. Four months ago, while in full command of the notebook, our mother revised her will and granted me power of attorney, with a lawyer present who satisfied himself that she understood what she was doing. My sister now intends to challenge both documents, arguing that our mother lacked capacity because she could not have reproduced the relevant facts unaided. And it is true that, notebook aside, she could not have.

I am not asking for legal advice. What I want to know is how to think about the underlying question: what did my mother know at the moment of signing? Is that question even well-defined independently of a decision about whether the notebook counted as part of her? And if it did count, was my sister's removal of it closer to tidying a cluttered room — or to inflicting a brain injury?
