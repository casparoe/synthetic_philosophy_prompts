At the closing dinner of a summer institute on philosophy and music, I have been asked to give a six-minute tribute to its retiring founder, a scholar and former orchestral conductor who has spent her career defending a demanding interpretation of Schopenhauer. On her reading, instrumental music is philosophically exceptional not because it depicts particular feelings and not because its formal patterns are merely beautiful, but because it presents willing itself more directly than the other arts, which approach reality through Ideas or enduring forms. Yet she also insists that music affords release: while absorbed in it, we temporarily cease to be the needy, striving individuals that ordinary life makes of us. The audience will be evenly divided between Schopenhauer specialists who will notice every technical error and musicians, administrators, students, and relatives who know her chiefly through rehearsals and faculty meetings.

Write the tribute so that this apparent tension does real structural work rather than serving as decoration: how can an art that puts us in unusually close contact with ceaseless striving also give us rest from striving? Develop a resolution that is more careful than simply saying that music expresses emotion. You may distinguish being shown the structure of willing from being driven by one’s own will, but test that distinction against familiar musical experience—expectation, tension, delay, resolution, and the fact that a cadence settles a phrase without abolishing desire altogether. Include one central musical analogy that the nonspecialists can follow, one affectionate joke involving a withheld resolution, and no quotations, titles, footnotes, or scholarly name-dropping. Explain Will and objectification without jargon, and avoid implying that music communicates doctrines, describes a composer’s private feelings, or permanently redeems suffering. The final praise should follow from the philosophy: she taught both scholars and performers how to encounter human wanting with unusual clarity without thereby becoming its servant.
