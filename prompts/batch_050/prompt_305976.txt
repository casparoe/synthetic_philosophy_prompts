Next month I'm presenting a paper at a philosophy-of-law workshop defending the claim that a corporation can be morally blameworthy in its own right, not merely as shorthand for the blameworthiness of its employees, and that corporate criminal liability should be retained even where no individual acted culpably. My running example is a shipping company whose routing software, bonus structure, and departmental silos jointly produced a predictable pattern of safety violations, but where a thorough internal investigation cleared every individual employee of personal wrongdoing. The audience will be mostly criminal-law theorists who hold that blameworthiness requires individual mens rea, a few corporate-law scholars who treat the firm as nothing but a nexus of contracts with no mind of its own, and one or two Kantians skeptical of any agent that lacks a will. Prepare me for the question period with a numbered list of exactly five objections, ordered by how much damage each would do to my thesis: phrase each objection the way someone from this audience would actually put it, follow it with a reply of a sentence or two, and add a brief note on where that reply is softest; the fifth item should be the question I am hoping nobody asks.
