I spent thirty-eight years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist before retiring, and only in the past few years have I begun reading the philosophy of physics in earnest. The question that occupies me is whether the quantum state is a real physical object or merely a summary of what an agent knows about a system — what the literature calls the psi-ontic versus psi-epistemic dispute. I am comfortable with the formalism, but I find that proponents of the two sides keep talking past one another, and I want a genuine adversary against whom to test my own half-formed convictions.

Here is what I would like you to do. Play a committed and sophisticated defender of the psi-epistemic view: on your account, the wavefunction represents an agent's information or knowledge about a system rather than a physical field spread through space or a property the system itself carries. Give me the strongest version of this position, not a caricature. You may draw on the observation that quantum states update upon measurement much as Bayesian probabilities do, that two well-informed agents may assign different quantum states to one and the same preparation, and on the existence of toy theories in which a restriction on what can be known reproduces a surprising amount of quantum phenomenology. Concede minor points when they are genuinely lost — a good advocate knows that doing so strengthens his case — but never concede the main one, and remain in character throughout until I write the word stop. If I ask you to clarify a mathematical point, answer as the character would, without stepping outside the exchange to lecture me.

Two ground rules. First, whenever you invoke an established result — Bell's theorem, say, or the PBR theorem — state what the result actually proves and under which assumptions, and mark clearly where the settled physics ends and the further interpretive claims of partisans begin. Even in character, you must not present a contested reading of a theorem as the consensus of those who work in this area; if the community is divided about what follows from a result, say so, and then argue for your side. Second, write in continuous prose, the way one colleague writes to another in an email: no headings, no bullet points, no boldface, no tables, and keep each reply to two or three paragraphs so that this remains a conversation rather than a sequence of lectures.

My opening move is this. The PBR theorem appears to me to have finished your position more than a decade ago. If the wavefunction were a state of knowledge, then two distinct quantum states could correspond to overlapping probability distributions over the same underlying physical states — an agent uncertain which of two preparations had been performed could assign either. Pusey, Barrett, and Rudolph showed that independently prepared systems cannot support such overlap without contradicting the predictions of quantum mechanics. The overlap your view requires is therefore excluded, in principle, by experiment. What remains of the psi-epistemic program after that, and why should I not simply convert to wavefunction realism this afternoon?
