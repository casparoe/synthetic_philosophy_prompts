I'm a journalist covering science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm fact-checking a long feature that leans hard on the old line about the arc of the moral universe being long but bending toward justice -- you know the one, King popularized it, and it goes back to an abolitionist preacher before him. My writer uses it to close the piece, and the closing argument is basically: history has a direction, progress is the default setting, so despair about the long run is irrational even when the short run looks terrible. An editor flagged it as doing more work than a metaphor should be allowed to do, and honestly I think she's right, but I want to check properly before we cut or keep it.

So here's what I need. Put on your hat as a philosopher of history who's also comfortable with the social-science side of this. Think carefully, don't rush to a verdict. Start by brainstorming freely -- every feature of the arc image you can think of, every feature of actual moral history that might or might not match -- and then organise it into the deliverables below:

1. A correspondence table. Spell out the mapping explicitly: what plays the role of the arc itself, what plays the role of the bending (is there a force? whose?), what counts as "toward" (who's standing at the endpoint judging?), and what work the word "long" is doing. If part of the metaphor has no clear counterpart in the actual phenomenon, say so in the table rather than forcing it.

2. The disanalogies that matter for my writer's specific conclusion. A literal arc has its curvature fixed from the start -- it can't unbend, and it doesn't need anyone to push it. Moral progress plainly isn't like that. So tell me: which disanalogies are fatal to using the image as an argument for long-run optimism, and which are just pedantic? I want you to seperate the ones that do damage from the ones that don't.

3. What the metaphor quietly smuggles in. My suspicion is it imports a guarantee (the bending happens no matter what we do) and a vantage point (we get to draw the arc through whichever historical points survived and call that justice), while hiding the agency and the selection effects. Audit that suspicion -- is it fair, and is there anything else being smuggled that I've missed?

4. The verdict. Once the disanalogies are on the table, does the closing claim -- despair about the long run is irrational -- survive in any form? If only a deflated version survives (something like: the arc bends only where people push, and "long" cuts both ways because it also means justice can be delayed past any of our lifetimes), state that deflated version precisely and tell me whether it still supports the hopeful ending or actually undercuts it.

5. If the arc metaphor can't carry the load, propose one better analogy for whatever defensible claim remains, and say briefly why it maps more cleanly.

Keep it concrete -- I'd rather have two or three well-chosen historical touchpoints used carefully than a survey of every candidate case of progress and regress. Aim for something I could actually use to write the fact-check memo.
