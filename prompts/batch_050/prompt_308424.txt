You are a philosophy professor specializing in epistemic logic and paradoxes involving knowledge and announcement. Please think carefully, and begin by briefly brainstorming candidate rules before committing to an answer.

I am a high school student, and our logic teacher referees disputes in our puzzle club about "surprise announcement" cases. His past rulings:

1. The classic five-day exam: a teacher announces there will be exactly one exam next week (Monday through Friday) and that students will not know which day it is on the evening before. Ruling: the students win — the announcement defeats itself by backward induction.

2. The two-day version (exam on Thursday or Friday, same surprise condition): same ruling — self-defeating.

3. The whole-semester version: the teacher announces there will be one surprise exam sometime this semester, with no deadline stated. Ruling: no paradox — with no last possible day, the induction has nowhere to start, and the teacher can simply be right.

4. The coin-flip version: the teacher announces that each morning she will flip a fair coin to decide whether the exam happens that day, repeating until it does. Ruling: no paradox — there is nothing to deduce, only probabilities to update.

Now the club is disputing a new case: the one-day announcement. On Wednesday afternoon the teacher says: there will be an exam tomorrow, and you do not know that right now. Half the club says this is obviously fine — an ordinary surprise, since no induction can even begin. The other half says it is just as self-defeating as the five-day case: if you believe the first half of the announcement, the second half becomes false, so the announcement cannot be both true and believed.

Which side should our teacher take, given his track record? Please (i) state the two most plausible rules consistent with all four of his earlier rulings, (ii) say what each rule predicts about the one-day case and why, (iii) give your best guess as to which rule he is actually applying, with reasons, and (iv) describe one further variant case that would best test whether your guess is right.
