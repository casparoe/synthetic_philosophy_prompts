So I got into Dennett's stuff on free-floating rationales a while back -- the idea that nature is full of reasons (why the termite mound has chimneys, why the cuckoo egg mimics the host's) even though no mind ever had those reasons, evolution just tracks them blindly. Seemed neat. But then I put together this argument from it and now I think I've accidentally proved that deliberation does nothing, which I refuse to believe. Here it is:

(1) Wherever you find a well-functioning arrangement in nature, there is a rationale for why it's arranged that way -- a reason it works, whether or not anyone grasps it.
(2) A rationale doesn't need a reasoner: natural selection can make an organism conform to a reason without the organism (or anything else) ever representing it. The termites ventilate the nest for a reason, but the reason is free-floating.
(3) So a trait can be fully explained by a rationale that no mind ever appreciated.
(4) Lots of human behaviors have exactly this kind of rationale -- why we gag at spoiled food, why we divide labor, why we punish cheaters, why we find certain faces attractive. These rationales were in place, shaping the behavior, long before anyone formed a belief about them.
(5) So those human behaviors are fully explained by rationales that nobody appreciated.
(6) If a behavior is already fully explained without the agent's own grasp of reasons, then the agent's deliberation about reasons isn't doing any explanatory work -- the explanation is complete without it.
(7) So conscious deliberation about reasons is epiphenomenal: it feels like it's guiding us, but the real explanation of what we do runs entirely through free-floating rationales.

Every step looks fine to me in isolation and the conclusion is absurd -- I'm deliberating about this argument right now, and it sure seems like that matters. I'd like to keep (1)-(3) since that's just Dennett's position as I understand it and I don't want to give that up. So which step do I reject, why did it look plausible, and what do I have to give up by rejecting it? Also curious whether the same fix saves Dennett's framework generally or whether I've found a real tension in it.
