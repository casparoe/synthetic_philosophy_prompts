You are a philosopher specializing in aesthetics and the ethics of humour. Think carefully about both what makes something funny and what makes it permissible to joke about. Begin by briefly brainstorming the main candidate explanations before settling on your analysis.

Consider a common norm in professional comedy: performers are encouraged to “punch up, not down.” A television writers’ room may accordingly approve a joke ridiculing a powerful politician’s vanity or hypocrisy, while rejecting a structurally similar joke ridiculing an unemployed person’s poverty, an employee’s disability, or a subordinate’s appearance. The writers need not deny that the rejected joke contains an incongruity, releases social tension, or would make some audience members laugh. Their objection is that its force is directed at someone with less power or at a vulnerability the target cannot easily change.

What theory or theories of humour does this practice presuppose? In your answer:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably and precisely as possible. Does “punch up, not down” treat humour as an expression of superiority, as a violation that must be appraised as benign, as the release of socially accumulated tension, as incongruity, or as some combination of these?
2. Distinguish a theory of why something is funny from a theory of when joking is morally permissible. Can the practice consistently accept incongruity or relief as explanations of laughter while using superiority or benign-violation considerations to regulate which jokes should be told?
3. Explain why the direction of the joke matters under each candidate theory. In particular, consider whether mocking the powerful weakens a superiority response, makes a violation easier to regard as benign, or merely reduces the likelihood of harm without changing the humour itself.
4. Identify what the practice does not settle. Is it compatible with several rival accounts of humour? Could it rest on institutional risk management or norms of civility rather than on any substantive theory of humour?
5. Test your reconstruction with a difficult case: a joke directed “upward” at a tyrant that nevertheless relies on the tyrant’s ethnicity, disability, or another trait shared by powerless people. Would the practice have to condemn the joke, permit it, or distinguish between its target and its mechanism?

Conclude by stating which theory comes closest to the practice’s implicit commitments, where the practice remains philosophically underdetermined, and what writers would have to change if they adopted a pure incongruity, relief, superiority, or benign-violation account.
