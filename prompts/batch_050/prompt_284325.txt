I am a retired biochemist who has spent the last two years reading philosophy of mind, and I keep running into an argumentative pattern that I recognize as powerful but cannot find properly named. Let me describe it through three instances, all from my own reading and conversation rather than from any single text.

First instance: a colleague explains a skilled tennis player's shot selection by saying she must be running through the tactical rules in her head before each stroke -- considering the opponent's position, weighing the options, choosing. But considering, weighing, and choosing are themselves skilled performances, which on the same logic would require a further inner consultation, and so on without end. Second instance: a book on cooking tells me the expert chef's competence consists in having internalized a body of propositions about flavor and heat. Yet applying a proposition to a particular pan at a particular moment is exactly the sort of thing the competence was supposed to explain. Third instance: I caught myself explaining my own facility at the laboratory bench -- the feel for when a reaction has gone wrong -- as a kind of silent recitation of laboratory lore, and immediately saw that the recitation, if it happened, would need its own skilled performer.

The pattern, as I see it, is this: an explanation of a capacity posits an inner operation, but that operation turns out to be an exercise of the very capacity under explanation, so the explanation either regresses or quietly assumes what it claimed to account for. I know this territory borders on Gilbert Ryle's The Concept of Mind -- his attack on what he derided as the ghost in the machine, his distinction between knowing how and knowing that, and his famous case of the visitor who tours the colleges and then asks to be shown the university. But I take the university case to illustrate a different diagnosis (misclassifying something as one more member of a series it is not a member of), and I am not convinced the regress I have described is the same mistake in new clothes.

So my questions. Does this specific argumentative pattern have an established name of its own, and if so, who is credited with its canonical statement? If it has no settled name distinct from Ryle's broader campaign, say so plainly and propose one, with a definition precise enough that I could sort fresh cases with it. Then, the harder part: is the regress vicious in every instance, or only some? Novices learning to drive genuinely do recite rules to themselves, so the rule-consultation story is not always empty; and a digital computer really does execute stored instructions, which suggests either that the regress is avoided there or that machines cannot truly know how to do anything. Finally, does the pattern, properly stated, refute all propositional accounts of skill, or only an inflationary subset of them?

I would rather you commit than survey. Tell me which identification you judge correct -- whether my pattern is a category mistake, a regress sui generis, an instance of some broader fallacy of explanation, or none of these -- defend the choice against the strongest alternative, and state where you think the pattern's edge lies: the class of explanations it genuinely threatens and the class it merely appears to threaten. If part of my description muddles two distinct phenomena, say which and pull them apart.
