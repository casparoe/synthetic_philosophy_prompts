Overheard this argument at a department happy hour last week and it's still bugging me. Background: those studies showing that countries scoring highest on gender-equality indexes tend to have *more* gender-segregated career choices -- e.g. fewer women going into STEM in the most egalitarian countries than in less equal ones.

Mark's opening move was that this basically settles it: when you remove barriers and let people choose freely, men's and women's average preferences diverge, so the gap is preference expressing itself, not injustice. Fixing equality of outcome isn't just impossible, it's not even a coherent goal once opportunity is equal.

Ana said that reading is upside down. In wealthy equal countries, choosing a career becomes about self-expression rather than survival, and people express themselves *through* gender -- so these societies don't have less gendered culture, they have a culture where gender is how you signal who you are, plus safety nets generous enough that you can afford to follow a gendered preference instead of chasing the highest paycheck. On her reading the paradox shows culture doing *more* work where formal equality is strongest.

Mark came back that this makes "culture" unfalsifiable: if unequal countries show gaps, that's culture; if equal countries show bigger gaps, that's also culture. A theory that predicts every outcome predicts nothing, whereas his story at least has a clean mechanism -- free choice reveals underlying preferences.

Ana's reply: the equality indexes measure stuff like pay gaps, political representation, legal rights -- not whether people *believe* men and women are naturally suited to different things. A country can score great on institutions while its everyday ideas about gender stay sticky, so the indexes simply don't measure the variable Mark needs. And anyway his "underlying preferences" weren't handed down from the sky; they were formed somewhere, so treating them as the bedrock explanation just relocates the question.

Then they both looked at me and I definetely didn't want to get involved. But now I'm curious: go through it move by move -- who's right at each step, what each of them is missing, and who's more right overall?
