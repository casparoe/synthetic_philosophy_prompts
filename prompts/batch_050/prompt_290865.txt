I've been stuck in a months-long argument with a coworker about AI governance, and I want either ammunition or a referee. His go-to move anytime someone mentions catastrophic risk from AI is to reach for the printing press: every transformative information technology in history was fought by incumbents and doomers, caused real chaos for a while (he'll cheerfully concede the Reformation and a century of religious war), and still left humanity enormously better off — so licensing regimes, training-run restrictions, and containment talk are just the modern censor's office, and history will laugh at them. Someone else in our group chat fires back that AI is obviously the new nuclear weapon: a strategic dual-use technology where the only sane regime is treaties, verification, and keeping capability from proliferating, and anyone treating it like a consumer gadget is historically illiterate. Both of them talk as if picking the right analogy basically settles the policy question, and I suspect neither has actually checked what thier analogy commits them to.

So: audit both. Give me the correspondence tables first — in the press analogy, what plays the role of Gutenberg, the scribal guilds, the pamphlets, heresy, the century-long lag before anything stabilized? In the nuke analogy, what plays the role of fissile material, enrichment facilities, test-ban verification, deterrence, the nonproliferation regime? Then the disanalogies that actually matter for the conclusions being drawn, not trivial ones like "computers aren't paper": e.g. could a single press print a better press, were the harms of the press mostly borne by people who chose to read the pamphlets, is enrichment loud and capital-intensive in a way training runs aren't, did the press diffuse slowly enough for institutions to adapt and does that hold here. Finally, once the breaks are on the table: does either policy conclusion — complacency from the press side, treaty-style containment from the nuke side — still stand on it's own feet, or was each analogy carrying the whole argument? And if both are bad, what's the least-bad historical analogy for transformative AI and why.

Format: exactly six numbered points, no introduction or wrap-up — points 1 and 2 the two correspondence mappings (mini-tables inside them are welcome), points 3 and 4 the load-bearing disanalogies for each analogy, point 5 the verdict on whether either policy conclusion survives, point 6 your best replacement analogy. A couple sentences per point is fine.
