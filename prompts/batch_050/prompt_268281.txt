Before I read any further attempts to reconcile Scanlonian contractualism with the conviction that the number of people affected must matter morally, I want a specification of what such a reconciliation would have to achieve. Write the desiderata for a theory of interpersonal aggregation that remains genuinely contractualist: the cases it must deliver the intuitively correct verdict on (including the one-vs-many case with equal claims, the case where many weak claims face one strong claim, and the case where no claim on the many-sided matches the strongest claim on the other side); the distinctions it cannot collapse, such as the strength of individual claims versus the number of claimants, and what an agent may be denied ex ante versus what may be done to her ex post; and the structural puzzles it must dissolve, above all how any counting at all can be squared with the requirement that principles be justifiable to each person taken separately, without either collapsing into unmediated utilitarian summing or into a strict pairwise rule that absurdly refuses to save the greater number. Include one test case of my own: a society that repeatedly chooses to spare each citizen minor harms in series rather than prevent one death, so that over a lifetime everyone is harmed often yet nobody dies — say whether the theory must aggregate over time within lives as well as across persons, and why. With each desideratum, give the case that motivates it, and flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Then, briefly, score the main contenders — Scanlon's own tie-breaking approach, weighted lotteries, and ex ante contractualism — against the list, keeping the weight on the criteria rather than the scoring.
