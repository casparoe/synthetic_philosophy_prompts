A close friend of mine is an emergency physician, and over years of conversation she has settled on firm verdicts about rescue cases. Her record so far: (1) Five strangers on one sandbar, one stranger on another, identical injuries, identical rescue costs, and she can reach only one side — she says you must save the five, and that flipping a coin would be wrong. (2) Same setup but one stranger on each side — she says you must flip a fair coin, and that simply saving whichever one you noticed first or find more sympathetic would be objectionable. (3) A surgeon may not kill one healthy patient to redistribute organs to five who will otherwise die, even though the arithmetic matches the sandbar case. (4) Choosing between saving her own daughter and saving two strangers, she says it is permissible — though not required — to save her daughter. (5) Choosing between saving her own daughter and saving forty strangers, she says you must save the forty. (6) Choosing between preventing one stranger's death and preventing five strangers from each permanently losing a hand, she says save the one from death; the five lesser harms do not add up to outweigh it.

Last week she was posed a new case she hasn't answered yet: her husband on one sandbar, two strangers on the other, everything else as in case (1). Predict her verdict. I am less interested in the prediction itself than in the reasoning: state the rule you take her to be running, given all six verdicts; identify at least two rival rules that fit her record equally well but diverge on the husband case, and say what each predicts; explain which of her verdicts do the most work in ruling out simple aggregation, and which verdict is doing the most work in your own prediction. Then name the single further case — a seventh verdict you could elicit from her — that would most efficiently distinguish the surviving rules from one another.

Lead with your predicted verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the setup. Close by telling me what would change your mind: which additional piece of her track record, or which consideration about how the candidate rules should be individuated, would most plausibly overturn your prediction, and where in your reasoning you think an error is most likely to be hiding.
