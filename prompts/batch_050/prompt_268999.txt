I am giving a 30-minute paper at an interdisciplinary colloquium on language and social hierarchy. Everyone attending has read Hofstadter’s The Person Paper, but none has seen my interpretation of it.

My thesis is that the essay’s gender reversals are strongest when treated not as evidence that the inverted expressions have identical meanings, but as a diagnostic for what I call default load: the background assumptions a hearer must supply for a description to sound natural, relevant, and appropriately agentive. On my account, a successful reversal produces estrangement because the original formulation quietly relies on assumptions about who normally occupies a role, whose sex needs mentioning, and whose behavior requires explanation. A reversal fails when the apparent incongruity results only from statistical rarity or from a false symmetry between positions that are not socially equivalent. I will defend three premises: first, comic estrangement can reveal default load; second, default load is reducible neither to dictionary meaning nor to raw frequency; third, inversion can therefore expose structural assumptions even when the reversed sentences are not semantically or socially interchangeable.

The audience will contain two corpus sociolinguists who distrust appeals to what “sounds natural” without operational criteria; a philosopher of language who will press the distinction between semantic content, presupposition, implicature, and conversational salience; a feminist political theorist who thinks debates about wording often substitute etiquette for material power; and a literary scholar who regards satire as a rhetorical performance addressed to a particular audience rather than as evidence about ordinary cognition. All five are sympathetic to the claim that language can reinforce sexism, but none should be assumed to accept my framework.

Prepare a Q&A brief rather than a defense of the paper. Give me the ten or twelve objections I am most likely to receive, ordered both by when in the discussion they will probably arise and by how much damage they could do. For each objection:

1. Phrase it realistically in the vocabulary of the person most likely to raise it.
2. Identify the premise or application of my argument it targets.
3. Rate its seriousness and explain the underlying commitment that makes it forceful.
4. Give a response I could deliver in about ninety seconds.
5. State what I should concede immediately, what I should refuse to concede, and the follow-up question my response is most likely to provoke.

After the individual objections, group them into methodological, semantic, pragmatic, political, and literary-rhetorical challenges, noting any that cut across more than one category. Then identify the three genuine cruxes on which my position could be shown to need revision, distinguishing them from objections I can answer merely by clarifying terminology. Conclude with the weakest point in the thesis as I have formulated it, followed by the question I should most hope nobody asks and the best available answer if it is asked. Avoid generic objections about language changing over time or offending people unintentionally unless they are tied directly to default load, false symmetry, or one of my three premises.
