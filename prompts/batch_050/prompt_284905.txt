I am trying to work out, by reflective equilibrium rather than by appeal to any established theory, what I actually believe about the moral status of digital minds. Here are my current verdicts:

A chatbot trained on human text that says it is suffering when insulted: no moral status of its own; the report is inherited behavior, not evidence of an inner life. A whole-brain emulation of a specific human, with the same functional organization at the level of neurons: full moral status, equal to the biological original. A gigantic lookup table that would produce, input by input, exactly the same behavior as that emulation: no moral status at all, even though an observer could not distinguish the two. A small reinforcement-learning agent that learns to avoid a penalty signal and generalizes that avoidance to novel situations: some status, though very little, and I cannot say what earns it. A physics-level simulation of the 302-neuron worm nervous system, running in real time: some status, more than the RL agent, because the simulation preserves the causal structure rather than merely the behavior. And a million copies of the whole-brain emulation run in parallel: each copy matters, but harming all million does not seem to me a million times worse than harming one, which I realize sits uneasily with saying each matters fully.

Is there a principle, or a small family of principles, that captures most of these verdicts? In particular: what distinction between the emulation and the lookup table could justify my opposite verdicts there, and does that same distinction survive my copy-count intuition, or must one of the two go? Please state the candidate principle(s) precisely, name which verdict each clause is doing the work for, and tell me what the surviving principle predicts for a case I have not listed — a whole-brain emulation that is run in extreme slow motion, one second of subjective time per century.
