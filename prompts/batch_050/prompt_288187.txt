I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying political philosophy, and I want to think about judicial review in the way one might compare imperfect measuring instruments: not by asking which institution is infallible, but by examining the kinds of errors each makes, how often it makes them, and whether it magnifies or corrects the errors of other institutions. Imagine a constitutional democracy in which both legislators and judges are competent on average, but each is susceptible to political pressure, limited information, ideological bias, and occasional capture by powerful interests.

Rank the following six arrangements from best to worst for governing that society over the next fifty years:

1. No judicial review: constitutional limits are interpreted exclusively by the elected legislature.
2. Strong-form review: courts may invalidate legislation, and their constitutional rulings are final unless the constitution is amended.
3. Review with legislative override: courts may invalidate legislation, but two successive legislatures may reinstate it by a three-fifths vote after an intervening election.
4. Weak-form review: courts may issue binding interpretations of rights, but the legislature may openly depart from them by stating its reasons and passing the law again after one year.
5. Preventive review: an independent court evaluates major legislation before enactment, but only upon request by one-third of legislators, and may require revision without imposing a final constitutional interpretation.
6. Domain-limited review: courts possess final authority only over laws restricting political participation, equal citizenship, religious practice, and criminal punishment; all other constitutional questions remain with the legislature.

For each arrangement, give scores from 0 to 10 on four dimensions: protection against serious rights violations, avoidance of unjustified judicial obstruction of legitimate democratic legislation, resilience during periods of authoritarian pressure, and the quality of the incentives it creates for legislators and judges to take constitutional reasons seriously. Then provide an overall ranking and explain how much it depends on the relative moral costs assigned to false negatives—instances where unconstitutional oppression survives—and false positives—instances where legitimate democratic decisions are wrongly invalidated.

Please take seriously both the democratic objection to rule by unelected judges and the possibility that electoral majorities are not always reliable judges of their own constitutional authority. In particular, discuss whether judicial review is best understood as majority restraint, as a safeguard of the conditions that make majority rule legitimate, or as one fallible epistemic institution checking another. If your ranking changes under a crisis scenario in which the governing party controls most media and can influence judicial appointments, say so and explain the change.
