I teach an undergraduate ethics course, and every year we hit the same wall in the population ethics unit: half the class decides total utilitarianism is obviously correct the moment they see average utilitarianism's counterexamples, and the other half treats the repugnant conclusion as a conversation-ending refutation, and the two sides talk past each other for ninety minutes. I've decided that what the unit needs is not another reading but a piece of satire I can distribute at the start of the seminar, so that students have to reverse-engineer which premise they object to rather than just registering recoil.

Please write a deadpan press release, roughly 600 words, from a fictional national government's newly established Ministry for Total Welfare, announcing its inaugural Five-Year Plan. The Ministry is staffed entirely by sincere and technically competent total utilitarians, and the Plan is simply their theory applied to domestic policy with bureaucratic cheerfulness: a phased program of subsidised "mere addition" of lives barely worth living, a revised cost-effectiveness methodology that reallocates healthcare spending away from improving already-excellent lives toward creating additional mediocre ones, and a long-range demographic target the Ministry is strangely proud of but presents only in a graph with an unlabelled y-axis. The humor must come from getting the underlying theory right, not from making the bureaucrats stupid. The Plan should exploit genuine features of the view — the logic of mere addition, the difference between making people happy and making happy people, the non-identity problem deployed to dismiss complaints that the *particular* people brought into existence might object — and at least one spokesperson quote should address, with a technically flawless and quietly horrifying answer, the objection that the same reasoning endorses a world of many tortured people provided it also contains enough ecstatic ones.

Include, at the end, a short "Questions from the Press" section of four or five exchanges in which journalists raise the standard objections — the repugnant conclusion, the sadistic conclusion, whether a critical level of welfare below zero would change anything, whether existing people count for more than possible ones — and the Ministry answers each one correctly on its own terms, conceding premises without ever conceding defeat. One rule: the press release may never use the phrases "repugnant conclusion" or name any philosopher; the reader should have to diagnose the view from its policy fruits. The tone should stay flat and administrative throughout, closer to a genuine budget announcement than to a comedy sketch, and a specialist in population axiology should be able to finish it thinking the satire was, if unkind, fundamentally fair.
