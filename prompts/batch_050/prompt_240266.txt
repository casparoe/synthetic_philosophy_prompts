I'm a high school student on my school's debate team, and I keep running into the same problem when I prep for a round: I gather several arguments or pieces of evidence for a claim, feel more confident with each new one, and then my coach tells me that two of them "boil down to the same thing," so I've been double-counting. From my reading I understand that people who weigh multiple lines of reasoning often tacitly presume the lines are independent of one another, and that a lot of overconfidence comes from that presumption failing. Write me a procedure I can actually run in the twenty minutes or so I have while preparing: an ordered sequence of tests to apply to any set of arguments for the same conclusion that tells me which ones are genuinely independent support and which are the same consideration wearing different clothes. It should cover at least the cases of arguments that share a source, arguments that share a hidden premise, and arguments that would all fail together for the same reason. It should also tell me what to do with my overall confidence when independence fails in some particular case -- whether to discard the weaker argument, keep it at reduced weight, or something else -- rather than just flagging the problem. Include two worked examples: one where arguments that look independent turn out not to be, and one where arguments that look redundant turn out to be checking different things. And please be explicit about which steps of the procedure are real tests and which steps are judgment calls that no checklist can eliminate, so I know where I'm on my own.
