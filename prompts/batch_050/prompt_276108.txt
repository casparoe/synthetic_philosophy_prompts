I'm the lead engineer on a flood early-warning system for a region with almost no relevant data: two recorded major floods in eighty years, none since the river was re-channeled. Our model outputs a seasonal probability of catastrophic flooding, and the city plans to size its barrier budget by multiplying that probability against estimated damages. Two senior colleagues disagree about how to treat the output. One says a probability is a probability: whatever its basis, acting on the expected value is the only rational procedure, and second-guessing the number behind the number is incoherent. The other says that with evidence this thin the figure is essentially invented, and we should refuse to report one at all. Both positions feel wrong to me. The first seems to treat a 5% estimate resting on two events as interchangeable with a 5% resting on a century of dense records; the second seems to abandon reasoning we plainly can do. Is there a coherent sense in which the amount and quality of evidence behind a probability is itself decision-relevant, over and above the probability's value? If so, what does that imply concretely -- for what we report to the city, and for how a decision-maker should behave differently in the thin-evidence case than in the rich-evidence one?
