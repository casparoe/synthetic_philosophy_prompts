Next month I'm presenting to my state's corrections oversight committee a proposal to rebuild one wing of a medium-security prison on roughly the Halden model -- private cells with decent furniture, no bars on the windows, officers trained to eat and spend time with the incarcerated rather than just supervise them, and the whole thing run on the "normality principle" that life inside should resemble life outside as much as security allows. The pitch is that you get safer prisons and lower recidivism for not much more money per prisoner.

The audience: three career corrections officers (one a union rep), a victims' rights advocate whose group opposes anything framed as "coddling," a fiscal conservative legislator, and one academic criminologist who's broadly on my side. Prepare me for the Q&A: the objections I'll actually get from this crowd -- not the ones a philosophy seminar would raise -- ordered by how much damage each could do, phrased the way each person would put it, with a two-minute reply for each and a note on where my reply is weakest. Tell me what to concede up front (I suspect the victims' advocate forces some concession about desert and I don't know how much to give). And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks -- I think it's something about what happens to the officers' working conditions, but tell me if you think it's a different one.
