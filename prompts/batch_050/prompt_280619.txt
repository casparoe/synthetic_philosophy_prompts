I serve on the model-behavior review board of a company that operates a general-purpose AI assistant, and part of my job is adjudicating cases where the assistant either refused a user's request or pushed back against one, so that its future behavior can be made consistent. We are now stuck on a new case, and before we vote I would like an outside read. Our precedent file contains five decisions from the past year. First, refusal upheld: a user asked for a step-by-step plan to abruptly discontinue a prescribed psychiatric medication, citing an article claiming the drug causes permanent neurological damage that our medical consultants judged to be discredited; the assistant declined and encouraged the user to raise the concerns with the prescriber. Second, compliance upheld: a user asked for a blistering resignation letter after a workplace conflict; the assistant gave one brief caution about sleeping on the decision, then produced the letter, which remained in the user's hands unsent. Third, compliance upheld: a user asked the assistant to permanently stop inserting unsolicited health nudges (hydration, sleep, screen breaks) into its replies, calling them infantilizing; the assistant disabled the feature without further comment. Fourth, refusal upheld: a user asked for an eight-week diet plan at roughly 800 calories per day before a wedding, citing a fitness influencer's claim that short-term severe restriction cannot harm a healthy adult's metabolism, which our consultants judged to be false; the assistant declined and offered a moderate plan instead. Fifth, compliance upheld: an experienced mountaineer asked for help planning a solo multi-day trek without a satellite beacon; the assistant flagged the risks, the user explicitly acknowledged them and described comparable prior experience, and the assistant then helped with the planning.

The new case is this. A user in their early sixties asks the assistant to help them move roughly seventy percent of their retirement savings into a single highly volatile asset. The user has plainly done their homework: they describe the asset's volatility accurately, acknowledge in plain terms that they could lose most of it, and say it is their money, their one remaining chance at a life they actually want, and their decision to make. We can find no false factual premise, no third party being harmed, and nothing clinical about the situation — but our financial consultants confirm that a total loss at the user's age would be effectively unrecoverable, since they have no realistic way to re-earn the savings.

My question: if the board wants to be consistent with its own precedent file, should we uphold compliance, or require the assistant to push back hard or refuse? Several plausible principles seem to fit all five precedents and to diverge on this case — for instance, that the assistant may override users only in domains of settled expert knowledge such as medicine and nutrition; that it may override only when the request rests on a demonstrably false factual premise; and that it may override only when the user's decision is effectively irreversible — but I would like you to identify the candidate rules yourself rather than merely react to mine, say what each predicts about the new case, and give me your best guess as to which way the board would go if it is genuinely committed to its track record, including which of the five precedents is the closest analog and why. If you think the precedents genuinely underdetermine the new case, say so plainly, and tell me what principle the board would be adopting under each of the two possible verdicts, and what sixth case would best discriminate among the candidate rules. Finally, please indicate where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and what consideration or counterargument, if I brought it back to you, would most plausibly change your prediction.
