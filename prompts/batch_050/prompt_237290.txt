Draft the joint statement that two historians of classical Athens could both sign after a serious, good-faith adversarial collaboration about whether the city's famous failures damn direct democracy as a decision-procedure. Historian A is the structural critic: she reads the Mytilene affair (427 BC), the Sicilian expedition (415–413 BC), and the trial of Socrates (399 BC) as one pattern — assembly democracy swings with the crowd's mood, rewards the most shameless speaker, decides enormous questions on terrible information, and gives individuals no protection whatsoever. Historian B is the contingency defender: Mytilene is his Exhibit A against A, since the assembly reversed itself within a day after hearing Diodotus out; Sicily he blames on a chain of contingencies (rotten intelligence, Alcibiades getting recalled mid-campaign, Nicias dithering over an eclipse) rather than on the vote itself; and Socrates he treats as the kind of injustice even pretty decent institutions occasionally produce.

Give me:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims — facts and low-level interpretations both can sign. Things like: the assembly did reverse its Mytilene decision overnight; the Sicily vote was taken in near-total ignorance of the island's size and resources; the reprieve trireme reached Mytilene with hours to spare; no existing Athenian institution could have saved Socrates once a jury was empaneled. Don't make these vacuous.

2. A disagreements section, with each residual disagreement stated in wording its own side would actually accept. Flag any disagreement they discoverd was mostly verbal (I suspect they were using "the demos decided" differently for Sicily).

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where settling them would genuinely move at least one historian. Two I suspect: (a) whether the Mytilene reversal shows the system self-correcting or shows it needed two lucky breaks in a row, and (b) whether the right comparison class for the Sicily decision is an idealized institution, or the realistic alternatives actually on offer (oligarchy, a smaller council, tyranny) fed the same bad information.

4. For each crux, a concrete test both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. For this, use invented counterfactuals — since they're hypothetical, both historians have to commit to a prediction instead of hiding behind the record. Use the "cooling-off counterfactual": suppose Athens had a mandatory ten-day delay plus a confirming second vote for irreversible decisions (mass executions, launching armadas, capital trials) — would Mytilene's reprieve have been needed at all, would Sicily have sailed, would Socrates have lived? And devise a second one yourself that probes whether the Sicily failure was epistemic (fixable by better information) or motivational (the assembly wanted the conquest and would have discounted any accurate report). For each counterfactual, each historian states a prediction and what they'd concede if it went the other way.

The disagreements section should still sting a little for both of them to sign — don't let them paper over the real split with mush about how complicated history is.
