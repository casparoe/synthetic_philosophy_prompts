So I sit on the grants committee of a small EA-adjacent foundation (we move maybe $2M a year, mostly global priorities stuff) and we've gotten a proposal that's split us right down the middle. A team of four researchers wants about $1.2M over three years to run a cliodynamics forecasting program: they'd extend one of those big coded historical databases (Seshat-style, hundreds of past societies coded for things like elite overproduction, state fiscal health, popular immiseration), fit structural-demographic models in the spirit of Turchin's secular cycles work, and then -- the actual deliverable -- preregister decade-scale forecasts of political instability for like 30 contemporary countries, with proper scoring against baselines.

The pro faction on the committee points out that Turchin basically called rising US instability years ahead of time using elite overproduction measures, and says this is the closest macrohistory has ever come to being an actual science, and even a modest hit rate would be insanely valuable for prioritization. The con faction says this is astrology with regressions: the n of historical societies is tiny, the coding is judgment-laden, the models get tuned on the same data they "predict," the cycle timescales are so long that the theory can absorb basically any outcome, and one famous correct call proves nothing (someone always calls the crash). One member keeps saying "even a stopped clock" and another keeps saying "this is how heliocentrism probably sounded," which tells you how productive our meetings have been.

Please draft the committee memo. It needs to come down somewhere concrete -- fund in full, decline, or fund with conditions -- and I'm fine with any of the three as long as the reasoning holds up. A few things the memo has to do, since these are where the committee actually disagrees rather than just vibes:

- Say what would actually count as the project succeeding or failing at the end of year three, in advance, in a way that doesn't let the PIs move the goalposts (e.g., what scores the forecasts get judged against -- and please grapple with what the right baselines even are: superforecaster-style expert judgment, dumb demographic extrapolation, markets where they exist).
- Take seriously the distinction between explanation and prediction. The con faction concedes that secular-cycle models might be good retrodictive sense-making while denying that licenses forward forecasts; if you think that's a fake distinction, argue it.
- Address the "database is the real value" argument -- i.e., fund it as infrastructure even if the forecasting is hopeless -- and whether that's a good reason or scope creep.
- Engage the strongest point on whichever side loses, seriously, not as a strawman. If you decline, steelman the "we'd regret not having tried when the science was nascent" case; if you fund, steelman the small-n/overfitting critique rather than waving at Turchin's track record.

Keep it under 800 words, memo format, something I could actually circulate before Thursday.
