Many hospitals will remove vital organs for transplant only from donors who are already dead — a policy I gather is called the dead-donor rule. What puzzles me is that death gets declared in two quite different ways under this one policy. Some donors are pronounced dead because their entire brain has permanently stopped functioning, even though machines keep the heart beating and the body warm and pink. Others are pronounced dead just a few minutes after their heart stops — a wait short enough that circulation could sometimes still be restored, and the death counts as final largely because the medical team has agreed, per the patient's wishes, not to attempt it. So a warm body with a beating heart is dead in the one case, and a body whose heart might still be restarted is dead in the other, and in both cases the timing lines up conveniently well with when the organs need to come out.

What conception of death does this practice actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory — and say whether it is one coherent theory or two different ones stapled together for practical purposes. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit: could several rival views of death license the same protocol? Is the apparent inconsistency between the two standards real or only apparent? Finally, tell me what would have to change in transplant practice if we instead defined death as the irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness alone, or if we kept a strict biological definition of death and simply abandoned the requirement that donors be dead before their organs are taken. Please write your answer in American English.
