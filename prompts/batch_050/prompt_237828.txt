I'm taking a course on the philosophy of economics, and our seminar recently revisited the spring 2020 lockdown decisions, which most Western governments framed as a trade-off between lives saved from Covid-19 and the harms of restrictions: unemployment and impoverishment, surges in depression and loneliness, missed cancer screenings and other delayed care, and a lost year of schooling for children. Some economists tried to discipline the trade-off by computing quality-adjusted life-years on both sides, or by putting a value-of-statistical-life figure on Covid deaths and comparing it to GDP losses; others insisted that no such comparison could be legitimate, since it weighs one person's life against another person's livelihood or mental health, and there is no common scale on which those sit. What struck me is that this is exactly the controversy over interpersonal comparisons of utility that welfare economics has carried since Lionel Robbins argued in the 1930s that utility is purely ordinal, that comparing one person's satisfaction against another's is an unverifiable value judgment rather than science, and that economists should confine themselves to Pareto improvements. Set against that are Sen's rejoinder that partial comparability is both possible and routinely used, and Harsanyi's extended-sympathy construction, in which one imagines having an equal chance of being anyone affected.

Please analyse the 2020 lockdown episode through the lens of this debate, in both directions. On the mapping side: identify precisely where an interpersonal comparison of wellbeing enters the lockdown calculation, and test whether the popular rejoinder that lockdown harms also cost lives, so the choice was really lives against lives, actually escapes Robbins's objection or merely disguises it. Consider in particular whether aggregating life-years across many different persons is itself an interpersonal comparison even when everything stays within a single dimension. Then ask what a policymaker genuinely committed to ordinalism could have done: would restricting herself to Pareto improvements, or to Kaldor-Hicks cost-benefit tests with hypothetical compensation, have yielded any decision at all under the time pressure of March 2020, or does the episode show that such scruples are practically untenable? On the reverse direction: say what the episode does to the theory. Does the fact that governments did decide, and that some decisions now look better and others worse in retrospect, refute Robbins by showing that rough interpersonal comparisons are workable? Or does the moral heat, inconsistency, and reliance on crude heuristics that characterised the debate vindicate him, showing that the comparisons being made were disguised ethical verdicts wearing scientific dress?

I'd like the analysis to reach a verdict rather than end in even-handed suspension: tell me whether you think the episode counts for Robbins or for his critics, and what it implies about how such emergencies should be handled.
