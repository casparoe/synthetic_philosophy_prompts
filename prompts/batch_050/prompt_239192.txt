Moore's paradox arises with assertions of the form "p, but I do not believe that p": such a sentence can perfectly well be true, yet sincerely asserting it seems to undermine itself. The standard diagnosis turns on the fact that assertion normally expresses belief, so the speaker repudiates in the second conjunct what the very act of asserting the first conjunct would ordinarily convey.

Now consider an AI writing assistant that composes first-person messages on behalf of a user, without itself holding beliefs about the subject matter — it predicts plausible continuations and produces what the user asked for, but nothing in it is committed to the truth of what it writes.

Give an example of a single sentence that would be straightforwardly Moore-paradoxical if a human asserted it about their own state of mind, but that loses its paradoxicality — or becomes paradoxical in a structurally different way — when generated by such an assistant and sent to a third party. Then analyze your example carefully: identify exactly where the absurdity resides in the human case (in the proposition expressed, in the act of assertion itself, or in the speaker's relation to the proposition), locate the corresponding trouble, if any, in the machine case, and explain what the contrast reveals about whether Moore-paradoxicality is fundamentally a logical matter, a doxastic one, or a pragmatic feature of assertion as a speech act. Finally, consider whether a recipient who cannot tell whether the message was written by a human or by the assistant is in a position to even detect the paradox, and what that implies about where Moore-paradoxicality "lives."
