I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but over the past year I've been reading Emmanuel Levinas on my own — mostly the idea that ethics begins not with rules, duties, or calculations, but with the encounter with the face of another person, which confronts me with a demand I never chose and can't argue my way out of. At the same time I reread Mary Shelley's Frankenstein, and I can't shake the feeling that the novel is quietly working through exactly this idea more than a century before Levinas — and possibly testing it harder than his commentators do.

Here is what I mean, and what I'd like you to help me think through. The creature is not mistreated because his inner life is hidden. Quite the opposite: he is extraordinarily articulate, he explains himself at length, and his face is fully visible — arguably too visible. Yet every sighted person who meets him responds with horror or violence. The one exception is the blind De Lacey father, who speaks with him kindly and without fear — until the moment the seeing members of the family return and drive him away. Shelley seems to have staged, inside one cottage, something close to a controlled experiment: same creature, same words, same face; the only variable is whether the people he's with can see.

Please read the novel through Levinas's account of the face, taking that scene as the hinge. Three things in particular I'd like the reading to address:

1. Levinas insists that the face in the ethical sense is not something seen — that what commands me in the other person exceeds their appearance, their features, anything I could catalog or compare. Does the De Lacey episode confirm this (vision blocks ethics, blindness permits it), or is that too tidy? Does the novel give us any sighted encounter that complicates the lesson? I'm thinking especially of Walton on the ice at the end, who recoils from the creature and yet is visibly stopped by his grief and his speech.

2. The creature's central demand is that Victor make him a companion — a second face that will not turn away from him. Is that a demand for recognition of his otherness, or a demand to stop being other? I keep going back and forth on whether Levinas's framework can even distinguish those two things, since his ethics seems to require that the other remain irreducibly other, while the creature wants nothing more than to be ordinary in someone's eyes.

3. Victor's failure: on a Levinasian reading, what exactly is Victor's transgression — animating the creature, refusing to make the companion, or something earlier and quieter, like never once asking the creature what he wants? The novel gives Victor long stretches of self-justification, and I'm curious whether Levinas has the resources to explain what a self-justification even is, ethically speaking, when it runs against a demand that supposedly precedes all argument.

Finally, I want the reading to be honest about where the novel pushes back on Levinas. His ethics can sound as though the face's command is simply given and irresistible, but Shelley seems far more interested in how reliably human beings override it — how fear, disgust, and the pressure of a watching community convert the command into permission for cruelty. If the command is that easily overridden, is Levinas describing an obligation or just describing a sentiment? I'd rather you say plainly where the fit breaks down than force the novel to serve as an illustration of the philosophy.
