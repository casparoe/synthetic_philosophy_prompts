In Groundhog Day, Phil Connors is trapped repeating a single day and passes through something like despair, nihilistic indulgence, and repeated suicide before settling into a life of local projects, skill acquisition, and care for others -- at which point the loop releases him. Read the film against Camus's absurdism and one or two contemporary accounts of meaning in life (Wolf's fitting fulfillment view would be a natural choice). My worry is this: the film looks superficially Sisyphean, but its structure may be deeply anti-absurdist, since the loop ends precisely when Phil becomes good -- the universe, it turns out, keeps score and rewards meaning achieved. Camus's Sisyphus gets no such exit. So: does the film actually dramatize the absurd, or does it smuggle in a cosmic guarantee that meaning will be recognized, which is exactly what absurdist thought denies us? And on the contemporary side, does Phil's final life satisfy Wolf's condition of subjective attraction to objectively worthwhile projects, or does the film cheat by making his projects worthwhile largely through their eventual payoff? I'd like a reading that is faithful in both directions -- neither reducing the film to an illustration nor distorting the philosophy -- and that says honestly where the film resists the reading. Please indicate how confident you are in your overall verdict, and single out the interpretive claim you are least sure of.
