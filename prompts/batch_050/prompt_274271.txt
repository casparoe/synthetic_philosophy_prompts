I teach an informal math-and-philosophy circle at a community college, maybe 14 people, mixed backgrounds (some have had a stats class, some haven't), and we meet for 75 minutes. I want to do a session on Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference, but I don't want to just tell them "different parametrizations give different answers" because that's boring and they'll nod and forget it.

Design an activity where they get burned by it themselves. Rough idea I have in mind: something like the classic random-chord setup (probability that a random chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle), where different groups are secretly assigned different procedures for "picking a chord at random" -- like picking a random midpoint inside the circle vs. picking two random points on the circumference vs. picking a random direction and a random distance from the center. Each group computes or estimates their probability with total confidence, maybe with some small stakes (winner gets to skip bringing snacks next time, or points toward some silly semester-long leaderboard), and then we reveal that they were all answering "the same question" and got different numbers. Then debrief.

Flesh this out properly: exact setup and materials, how to run the randomization with physical stuff or phone random number generators so it's actually doable in a classroom, how to keep groups from realizing the trick too early (or whether I should let them realize), the timeline for 75 minutes, and the debrief that connects what happened to the principle of indifference and why "random" isn't a complete specification. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong -- e.g. someone will say the answer is "obviously" one value, or groups will accuse each other of doing the math wrong rather than questioning the setup -- and how to handle it. And be honest about what the activity can't capture: I suspect the real lesson about when there IS a privileged parametrization (symmetry/invariance considerations) is hard to stage as a game, but tell me if there's a way to gesture at it.

One more thing: when you explain the background in the debrief section, be clear about what's basically settled (the math of the three classic solutions) versus what's still genuinely argued about among people who work on this (whether Bertrand-style problems have a principled unique answer via invariance, whether the principle of indifference can be saved in some restricted form, that kind of thing). Don't present the contested stuff as if everyone agrees on it.
