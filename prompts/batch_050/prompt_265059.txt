I am a university student currently enrolled in an upper-level seminar on epistemology and the philosophy of language, and I am preparing a study guide for our unit on norms of assertion. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher with expertise in this debate, and to produce one component of that study guide for me.

Please construct a decision tree that classifies the main positions on what norm governs assertion — that is, on what condition a speaker must satisfy for an assertion to be proper or permissible. The intended audience is advanced undergraduates, and the tree should have between eight and ten leaves. Before writing the tree itself, please spend a short paragraph brainstorming the logical space: what are the candidate norms, what kinds of cases distinguish them, and which combinations of answers are even available. Then present the tree, with each branch labeled by the diagnostic question that splits it.

I have two specific requirements that go beyond a standard overview of the literature.

First, the diagnostic questions must be phrased as concrete cases rather than as labels, so that a reader can sort themselves without already knowing the terminology. Please use at least these four cases somewhere in the tree: (a) a speaker who asserts a lottery proposition — that their ticket will lose — purely on the basis of the odds, without having seen the draw; (b) a speaker who sincerely asserts something they believe with excellent but misleading evidence, which turns out to be false; (c) a speaker whose belief is true and justified in the ordinary way but only luckily so, in the manner of a Gettier case; and (d) a speaker who passes on something they were told by a normally reliable source who this one time spoke carelessly.

Second, the tree must contain, as leaves, the familiar positions — a knowledge norm, a truth norm, a justification-or-warrant norm, and a mere-belief norm — but also two less standard ones: a stakes-sensitive norm, on which how much one must have in order to assert properly rises with what hangs on being right, and an audience-relative norm, on which the standard is set by the hearer's situation and informational needs rather than the speaker's. For each leaf, give the position's name (or "unnamed" if you believe no one in the literature defends it), one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and note which of the four cases above delivers a verdict the position would prefer to avoid.

Finally, please do two pieces of meta-commentary at the end. If there are combinations of answers the tree allows that correspond to no actual position, say whether the gap exists because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. And if any two positions on the tree cannot be separated by the diagnostic questions you used — I suspect the knowledge norm and the stakes-sensitive norm may come apart only under a particular reading of what knowledge requires — flag them explicitly rather than papering over the difference.

Please think carefully about the order of the branching questions so that the most fundamental disagreements come first, and double-check that every leaf is reachable by a consistent path of answers.
