So I'm prepping for a reading group on Buddhist ethics and I have to write down my "position" so people can attack it, but honestly I want to attack it myself first. Here's what I currently believe:

1. Buddhist ethics is, at bottom, a kind of consequentialism. I'm sympathetic to the reading (Goodman and others) where the whole point of the path is reducing suffering for all sentient beings, and the precepts, virtues etc. are justified instrumentally -- they're the rules and dispositions that happen to lead to the best outcomes.

2. The karmic quality of an action is fixed by the intention behind it, not the outcome. The suttas identify karma with cetanā, volition. So accidentally killing someone in a car crash and deliberately running them over are morally worlds apart, and a failed attempt at murder is as bad as a successful one.

3. Impartiality: suffering counts the same wherever it occurs. My suffering, my family's, a stranger's, an animal's -- the fact that it's *mine* gives it no extra weight.

4. The focus of my actual practice should be purifying my own mind. Mine is the only mindstream I can directly work on, my karma is the only karma I can shape, and getting tangled up in fixing other people before you're stable yourself is a classic beginner's mistake.

5. Anattā: there's no persisting self, what we call a person is just a causal stream of events. But I also take rebirth seriously -- after this body dies, some future being inherits the karmic momentum of this stream, and that being is "me" in whatever sense matters morally.

Which of these are genuinely in tension and which only look like it? Please rank any tensions by severity instead of just flagging every pair that sounds funny, and for each real one tell me what the cheapest fix would be and what I'd lose by making it. If one single revision cleans up most of the mess, tell me which. Also feel free to tell me if some pair I'm worried about (I'm not saying which) actually dissolves on closer inspection.
