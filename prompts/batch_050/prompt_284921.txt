I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, economics, or social choice theory, though I've picked up the informal statement of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem: with three or more alternatives, any deterministic voting rule that isn't a dictatorship and that allows every alternative to win is manipulable -- some voter can sometimes get a better result by lying about their preferences. I want to really understand what that means by seeing a concrete case built to order, not by reading a textbook example.

Construct a thought experiment in which a small engineering team uses an explicit, reasonable-looking voting rule to make a real technical decision, and one team member can profitably manipulate it. Constraints:

1. The setting must be mundane and realistic: a handful of engineers (four to six) choosing among at least three concrete technical options -- say, which database to migrate to, which of three architectures to adopt, or which deployment strategy to ship. No governments, no presidential elections, no abstract candidates A, B, C without a story.
2. The voting rule must be specified completely and mechanically, the way I'd specify it in code: exact input format (rankings), exact tie-breaking procedure, exact winner determination. It must be something a team could plausibly adopt in good faith -- Borda count, instant runoff, Copeland, or something comparably reasonable -- not a rule rigged to be manipulable.
3. The manipulation must be achievable by a single voter acting alone, with no coordination, and the manipulator's true preferences must be fully specified so I can verify they genuinely prefer the post-manipulation outcome.
4. The preference profile must not be contrived to the point of unreality: no voter's ranking should be something no actual engineer would hold.

After the scenario, demonstrate that it works: (a) give the full preference table and compute the honest outcome step by step; (b) give the manipulator's false ballot and compute the new outcome step by step; (c) confirm, using the manipulator's true ranking, that the new outcome is strictly better for them; (d) verify that your rule satisfies each premise of the theorem as I've stated it -- non-dictatorial, every alternative can win under some profile, at least three alternatives, deterministic -- so the theorem genuinely applies rather than being gestured at.

Then do the teardown. Tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to manipulability if that feature is removed -- in particular, if the technical options sit naturally on a one-dimensional spectrum (e.g., how much latency we're willing to tolerate, or how much complexity we'll accept) and every voter's preferences are single-peaked along it, would a median-based rule be strategy-proof, and why does that escape the theorem rather than refute it? Finally, steelman the defense a voting-rule optimist on my team would offer: that profitable manipulation requires knowing everyone else's ballot in advance, so the theorem is practically toothless for a small team with private ballots. Assess honestly whether that defense holds up.
