I'm a journalist writing a long-form piece on cooperation between enemies, and I want to use the "live-and-let-live" system of the First World War trenches as a case study in superrationality, in Douglas Hofstadter's sense: the idea that a rational agent in a perfectly symmetric situation can reason that the other side, reasoning identically, will reach the same conclusion, and can therefore cooperate in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma without communication, repetition, or enforcement. The material I have in mind is the standard picture: informal truces between opposing units, ritualized and deliberately inaccurate shelling at predictable hours, mutual restraint around mealtimes and latrines, and most dramatically the spontaneous Christmas truce of 1914.

Do the mapping carefully in both directions. First: identify the players, the options, and the stakes, and say exactly where the symmetry that superrational reasoning requires actually held between the two sides and where it broke down. Second: the conventional reading treats the system as an iterated prisoner's dilemma stabilized by reciprocity, and the historical texture seems to support that -- units were rotated precisely to disrupt established truces, and high commands ordered raids in part to reignite aggression. So does superrationality explain anything the iterated-game account leaves unexplained, or is it explanatorily idle here? And what does the episode suggest about the conditions, if any, under which superrational reasoning can actually get a grip among real agents?

Please commit to a verdict -- either that the trench case is genuine evidence that superrationality does explanatory work beyond reciprocity, or that it is a case the concept cannot illuminate -- and say what argument, evidence, or consideration would lead you to reverse that verdict.
