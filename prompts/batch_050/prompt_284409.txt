I’m putting together a two-page proposal for a six-week undergraduate independent study and want help making the idea precise enough for a funding comittee. The project starts from this passage in Plato’s Republic, Book VII, in Benjamin Jowett’s translation:

"At first, when any of them is liberated and compelled suddenly to stand up and turn his neck round and walk and look towards the light, he will suffer sharp pains; the glare will distress him, and he will be unable to see the realities of which in his former state he had seen the shadows; and then conceive some one saying to him, that what he saw before was an illusion, but that now, when he is approaching nearer to being and his eye is turned towards more real existence, he has a clearer vision, -what will be his reply? And you may further imagine that his instructor is pointing to the objects as they pass and requiring him to name them, -will he not be perplexed? Will he not fancy that the shadows which he formerly saw are truer than the objects which are now shown to him?"

My half-formed thought is that discussions of the cave usually treat the prisoner’s initial rejection of the outside world as confusion, weakness, habituation, or bad education. I want to explore a different possibility: an epistemic reliability trough, meaning a stage where someone’s judgment genuinely becomes less reliable because they have encountered a higher standard but have not yet learned how to use it. The prisoner sees the shadows more accurately than the objects, so at that moment his retreat may be evidentially reasonable even though staying in the cave would leave him worse off overall.

Turn that into a research proposal. The central question should be something like: Is the prisoner’s initial preference for the shadows epistemically rational, and can Plato’s theory of Forms explain why someone ought to persist through a reliability trough without simply assuming from the start that the Forms exist?

Please include:

- three competing interpretations of the passage, including at least one under which the prisoner’s response is rational;
- why the familiar “education is painful” reading does not by itself settle the issue;
- the method I’d actually use over six weeks, including close reading and, if useful, a simple philosophical model of evidence and reliability;
- what the possible conclusions are and what considerations would decide between them;
- what a successful final paper would establish, and what would count as the project failing;
- a realistic week-by-week plan.
