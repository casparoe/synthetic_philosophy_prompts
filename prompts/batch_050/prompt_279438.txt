But there's one nagging objection I can't shake and also can't quite articulate: something about how nobody actually *chose* these people, and I don't know if that's a real problem or just a vibe. So I want you to build me a thought experiment that stress-tests this specific worry.

Here's the spec. Construct a scenario, realistic, like a mid-sized city council or a big union, where:

- The lottery-picked body genuinely beats the elected one on all the usual dimensions: it's more representative of the population, less captured by moneyed interests, everyone on it is competent and gets proper support and information. No stacking the deck by having the lottery draw a bunch of fools or extremists, that would be cheating.
- The elected body you're comparing it against should be realistically mediocre, not cartoonishly corrupt.
- And yet the lottery body still clearly goes wrong or loses legitimacy in a way the elected body wouldn't, and the failure has to trace back to the fact that nobody authorized or can punish them, NOT to them being dumb, uninformed, or unrepresentative. The whole point is to isolate that one variable.

Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph. Then walk me through it: explain exactly what goes wrong and why, which structural feature of the setup is doing the work, and what happens to the case if you tweak that feature (say you add recall by lottery-picked panels, or rotation, or whatever). Finally, give the sortition fan their best comeback and tell me honestly whether it dissolves the problem or just relocates it.

What I'm trying to figure out is whether the "nobody chose them" objection has real content or whether it collapses into one of the other objections once you poke at it. So don't just assert that it matters, make the scenario show it mattering, if it really does.
