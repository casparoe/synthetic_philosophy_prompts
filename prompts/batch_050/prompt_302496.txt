The question was: Music is just sounds, and sadness is a state of a mind. Sounds, it seems, can no more be sad than a lamppost can. Yet we call passages sad all the time, and competent listeners largely agree about which ones. Set out the puzzle this raises, lay out the three main strategies for resolving it — contour or resemblance accounts, persona accounts, and arousal accounts — and test each against two cases: (i) a passage nearly everyone agrees is sad but that leaves most listeners emotionally unmoved; (ii) a passage generated by a purely random process (say, a program that converts intervals between radioactive decays into note durations and pitches) that listeners consistently hear as deeply sad. Then say which strategy survives best and why.

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"The puzzle is that emotion predicates apply literally only to sentient beings, so calling music sad is either loose metaphor or else stands in need of a theory explaining what makes such ascriptions apt. Note that this is a puzzle about ascription, not about what music does to us. The contour account (Kivy, Davies) says music is expressive in virtue of resembling the way sad people characteristically appear — the music mirrors the contours of a sad person's face and their slowed, drooping comportment, and sadness in music is an appearance property, like the sadness of a basset hound's face, which nobody thinks requires an actually sad dog. This handles case (i) comfortably: an appearance property does not need to move anyone, just as the hound's face is sad-looking whether or not it saddens you. Its weakness is that it explains sad sound by comparison to sad faces, so it will struggle with music whose sadness has no facial analogue, and arguably much instrumental sadness is like that. The persona account (Levinson) says we hear the music as the expression of an imagined agent, a persona, whose sadness the music manifests. This handles case (i) as well as contour does, since imagining a persona does not require being moved. But it founders on case (ii): where a passage is generated by radioactive decay, there is no actual agent whose emotional state the music expresses, and a listener who knows the origin is random cannot conjure a persona out of noise she knows to be sourceless. The arousal account says sad music is sad because it arouses sadness in suitably situated listeners. Case (i) is fatal to it: sad music often fails to sadden anyone, and on this view expressiveness would shift with the audience, which it plainly does not. The reply that listeners are saddened but too faintly to notice is unfalsifiable. So the contour account survives best: it explains our agreement about which passages are sad without making expressiveness depend on anyone's inner state."
