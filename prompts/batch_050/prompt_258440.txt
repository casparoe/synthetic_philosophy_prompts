So I'm a novelist and my current book is driving me up a wall. It follows two infantrymen on opposite sides of a war — one army launched the invasion, the other is defending — and I want to write them both as decent men doing awful things, but I keep stalling on what that even commits me to morally. Turns out philosophers fight about exactly this: the traditional view that soldiers on both sides are moral equals, each permitted to kill the other so long as they follow the rules of combat, versus the revisionist camp that says the unjust-side soldier does something murderer-like when he kills a defender, no matter how blameless he personally is, because the defender has a right not to be killed. Think Walzer's side versus McMahan's side, roughly.

Write the joint statement these two camps could actually sign after an honest adversarial collaboration. I want:

1. A numbered list of claims both can put their names to — stuff like what the laws of war do and don't settle, what excuses like duress or reasonable ignorance about your own side's cause can and can't do, and what the fight is NOT about (e.g. neither side thinks every soldier should be arrested after the war).
2. The remaining disagreements, each worded so that the side it describes would accept its own formulation. No strawmen, I'm allergic to them in prose.
3. The cruxes — my guess is one is whether being excused changes what the act itself was, and another is whether an unjust combatant can be blameless in the relevant sense at all — and for each crux, one concrete case both sides agree bears on it (say, a conscript who reasonably beleives his war is just), with each side's verdict on that case recorded.
4. If part of the dispute turns out to be about words rather than the world, say which part and why.
5. And the selfish bit: for each crux, give me one scene beat or character choice that would dramatize it without putting a thesis in anyone's mouth. Seperately for each side's reading of the scene.

Don't give me a reading list, I won't read it. Numbered agreed-claims section first, then the rest.
