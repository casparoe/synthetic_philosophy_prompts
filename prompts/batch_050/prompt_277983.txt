My sixteen-year-old daughter asked me last week why I love her. I answered in the way that seemed most affectionate: because she is kind, quick-witted, and more honest than anyone else I know. She went quiet, then said: if those are your reasons, then you would have to love anyone who had those traits — a kinder, funnier stranger more than me — and if I lost them, you would stop loving me; so you do not love me, you love a list I happen to instantiate. I told her that could not be right, but I could not explain why, and I worry she has seen something true about how I think of her. Searching for help, I came across this passage in Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics (Book VIII):

"Now those who love each other for their utility do not love each other for themselves but in virtue of some good which they get from each other. So too with those who love for the sake of pleasure; it is not for their character that men love ready-witted people, but because they find them pleasant."

I am not sure this rescues me. Loving her "for her character" seems to replace a list of shallow traits with a list of deeper ones, and character too is something a stranger could have in greater measure — so has Aristotle actually answered my daughter, or only relocated her objection? If her traits cannot be what my love is for, what is it for, and what should I have said to her? Answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
