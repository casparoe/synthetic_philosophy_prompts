I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of action to 18 students, with one 75-minute session available. Design an activity that makes participants feel, before any terminology is introduced, why joint action is not reducible to several agents intentionally pursuing the same outcome. The central task should require two or three interdependent subtasks whose success conditions cannot be fully specified in advance: participants must form provisional plans, detect and repair mismatched expectations, and decide what to do when one person privately discovers an attractive option that benefits that person while undermining the group’s plan. Build in a practical reason for participants to acknowledge one another’s participation, together with a standing opportunity to object, pause, or renegotiate, so that the activity brings out both the need for meshing subplans and the distinctive normative standing involved in acting together. Provide roles, materials, exact rules, a nonmonetary scoring scheme, a timeline, and facilitation instructions, including how to prevent one dominant participant from solving the task alone. The debrief should map likely episodes onto shared intention, common knowledge, commitment, and the authority to object; identify what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run; and state candidly which feature of genuine joint action the activity cannot reproduce.
