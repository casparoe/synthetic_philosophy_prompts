Two philosophers in my department -- one an illusionist about phenomenal consciousness, who holds that introspection systematically misrepresents our brain states as having qualitative properties they do not in fact have, and one a phenomenal realist, who holds that the existence of phenomenal properties is the one thing we know more directly than anything else -- have agreed to stop exchanging conference papers and instead draft a joint statement, as in an adversarial collaboration. They have asked me to produce the first draft, and I would like you to write it.

The statement should contain the following, in British English:

1. A numbered list of claims both parties can sign without reservation. I expect this to include at least: what the neuroscience and psychology of introspection does and does not establish; that our introspective reports are produced by physical processes that could in principle be characterised without residue; that something obviously needs explaining about why we say and believe the things we do about experience; and what, if anything, follows from conceivability arguments at the level of pure coherence rather than truth. Find formulations that are genuinely substantive rather than vacuous -- if a candidate agreed claim turns out to be empty, say so and drop it.

2. A disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated in one sentence that the illusionist would accept as a fair wording of her own position and one that the realist would accept as a fair wording of his. Particular care is needed here, because each side routinely describes the other's view in terms the other rejects (the illusionist says the realist mistakes a representation for its object; the realist says the illusionist denies the obvious). The wordings must not beg the question in either direction.

3. An explicit treatment of whether the dispute contains a merely verbal component. My own suspicion is that part of it turns on what each side means by denying or affirming that there is something it is like to undergo a brain process, and that the illusionist's claim that phenomenal properties only seem to exist is either self-defeating or trivial depending on how seeming is cashed out. Have the document adjudicate this: which uses of seem, appear, and seem-to-me are doing load-bearing work, and would either party concede anything if a shared usage were imposed?

4. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. For each crux, state what each party predicts and what argument, thought experiment, or empirical finding -- concerning, for instance, the mechanisms of introspection, the explanation of our disposition to report qualia, or cases of radical introspective error such as the fading or disappearance of unattended experience -- both parties agree in advance would bear on it. If there is no finding either party would count as evidence against their own view, the document should say so plainly and explain what that implies about the state of the debate.

The tone should be that of a document both philosophers could actually sign their names to: measured, exact, and scrupulously neutral, with the residual disagreement made as sharp as possible rather than smoothed over.
