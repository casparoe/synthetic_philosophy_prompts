I run a small science-literacy workshop at my local library, and this fall we're doing a session on the demarcation problem -- how to tell science from non-science or outright pseudoscience. Every article I find just walks through Popper, Kuhn, Lakatos, Feyerabend, etc. in chronological order, which is fine for a history lecture but useless for what I actually want: something people can *use* on the stuff in their own lives. So I'd like you to build me a decision tree that starts at "here is a practice some community treats as knowledge" and ends at a verdict, where every branch node is a concrete question you could answer just by observing the practice -- things like: does it make predictions that could fail in a way its defenders would count as failure, does the community respond to anomalies by revising the theory or by adding epicycles and ad hoc excuses, does its body of claims grow and reorganize over time or stay frozen, do practitioners ever abandon the approach because of results? Phrase the questions so someone can answer them without knowing anything about Popper or the labels.

The leaves should be verdicts, not names: genuine science, immature science, non-science that's honestly not even trying to be science, and pseudoscience (a position in the costume of science). Here's the twist that makes it worth doing: annotate each branch question with which classic demarcation criterion "owns" it -- i.e., which one would object if you deleted that node (roughly Popper's falsifiability, Kuhn's puzzle-solving, Lakatos's progressive vs. degenerating research programs, Thagard-style comparison against rival theories), and note explicitly where Feyerabend's "no single method" objection and Laudan's "scrap the whole demarcation project" argument would saw the tree off at the trunk instead of pruning a branch. Also, identify at least one combination of answers the tree logically allows but that no real practice seems to occupy, and say why you think that slot is empty (incoherent? just unattractive? merely unexplored?).

Then stress-test the finished tree against three cases: a community of professional astrologers, a 1980s psychoanalytic institute, and a contemporary "wellness data" startup I'll call ResonantMetrics, which sells employers personality scores derived from voice recordings, cites its own internal studies, and updates its model quarterly but has never published a failed prediction. Tell me where each one lands and whether you're comfortable with that verdict. Aim for around 8-12 branch questions and flag any pair of cases your tree sorts the same way even though your gut says they shouldn't be.
