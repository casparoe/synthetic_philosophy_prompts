I am a novelist, and a colleague recently defended her method of composition with an analogy I have not been able to stop turning over. A well-made plot, she said, is like a chaotic physical system: the opening chapters fix everything that follows, the way initial conditions fix a trajectory, yet the outcome cannot be predicted in advance because small differences compound as the story unfolds. She drew two practical morals from this. First, an ending that astonishes the reader is not for that reason unearned -- surprise is compatible with total determination by the setup, so a twist needs no foreshadowing beyond the opening's bare facts. Second, elaborate advance planning is futile, because changing even a minor detail in an early chapter -- a character's habit, an offhand remark -- entitles one to expect the conclusion to fall out completely differently, so all outlines are castles built on sand.

I would like an audit of this analogy. Please lay out the mapping explicitly: what plays the role of initial conditions, of the deterministic dynamics, of sensitive dependence, of the forecaster who cannot see far ahead. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. I can already see some candidate trouble spots, and I want them assessed rather than merely listed: the author is not an external observer of the system but something more like its God, able to revise the initial conditions at will after seeing where they lead; a reader's failure to predict a plot often owes more to withheld information than to the compounding of small differences, which suggests ordinary mystery rather than chaos; and on rereading, a good plot typically becomes more legible rather than less, which is the opposite of what one would expect if unpredictability were built into the dynamics. For each disanalogy you find, say whether it damages one of my colleague's two practical morals, both, or neither. Finally, if the analogy cannot carry the weight she puts on it, I would like to know whether some better comparison -- drawn from elsewhere in science, mathematics, or ordinary life -- would support either of her conclusions, or whether the conclusions must simply be defended on other grounds.
