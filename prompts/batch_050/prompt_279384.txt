I have spent enough time reading rationalist blogs and forums to be wary of arguments that multiply several weak or overlapping considerations into apparently overwhelming support. So when a friend defended what he calls evidential austerity, I initially found it plausible. Yet it seems to imply that replication, triangulation, and the ordinary accumulation of clues can almost never justify more confidence than the strongest single clue, which I cannot accept. His argument runs as follows: (1) The rational impact of evidence depends only on the total information received, not on how an analyst chooses to divide that information into separate items. (2) Any body of evidence can be redescribed as one composite observation, or else split into indefinitely many finer observations: the experiment produced a result, the instrument was calibrated, the researcher was awake, the software ran correctly, and so forth. (3) Unless the dependencies among these items are explicitly modeled, treating each item as a separate update lets arbitrary bookkeeping determine the posterior. (4) Therefore, absent a justified dependency model, a rational agent should aggregate evidence under a conservative common-cause assumption: the items may all be manifestations of one underlying source. (5) Under that assumption, N observations cannot support a hypothesis more strongly than the single most informative observation. (6) Consequently, heuristics that treat convergent studies, witnesses, arguments, or failed searches as multiplicative evidence are unjustified unless one can demonstrate that their sources are conditionally independent, given both the hypothesis and its negation.

He presents this as merely formalizing the familiar worry about correlated evidence: independence is something one must earn, not presume. I agree that naïve multiplication can double-count, but his argument appears to prove too much. If two laboratories use different methods and each is more likely to obtain its result when a hypothesis is true than when it is false, demanding proof of exact conditional independence before their conjunction can matter would paralyze science. Which numbered step first goes wrong, and why did it initially look like responsible Bayesian bookkeeping? If the problem lies in the common-cause assumption or the invariance premise, give a small numerical model in which two genuinely correlated observations jointly provide more support than either observation alone. If the argument is instead valid, identify the premise we should abandon and propose a practical heuristic to replace the presumption of independence—one that neither treats the number of evidence items as a mere writing convention nor collapses all uncertain evidence into its strongest component.
