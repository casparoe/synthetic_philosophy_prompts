I'm a law student writing a paper on when statistical evidence alone can support a verdict, and I keep finding that the literature talks past itself because authors never state what they're trying to save. Before I commit to a position, I want the specification written down. So: suppose the goal is a theory of legal proof adequate for telling courts when a finding of liability or guilt may rest on statistical evidence -- evidence whose probative force comes from base rates or population frequencies rather than from anything that discriminates the defendant from other members of some reference class. Write the desiderata such a theory must meet, with the case or consideration behind each, and mark which you take to be non-negotiable and which are partisan demands of one camp.

The list must at minimum reckon with the following pressure points, and you should add any I've missed. It must deliver the standard verdicts on the two canonical hypotheticals: the bus case, where a plaintiff proves a hit-and-run bus was one of a fleet majority-owned by the defendant company and courts balk, and the gatecrasher, where 501 of 1000 rodeo attendees are known to have entered without paying and yet liability for any one of them chosen at random seems perverse. It must explain why a 99.9% DNA match can ground conviction while a 99.9% population-frequency match on naked demographics cannot, without cheating by relabeling the DNA evidence as 'individualized'. It must say something about why courts prefer a shaky eyewitness with 60% reliability to airtight statistics at 95%, and whether that preference is defensible or superstition. It must handle my own test case: a parking lot holds 200 cars, 180 of them the same model and color as the hit-and-run vehicle; the defendant owns one such car and was in town that day -- then we add, one at a time, that his car has a fresh dent, that a toll camera logged a matching car nearby, and that a partial plate was recorded -- and the theory must say at which increment, if any, the evidence changes kind rather than merely degree. It should confront whether civil and criminal standards differ in a way the theory can express, and whether the conjunction problem for multi-element claims is a separate desideratum or a red herring.

The part I most want: identify any subset of these desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied -- for instance, whether 'verdicts should track posterior probabilities above threshold' is consistent with the canonical verdicts on both hypotheticals at once, and what has to give. Then, in a paragraph each and no more, run the main contenders against the list: legal probabilism, knowledge-based accounts, sensitivity- or safety-based accounts, normic-support accounts, and relative-plausibility or story-based accounts -- naming which desiderata each one sacrifices. Keep the weight on the criteria themselves; I'll do my own scoring in the paper.
