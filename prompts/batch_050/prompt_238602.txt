Here's a short essay I drafted for a reading group on Aquinas and political violence. It turns on a passage from the Summa Theologiae II-II q.42 a.2 (the question on sedition), reply to objection 3, where Aquinas writes: "A tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler, as the Philosopher states (Polit. iii, 5; Ethic. viii, 10)." He goes on to say that overthrowing such a regime is not sedition strictly speaking, that those who defend the common good "are not themselves seditious, even as neither is a man to be called quarrelsome because he defends himself," and then qualifies it all by noting the overthrow can still be wrong when it unleashes worse disorder than the tyranny itself did.

My essay: Aquinas usually gets filed with the anti-tyrannicide medievals, but this passage shows the real architecture is different. His move isn't to weigh whether killing tyrants is a special crime; it's to define sedition against the unity of a people ordered to the common good, and then deny that a tyranny constitutes such a unity. So the resister gets reclassified rather than excused -- the tyrant is the genuinely seditious party, since he's the one who broke the common-good ordering. The self-defense analogy does the same work twice: just as the defender isn't quarrelsome, the resistor isn't seditious. The caveat about worse disorder isn't a prudental afterthought; it reveals that the whole analysis is consequentialist at bottom, since what ultimately settles the question is which outcome is worse for the multitude. And because the passage is about what counts as sedition rather than about who may act, it licenses any member of the multitude to kill the tyrant, provided the prognosis isn't worse chaos.
