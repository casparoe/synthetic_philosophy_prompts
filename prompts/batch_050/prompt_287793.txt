For my ancient Chinese philosophy seminar (junior year elective) one of the assignments is to record a 20-minute "mini interview" with an actual scholar, and I somehow lucked out and got our department's visiting prof who just published a book arguing that Mozi basicly wins the old debate with the Confucians -- his line is that jian'ai, impartial care, is not the psychologically impossible fantasy everyone says it is, and that the Confucian doctrine of graded love (love by degree, strongest for family, fading as you move outward) is actually the position with the deeper problems. We've read the Mozi chapters on impartial care and some of Mencius's attacks on him in class, and I don't want to waste the whole interview on "so what is jian'ai" since our prof can just assign his own book for that.

So: draft me eight to ten interview questions that press where people who know this debate actually press. I want at least one question on the naturalness objection -- the Mencius-style point that care just does grow from the inside out and that the bond with one's parents is the seed of all the rest -- with a follow-up for when he replies (as I'm betting he will) that Mencius himself thinks morality works by extending a small natural impulse outward, so extension beyond family shouldn't be off-limits. I also want a question on whether what Mozi calls care is really care at all or just universal concern for people's material interests -- nobody seriously thinks you can feel about a stranger's child the way you feel about your own -- plus a follow-up for the answer he'll most likely give there. Then one on the justification problem: Mozi argues for impartial care partly by asking what happens in states where people are partial (war, theft, disaster) and partly through his tests of doctrine, so is impartial care grounded in its benefits, and what happens to the view in a case where partiality actually pays better? And one comparing him to a modern impartialist like Singer, specifically on whether Mohist care demands equal treatment or only equal consideration, since those come apart fast once you think about raising your own kids.

A few constraints: every question should include the follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, not just the opener. No generic stuff about what drew him to Chinese philosophy. Keep each question short enough to actually ask out loud, like two or three sentences max. And at the end, flag which of the questions you think he'll find genuinely hardest to answer as a defender of Mozi rather than as a scholar describing him -- he keeps slipping between "Mozi argued X" and "X is true," and I'd like to pin him into the second mode.
