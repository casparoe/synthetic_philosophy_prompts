I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and our department has recently been audited, which has left me thinking about the epistemology of what we do. Our fact-checking desk operates on a simple rule: every factual claim in a piece must be traced back to a source, and that source must itself be checked -- a study is read rather than taken on the abstract's word, a quote is verified against a recording, a statistic is chased to the original dataset rather than to another outlet's reporting of it. The chain has to terminate somewhere, and in practice it terminates in a small set of endpoints we treat as bedrock: what a researcher saw with her own eyes, what an instrument registered, what a primary document says, what a source told one of our reporters directly. Nobody at the desk regards "everyone in the field says so" as a stopping point; consensus among experts counts as a reason to keep digging, not as the bottom of the dig.

Here is what puzzles me. This workflow seems to presuppose a particular picture of what justification is: that a belief is warranted only if it sits at the end of a chain anchored in some privileged class of basic deliverances -- perception, testimony, records -- and that a chain which loops back on itself, or never bottoms out, counts as a failure of verification rather than as a different kind of success. But our own practice doesn't fully live up to that picture. When two independent outlets report the same event and their accounts cohere, we treat that mutual fit as strong confirmation even when neither account has been independently traced to bedrock; and when a claim coheres beautifully with everything else we know but can't be traced to a primary source, editors will sometimes run it with a hedge rather than kill it. So the desk's official rule looks foundationalist while its actual habits look partly coherentist, and the audit praised both without noticing the tension.

My question: what does a practice like ours strictly presuppose, as opposed to merely sit comfortably with? Must anyone who insists that verification chains terminate be committed to foundationalism, or could a coherentist or an infinitist reconstruct the same workflow -- treating the demand for primary sources as a heuristic for improving the mesh of mutual support rather than as a search for indubitable foundations? Conversely, does our reliance on independent converging reports quietly presuppose that coherence is itself evidence, and if so, is the desk committed to an inconsistent epistemology, or is there a single view under which both rules make sense? I'd also like to know what would concretely have to change in a newsroom whose editors explicitly adopted coherentism or infinitism -- which of our standards would survive and which would become unjustifiable -- since I'm drafting a piece on the philosophy of verification and want to get the stakes of the abstract dispute right.
