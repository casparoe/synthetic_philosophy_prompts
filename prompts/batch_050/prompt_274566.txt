A student in my graduate seminar on early modern metaphysics has produced what she calls an internal refutation of Berkeley, and I would like your help diagnosing it before our next meeting. She takes as her starting point the so-called master argument from the Principles — the passage where Berkeley challenges the reader to conceive of a tree (or any sensible object) existing unconceived by anyone, and claims that the attempt defeats itself, since the tree one conceives is ipso facto conceived. Her contention is that the argument, if sound, proves too much: run honestly, it undermines Berkeley's own appeal to God and to other finite spirits as perceivers of the world in our absence, and leaves him a solipsist. Her reconstruction runs as follows. (1) To conceive of a thing is to have an idea of it before one's mind. (2) An idea can exist only in the mind that perceives it. (3) Hence anything I conceive exists, insofar as it is conceived, in my own mind. (4) To affirm that God, or another finite spirit, perceives a tree when I do not, I must be able to conceive of that tree as perceived by a mind other than mine. (5) But by (1)–(3), any tree I conceive is conceived by me; it is an idea in my mind and nowhere else. (6) Therefore I cannot conceive of any idea existing in a mind other than my own. (7) The master argument itself licenses inferring impossibility from inconceivability — that is its whole force against the existence of unperceived matter. (8) Therefore, by Berkeley's own lights, ideas cannot exist in other minds; there is no warrant for positing other spirits, divine or finite, and the system collapses into solipsism. She adds that, if forced to choose, she would rather preserve the master argument's force against material substratum than the appeal to God's continuous perception — but she would prefer not to choose.

You are a specialist in early modern philosophy with particular expertise in Berkeley's metaphysics and its contemporary reconstructions. Think carefully about this before committing to an answer: first sketch, in your own mind, the two or three most plausible replies Berkeley could make — including the appeal to notions rather than ideas in the case of spirits, and the charge that the argument slides between conceiving of a thing as existing unconceived and conceiving of a thing while in fact conceiving it — and weigh what each reply costs the broader system. Then tell me which premise or inference my student should surrender, why that step looked innocent when it was laid down, and what the principal alternatives (rejecting (1) via the idea/notion distinction, rejecting (7) by restricting inferences from inconceivability to impossibility, or diagnosing a scope confusion somewhere in (4)–(6)) each require Berkeley to give up elsewhere — particularly whether rescuing other minds via notions weakens his case against the materialist, who might claim a notion of matter in return. Begin your response with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single step at which you judge the argument to fail — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
