I teach an undergraduate seminar called "History, Memory, and Politics" — about 24 students, mixed years, one 90-minute session. We are approaching the question of what should happen when a community's settled memory of an event conflicts with the documentary record, and I want students to feel, before we name any theory, why communities resist correction even when the evidence is public and the correctors mean well.

Design a runnable in-class activity built on the following spine, fleshed out and improved where you see ways to make it work better:

1. A source event. Something the whole class witnesses once, briefly, and cannot revisit — for instance a staged two-minute scene or a fixed sequence of image cards — that is complex enough to be misremembered and that involves a dispute between two identifiable parties.
2. Motivated transmission. The class splits into two "communities," each assigned as descendants of one party to the dispute. Across several rounds they must retell the event under constraints that pull their account toward self-flattering shapes, until each community has produced a fixed, written "founding narrative" it has voted on and publicly endorsed.
3. Load-bearing memory. Each community must then use its narrative to justify a claim on a real but small stake, so the story stops being a description and becomes an asset.
4. The archive. A few students play "historians," with a defined role and limited power: they alone may consult a record of the source event, and they publish findings that contradict each community's narrative at different points.
5. The forced choice. A structured decision in which correcting the narrative visibly costs the community something, and defending it requires discrediting evidence or the historians themselves.

I need: the complete ruleset and materials list; a minute-by-minute timeline; the design choices that guarantee drift actually occurs (specifically, what makes accuracy expensive and self-flattery cheap); the historians' exact powers and limits; the stakes scheme, which must not involve real money; and a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the session onto the conceptual material — motivated reconstruction of the past, narrative as identity capital, the different social functions of memory and of the record, and the question of which ought to give way, and in what kinds of forum. Tell me also what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — students treating it as an accuracy contest, historians being ignored, drift failing to materialize — and how to head each problem off. Finally, be frank about which features of real memory-versus-history conflicts the activity cannot reproduce (grief, generational depth, ongoing material injustice) and how I should supply that dimension in the debrief rather than pretend the game delivered it.
