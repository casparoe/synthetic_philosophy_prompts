Write a contrapuntal poem about the statue and the clay: two columns of verse, one in the voice of the statue and one in the voice of the lump of clay, which can each be read straight down as a coherent poem but can also be read across, line by line, as a third poem. The columns should diverge exactly where the puzzle says they must -- the statue speaks of being made in the sculptor's studio last year, of being an artwork worth a fortune, of perishing the day it is squashed flat; the clay speaks of being dug from a riverbank millennia ago, of being neither beautiful nor valuable, of calmly surviving the squashing -- while the across-reading keeps insisting on what they share: the same pedestal, the same weight, the same atoms, the same shadow. The form should do the philosophical work: reading a column vertically should feel like the pull of "two things with different histories and different fates," and reading across should feel like the pull of "one thing, surely, sitting right there." Roughly 36 to 44 lines in total, free verse, no title that gives away the puzzle, and no final stanza resolving whether constitution is identity -- the last pair of lines should leave the two voices saying something that is, word for word, the same sentence meaning two different things.
