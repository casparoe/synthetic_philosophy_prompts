So I spent my career in chemistry and have been reading American abolitionist stuff in retirement, and I've gotten stuck on Frederick Douglass's Glasgow speech, "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?" (1860). His verdicts on the contested clauses, as I read him: the three-fifths clause is anti-slavery, because it docks slave states two-fifths of potential representation and so punishes rather than rewards slavery; the "person held to service or labour" clause in Article IV isn't about slaves at all, since it describes people bound by contract, like indentured servants, and slaves are held by force, not contract; the clause barring Congress from banning the Atlantic trade before 1808 is anti-slavery because it explicitly hands Congress the power to end the trade, which it did; and the total absence of the words slave and slavery from the document shows the framers were ashamed to write it in. His stated method is this bit:

"the intentions of those who framed the Constitution, be they good or bad, for slavery or against slavery, are so respected so far, and so far only, as we find those intentions plainly stated in the Constitution. ... It was what they said that was adopted by the people, not what they were ashamed or afraid to say, and really omitted to say."

Now here's the case he doesn't touch in the speech: Article IV, Section 4, where the federal government must protect each state "against domestic Violence." Historians point out that southern delegates wanted that language partly as insurance against slave revolts -- the feds would have to help put down a Nat Turner. The text itself says nothing about slavery; it covers any insurrection. What verdict does Douglass give here? I can see two rules that both fit his earlier verdicts: a strict text-only rule, which would call the clause neutral since slavery is nowhere named, and a tendency rule -- judge a clause by what it actually does for or against slavery -- which would force him to call this one pro-slavery. His earlier cases don't seem to seperate these rules, since in each the plain-text reading and the freedom-leaning reading happily coincide. So: predict his verdict, tell me which rule you think he's really running and why, and name some other clause or hypothetical that would actually tell the two rules apart if my new case doesn't.
