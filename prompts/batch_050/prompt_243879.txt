I'm a novelist working on a book where one of the main characters quietly follows this worldview I came across called tranquilism -- the idea that the only thing that really matters is the absence of suffering and craving, and that pleasure is basically just relief, not a positive good in itself. The problem is I've realized the character's philosophy is basically my own, and before I put it in his mouth I want to know if it actually hangs together, because my editor will absolutely tear it apart if it doesn't. So here are the things I believe, roughly:

1. All that ultimately matters is the absence of suffering and unsatisfied craving. Pleasure is valuable as relief of a need, but it never adds something positive on top of a neutral state.
2. There's no duty to bring happy people into existence, and adding a perfectly happy person to the world doesn't make the world better.
3. It would still be seriously wrong to painlessly kill a contented person in their sleep, even in the contrived case where nobody would ever find out, nobody depends on them, and there's no grieving.
4. A life of perfectly contented meditation with zero suffering is at least as good as a passionate life full of love, ambition and achivement that also contains grief, longing, and occasional real misery.
5. Great art is genuinely valuable, full stop. My novel would be worth writing even if its main effect on readers were delight rather than relieving loneliness, boredom, or any other lack.

I also have a worry adjacent to this: as a writer I basically worship desire and striving -- my whole book runs on characters wanting things -- and I suspect that's in tension with tranquilism, though maybe not? Anyway, don't tell me which commitment you think I secretly want to abandon, because I don't know myself.

Format-wise, can you structure the reply like this: "Scorecard" -- a quick list, one line per commitment, flagging compatible / tension / depends (very short); "The real tensions" -- the genuine conflicts in my set, ordered from most to least severe, about a paragraph each, and for each one spell out what exactly I'd have to give up on either side; "The tension that dissolves" -- one apparent conflict in there that isn't real, and why (short); "Cheapest fixes" -- the minimal revision that resolves each real tension and what it costs me philosophically; and finally "If I only get one revision" -- your verdict on which single change cleans up the most of the set.
