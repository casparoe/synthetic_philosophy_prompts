I help run a "library of things" in my town -- members pay a small yearly fee and can borrow from a collection of maybe 900 items: power tools, kitchen gear, camping stuff, board games, kids' toys, sewing machines, that sort of thing. Because our old coordinator moved away, I've ended up in charge of intake, which means when somebody donates something I have roughly 15 minutes to decide which category it goes in. The catch is that a category here isn't just which shelf it sits on, it sets the actual rules: "power tools" are 18+ and need the safety waiver signed, "toys" can go out on a kid's card with no deposit, "kitchen" stuff carries a cleaning fee, "electronics" need to be PAT tested before they can circulate. I make maybe three or four of these calls a week, I'm definately not an expert on anything, and the board member who has to sign off my decisions knows even less than I do.

So here's why I'm writing. I never studied psychology or philosophy, but I fell down a wikipedia hole a while back about how people decide what category something belongs to, and I keep noticing it in my intake decisions. There's the prototype idea, where you compare a thing to the most typical member of the category (a robin feels birdier than an ostrich). There's the exemplar idea, where you compare it to specific items you actually remember. And there's something called the "theory theory," where even little kids sort things based on what they think the thing really is or does underneath, not what it looks like. All three show up in my work and they fight each other constantly. Our stand mixer has a motor and a gearbox and could take your finger off, which screams power tool, but every exemplar in my head says kitchen. The donated drone looks exactly like the toy drones on our shelf but applying our toy rules to it (12-year-olds, no deposit, no waiver) seems insane. The antique wooden rocking horse matches my exemplars for "toy" perfectly but it weighs 40 kilos and the function is basically furniture at this point.

Can you turn these three theories into an actual procedure me and the other volunteers can follow? Like an ordered set of questions or tests to run on an item, that takes a few minutes, spells out when each of the three approaches gets a say and which one wins when they disagree, and works through two or three of my problem items above as examples -- including at least one case where the procedure hands me an answer I'm going to hate, so I can see whether I trust it anyway. Also please be honest about the calls no procedure can settle and a human just has to decide (I suspect brand-new kinds of objects that didn't exist when our categories were written, and one-off groupings like "stuff for the fundraiser table," are in this bucket but maybe I'm wrong), and which distinctions from the real theories have to survive the simplification versus which ones I can safely drop for a volunteer with no background and a line of donors waiting.
