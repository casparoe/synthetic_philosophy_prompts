Longtime reader of LW/ACX and the usual suspects here. There's a move I keep seeing in arguments that use thought experiments, and I want to know if it has an established name, becuase I keep needing a word for it.

The pattern: someone wants to convince you of conclusion Z. Instead of arguing for Z directly, they walk you through a sequence of hypothetical cases -- A, then B, then C -- where each case differs from the last by one small feature, and at each step they get you to say "yeah, obviously you'd do X there." Then they reveal that the chain ends at Z, and if you balk at Z, they replay your earlier agreements back at you like you've signed a contract. Classic example shape: start with the basic trolley problem, tweak one detail at a time, and twelve steps later you've "committed yourself" to something about organ harvesting you'd have rejected out loud at the start. I also see it in the transhumanist corners with chains of tiny modifications to a person that end somewhere about uploading.

It's not quite a slippery slope argument, because nobody's claiming the steps will actually happen in the real world. It's not quite sorites either -- the steps aren't vague predicates, they're morally relevant differences, just small ones. And it's not simple anchoring, since the point is the *commitments*, not the first number you hear.

Is there an established term for this technique? If not, what would you call it -- and, more important to me than the label: where exactly is the line between this being a legitimate method of inquiry (it seems related to how philosophers use the method of cases, and sometimes the chain genuinely surfaces a contradiction in your views) and it being a rhetorical trap? Please answer in American English.
