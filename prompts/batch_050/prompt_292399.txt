For a graduate seminar on impartial morality I've been asked to circulate a short statement of my commitments so the group can probe them, and I'd like a consistency audit before I defend it in person. Here is where I've landed:

(1) Williams is right about the famous rescue case: a husband who pauses to check whether morality permits him to save his wife rather than a stranger is manifesting a defect of character; in moments like that, love should motivate directly, with no mediating moral thought. An acceptable moral theory must therefore not require -- or even recommend -- deliberation from the impartial standpoint in such cases.

(2) Consequentialism is nonetheless the correct criterion of right action, and not merely for public policy or institutional design; it governs personal conduct as well.

(3) The moral worth of an action depends on the motives from which it is actually performed: the very same act is morally better when done from direct concern for a person than when done from a calculation that duty requires it.

(4) The demandingness objection to consequentialism fails: the fact that complying with a theory would cost an agent her ground projects and closest relationships is not, by itself, any reason to reject the theory.

Does this package hang together? Where commitments are in tension, rank the tensions by severity, explain precisely what generates each conflict rather than just noting that the labels seem to clash, and state what would have to give in each case. If any apparent tension here dissolves on inspection, identify it and explain why it is only apparent. Finally, recommend the single revision that would clean up the most, and justify it from the structure of the views themselves rather than from a guess about which commitment I am least attached to.
