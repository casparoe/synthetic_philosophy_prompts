I work at a think tank (junior researcher, mostly I write background memos for people who don't read them) and at our weekly seminar last week a colleague rolled out an argument that's been eating at me because the conclusion seems insane but every step looks fine when I stare at it. Context: we were arguing about whether the "Munich analogy" should count for anything in debates about how to respond to a current territorial dispute, and he basically said nothing doing, and then generalized it. Roughly his argument, numbered so you can point at the culprit:

(1) A historical analogy gives you reason to adopt a policy only if the old case and the current case share the features that actually caused the old outcome -- not superficial resemblances, but the features that did the causal work.
(2) We never have independent, settled knowledge of which features those were. Outcomes in history are massively overdetermined, historians still fight about what caused what in even the most studied cases, and we can't rerun 1938 with the appeasement variable toggled off.
(3) So in practice, which features get labeled "the relevant ones" is settled by whichever policy the person already favors. The interventionist reaches for Munich, the restraint person reaches for Vietnam or Iraq 2003, and both claim the analogy is on their side.
(4) But if your choice of "relevant features" is driven by your policy preference, the analogy can't then be cited as evidence *for* that preference. That's circular -- the conclusion was built into the setup.
(5) Therefore historical analogies never provide genuine evidence for or against a policy. They're rhetoric, possibly useful for motivating people, epistemically worthless.
(6) And here's the kicker he didn't seem bothered by: this applies to the analogies *we* like too. "Don't trust WMD intelligence after 2003," "don't let great-power rivalry spiral like 1914," that whole Thucydides trap thing people keep invoking about rising powers -- all of it, on his view, equally worthless.

What I can't shake: (5) also seems to be itself a lesson drawn from history, namely the history of policymakers badly overusing Munich and domino-theory reasoning, so the argument appears to saw off the branch it sits on. And (6) just can't be right, surely we did learn *something* from Iraq about discounting confident intelligence assessments. But when I push on specific steps he concedes little patches ("okay, analogies are *weak* evidence") and then says weak evidence selected post hoc to fit your prior isn't evidence at all, it's just your prior wearing a costume. Which step do you actually reject, why did it look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost me? If I have to give something up I'd most like to keep (3), since the "both sides find an analogy" observation seems obviously true of actual debate, but tell me what that commits me to elsewhere.
