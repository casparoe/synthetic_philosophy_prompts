I'm a PhD student (linguistics, not law or philosophy, so bear with me) and I help run our department's board game society. Years ago we wrote a charter whose main clause says any rule can be changed by a simple majority vote at a meeting. Last semester someone proposed changing that clause so rule changes require unanimous consent instead, and it passed — by majority, under the old clause. Fine. But now a big majority wants to amend a totally different rule and one guy keeps blocking it, so the majority wants to just vote, by majority, to scrap the unanimity requirement and go back to how things were. He says they can't do that: repealing the unanimity clause is itself a rule change, so it needs his consent too. The majority's comeback is that the clause only got in by majority vote, so why does it get to protect itself by a stricter standard than the one it passed under? And meanwhile someone else has proposed a new clause that says "this clause can never be amended or repealed," and honestly I don't even know whether, if it passes, it would mean anything or whether a later majority could just ignore it. Half the club thinks the blocker is right, half thinks he's rules-lawyering. Who's actually correct here, and is there a principled way to settle it that doesn't just reduce to whoever has the votes at that particular meeting?
