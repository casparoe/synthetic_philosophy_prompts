I recently read a news article about in vitro gametogenesis -- the (still largely experimental) technique of deriving functional sperm and egg cells from a person's ordinary skin cells -- and it sparked an argument with my roommate. She claimed that if her parents had conceived a child using lab-grown gametes made from their own skin cells rather than their natural ones, that child could still have been *her*, since the genetic raw material would have been "the same stuff, just reshuffled." I pushed back: it seemed to me that her identity is anchored to the particular sperm and egg she actually originated from, so that any child produced from different gametes would at best have been a remarkably similar sibling, not her. She replied that this makes personal identity hostage to a biological accident, and that surely she -- the same person, with the same genome and the same parents -- would have existed in the counterfactual scenario.

Write an essay adjudicating this dispute. Take origin essentialism seriously: lay out the strongest version of the intuition that a person's origins (or at least the particular gametes or zygote they came from) are essential to them, and explain what motivates it -- for instance, worries about where one would draw a principled line if origins were *not* essential. But also give the opposing side its due: consider whether the genetic reshuffling that occurs in ordinary reproduction already undermines the idea that any *particular* gamete is doing identity-fixing work, and think carefully about tricky edge cases, such as whether "I" could have been my identical twin (who came from the very same zygote), and whether the lab-grown gamete case is really so different from ordinary variation in which sperm cell happens to win the race.

The essay should weigh these considerations against each other rather than merely cataloguing them, and it should arrive at a defended verdict: either some form of origin essentialism is correct (specify exactly what is essential -- the zygote, the gametes, something else), or origins are not essential and my roommate is right. Concessions to the losing side are welcome; a shrug is not. Aim for roughly 800-1200 words, and write for an audience familiar with basic modal talk (possible worlds, necessity, essence) but not with the specialist literature.
