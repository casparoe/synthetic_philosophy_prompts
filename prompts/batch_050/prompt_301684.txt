In Book I of Plato's Laws (649d–650b, trans. R. G. Bury), the Athenian Stranger defends the supervised drinking party as an instrument of statecraft:

"And for the purpose, first, of providing a cheap and comparatively harmless test of these conditions, and, secondly, of affording practice in them, what more suitable pleasure can we mention than wine, with its playful testing—provided that it is employed at all carefully? For consider: in the case of a man whose disposition is morose and savage (whence spring numberless iniquities), is it not more dangerous to test him by entering into money transactions with him, at one's own personal risk, than by associating with him with the help of Dionysus and his festive insight? [...] herein we have a fair test of man by man, and that for cheapness, security and speed it is superior to all other tests. [...] This then—the discovery of the natures and conditions of men's souls—will prove one of the things most useful to that art whose task it is to treat them; and that art is (as I presume we say) the art of politics: is it not so?"

Construct a thought experiment in which the Stranger's proposal is implemented in a plausible modern institution — a magistracy's vetting board, a diplomatic service, a professional school, a family firm — and in which every advantage he claims for it comes out true, yet the practice still fails for a reason his own argument gives you. The purpose of the case is to test whether the Stranger's reasoning survives transplantation into an institution whose aims differ from the one he names in the final sentence.

Specifications for the scenario:

1. The scenario is a single paragraph, realistic, with no exotic technology and no stipulation that the overseers are corrupt, careless, or incompetent.
2. The wine test works exactly as advertised: it reveals dispositions the candidates genuinely have, under conditions of cheapness, security, and speed that beat the alternatives. Do not rest the failure on the standard objections — that drunk behavior is a mask rather than a revelation, or that alcohol is unhealthy, or that the practice invites lawsuits. Grant the Stranger his epistemology and his cost comparison.
3. Include modern analogues of the riskier tests the Stranger says wine replaces (entrusting a suspect man with money; exposing one's household to a man enslaved by appetite), and show the wine test genuinely outperforming them.
4. The failure must instead be engineered out of the passage's own structure: the Stranger names two purposes — testing and practice — and assigns the whole enterprise to an art whose task is to treat souls. Build the case so that the test's diagnostic success is precisely what induces the institution to use results for exclusion rather than treatment, and so that this substitution, and nothing else, produces the bad outcome.

After the scenario, demonstrate that the specifications are met: state the verdict the diagnostic portion of the argument delivers on the practice; state the verdict the passage delivers once its final move to the political art is taken seriously; confirm that cheapness, security, and speed all survive in your case; identify the single feature doing the work and say what happens to the case if it is removed. Close with the strongest reply available to a defender of the Stranger who protests that he never licensed selection, only inspection for the sake of treatment — and say whether that reply concedes that the argument cannot travel to institutions whose purpose is sorting rather than treating.
