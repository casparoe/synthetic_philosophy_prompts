Last week I got into it with a coworker over whether it's rational to change your own behavior about collective problems. She flies constantly, eats beef basically every day, and her whole position is: one person's choices don't move the market on anything, so cutting back is just pointless self-denial. She said philosophers call this the "inefficacy objection" (she did a minor in college) and I ended up in a rabit hole on it all weekend. From what I can tell the debate is: does your individual contribution to some enormous aggregate harm -- emissions, factory farming, and voting seems like the same shape of problem -- actually matter, when any one person's effect on the total is effectively zero? And then there are a bunch of replies involving thresholds, expected value, some distinction between causing a harm and being complicit in it, and some rule-based move I only half followed.

Here's what I actually want though. I'm a backend engineer, ~8 years, most of it on distributed systems, and my gut says this is just a scaling problem I've seen a hundred times: any single request is a rounding error, everything gets quantized into batches, and yet we still reason perfectly rigorously about marginal load and threshold effects. So: restate the inefficacy objection and the main replies to it in the vocabulary I actually think in -- throughput, marginal load, batching, thresholds, load balancing, "what if every client did X," tiny probabilities multiplied by big stakes, that sort of thing. Be as faithful as you can, don't smuggle in engineering assumptions and call it translation. Then give me a residue report: which parts of the philosophical debate map cleanly onto systems reasoning, which parts you had to fudge to fit, and whether anything (complicity? the rule-based reply?) just has no counterpart at all -- and if so, whether that's my vocabulary missing something or that part of the debate being mushier than it looks. One thing I suspect won't survive the translation: in my world, if dropping one request is fine then dropping all of them is fine, there's no special fact about *my* request. I have a feeling the ethicists would say that's exactly where the analogy breaks, but I can't articulate why they'd say it.
