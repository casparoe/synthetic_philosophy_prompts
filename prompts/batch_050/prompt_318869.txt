In my philosophy of physics seminar, students reliably stall at the same point in the debate over gauge freedom: everyone can recite that gauge transformations are "mere redescription," and everyone can recite that the Aharonov–Bohm effect "shows the potential is physically significant," but almost no one can say what a world would have to be like for one of these slogans to be wrong. I would like you to repair this by constructing a thought experiment that forces the issue.

The positions to be discriminated are these. On the eliminativist reading, a gauge transformation relates two descriptions of one and the same physical state; only gauge-invariant quantities are real, and the surplus mathematical structure (the vector potential taken pointwise, the redundant degrees of freedom of the fiber-bundle formalism) has no physical correlate. On the opposing reading, call it the realist reading, some of that structure does correspond to something physical, with holonomies, relative phases, or boundary degrees of freedom serving as the usual witnesses. Do not feel bound to these labels; define the positions by their commitments.

Construct a scenario in which these two positions deliver genuinely different verdicts about what is real, subject to the following constraints. First, stay within physics that is currently accepted and experimentally established — electromagnetism or non-relativistic quantum mechanics, no quantum gravity, no hypothetical future apparatus whose behavior we could only guess at. Second, the divergence between the two positions must be generated by the physics plus the stated commitments of each position, not stipulated: it would be cheating to write "and then the observer directly perceives the gauge-dependent quantity." Third, isolate a single variable: include a control version of the scenario in which both positions agree, so that it is clear which feature of the main scenario does the discriminating work. Fourth, the whole setup must be describable in plain prose that an advanced undergraduate who has met the Aharonov–Bohm effect once could follow.

After presenting the scenario, do the following, in order. State the verdict each position gives about which elements of the situation are physically real, and derive each verdict rather than asserting it. Identify the single feature of the case on which the disagreement turns, and show that removing it collapses the case back into the control scenario where both sides agree. Then give the strongest available reply from whichever side comes out worse — presumably either the eliminativist insisting that the apparently gauge-dependent witness can be redescribed in invariant terms (at the price of some non-locality or holism), or the realist denying that the witness you chose was ever gauge-dependent in the relevant sense. Close with your own assessment of whether the thought experiment actually adjudicates the dispute or merely relocates it, and what that suggests about whether the question of surplus structure is an empirical question at all.
