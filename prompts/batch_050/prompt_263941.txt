I'm in a reading group on perpetrators of the Holocaust and every year we end up on Browning vs Goldhagen, and every year the argument stalls in exactly the same spot. One side points to the records of Police Battalion 101: the men were offered the chance to step out of the killing, most stayed, but lots were visibly disturbed, got drunk, found ways to evade shooting — which looks like fairly ordinary people ground down by peer pressure, deference and gradual habituation. The other side says the opt-out offer proves the opposite: they stayed because they believed the killing was justified, and the situational reading can't account for the cruelty that went well beyond what any order required. What's striking to me is that both sides cite the same battalion, often the very same episodes, and walk away with opposite conclusions. So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? A factual dispute about the sources, a dispute about what would even count as evidence for someone's motives, a deeper split over what an "explanation" of action is (situational triggers vs settled convictions), or just bedrock intuitions about what ordinary people are capable of that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that could move this debate — new archival material, a better theory of motivation — or is it the sort of disagreement we should just expect to run forever?
