I TA for a big intro philosophy of biology course and next friday I get one 75-minute section to teach the Gould and Lewontin spandrels critique to a room that's about two thirds bio majors and one third philosophy students. The bio kids have basically been trained to ask "what's it for?" about every trait and the prof wants me to complicate that without turning them into people who think natural selection is fake.

Don't give me a lecture on the critique, I know the paper. Plan the class. Specifically:

- The order of ideas. What has to be in place before the spandrels move makes any sense (I'm assuming they need the difference between "this trait was selected for X" and "this trait exists because of X" first, but tell me if I'm wrong) and what I should build up to last.
- One carrying example. Everyone reaches for the San Marco basilica but that's the metaphor, not a biological case, and I think it confuses people. Give me one actual biological example that can carry the whole class -- it has to be something where the byproduct reading and the adaptive reading are both live, not a case where the byproduct reading obviously wins. Tell me why your example works and what a tempting-but-bad example would be.
- The misconception map. I'm expecting at least: (a) "so Gould and Lewontin think adaptation is rare" (it's a methodological critique, not a census), (b) "a spandrel is a trait with no current use" which collapses it into a different category the paper itself distinguishes, and (c) from the bio students, "an adaptive hypothesis is guilty until proven innocent," which isn't the ask either. Tell me where in the sequence each of these will naturally form and the exact moment to head each one off -- ideally before someone says it out loud.
- One wrinkle I want in there: the difference between criticizing a *specific* adaptationist story as untested and criticizing adaptationism as a research strategy. Students merge these and I think half the literature merges them too.
- One question I can put to them in the last ten minutes that shows whether they got it -- something a student who only learned "adaptive stories can be wrong" would fail.
- What to explicitly leave out at this level (I'm assuming the empirical follow-ups and the later exaptation machinery) and how to say we're skipping it without making it sound like a cover-up.

Keep it realistic for 75 minutes with a crowd that won't have done the reading. End with a final line of the form: \answer{the single sentence I'd want them to remember a year later}
