So I write literary fiction (two mid-list novels, one prize nobody's heard of), and my oldest friend Danica, who bailed on a philosophy PhD years ago, has become my unofficial consultant on the moral psychology of my characters. Her whole method is basically one Nietzsche hobbyhorse. When I run a character or a community past her, she doesn't ask "are they sympathetic," she asks whether their morality is built nobly or reactively, and she's got this chunk of the First Essay of The Genealogy of Morals (the Horace Samuel translation, section 2, "Good and Evil, Good and Bad") taped above her desk:

"The judgment 'good' did not originate among those to whom goodness was shown. Much rather has it been the good themselves, that is, the aristocratic, the powerful, the high-stationed, the high-minded, who have felt that they themselves were good ... It was out of this pathos of distance that they first arrogated the right to create values for their own profit, and to coin the names of such values"

Here are her verdicts so far, because I've been keeping score. One: my own old-money Boston relatives, who think of themselves as simply "good people" — modest, civic, faintly embarrassed by the poor, whom they almost never think about. She said noble in structure, even though they're about as far from warrior aristocrats as you can get. Two: an online scene of failed artists whose whole vocabulary of "integrity" and "real work" exists only as a weapon against "sellouts," whose careers they track obsessively. She said reactive, slave-type, even though they loudly call themselves the good ones. Three: a union organizer in my last book who genuinely hates the boss class and defines virtue as us-against-them solidarity, but who spends all her actual days joyfully building things — strike kitchens, a clinic, a credit union — and would keep building them if the bosses vanished tomorrow. She said noble, which surprised me, since the movement's values were obviously born in opposition. Four: the rank and file of a populist movement who are genuinely powerless but basically never think about elites at all — they just love their own way of life and want to be left alone. Noble, she said, to my even greater surprise.

Now I'm drafting a new character and she's not speaking to me this month (long story, my fault), so I need to predict her verdict myself. The character: a self-made logistics magnate, enormously powerful, acts decisively all day every day, nothing is blocked for him — but his entire inner value-world is built on negation. "Serious people," "makers," "merit," all of it only means anything to him against a hated class of clerks, critics, and humanities types, and he falls asleep hate-reading his detractors like other people say their prayers. The contrast with the enemy isn't an afterthought for him, it's the foundation. What does Danica say — noble or reactive? More than the verdict, I want to know what rule she's actually been running: is it an origin-story rule (how did the values historically get coined?), a current-psychology rule (is the enemy an afterthought or the load-bearing wall, right now?), or a power rule (does the person have a real outlet in action, or only fantasy compensation?)? As far as I can tell all three fit her track record, but they split on my magnate. Tell me which reading of the passage best explains her four verdicts, give me your best guess on the verdict with reasons, and then describe the one further test case I could put to her that would definately settle which rule she holds.
