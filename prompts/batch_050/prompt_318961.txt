Two physicians I know, both members of a hospital's ethics committee, got into a sustained argument after a meeting about controlled organ donation after circulatory death -- the protocol in which life support is withdrawn from a patient with a devastating, non-survivable injury, death is declared after circulation ceases and a short observation period (typically two to five minutes) elapses, and organs are then procured. I listened without taking a side, and I'd like a referee. Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) Elena opened by saying that DCD donors are not dead when procurement begins. The declaration of death rests on permanent cessation of circulation: the heart has stopped and will not restart because no one will attempt to restart it. But that makes death hinge on a decision, and in DCD the decision not to attempt resuscitation is entangled with the plan to procure. Death ought to be a biological fact about the patient, not a downstream consequence of a choice made partly for the sake of the organs.

(2) Marcus replied that permanence, not irreversibility, has always been the operative standard. When paramedics pronounce death at a scene, they do not establish that circulation could never be restored; they establish that it will not be, because resuscitation has been judged futile or inappropriate. DCD applies the same logic, and the observation period exists precisely to guard against the heart restarting on its own.

(3) Elena answered that this glosses over two differences. First, when paramedics forgo resuscitation, the judgment concerns only the patient's condition and interests, so the permanence is not manufactured by the purpose the declaration will serve. Second, and more importantly, the observation period only rules out spontaneous recovery; it says nothing about what intervention could achieve. If mere permanence suffices for the circulation, then consistency demands that a brain whose blood supply we have simply decided not to restore also counts as permanently nonfunctional -- and nobody is willing to call such a brain dead.

(4) Marcus said her demand for a purely biological criterion proves too much, because brain death fails it too. Some patients who satisfy the neurological standard retain hypothalamic functioning and, with support, can be maintained physiologically for months. If Elena's objection were accepted, it would undermine the neurological standard she presumably accepts, not just the circulatory one. Every workable definition of death, he claimed, operationalizes a permanent loss of some critical function within a context that includes which interventions are appropriate.

(5) Elena closed by saying that his defense actually supports a different conclusion: if the real justification is that the patient is dying, has consented, and will not be harmed, then the honest policy is to abandon the dead donor rule and permit procurement from such patients directly, rather than stretching the word "dead" to cover them. Marcus responded that this changes the moral character of procurement from recovering organs from the deceased to ending a life in the course of recovering organs -- a line most clinicians, donor families, and the public will not cross, and perhaps should not cross, whatever the abstract arguments say.

I genuinely could not settle it. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five numbered moves, say whether it succeeds against the position it targets and briefly explain why. Then say who is closer to right overall, and identify anything significant that both speakers are missing.
