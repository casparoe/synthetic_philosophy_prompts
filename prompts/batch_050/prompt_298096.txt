I'm a graduate TA responsible for a 75-minute session of a second-year undergraduate philosophy of language course: sixteen students, working in four teams of four. Next week the course reaches metaphor, and my supervisor and I disagree about pedagogy. She wants the standard lecture sequence — first Davidson's denial that metaphors carry any meaning beyond the literal, then the Lakoff–Johnson tradition of systematic cross-domain mappings. I want the students to collide with the problem before anyone tells them there is one. Design me a classroom activity that does this: the two opposing pressures should show up in what the students produce and are forced to decide, not in anything I say from the front, and the names Davidson, Lakoff, and Johnson should not be uttered until the final ten minutes.

The rough shape I have in mind — restructure freely if you can do better — is at least two rounds whose results point in opposite directions, plus a mechanism that forces the contradiction into the open. In the first round, students should be made to feel that a metaphor has no paraphrasable content: every attempt to state what a given metaphor "means" either goes on without end or leaves something behind, pushing them toward the thought that a metaphor means only what its words literally (and falsely) say, and that everything else it accomplishes is an effect it causes in the hearer rather than a message it conveys. In the second round, they should feel the opposite pull: that metaphorical talk is eerily systematic, that novel instances are understood for free, and that inferences hitch rides across domains in ways that would be miraculous if metaphor were merely a causal prod. The third stage should then extract a price from this tension — a vote, a trade, or a forced ruling in which the deliverables of the two rounds cannot both be honored. Please build the whole thing around one concrete shared stimulus (my current idea is a short staged or carefully narrated incident — a disastrous committee meeting, or a breakup recounted in scrupulously neutral terms — so that every metaphor the students work with has the same referent and no failure of paraphrase can be blamed on missing context).

What I need from you: a minute-by-minute timeline; the exact materials, including sample metaphor sets, any cards or worksheets, and the precise wording of instructions I should read aloud; a debrief script that finally names the two positions and maps specific moments of the activity onto specific claims of each side — including the distinction between a metaphor's meaning and its effects, and the notion of a systematic mapping between conceptual domains; a list of the ways this predictably goes wrong when run (students concluding that metaphors are simply falsehoods; students assuming the debate is about whether metaphors can be true; a team that coasts on one clever paraphrase and declares the problem solved), with a handling note for each; and an honest closing paragraph on what the activity cannot reproduce — for instance, that Davidson's position leans on a truth-conditional picture of meaning, and the empirical wing of conceptual-metaphor theory leans on psycholinguistic evidence my students haven't seen — so I know exactly what I must supply by lecture afterwards. The students should leave having generated, with their own hands, the very data the two theories are fighting over.
