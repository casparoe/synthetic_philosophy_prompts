I write literary fiction, and my last two novels have been narrated in part by animals — a working sheepdog in one, a captive octopus in the other. A literary magazine has asked me for a craft essay, and rather than write the usual piece about empathy and research trips, I've drafted notes for an essay arguing that the novelist who writes an animal mind faces essentially the same evidentiary problem as the comparative psychologist, and should adopt a literary version of Morgan's canon. Before I expand the notes into prose, I'd like comments on the argument itself.

The notes, in condensed form:

Thesis. When a novelist attributes a motive, a memory, or an inference to an animal character, she is making the same kind of move a scientist makes when explaining observed behavior — and most fiction fails the test Morgan proposed more than a century ago: do not explain by a higher psychological faculty what a lower one can explain.

Section 1 argues that narrative form is itself an anthropomorphizing engine. Stories need agents with reasons, so even a scrupulous writer smuggles in intentionality, because a plot without motives is not a plot. The default novelistic animal is therefore always mentally over-described, whatever the writer's research.

Section 2 proposes a craft principle I call narrative parsimony: never give an animal character a cognitive attitude — a belief, a grudge, a plan — unless the scene's behavior could not be rendered through something lower on the scale, such as perception, association, or drive. I insist this is a strictly literary constraint, not a scientific one: it governs what the writer is entitled to put on the page, not what the animal actually has.

Section 3 argues — and this is where I am least sure of myself — that the principle can be deliberately inverted for effect. A narrator who flagrantly over-attributes, such as the dog who understands the divorce, works only when the text marks the attribution as the human narrator's projection, so that the reader's pleasure lies in watching a mind fail to escape itself. I claim this is what separates kitsch animal fiction from serious animal fiction.

Section 4 sketches two case studies from my own drafts, one a failure and one a partial success, and closes by suggesting that Morgan's canon, transposed into fiction, does the work that negative capability is usually credited with doing.

What I want from you: comments on the notes as they stand — specifically, on whether I have characterized Morgan's actual principle accurately or flattened it into a ban on anthropomorphism; whether the science-to-craft analogy in the thesis does real work or is merely decorative; whether Section 3's inversion survives contact with Section 2 or quietly contradicts it; and whether the closing comparison with negative capability earns its place. Please give exactly five numbered comments, one to two sentences each, aimed at the weakest points of the argument, with no introduction and no conclusion.
