I've somehow become the person who opens each meeting of my moral philosophy reading group with a short poem, and next week we're doing Parfit's hitchhiker, which has been giving me trouble. The case, for the record: you're stranded in the desert, about to die of thirst, when a driver pulls up and offers to take you to safety -- but only if he believes you'll pay him $1000 once you're home, and he's an uncanny judge of character, so faking the promise won't work. The twist we keep circling is that once you're safely home, paying seems to have nothing going for it by the lights of ordinary self-interested rationality: you already have the rescue. And yet the only way you get rescued at all is by genuinely being the sort of person who pays anyway. So it looks like it can be rational to make yourself irrational, at least dispositionally, and half our group thinks that's a deep result and half thinks it's a trick of description.

So: write me a sonnet about this. The octave should be the desert and the driver's offer, building up the natural picture where a promise is just words and the smart move is whatever pays off right now. The volta should be the moment of safety -- the reason to pay evaporating -- and it should feel like a door swinging shut. The closing couplet should land the puzzle itself: that the only road out of the desert ran through a disposition which, from the armchair afterward, looks like a glitch.

A few constraints, because I've been burned by poems that just name-drop the case. It should be accurate enough that someone who knows the puzzle can tell which lines are doing the work of the commitment problem -- in particular, don't let it drift into a poem about ordinary gratitude or keeping your word because honor demands it, since the whole bite of the case is that those reasons are exactly what's missing once you're home. The driver's character-reading matters and should be in there somehow. Shakespearean-ish form, slant rhymes are fine, and you can give the speaker a bit of bitterness toward the driver if it helps the volta, as long as the couplet stays honest about the fact that the driver is the reason there's a speaker at all.
