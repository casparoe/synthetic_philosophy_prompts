Every other week I host a small rationality-adjacent reading group — mostly SSC/LW veterans, a couple of academic philosophers, one long-suffering economist — and next month's session is on whose values an aligned AI is supposed to serve. About 14-16 people, 90 minutes, hired room with a flip chart and a box of index cards.

The thing I want to break: whenever this comes up, half the room says "obviously it should just aggregate everyone's preferences" or waves at CEV, as if that dissolves the question rather than renaming it. I want them to feel the aggregation problem in their stomachs before anyone says the word Arrow.

So design me the activity. Constraints:

- Participants do something; no lecture up front. I'd like one person (rotating, probably) to play the aligned AI and actually have to output decisions on behalf of the whole group, with the values coming from cards people wrote at the start.
- The failure modes should emerge from the rules, not from me explaining afterwards: cycling when you majority-vote over three or more options; intensity (one person cares enormously, ten shrug); strategic misreporting once they work out the aggregation rule; and the question of whether the AI may launder or "extrapolate" what people actually wrote down — especially the one person who will definately write down something monstrous or self-destructive.
- No real money. Tokens or points are fine, but I'd rather the stakes be "getting the group outcome you wanted" than "winning the most points", since winning-as-frame is part of what I want to complicate.
- Include at least one mid-game twist: a new "citizen" joins holding values that break whatever rule the group had settled on, or the AI gets told it must stay neutral between conceptions of the good and someone notices neutrality is doing a lot of work.

Then the debrief: map each thing that happened onto the real concepts (Arrow-style cycling, interpersonal comparison of intensity, liberal neutrality and what it quietly smuggles in, revealed vs. extrapolated preferences), tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and be honest about which part of the actual problem a parlour game structurally cannot reproduce — e.g. the player-AI can ask clarifying questions and see everyone's face, whereas the real thing acts on garbage inputs at civilisational scale.

Usual deal: if there's a confused premise in here — if you think the felt insight I'm aiming at is wrong, or that any game like this inevitably teaches the wrong lesson — say so plainly rather than politely designing around my mistake.
