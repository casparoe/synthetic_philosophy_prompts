Okay so I keep going back and forth on the "meaningful human control" debate around autonomous weapons, and I want to stress-test my own intuitions with a properly engineered case rather than another vague thought experiment. You're a philosopher who works on ethics of emerging tech — think carefully about this one, take your time, I'd rather get one airtight case than four mediocre ones.

Design a thought experiment with TWO weapon systems, call them A and B, deployed in the same near-future conflict, using only technology that plausibly exists today (no superintelligence, no perfect situational awareness, no sci-fi). The spec:

1. System A has a human in the loop for every single engagement: a human operator reviews each proposed target and must actively approve before it fires. On paper it satisfies every checklist version of meaningful human control you see in the NGO/policy literature — human authorization per strike, ability to intervene, clear accountability chain.
2. System B is fully autonomous at the tactical level: no human approves individual engagements. It selects and engages targets on its own within constraints that humans set earlier.
3. The scenario should make it plausible, without stacking the deck, that humans exercise MORE meaningful control over B than over A. A reader should feel the pull of "A is rubber-stamping, B is genuine governance," even if they ultimately resist it.
4. No villainy and no negligence. If the case only works because someone is lazy, undertrained, or cutting corners, it fails — the divergence has to come from structure, not bad actors. Both militaries are reasonably professional.
5. Hold everything else as fixed as you can: same conflict, same target types, similar casualty figures. Don't make B magically accurate either — that's cheating in the other direction.
