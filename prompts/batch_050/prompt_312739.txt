Having spent my career in experimental science and only lately turned to philosophy, I keep stumbling over what the literature calls the paradox of self-deception: genuinely deceiving oneself seems to require both believing a proposition and believing its negation, or else intending to bring about a belief one knows to be false, either of which strikes many as psychologically impossible. I would like a thought experiment built to order that engages this puzzle directly. Construct a realistic scenario -- a laboratory, a clinic, or a marriage, with no hypnosis, amnesia, split brains, or other exotic devices -- in which a person is intuitively and unmistakably self-deceived, yet at no single moment holds contradictory beliefs, possesses no homunculus-like inner subsystem that plays the role of deceiver, and arrives at the false belief through steps of evidence-gathering and reasoning each of which is individually ordinary. Then demonstrate that the case meets its specifications: state which account of self-deception it vindicates (say, a deflationary or motivationalist view on which biased belief-formation driven by desire suffices) and which it defeats, identify precisely which feature of the scenario is doing the work and whether removing it collapses the case back into mere wishful thinking or ordinary error, and give the strongest reply a defender of the intentionalist, contradictory-belief model could make to stop the case from counting. Close by saying whether, in your judgment, the construction shows that self-deception is possible without paradox, or whether it merely relocates the puzzle to the definition of belief itself.
