Last week I spent an entire evening arguing with a friend about expressivism in metaethics, and we parted exactly where we began, both vaguely irritated. The sticking point was the Frege–Geach problem, and what I want is not another verdict on who is right but a diagnosis of why this debate, now several decades old, keeps regenerating instead of terminating — because I have a hypothesis about that, offered as a hypothesis, and I would rather have it stress-tested than confirmed.

The state of play as I understand it. Expressivism holds that a sentence like "stealing is wrong" does not report a belief about a moral fact but expresses a non-cognitive attitude: disapproval, a plan, a state of being for blaming thieves. The Frege–Geach problem is that moral sentences embed freely in unasserted contexts — the antecedents of conditionals, negations, disjunctions — where nothing is avowed. The inference "if stealing is wrong, then getting your little brother to steal is wrong; stealing is wrong; so getting your little brother to steal is wrong" is plainly valid, but validity requires the embedded and freestanding occurrences of "stealing is wrong" to have the same content, and if one expresses an attitude and the other does not, the argument looks like a bare equivocation. The responses I know of fall into families: Blackburn-style accounts on which the conditional expresses a higher-order attitude toward combinations of attitudes; Gibbard-style frameworks in which attitudes and beliefs are treated as formally parallel states with a shared notion of consistency; Schroeder's strategy of finding one attitude type (being for) whose objects can vary so that negation and embedding get a uniform treatment; and the minimalist maneuver on which, once enough logic and semantics is in place, the expressivist may happily speak of moral truth, moral belief, and moral facts in a deflated sense. That last move triggers the creeping-minimalism objection that the position has thereby lost whatever made it distinctive.

My friend holds that the problem was solved: modern expressivists have compositional semantics generating exactly the right inference patterns, and continuing to complain is moving the goalposts. My instinct is that every proposed solution relocates the problem — the attitude-logic validates the inferences only by building their validity into the primitives — and that the newer "revenge" difficulties (the negation problem of distinguishing "not wrong" from "permissible," the wishful-thinking objection about mixed descriptive-normative inferences) are evidence that the underlying tension is structural, not technical.

My hypothesis, then: the debate is really two disagreements running in parallel under one name — a technical one about whether attitude-logics can be made fully compositional, and a methodological one about what would count as solving the problem at all (generating the right logical forms versus explaining validity non-stipulatively) — and no amount of progress on the first can move the second.

So: is that the right diagnosis? What kind of disagreement is this, actually — substantive, verbal, a clash of starting intuitions about the explanatory priority of mind and language, or an ill-posed question? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal impossibility result, an agreed adequacy criterion, something from moral psychology — or should we expect none?
