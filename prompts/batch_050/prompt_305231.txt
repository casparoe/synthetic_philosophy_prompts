I am drafting the core chapter of my dissertation on the ethics of forgiveness, and before I commit to a framework I want an audit of the position I've assembled. My claims:

(1) Forgiveness can be morally obligatory. In at least some cases -- say, a small wrong, long past, where the offender has apologized sincerely and made amends -- a victim who flatly refuses to forgive is not merely cold but blameworthy.

(2) Ought implies can: no one is obligated to do what is not within their power, and this applies to the will as much as to the body.

(3) What makes forgiveness forgiveness, as opposed to mere forbearance, excusing, or simply moving on, is a change of heart toward the wrongdoer: the deliberate overcoming of one's resentment for moral reasons. Someone who renounces revenge and resumes civility while inwardly seething has not forgiven.

(4) Forgiveness need not wait on the wrongdoer. One can forgive the unrepentant, the absent, even the dead, and such unconditional forgiveness is sometimes genuinely admirable rather than a failure of self-respect.

(5) Forgiveness is essentially something one gives the wrongdoer -- it is addressed to them, an offering between persons. A purely private cessation of anger with no relation to the offender is at most self-healing, and should not be counted as forgiveness proper.

My questions: Which of these claims are in real tension with each other, and which only appear to be? I suspect at least one pair is genuinely incompatible but I'm not confident I've located it, and I worry that two of my commitments derive their plausibility from background pictures of forgiveness that cannot both be right. Please work through the pairs, order any tensions by severity, and for each say exactly which commitment would have to give and what the cheapest revision would cost the overall view. Also check whether any tension dissolves on inspection and explain why it merely looks like one.

Finally, be candid about your own epistemic position: state how confident you are in each tension you diagnose or dismiss, and single out the single point in your analysis where you are least sure of yourself.
