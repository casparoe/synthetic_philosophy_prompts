Treat the way established liberal democracies actually handle secession referendums as an institutional practice to be reverse-engineered, using the three best-documented cases as your raw material: Quebec in 1980 and 1995, Scotland in 2014, and Canada's Clarity Act of 2000, which conditions the federal government's duty to negotiate on a clear majority voting on a clear question. Consider the following concrete features of that practice:

1. Franchise. Scotland's 2014 vote enfranchised resident EU and Commonwealth citizens but excluded Scots living elsewhere in the United Kingdom. The electorate was defined by residence within a territory rather than by descent or national citizenship — and the boundary of that territory was fixed by the very state whose authority was being rejected.

2. Threshold. A simple majority of ballots cast on a single day is treated as sufficient to dissolve a political order centuries old, while amending an ordinary constitution typically requires supermajorities, repeated votes across election cycles, or ratification by sub-units. The most consequential constitutional act available faces the lowest formal bar.

3. Asymmetry of permanence. A No settles nothing: Quebec voted twice and a third referendum remains entirely thinkable, and the losing side may campaign for a rematch indefinitely. A Yes, by contrast, is treated as irreversible — no serious proposal contemplated a confirmatory vote once exit terms were known. The practice thus encodes a one-way ratchet rather than a stable decision procedure.

4. Vetting. The existing central state claims the authority to judge the clarity of the question and the adequacy of the margin — that is, the incumbent sovereign reserves the right to certify the conditions under which sovereignty may legitimately be transferred away from it.

What conception of popular sovereignty and self-determination does this package of rules presuppose? In particular: does it presuppose that a people exists prior to the vote and merely expresses its will through it, or that the referendum itself constitutes the people whose will it records — and which of the four features above cannot be made sense of on one of those two pictures? Does the threshold asymmetry presuppose that state dissolution is more like an ordinary policy choice than like a constitutional amendment, or does it instead presuppose a view on which the entitlement to secede is a pre-political right that no supermajority requirement may legitimately burden? And can the vetting feature be rendered coherent at all, given that it grants one party to the dispute a gatekeeping role over its own potential dissolution?

In your answer, distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes — the commitments without which some feature becomes unintelligible — and what it merely fits well with, where several rival theories (remedial-rights-only accounts, on which secession is justified solely as a remedy for grave injustice; primary-right accounts, on which any territorially concentrated group may unilaterally choose statehood; and nationalist or ascriptive accounts, on which self-determination belongs to peoples as such) would each license the same institutional arrangements. Identify where the practice is genuinely underdetermined among these theories, and say which single feature, if any, most sharply discriminates between them. Finally, indicate what would concretely have to change in each of the four features if the underlying theory were swapped for one of its rivals.
