Construct a thought experiment in which the precautionary principle and expected-utility maximisation deliver opposed verdicts on the same policy question, and in which the precautionary verdict is determinate rather than paralysing. The case is intended for a seminar on decision-making under deep uncertainty, so it must generate its verdicts from the theories rather than stipulate them.

Constraints on the scenario:

- Realistic and contemporary: a decision a regulator, hospital board, or municipal government could plausibly face this decade (approving a novel chemical, green-lighting a geoengineering trial, licensing a medical device, or similar). No exotic physics, no extinction-scale stakes, no omniscient advisors.
- The uncertainty must be genuinely deep rather than merely risky: there must be no well-grounded probability for the worst outcome, only a contested model-based estimate whose generating assumptions could be systematically wrong. This is what prevents expected-utility maximisation from simply absorbing the case, so the scenario must make the groundlessness of the estimate credible rather than asserted.
- The paralysis objection must be structurally blocked. As the standard critique runs, if both acting and refraining carry some possibility of serious harm, a strong precautionary principle forbids both and counsels nothing. Your case must contain a feature that breaks this symmetry, so that the precautionary principle comes down on one side. Do not smuggle in an answer by making one option harm-free; both options should carry genuine potential harms.
- The expected-utility verdict must go the other way using the contested estimate, and must be the verdict a reasonable expected-utility maximiser would actually reach, not a straw one.
- The scenario itself should be a single paragraph of no more than 200 words.

After the scenario, provide the following analysis:

1. State explicitly which formulation of the precautionary principle you are applying (weak versions that merely permit precaution in the absence of certainty, versus strong versions that require preventive action or shift the burden of proof), and note whether the verdict changes under a different formulation.
2. Walk through the verdict of each theory and the reasoning that produces it.
3. Identify the single feature of the case that breaks the symmetry and thereby does the work of making the precautionary verdict determinate — for instance, an asymmetry in reversibility, in the distribution of the burden of error, or between harms of commission and omission — and explain what happens to the case if that feature is removed: does it collapse back into paralysis, or into an ordinary risk problem?
4. Give the strongest available response from the expected-utility defender: either a way of assigning a defensible probability after all, or a principled argument that refusing to act on the best available estimate is itself a decision with expected costs.

Write the whole response in British English.
