So I keep running into this machine picture of the body and I want to know how much weight it can actually carry. Doctors and health people talk like this all the time: organs have a job, disease is when a part malfunctions, treatment is repair. My GP literally told me my thyroid "isn't doing its job properly." And I've seen people use this picture to argue that whether something is a disease is a purely scientific question — you just check whether some part is malfunctioning, no value judgements needed, it's as objective as a mechanic diagnosing a car. I got into a long argument with a friend about this because I think it can't be that simple. For one thing, a car malfunctioning means it's not doing what the manufacturer intended, but bodies don't have a manufacturer. Who wrote the manual for the human machine?

Please act as a professional philosopher of medicine and audit this analogy carefully for me. Take your time and think carefully, accuracy matters more to me than a tidy answer. Specifically I'd like:

1. Start with a quick brainstorm of everywhere the analogy seems to work and everywhere it seems strained, before you commit to any verdicts.
2. Then give me the correspondence table. What plays the role of the designer, the blueprint, the intended function, wear and tear, the mechanic? If evolution is supposed to be the designer, does that actually work, given that evolution doesn't intend anything?
3. Where does the analogy break, and do the breaks matter for the conclusion it's usually carrying, i.e. that "disease" is objective and value-free? Test it against hard cases: things that look like clear malfunctions but nobody calls diseases, or the reverse; normal aging; ordinary sadness after a breakup vs. depression; blood pressure, which is definately defined by a cut-off someone had to pick. Can the machine picture draw the disease/health line without smuggling in what people happen to value?
4. If it can't carry the conclusion, is there a better analogy? Or should we just accept that "disease" is partly a value judgement and be honest about it?

One more thing that bugs me, and I want to know if this move is as clever as I think it is or if there's a hole in it: even for actual machines, is "malfunction" really value-free? A racing bike that can't carry groceries isn't malfunctioning, because that's not what it's for. So what a machine is "for" seems to come from what we want from it — which means the analogy might not even establish objectivity for machines, let alone bodies. Poke holes in that if you can.
