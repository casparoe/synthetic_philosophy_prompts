I design typefaces for a living, and at a recent critique I asked whether a glyph I had drawn—a lowercase 'a' stripped down to a single arc with a detached dot—still counted as an 'a'. A senior designer shut the question down with "an 'a' is an 'a' is an 'a'," said in the tone of someone stating a fact too obvious to argue with. But the longer I turn the phrase over, the less I can tell what it actually asserts. It might be a bare logical truth (everything is identical to itself), in which case it says nothing about letters. It might be the claim that all and only 'a's share some essence—a shape, a skeletal structure, a role in a writing system—in which case my question was exactly the right one and the slogan is no answer to it. It might be a stipulation: whatever we collectively accept as an 'a' is an 'a', end of story. It might even be a Platonic remark to the effect that the letter itself transcends every font and every idiosyncratic token.

Please lay out, as a list I can bring to the next critique, the distinct precise theses this slogan could be making, ordered from the trivial to the substantive. For each: state the thesis carefully, say whether it is a platitude, a genuinely contentious position, or a mistake, and indicate whether it would actually support dismissing my question about the arc-and-dot glyph. Then say which reading my colleague most plausibly meant, and whether his dismissal succeeds on that reading.
