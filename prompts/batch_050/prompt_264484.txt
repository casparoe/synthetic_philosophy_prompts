So I'm taking a philosophy of time course this semester and we just did a week on Gödel's weird contribution to the Einstein festschrift -- the rotating universes, closed timelike curves, and his argument that if time travel is physically possible in some possible worlds then time can't be "real" in the flowing sense, it has to be more like another dimension of space. Our prof kept calling this the "ideality of time" argument.

Meanwhile in my lit seminar we're reading Slaughterhouse-Five and I can't stop thinking about the overlap. The Tralfamadorians literally perceive all moments at once the way we see a stretch of the Rocky Mountains, and Billy Pilgrim comes "unstuck in time" and experiences his life out of order. Vonnegut is basically giving us a dramatized block universe, decades after Gödel argued for one on physics grounds.

Can you read the novel through the lens of Gödel's argument? Some things I'm stuck on:

- Gödel's argument is modal -- he says since our world's laws allow rotating solutions, time in our world must be ideal too, even though our universe doesn't actually rotate (as far as I remember?). Does Billy Pilgrim's time travel work as an intuitive version of this move, or is it doing something totally different, like just showing us what a block universe would feel like from inside?
- The Tralfamadorian attitude is basically fatalism plus detatchment -- the "so it goes" refrain after every death. Does that follow from Gödelian ideality of time? Gödel himself apparently thought the argument had ethical implications (something about it being a comfort re: death?) but my notes are messy here. Does the novel endorse the Tralfamadorian stance or undercut it -- Billy's numbness reads more like trauma than wisdom to me.
- Where does the novel resist the philosophical reading? E.g., Billy's "time travel" is involuntary and fragmented, which seems more like PTSD flashbacks than a coherent metaphysics. Should I treat that as a problem for my reading or actually the most interesting part of it?

I might write my term paper on this so I'd love a reading that's faithful to both sides -- don't flatten the novel into an illustration of the philosophy, but also don't water down what Gödel actually argued to make it fit.
