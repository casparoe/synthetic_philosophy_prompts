I volunteer at a benefits advice charity in the UK (though American friends tell me the same stories about their system) and there's a pattern I keep seeing that I don't have a proper word for. I want to know if there's an established concept for it, or if I need to coin one.

Two typical cases. First: a disability benefit adds an in-person reassessment at an assessment centre an hour away, justified as cracking down on fraud. Fraud detected barely moves, but loads of people with severe conditions stop recieving the benefit — not because anyone decided they're fit for work, but because they missed the appointment, couldn't face the journey, or panicked and didn't open the letters. The people who were actually exaggerating mostly show up and talk their way through. Second: an unemployment benefit requires weekly online check-ins. The claimants who get cut off are the ones with chaotic lives, no reliable internet, bad literacy, a kid in hospital that week. The determined scammers just automate the check-ins or comply quietly.

So the pattern is: a screening mechanism gets added to sort the deserving from the undeserving, but what it actually sorts on is the ability to navigate the process. And since the neediest claimants are very often the worst navigators (illness, crisis, chaos, low literacy), the filter removes the most deserving at a *higher* rate than the undeserving. The undeserving-but-organised sail through. The system ends up selecting against the very thing it claims to select for.

I know this is in the neighbourhood of some ideas I've half-heard. There's the fact that plenty of entitled people never claim what they're owed (is that called non-take-up?). There's the general point that compliance costs fall heaviest on the worst-off. And I know some economists actually *defend* making benefits a hassle to claim, on the grounds that only the truly needy will bother jumping through the hoops — which is almost the opposite of my cases, because in mine the hassle doesn't track need at all, it tracks form-filling. None of these is quite it. My thing isn't "the process is burdensome" and it isn't "people don't claim." It's that the burden functions as a selector with the polarity reversed, and — here's the extra wrinkle — the program then reports the resulting drop in claims as money saved on fraud, because from the spreadsheet's point of view a deterred fraudster and a deterred genuine claimant look identical.

So, concretely: does this failure mode have a name? If an existing concept covers it, tell me which one and where its edges are, especially how it differs from the "hassle as deliberate screening device" idea and from plain non-take-up, since those seem to be the two nearest neighbours. If nothing covers it exactly, please coin a term, define it, and give me its boundary cases: is it still the same phenomenon when the navigational barrier is an unintended side effect rather than the stated point of the policy? Does it count if the barrier filters on something that merely correlates with need, imperfectly, rather than on form-filling skill? And is the measurement wrinkle (lost genuine claims booked as anti-fraud savings) part of the phenomenon itself, or a separate problem that just always tags along? I'm not after a bare label — I want to know what the thing is, what it isn't, and where it stops.
