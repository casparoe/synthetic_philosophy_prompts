So I sit on the grants commitee of a small effective-giving fund (think: a few million a year, mostly donors who read the usual cost-effectiveness blogs), and at our last meeting we got into an actual argument about whether our past decisions add up to any coherent policy at all. I'm hoping you can help me figure out what rule we've been running. Our track record so far: (1) we funded a big malaria bednet distribution -- huge problem, extremely well-evidenced intervention, but honestly a crowded space where lots of other funders were already active; (2) we funded a scrappy little pilot lobbying for reform of an obscure construction-safety regulation nobody else works on, where the total problem is small but the pilot was dirt cheap; (3) we rejected a speculative bid to work on engineered pandemic governance -- the problem is enormous and almost totally neglected, but the applicants couldn't point to anything their money would concretely change, just a plausible story about raising salience; (4) we rejected a perfectly decent vocational-training program where the evidence was okay but three much larger foundations were already pouring money into essentially identical programs. So far so inconsistent-looking, at least to half the committee.

Now the new proposal that's forcing the issue: funding lead exposure abatement in a couple of countries -- the scale of the harm is massive, a few other funders have started moving into the area but it's far from saturated, and the intervention has boring, reliable, roughly linear returns, meaning each dollar buys a small but dependable chunk of harm reduction at a rate comparable per dollar to bednets, though because the total problem is so huge no realistic funding increase would solve even one percent of it. If we want to be consistent with our own precedents, do we fund this or not? Which of the four earlier decisions is the closest analog, and why? If the precedents genuinely underdetermine this one, lay out the two (or more) prioritization rules that fit our history equally well -- I'm guessing something like a multiplicative importance-tractability-neglectedness scorecard versus a pure marginal-cost-effectiveness-per-dollar rule, but tell me if I'm wrong -- and say what principle we'd be committing ourselves to with each verdict. One thing I already realize: a pure "only fund what's neglected" rule can't be ours, since bednets were crowded and we funded them anyway. Also, if you think we're really running one of these rules rather than the other, what's a future case that would confirm it?
