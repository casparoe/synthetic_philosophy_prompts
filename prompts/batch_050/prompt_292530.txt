I don’t have an academic background, but I’ve been trying to understand Dōgen’s idea of being-time (uji) on my own, and two friends ended up arguing about it. I don’t want to say which one I agree with.

1. Mira said Dōgen’s point is that time is not a neutral container that things pass through. Things themselves are time. So the past cannot simply be gone: my childhood, for example, is still a form of time, even if it is not present now.

2. Jules said that confuses “not inside a container” with “all existing at once.” Dōgen can say being and time are inseparable without saying the past is still actual. My childhood leaves memories and consequences, but the child I was is not currently doing anything somewhere in the past.

3. Mira replied that Dōgen gives each thing its own before and after. If a candle burns down, the unburned candle, flame, and smoke are not all simultaneous, but neither are they completely disconnected units arranged on an external timeline. Each stage occupies its own position while belonging to the candle’s temporal life. So Jules’s “gone but leaving traces” picture makes time too much like a series of isolated nows.

4. Jules said there is a third option between isolated nows and everything coexisting. The stages can be internally connected without all remaining actual. A novel’s ending depends on its beginning, but that does not mean the beginning is happening while the reader finishes the book.

5. Mira said that if Jules is right, Dōgen is not saying anything very unusual: time passes, things cause later things, and we remember earlier things. Calling that being-time just seems like ordinary common sense dressed up in difficult language.

6. Jules answered that the unusual part is not a claim that everything is eternally present. It is the rejection of a persisting object that travels through time while staying fundamentally itself. The candle does not first exist and then happen to occupy different times; the candle at each stage just is a particular occurrence of time. That is different from both common sense and everything-exists-at-once.

Please adjudicate the argument move by move, rating each numbered move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief explanation for each. Then say what both friends may be missing. In particular, please distinguish at least three positions: time as an external container, all times existing together in a block-like way, and temporal stages as connected without being simultaneous. Also note where Dōgen may not fit neatly into any of those three modern-looking options. I’m not asking for citations or a bibliography, just careful reasoning about the ideas, even if that gets a bit weird or suprising.
