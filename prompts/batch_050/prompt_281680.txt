I run the assessment division of a national certifying body for paralegals, and my team writes the written exams that candidates must pass to be licensed. We have a recurring problem: our item writers know how to test whether someone has memorized a definition, but not how to test whether someone actually understands a concept — can reason with it, not just recite it. A colleague with a philosophy background told me this is basically the question Robert Brandom's inferentialism answers: to grasp a concept is to master its role in reasoning, meaning you know what would entitle you to apply it, what you commit yourself to by applying it, and what its application rules out. I've read a summary of the view and find it plausible, but my writers are not philosophers and never will be, and I can't hand them a dense theoretical text.

What I need is a reusable procedure that operationalizes the theory: something an item writer can follow, in order, to turn any target concept from our content outline into a cluster of exam items that probe the three dimensions above — grounds for application, consequences of application, and incompatibilities — and a scoring guide that distinguishes a candidate who has mastered the concept's inferential role from one who has merely memorized verbal formulations that happen to contain the right words. The translation is the hard part, so please be explicit about which of Brandom's distinctions have to survive the simplification for the procedure to still be doing its job, and which we can safely drop for testing purposes. Include, at each step, what counts as a sufficient result before moving to the next step, where the procedure should stop entirely (I'm thinking of concepts whose proper inferential role is genuinely contested within the legal profession, where there is no settled answer to test against), and which steps are unavoidably judgment calls that no checklist can eliminate. Constraints: a writer gets about ninety minutes per concept, items have to survive legal challenge when a failed candidate appeals, and everything must be sign-off-ready by a review board of practicing lawyers who are even less philosophical than my writers.

Please apply the procedure to two or three worked examples from our outline — I'd suggest the concept of a contract, the concept of negligence, and one concept of your choosing — and make sure at least one worked example produces an outcome I won't like: either it certifies as competent a candidate response that I suspect reflects no real understanding, or it fails a response that looks perfectly fine to my reviewers, and you explain why the procedure is right and my intuition is wrong (or concede that the procedure misfires there and say why). Finally, tell me what a procedure built on this theory cannot do for us, so I don't oversell it to the board. Please write your answer in American English.
