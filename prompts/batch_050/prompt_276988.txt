I'm organizing a reading group on contemporary egalitarianism, and for our session on the rivalry between distributive and relational conceptions of equality I want a piece we can genuinely argue over rather than another textbook summary. Near the beginning of his Discourse on the Origin and Foundation of the Inequality Among Mankind (1755), Rousseau draws a distinction that seems to me to be quietly doing all the work in the modern debate. He writes:

I conceive two species of inequality among men; one which I call natural, or physical inequality, because it is established by nature, and consists in the difference of age, health, bodily strength, and the qualities of the mind, or of the soul; the other which may be termed moral, or political inequality, because it depends on a kind of convention, and is established, or at least authorized, by the common consent of mankind. This species of inequality consists in the different privileges, which some men enjoy, to the prejudice of others, such as that of being richer, more honoured, more powerful, and even that of exacting obedience from them.

Write a dialogue of roughly 900 to 1,200 words between two political philosophers who disagree about what this passage licenses and whether it survives contact with contemporary egalitarian theory. One speaker defends luck egalitarianism in the tradition of Dworkin, Cohen, and Arneson: she argues that Rousseau's distinction is unstable, because the distribution of so-called natural endowments is itself a matter of brute luck and hence morally arbitrary, so a society that lets moral inequality track natural inequality merely launders luck into desert; justice instead requires compensating people for disadvantages they could not have chosen while holding them responsible only for the outcomes of genuine choices. The other speaker defends a relational or democratic egalitarianism in the spirit of Elizabeth Anderson's critique: he contends that his interlocutor has misread the passage's point — note that Rousseau's inventory of moral inequality culminates in the privilege of exacting obedience — and that the aim of egalitarianism is not to apportion advantage according to a cosmic ledger of luck and choice but to secure relations of equality among citizens; a theory that must first certify the disabled as the deservingly unlucky before assisting them, and that abandons the imprudent to their option luck, reproduces in new form the very dependence and condescension Rousseau was attacking.

Both speakers should be equally competent: no strawmen, no talking past one another. The luck egalitarian must give a real answer to the harshness objection, and the relational egalitarian must answer the charge that his view offers no criterion for distinguishing unjust inequality from mere misfortune. Each should return repeatedly to the quoted passage — the two-species distinction, the inclusion of the qualities of the mind, and the obedience clause — so that the dialogue also works as two rival close readings of Rousseau. Do not resolve the dispute or declare a winner; the disagreement should end sharper than it began, not smoothed over.
