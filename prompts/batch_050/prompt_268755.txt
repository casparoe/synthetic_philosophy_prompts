After thirty-five years as a research physiologist, I retired and, rather to my own surprise, fell in with a philosophy reading group that meets fortnightly at the university. This term we have been working through theories of emotion, and in three weeks I am to present and defend my own position: a descendant of William James's view, on which an emotion just is the perception of a distinctive pattern of bodily change, though I add the proposal (borrowed from the enactivist literature) that these bodily perceptions represent the world evaluatively rather than merely registering the viscera, so that fear perceives the racing heart as the presence of a threat rather than as a bare pounding. The group contains roughly eight regulars, and three of them worry me: a committed judgment theorist in the Stoic-Nussbaum mould who holds that emotions are evaluative judgments or appraisals and that bodily feelings are at best inessential accompaniments; a perceptual theorist sympathetic to Prinz and Tappolet who will grant me the perceptual framing but deny that the body is needed once the emotion represents evaluative properties; and an older analytic philosopher who suspects the whole dispute is a verbal muddle about the word emotion.

Please prepare me for the question period. I would like the objections in roughly the order I should expect them, each phrased as that particular member would actually put it rather than in generic terms, since I want to recognize them in the moment. For each objection, indicate how much damage it does to my position if left unanswered, give me a reply I could deliver in two minutes or less, and flag honestly where the reply is soft rather than let me discover it on the night. I am particularly unsure how to handle two things and would like your candid assessment of both: first, the stock counterexample that one can fear an impending interest-rate rise or feel indignation at an injustice without any detectable bodily perturbation, which seems to strike at Jamesian views generally; and second, the regression worry that if bodily perception represents threats as threats, something must already have evaluated the situation as threatening, so the evaluative work is done before the emotion arrives. Finally, tell me the question I should be quietly hoping nobody asks, and what I could salvage if someone does.
