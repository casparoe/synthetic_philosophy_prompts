I'm on the five-person grants committee of a mid-sized charitable foundation (endowment around £200m) that has run itself on roughly effective-altruist lines for a decade now: cost-effectiveness estimates, room-for-more-funding analysis, the whole toolkit. We have a written policy that every grant needs a measurable success criterion we can actually check at a three-year review, otherwise programme officers can't renew it.

This cycle we're deadlocked 2-2 (I'm the swing vote, lucky me) over the last £3m:

- A vitamin A supplementation programme in two states of a low-income country. Solid RCT base, very legible numbers, cost per life saved in the usual top-charity range. Nobody's excited by it, nobody objects to it.
- A five-year bet on a small outfit doing tax-policy advocacy and civil-service training in the same region. The theory of change is genuinely plausible — decent revenue collection there would dwarf anything we could ever fund directly — but there's no honest metric you could attach at year three. Counterfactual attribution is hopeless and everyone in the room knows it.

To get the second one through committee, the programme officer did something I want the memo to address almost as much as the grant itself: she assigned it an expected value by estimating its chance of success ("somewhere between 5% and 30%, call it 12%"), multiplying by an enormous upside, and concluding it beats the vitamin programme ten-to-one. One trustee called this numerology in a lab coat. Another replied that refusing to put numbers on things is just letting vagueness win by default, and at least her 12% can be argued with.

There's a third wrinkle. The dissenting trustee argues the measurability policy is itself the problem: after ten years of only funding what we can measure, we've built up evidence, expertise and grantee capacity in the measurable interventions and let everything else wither, so the evidence gap is partly of our own making. She calls this the measurability ratchet — measurement doesn't just select for legible work, it manufactures more of it. I find this worryingly persuasive but I can't tell whether it's an argument for waiving the policy or just a lament.

Draft the committee's decision memo. It should recommend one of the two grants (or a split, if you can actually defend that — we normally hate splits as a cop-out); state clearly whether the three-year measurability requirement should be waived here, amended going forward, or kept as is; take the strongest argument on the losing side seriously enough that the trustees who lose the vote would still sign off the memo as a fair record; and give an honest answer on what to do with disciplined-but-made-up probability estimates — whether treating them as real numbers is better or worse than openly admitting you're guessing, and what policy we should adopt on them either way.

Around 600-800 words, in British English please.
