You are a philosopher of mind who has spent the last several years following empirical work on interpreting neural networks, and I want your honest, considered judgment rather than a survey of positions. Here is the situation that motivates my question. Interpretability researchers keep reporting that they can find, inside trained networks, directions or circuits that seem to track human-understandable concepts: a direction that seems to encode whether text is in French, a circuit whose activation predicts when the model is about to state something false, a feature that fires on mentions of the Golden Gate Bridge and, when artificially amplified, steers the model's outputs toward that topic. But a skeptical friend of mine insists this shows much less than advertised. His analogy: a spectroscopist can decompose starlight into neat spectral lines, but that tells you about the prism as much as the star; similarly, our decomposition tools might carve the network's activity into legible pieces without the network itself ever having used those pieces. The model, he says, might be like a turbulent fluid that our instruments render as if it were made of clean eddies.

So here is what I want from you. Give me your actual credence, as a number, that the following proposition is true: in at least some current large language models, there exist internal structures -- directions, features, or circuits -- that (a) the training process settled on because they were useful for the task, and (b) a human-interpretable concept correctly describes, such that the interpretability tools are *discovering* rather than *imposing* the structure. Then decompose that credence into the sub-questions it actually depends on. Candidates I can think of: whether causal intervention results (amplifying a feature changes behavior the way the concept predicts) are strong evidence of the right kind; whether the distinction between "discovering" and "imposing" structure is even sharp, given that any decomposition involves choices by the analyst; whether usefulness-for-the-task implies the model itself must be exploiting the structure in a way that counts as representing; and whether the human-interpretability condition is doing independent work or is just a bonus. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question with one line of justification, show how they combine into your final number, and tell me which sub-question your estimate is most sensitive to -- i.e., what empirical result or argument, if it came through, would move your credence the most.

Please think carefully before committing: it may help to first sketch, in a few sentences, what you take the strongest case on each side to be, and to be explicit about where you are treating two sub-questions as independent when they might not be. I am not asking for citations or a literature review; I am asking for your best decomposition, with the weak joints of your own reasoning marked.
