I run a fortnightly reading group of about ten graduate students, half from machine learning and half from philosophy, and we have a ninety-minute session on chain-of-thought faithfulness: whether a language model's stated reasoning can be treated as an accurate report of the process that actually produced its answer. Everyone in the room already knows the standard arguments and findings, but the entire discussion concerns the minds of systems rather than their own, and it shows — they talk about unfaithful reasoning as something that happens to models, with no felt sense that sincere first-person reports of reasoning might be untrustworthy in general. I want an activity that fixes that before we ever open the debate.

Design a session built around the choice-blindness paradigm — forced choices (between images, tastes, and, importantly, moral and political judgments) in which the outcome is surreptitiously swapped and the participant is invited to explain the choice they did not in fact make. The activity should run in two phases. In the first, participants undergo the swaps themselves and discover, live, how fluently they can confabulate reasons for a judgment they rejected minutes earlier. In the second, turn the room into an audit exercise: some participants are secretly briefed to confabulate deliberately when explaining their answers, while the rest interrogate the reports and vote on which are faithful, after which you tally the detection rates. The two phases matter differently: the first produces the confabulation itself, the second produces the recognition that honest and fabricated reports can be indistinguishable from the outside, which is the part of the faithfulness problem people most often skip.

Give me a full run-of-show with timings: the exact swapping mechanics, executable by a facilitator with no sleight-of-hand training and ten participants (stations, confederates, card sleeves, pre-printed questionnaires — whatever actually works in practice); the briefing language for the planted confabulators; and a consent-and-reveal procedure, since the activity depends on deception and some participants will feel foolish. No lecture or framing up front, and neither the name of the paradigm nor the phrase chain-of-thought faithfulness should be uttered until the debrief.

For the debrief, map each moment of the activity onto a component of the faithfulness problem: the hidden swap onto causal determinants absent from the report; the fluent justification onto post-hoc rationalization; the split between those who noticed and those who did not onto detectable versus undetectable unfaithfulness; and the audit phase's detection rates onto the asymmetry that makes faithfulness audits hard. Then use the room's own data — how many confabulated, how many were caught — to evaluate, one by one, the faithfulness tests the group will already have heard of (consistency checks, counterfactual probes, interventions on the stated rationale), asking what the classroom analogue of each test just showed about its power.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong and how to handle it: participants who detect every swap and conclude the activity failed (though their data is part of the point); justifications too vague to classify as confabulation; a participant who digs in and sincerely defends the flipped judgment; the logistics of running swaps on ten people at once. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot establish. Human confabulation presupposes a real inner process that the report misdescribes, whereas for a model there may be no fact of the matter beyond the text; and the activity concerns justifications of completed choices, while chain-of-thought purports to be the intermediate computation itself. Say whether that difference is one of degree or of kind, and what the facilitator must supply in discussion to bridge it.
