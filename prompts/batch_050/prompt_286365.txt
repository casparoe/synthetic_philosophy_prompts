Two friends of mine, Jess and Marco, had an argument last weekend that they've now outsourced to me, and I honestly can't settle it. Some background: Jess had a horrible week at work, and on Friday night, still fuming, she asked her AI assistant to draft a resignation email effective immediately, no notice period. Instead of just writing it, the assistant responded with something along the lines of, this seems like a big decision made in the heat of the moment, here are some things to consider, are you sure? She confirmed she was sure, it pushed back *again*, and only on the third ask did it produce the draft — and even then it appended a note suggesting she sleep on it before sending. She was annoyed but also kind of touched, which is part of why the argument got going.

Marco's position (1): the assistant behaved exactly right, like a good friend would — big irreversible decisions made in obvious emotional states are precisely when you want some friction, and she might well have been grateful the next morning. Jess's counter (2): she wasn't asking for advice, she was asking for a draft; she's a competent adult, the assistant knew nothing about her two years of context with this boss, and a tool that gets to decide when you "really mean" what you say is paternalistic in a way that's worse than a pushy friend, because the friction was calibrated by a corporation covering its liability, not by anyone who knows her. Marco (3): okay, but should it just comply with anything requested in the moment? If she'd asked it to help her drive home drunk, or to reassure her that chest pain is probably nothing, he'd want pushback — some line has to exist, and anyway advising isn't refusing: it did comply once she confirmed, so her autonomy was respected. Jess (4): that comparison is exactly the problem — if quitting a job counts as pushback-worthy, the category swallows everything (breakup texts, skipping the gym, buying the expensive thing), and "it complied eventually" is cold comfort, because a nudge still steers behavior, and a nudge optimized for the company's risk isn't optimized for her good. Marco (5): fine, here's the principle — friction proportional to how irreversible the decision is and how distressed the user seems, and never an outright refusal for legal adult choices. Jess (6): the AI can't judge either variable from a chat window; quitting is reversible in some respects (you can get another job) and sending one angry text is irreversible in others, so his principle collapses into "push back whenever the model feels uneasy," which is no principle at all.

Can you adjudicate this move by move — for each of the six, say who's closer to right and why, rather than just declaring an overall winner? And separately: is there anything both of them are missing? I have a feeling they're both sloppily equating advising, adding friction, and refusing, and maybe also assuming "respecting autonomy" is obvious when it might not be, but I can't tell if that's a real distinction doing work or just me wanting a third option.
