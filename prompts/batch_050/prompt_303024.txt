I’m a retired biologist who has recently gotten into philosophy, and I’ve been trying to sort out two criticisms of effective altruism that I may be running together. As I understand it, measurability bias means that interventions with countable, short-term results—like malaria nets—get favored over messier work such as changing migration rules, tax policy, intellectual property law, or labor institutions. The institutional critique seems related: EA treats poverty as an engineering problem while leaving the political and economic structures that produce poverty largely untouched.

Here’s my confusion. If measurable interventions are systematically overvalued because their effects are easier to count, does that mean unmeasurable systemic interventions are probably undervalued and therefore likely to be more effective on the margin? I’m tempted to conclude that EA’s recommended charities are almost necessarily less effective than political or institutional reform, but that inference feels a little too neat.

What’s wrong with my reasoning, if anything? Please don’t just say that systemic change is uncertain. Distinguish between a bias in our evidence, a bias in what organizations have incentives to fund, and the actual expected value of an intervention. Also explain whether the institutional critique adds something beyond measurability bias. I’d like the answer in American English, and feel free to use a small hypothetical example—my statistics is rusty but not completely gone.
