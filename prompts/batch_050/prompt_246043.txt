I’m a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I’ve somehow ended up agreeing to give the opening speech at a dinner for my partner’s philosophy society. The theme this year is promises, intentions, and trust. Most of the audience will be normal humans rather than philosophers, although a few people there will definitely know far more than I do, so I can’t get away with vague inspirational waffle. My partner once explained Kavka’s toxin puzzle to me while we were debugging a deployment: someone can benefit from sincerely intending to do something later, even if, once later arrives, there seems to be no reason to follow through. It reminded me uncomfortably of software teams saying they’ll definitely refactor the codebase after the deadline.

Please write a five-minute speech for the dinner, in British English, that uses the toxin puzzle as its central idea rather than merely mentioning it. Include an example I invented: a startup founder publicly commits today to deleting all customer data tomorrow, knowing that the commitment will persuade investors to transfer funds tonight; the founder genuinely intends to delete the data, but also realises that after the money arrives it would be commercially irrational to do so. Explore whether there is a morally relevant difference between intending, predicting that one will act, promising, and setting up circumstances so one cannot back out. I’d like the speech to connect this to ordinary trust between friends and colleagues without turning into a lecture, and ideally end on something thoughtful rather than neatly resolving everything. Casual is fine, but don’t make me sound thicker than I already feel.
