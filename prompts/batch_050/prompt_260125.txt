I've never studied philosophy, but I keep a notebook of situations that bother me, and one pattern shows up often enough that I'd like to know whether it already has a name.

The pattern is this: someone does the right thing, and the right reason for doing it is genuinely present in their mind -- they would sincerely give it if asked -- but it isn't the reason that actually moved them, and in fact it wouldn't have changed anything if it were absent. Three examples from the notebook. A wealthy acquaintance gives a large sum to a famine-relief fund and tells everyone, including himself, that he gives because children are starving; but I happen to know the same sum was going out the door this quarter regardless, for tax and reputation reasons, and if the cause had been merely fashionable rather than urgent he'd have given it all the same. A colleague keeps an inconvenient promise, and when I ask why, she says promises are to be kept -- but keeping this one also happens to serve her interests perfectly, and I strongly suspect she'd have kept it for that reason alone even if it hadn't been owed. Contrast both with a third case: a friend told a hard truth that cost him a friendship, when lying would have been easy and profitable, and the only thing that carried him through was the conviction that lying was wrong. In his case the reason did all the work.

I know about the example everyone mentions of the shopkeeper who deals honestly because honesty is good for business, where the decent motive is simply absent. My cases are different and, I think, harder: the decent motive is present, avowed, even felt -- it's just inert, a passenger rather than the driver. And there's a further wrinkle I can't sort out. Suppose my colleague would have kept the promise either way -- out of duty if self-interest had failed, out of self-interest if duty had failed. Then no single test about what she'd have done settles which reason was driving, and yet it still feels like there should be a fact of the matter.

Is there an established term for the difference between a reason's being present and its being the one that does the work -- and for the specific case where the right reason is present but inert? If so, I'd like to know how the literature handles my third wrinkle, where both motives are sufficient on their own and any counterfactual test seems to come out the same. If there is no established term for exactly this, I'd like you to coin one, define it precisely, and mark its edges: how it differs from the absent-motive case, from hypocrisy (my acquaintance isn't lying -- he believes his own account), and from simply having mixed motives, since a mixture implies both reasons are pulling some weight and my claim is that one is pulling none. I'd also like to know whether the distinction, once drawn, actually matters for how much credit the person deserves, or whether credit should track only what was done and not which reason was in the driver's seat. A bare label won't satisfy me; I want the carving.
