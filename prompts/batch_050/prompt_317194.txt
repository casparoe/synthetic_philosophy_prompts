So I write novels for a living, but my old college roommate teaches at the high school down the road and talked me into doing one of her math club's enrichment afternoons. I picked my topic because I went down a rabbit hole researching a character in my last book who was a logician: why some mathematicians refuse to accept that every statement is either true or false, and insist that a proof has to actually show you how to build the thing it claims exists. The kids are sharp -- they've all seen the usual proofs by contradiction (square root of 2 is irrational, infinitely many primes, one kid will definately bring up the halting problem) -- but none of them will have heard of Brouwer or constructivism, and I get forty minutes and a whiteboard. I don't want to lecture them on the history; I want them to feel, by the end, that the classical way of proving existence can be genuinely fishy, and that rejecting it is a principled position rather than a weird superstition.

Plan the session for me. What idea has to be solidly in place before the rejection of excluded middle will even parse for them as something other than mysticism -- my guess is they need to first see proofs as recipes rather than verdicts, but tell me how to get that across and what it depends on. Then the carrying example: I was thinking of that proof that there exist irrational numbers a and b with a^b rational, where you consider sqrt(2)^sqrt(2) and never actually find out which pair works, but if there's a better one for teenagers, use that instead and tell me what the example needs to have and needs to avoid. Lay out the misconceptions in the order they'll surface -- I'm bracing for "so there's a third truth value," "so normal math is wrong," and the smart-aleck "but double negation elimination is obviously just logic" -- and where in the arc each one gets defused rather than dismissed. Give me a check at the end that shows the idea landed: maybe handing them a proof and asking them to find where the law of excluded middle snuck in, but you design it. And tell me what to openly say I'm skipping at this level (Kripke models, the proofs-as-programs thing) so it reads as a teaser rather than a hole.
