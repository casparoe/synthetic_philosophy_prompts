So I was up way too late last night arguing with my friend Marcus about why promises actually bind anyone, and we got nowhere. His line the whole night was basically that promising is just a way of deliberately making someone expect something from you, so a promise binds exactly when and because the other person reasonably counts on you, and that's the whole story. I kept throwing cases at him and he kept biting bullets I'm not willing to bite. But honestly some of my own gut verdicts don't obviously hang together either, so I want to lay out where I land on the cases and get someone to tell me what principle, if any, captures my verdicts -- and if the answer is that I'm incoherent, tell me which verdict I should drop and why that one rather than the others.

My verdicts:

1. My buddy Sam tells me his car got totaled and asks me to promise to drive him to the airport Saturday. I promise. Two days later I find out his car is fine, he just didn't want to pay for parking. Verdict: not bound. Screw him, I'm sleeping in.

2. My aunt was dying and basically out of it, and at her bedside I promised her I'd scatter her ashes at the lake she grew up on. I'm honestly not sure she heard or understood me at all. She died three days later. Nobody else knows I said it, nobody will ever check. Verdict: completely, utterly bound. This one I'm not budging on.

3. On the phone with my cousin, the call is breaking up, and right as it drops I say "ok I promise I'll be at the wedding." I find out later she never heard it -- the call cut out before I got the words out. Verdict: I should probably still go, but I didn't actually promise anything. No promise, no binding.

4. I promised my coworker Priya I'd water her desk plant over the holidays. She quit the following week and moved to Portugal and never mentioned the plant again. As far as I can tell nobody expects or relies on anything. Verdict: the promise just... dissolves? I'm not bound anymore.

5. I promised my neighbor Greg I'd help him move. The day before, he texts "honestly don't worry about it, got movers, but the offer means a lot." Verdict: released, obviously. But compare: last year we had a stupid argument about his dog and he texted "ugh fine, don't bother coming then" in a huff. Verdict: still bound, or at least I couldn't treat that as a release -- I showed up and felt I had to.

6. I once promised my brother I'd cover for him with his wife about where he'd been. Verdict: not bound, and I was wrong to make the promise in the first place. The promise doesn't even get off the ground.

7. Guy mugs me at knifepoint, makes me promise to send him money, I promise so he'll leave. Verdict: not bound, not even slightly.

So here's where I think I'm in trouble, and where Marcus hammered me: case 2 and case 3 both involve the other person never actually receiving the promise, but I say one binds and the other doesn't. And case 2 vs case 4: in both, by the time performance comes around, there is literally no one left who expects or relies on anything, and I say the first binds with full force and the second evaporates. Marcus says his expectation story cleanly handles 3, 4, 5, and 7, and that my verdict on 2 is just sentimentality I should give up. I say his story is dead because of 2, and also that it gets the *reason* wrong in cases like 1 -- the problem isn't that Sam's expectations were unreasonable, plenty of expectations based on lies are reasonable, the problem is that *he* can't hold me to something he procured by lying.

What I want: give me a principle, or two or three candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I can actually apply them. For each principle, tell me which clause is doing the work in each of my cases -- like, explicitly map it. Tell me what it says about cases I didn't think of: promises made to kids too young to understand what a promise is, promises made as part of a joke where everyone including me knows its a joke, promises I make to myself, and whether a promise can bind me to something the promisee actively doesn't want anymore. And be honest about which of my seven verdicts your best principle has to throw out -- if saving case 2 requires a principle that also makes case 4 binding, say so and argue for which one of us, me or Marcus, has to eat the bullet. Don't just split the difference and say it's complicated.
