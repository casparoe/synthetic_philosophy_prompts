I teach an undergraduate bioethics course, and every year the unit on the definition of death goes the same way: students assume that "dead" is a straightforward medical fact that physicians discover rather than a status that gets fixed by a cluster of philosophical commitments, and they collapse coma, persistent vegetative state, locked-in syndrome, and brain death into one vague category of "basically gone." To break this up, I want to build them a decision tree that starts from a place any reasonable student already occupies -- something like "being dead is not simply a matter of whether the heart is beating at this instant" -- and walks them through a series of questions, each of which they can answer from their own intuitions about concrete cases, until they arrive at a named position. The point of the exercise is that two students who both nod along to the phrase "brain death" should discover, by landing on different leaves, that they actually disagree about what death is.

Please write this decision tree with roughly six to eight branching nodes and about eight leaves. Each node should be phrased as a question about a specific case, not as a statement of a theoretical doctrine, so that a student can answer it without knowing which position it steers them toward. Please build the tree around the following cases, which I will hand out in advance: (A) a patient in a persistent vegetative state -- brainstem functions intact, eyes open, sleep-wake cycles present, no evidence of awareness; (B) a patient with total irreversible destruction of the entire brain whose body is being ventilated and has been kept physiologically stable for months; (C) a patient declared dead under circulatory criteria two to five minutes after cardiac arrest, in a context where resuscitation is technically possible but will not be attempted, as in controlled donation after circulatory death; (D) a cryonics patient whose brain's fine structure appears preserved well enough that a hypothetical future technology could read off the information encoding their memories and dispositions, though nothing today can revive them; and (E) a locked-in patient, fully conscious, who can communicate only by vertical eye movements. The leaves should include the traditional cardiopulmonary account, whole-brain death, a higher-brain or neocortical account, a person-centered account on which death is the irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness, and an information-theoretic account on which death occurs only when the information constituting the person is unrecoverable in principle. If two of these collapse into the same leaf given my cases, say so rather than forcing a separation, and note what further case or question would be needed to split them. If some combination of answers is coherent but lands on no defended position, mark that leaf as unoccupied and say briefly whether you think it is empty because the view is incoherent, merely unpopular, or simply unexplored.

One more constraint: the tree is a teaching instrument, not an advocacy document, so keep the questions neutral -- none of them should smuggle in the answer (for instance, case C's node should not presuppose that "irreversible" means "cannot be reversed" rather than "will not be reversed," since that equivocation is exactly what I want students to notice). After the tree itself, add a short section -- a few sentences -- assessing where the tree is most likely to mislead: which of its branch points a critic would argue is doing illicit philosophical work, which distinction a defender of the standard clinical picture would say is overdrawn, and what kind of argument or empirical development (say, a reliable technique for reviving cryonics patients, or evidence of covert consciousness in vegetative patients) would force you to redraw the tree rather than merely relabel a leaf.
