You're a pure mathematician who's taught set theory and algebra for years and has strong opinions about how abstract results should be motivated. Take your time and think through the dependency structure before answering — I'd rather have actual reasoning than a generic lesson plan.

Some context. I run a weekly problem class for first-year maths undergraduates, and I want to give one 50-minute session over to Zorn's lemma. Their background: they've seen the definition of a partial order exactly once, they can do ordinary induction, and they have never knowingly used the axiom of choice. What I want from you is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself — and I want you to commit. Don't hand me a menu of options and leave me to pick; make definite choices and defend them.

Specifically:

1. Order of ideas. What has to be in place before the statement of the lemma can even be parsed? Chain, upper bound, maximal element, and the maximal-versus-greatest distinction are the obvious candidates, but tell me the order you'd introduce them in and why — what depends on what. And make a call on this: should the statement of the lemma come early, as a target the session is aiming at, or late, as a payoff? Commit to one and say what tips it.

2. The carrying example. Pick ONE example the whole session is built around — every vector space has a basis, every ring has a maximal ideal, extending a partial order to a total order, or something else you prefer — and defend the pick. What does the example need to have (familiar objects? a conclusion that's genuinely non-constructive? a partial order students can actually picture?) and what must it lack? If you go with the vector space example, tell me how you stop the finite-dimensional intuition making the conclusion feel trivial. If you don't, tell me why you passed on the most obvious choice.

3. The misconception map. List the misunderstandings they'll predictably bring or form, in the order they'll surface, and mark the point in your sequence where each one gets headed off — not just 'address it if it comes up'. I'm expecting at least: maximal confused with greatest; the lemma read as a procedure you could actually run; the upper-bound condition treated as pedantry that never fails in practice; and the 'this is obviously true, why is it a big deal' reaction (or its twin, 'this is obviously false'). Add any I've missed. Separately: when in the session, if at all, do you mention the equivalence with the axiom of choice, and how do you do it without derailing everything?

4. The check. Give me the actual question I'd put to them at about minute 45 whose answer would show the idea has landed rather than that they've memorised the statement — plus what a good answer sounds like and what the tell-tale wrong answers are.

5. What to leave out. Name the things a textbook would include that I should deliberately skip at this level (the transfinite-induction proof? the well-ordering theorem? the Bourbaki–Witt route?), and justify the skipping rather than just listing them.

One more thing: be honest about the weaknesses of your own plan. If the example you've committed to has a real drawback, or if two of the misconceptions fight each other so that heading one off invites the other, say so plainly instead of papering over it.
