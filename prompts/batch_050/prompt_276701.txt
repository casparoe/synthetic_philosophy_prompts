I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on rational choice, about 18 students, and I have one 75-minute session on decision under ignorance. Design an in-class game with two rounds of real choices (small stakes, no money needed) that makes students feel the difference between maximin and minimax regret -- and, specifically, feel the force of the objection that minimax regret is sensitive to irrelevant alternatives. In round one, students choose among options whose payoffs vary across a few states of nature, with no probabilities given. In round two, the same options return plus one new option whose worst case is so bad that no maximin reasoner would ever switch to it, but which, because regret is measured against the best achievable payoff in each state, predictably flips some students' regret-minimizing choice. Give me payoff tables tuned so the flip actually happens and isn't trivial to spot, a protocol for both rounds, and a debrief that maps what students did onto the two decision rules before naming them -- they shouldn't hear the words maximin or regret until after round two. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I expect students to quietly smuggle in probabilities, for one), and say how confident you are that your payoff tables produce the intended flip, singling out the design choice you're least sure of.
