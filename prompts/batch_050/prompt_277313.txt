My mother died last March, and next month we're holding the tombstone unveiling at the cemetery in Soweto, roughly a year after the funeral, which is how our family does it — the stone gets laid once the ground has settled and everyone gathers again to say her name and formally close the mourning period. I've been asked to give the main speech, about fifteen minutes. The audience will be split down the middle: half her church community, the women she sang with in the choir for thirty years and the neighbours she fed, and half academics, because I teach philosophy in the UK and a few of my colleagues are flying out, plus some of her old students who are teachers themselves now. She was a primary school teacher for forty years, not a philosopher at all, but she had this habit of saying she was made of other people — that anything good in her was on loan from her mother, her choir, her students, and she was just the place it all passed through.

To prepare I've been reading about relational conceptions of personhood in African philosophy — the idea behind the Nguni saying that a person is a person through other persons, and the stronger version some philosophers defend, that personhood isn't something you simply have from birth like a soul or a set of rights, but something you achieve over a lifetime by participating in a community and discharging your obligations to it, so that a full human life is literally a process of becoming more and more of a person. I find this the truest description of her I've ever come across, but it creates a real problem for the speech. The church half of the room will expect to hear that she's with God and the ancestors now; my colleagues and I are mostly secular, and I'd feel dishonest affirming that, and cruel denying it. The relational view seems to offer a third path: she isn't gone the way an isolated atom would be gone, because the person she became still exists in the network of people who made her and whom she made — the choir still sings the harmonies she drilled into them, her former students still use her classroom phrases without knowing where they got them. But I don't want the speech to turn that into a cold consolation prize, as though I'm saying she's only a pattern in other people's heads, and I definately don't want it to become a lecture with the aunts glazing over in the front row.

So: write the speech. The relational personhood idea should do the actual structural work — it should explain what we're really doing when we unveil the stone and speak her name, not just get name-dropped in paragraph two — while threading between the ancestral language the church half needs and the honesty I need. Build it around one concrete story about her (invent the details: she was famous for feeding anyone who knocked and for marking schoolwork in red pen at the kitchen table late into the night) and land on an ending both halves of the room can say amen to, whatever each of them means by it.
