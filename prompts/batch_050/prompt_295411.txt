I am designing the syllabus for a graduate seminar on mental causation, and I want a decision tree that sorts a visitor to the seminar into a position on the causal exclusion problem, starting from the single commitment that mental properties are real and that at least some of our behavior is caused by our beliefs and desires. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: each branch node should be a question about a specific commitment -- causal closure of the physical, the exclusion principle, whether mental properties are identical to or constituted by physical properties, whether causation and causal relevance are the same relation, whether systematic overdetermination of behavior is acceptable -- and each question must be phrased as a concrete case or claim the visitor can answer without knowing which position it leads to (for example, by asking whether a neural event and a desire can both be full causes of one arm movement without this being like two assassins shooting the same target).

Required leaves: reductive type-identity theory, epiphenomenalism, interactionist dualism, the overdeterminationist who accepts two sufficient causes, and at least two non-reductive physicalist positions that resist exclusion by different means (for instance, one that denies the exclusion principle via a proportionality or difference-making account of causal relevance, and one that distinguishes causal efficacy from causal relevance). Include eliminativism about propositional attitudes as a leaf only if you can reach it through a node question that does not presuppose it.

Mark every combination of answers that your questions allow but no actual position occupies, and diagnose each gap in one sentence: is it empty because the combination is incoherent, because it inherits the worst of both parents, or because it is simply unexplored? Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and say what further question would be needed to split them. Cap the tree at eight branch nodes and nine leaves.

Begin with the tree itself, with no preface or commentary; put the diagnostics of empty combinations and unseparable leaves after it.
