I've never studied philosophy formally, but I've spent a lot of time thinking about whether death is actually bad for the person who dies, and I'd like a consistency check on where I've landed before I trust these views any further. Here is what I currently believe:

1. Only experiences can be good or bad for a person. If something never touches my experience at any point in my life, it cannot affect how well my life goes for me.
2. My own death cannot be bad for me: while I am alive, my death hasn't happened yet, and once it has, there is no one left for anything to be bad for. A harm needs a subject.
3. It is nonetheless rational to prefer living to eighty over dying at forty, and rational to undergo unpleasant medical treatment in order to extend one's life.
4. The eternity before I was born and the eternity after I die are the same kind of nothing; since the first doesn't distress me, the second shouldn't either. I've genuinely made peace with both.
5. When a child dies, something terrible has happened to the child, not just to the grieving parents. The child has been robbed of a life, and that loss is the worst of it.
6. Nothing after my death can affect my welfare. Posthumous slander can't harm me, and posthumous fame can't benefit me.
7. If I finish a novel and die the day after mailing it to a publisher, I would genuinely rather it be published and loved than pulped, even though I could never know — and I take this preference to be about my good, not merely a quirk of my tastes.

Which of these are actually in tension with each other, and which only look like they are? Please go through them, rank any tensions you find by severity, and for each one say what would have to give and what the cheapest fix would be. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most overall.
