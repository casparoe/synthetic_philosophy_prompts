I am a novelist at work on a book about a de-extinction venture, and I keep returning to Jurassic Park as the genre's template. Read the film through the ethics of irreversible intervention in ecosystems: Malcolm's chaos-theoretic warnings are usually treated as the film's whole argument, but I suspect the deeper case against Hammond lies in the asymmetry between actions one can undo and actions one cannot, and in what that asymmetry should do to the standard of justification we demand before acting. At the same time, the film plainly resists a purely precautionary reading -- it lingers on the wonder, and once the animals exist it treats them as beings with claims on us rather than as mistakes to be corrected, which raises the uncomfortable question of whether regretting an intervention licenses hostility toward its products. Which scenes bear the weight of each side of this tension, and does the film capture something about deliberately self-propagating, unrecallable interventions -- gene drives being the live example -- that the policy literature, with its cost-benefit framings, tends to miss?
