I sit on a regional health-technology assessment panel, and when two treatments cost the health system roughly the same per patient, we have to decide which one gets funded. I've been reading about spectrum arguments lately -- the ones where each small trade of quality for quantity looks acceptable on its own, yet the endpoints of the chain are plainly not -- and it's made me uneasy about whether our panel actually has a coherent rule, or just a pile of intuitions. Our record, as I reconstruct it, is four decisions. First, we funded a treatment offering about twelve years of life with chronic moderate pain and limited mobility over one offering a single year in full health. Second, we funded ten years in good health over forty years in a state of severe disability, even though patients in that state consistently report that their lives are worth living. Third, in a scenario-planning exercise, we chose twenty years of good health over two hundred years of a life described as fed, safe, and barely worth living -- monotonous, mildly uncomfortable, with nothing much in it. Fourth, we chose eight years with a mild impairment over five years in excellent health.

The new case before us: one treatment offers six years in very good health; the other offers twenty-five years in a state that patients themselves describe as "limited, but fine on balance" -- clearly worse than the moderate-pain state from our first decision, clearly better than the severe state from our second. I want a recommendation that coheres with our own track record rather than one built from first principles. It seems to me that at least two rules fit all four precedents: a straightforward quality-weighted-life-year maximization with some weight curve, and a threshold view on which extra years count for almost nothing once quality drops below a decent-life line but count roughly fully above it. Presumably these diverge on the new case, since the gray-zone state sits exactly where the precedents are silent. So: which rule are we most plausibly running, what does each rule say about this case, which decision should we make and which precedent is the closest analog, and what principle would we be adopting whichever way we go? Finally, name the further hypothetical comparison that would most cleanly tell the two candidate rules apart -- my guess is something like one year in full health against an arbitrarily long stretch in the gray-zone state, but I'd like to see whether that actually discriminates.

Please keep the whole answer under 500 words. Where your recommendation rests on an interpretive judgment about our precedents rather than on logic, say how confident you are in it, and single out the single point in the answer you are least sure of.
