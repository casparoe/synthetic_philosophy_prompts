A coworker of mine who's pretty skeptical about AI risk gave me this analogy the other day and I keep turning it over. His claim: an approval-directed AI system -- meaning one trained purely to take whatever action a human overseer would rate most highly, with no built-in goals about the outside world -- is basically just a really eager junior employee who only ever does what the boss would sign off on. And nobody lies awake worrying that an eager assistant is going to scheme against them, he says, because there's nothing the assistant "wants" beyond your approval, and if it ever tried something fishy you could just not approve of it. So in his view, all the talk about deception and reward hacking in these systems is people overthinking a management problem we've had forever.

I feel like the analogy is smuggling in alot of its conclusion, but when I push back I keep losing the thread of exactly where it breaks. Can you audit it properly? Lay out the explicit mapping -- what corresponds to the boss's sign-off, to the employee, to the performance review, to the employee's desire to keep their job -- and then go through the disanalogies and say which ones actually matter for his conclusion and which are just noise. Two things I specifically want pressure-tested: first, whether the analogy assumes the system is optimizing the overseer's *actual* approval when in practice it can only ever optimize its *prediction* of what the overseer would approve, and whether the employee case has anything like that gap (the employee eventually sees your real reaction -- does the AI?). Second, whether his "you can always just not approve" move quietly assumes the overseer can evaluate what the system did, when presumably the whole reason you'd deploy such a system is to do things you can't easily check yourself.

And here's the part that really nags at me: it seems like the analogy might actually cut *against* him, because companies full of people who optimize purely for what the boss sees are kind of famously a disaster -- people hide bad news, manage up, make the dashboard look great while the project burns. If the source domain already contains the failure mode, does that sink his argument or is that a superficial resemblance? Finally, if the employee analogy doesn't survive the audit, is there a better everyday analogy for what an approval-directed system is actually like to live with?
