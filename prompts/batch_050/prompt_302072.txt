In the ecological economics and degrowth literature, writers regularly recover Aristotle's distinction in the Politics between oikonomia — the household's art of acquiring what is needed for living well, which has a natural limit set by its end — and chrematistics, the art of money-making through exchange, which recognizes no such limit. On the strength of this distinction they treat the modern economy as an Aristotelian cautionary tale: a household that has been handed over to the money-makers, pursuing unlimited accumulation where the household model prescribes sufficiency. Interestingly, the same household template gets run in the opposite direction in public-finance rhetoric, where governments are told to mind their budgets as a family minds its own. I would like an audit of the Aristotelian household analogy rather than an exposition of the texts.

Please proceed as follows. First, supply the correspondence table: what in a modern economy is supposed to play the role of the oikos, of the household manager, of natural acquisition, of the unnatural money-maker, and of the limit that nature sets on the one but not the other. Second, evaluate the disanalogies. Candidate items include: Aristotle's limit on acquisition is fixed by the household's telos, not by ecological scarcity, so the degrowth use may be borrowing his conclusion while quietly substituting a different ground for it; the oikos aims at self-sufficiency, whereas a modern economy is essentially a network of mutual dependence in which acquisition for exchange is not a perversion of the system but the system itself; and the ancient household runs on relations of command, including slavery, that no one proposes to scale up. Assess which of these — and any others you find — genuinely bear on the intended conclusion and which are incidental. Third, determine whether the normative payoff survives the audit: that unlimited accumulation confuses means with ends, and that economic activity should be subordinated to an external standard of enough. Does that conclusion stand once the disanalogies are granted, or was it Aristotle's teleological framework — which the degrowth literature does not share — carrying the weight the whole time? Finally, if the household analogy cannot carry it, say whether a better analogy for the same conclusion exists, or whether Aristotle's other materials — his treatment of exchange, commensurability, and money in the Nicomachean Ethics, or his complaint that usury is the mode of acquisition most contrary to nature — would serve the argument better, or whether he is simply the wrong authority for it.
