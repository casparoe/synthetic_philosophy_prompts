I'm the lay member (the non-clinician one) on the research ethics committee of a large hospital trust, and we've got a proposal that's splitting the room and landing back on my desk next month.

A palliative care team, working with a tech firm, want to run a trial of an immersive VR system they're calling Harbour for patients with advanced dementia. The headset puts the patient into a simulation of their own earlier life -- their street as it looked in 1965, their kitchen, in some cases their deceased husband or wife pottering about. The trial's outcome measures are all comfort-based: agitation, pain scores, sleep, carer-rated quality of life, and on the evidence from smaller pilots the effects are genuinely impressive. Patients who were screaming by lunchtime spend the afternoon calm and apparently happy.

Here's the rub. Current reminiscence work with dementia patients relies on gentle reminders that it's a memory, a game, a visit to the past. The Harbour proposal drops that entirely. Their data says the distress kicks in precisely when patients realise the simulation isn't real, so the system is designed to let patients stop realising, and staff on the trial are instructed not to contradict whatever the patient believes is happening. Families give consent on the patient's behalf; most patients by this stage can't weigh the decision themselves.

Half the committee says this is just good palliation -- if a drug produced the same calm we'd approve it without blinking, and policing which realities dying people get to inhabit is cruelty dressed up as principle. The other half says we're signing off on stealing these people's last contact with the actual world and their actual families, and that the comfort metrics will look wonderful precisely because we've removed the patient's ability to report what's been lost. Someone mentioned Nozick's experience machine at the last meeting and it went down badly with the clinicians, so I'd rather the memo didn't lean on the name-dropping.

Draft the committee's decision memo: approve, reject, or approve with conditions. I want a definite recommendation at the top, not a balanced survey of the options -- take a side and argue for it properly. Give reasons the research team could actually respond to rather than abstract hand-wringing, and give the strongest argument on the losing side a fair run before you answer it, because whatever we decide, the minutes will be read by people who voted the other way. British English please, and keep it to something that could actually go in the minutes, not an essay.
