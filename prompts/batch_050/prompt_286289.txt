The 'what is life' debate feels like it's been spinning its wheels for decades. Everyone knows the usual landmarks: the NASA working definition (a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution), the people who say viruses are a decisive counterexample or a decisive confirmation depending on which side they're on, Carol Cleland's argument that we can't define life until we have a theory of life, and Machery-style eliminativists who say 'life' just isn't a natural kind and biologists should stop trying to define it. As far as I can tell none of these positions have budged in twenty years.

Here's my hypothesis, which you should treat as a hypothesis: the debate isn't actually one debate. I think there are at least three different projects being run under the word 'life' -- astrobiologists who need a detection criterion for weird chemistry on Titan or wherever, theoretical biologists who want to know what the concept carves out in nature, and philosophers arguing about what the everyday English word means -- and each group keeps 'refuting' the others with cases that are only counterexamples to their own project. But maybe I'm wrong and the disagreement is deeper than that, e.g. a real clash of intuitions about whether evolution is essential to life or just one route to it.

So: diagnose the stall. Is this verbal, substantive, or different questions under one label? What do the actual positions commit to that makes them immune to each other's arguments? And given your diagnosis, what would progress even look like -- a better definition, a decision to split the concept into several, an empirical result about borderline cases, or should we expect it to just stay stuck? Please engage with the specific positions I mentioned, not a generic 'defining things is hard' take.
