I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I keep running into versions of Goodhart's law and specification gaming at work. I have strong gut verdicts about when a metric has been "Goodharted," but I suspect my verdicts don't add up to anything coherent. I'd like you to find the principle behind them — or tell me which verdicts I have to abandon.

Act as a philosopher of science with real expertise in measurement theory, plus working knowledge of machine learning and engineering management. Think carefully rather than pattern-matching to the standard slogans. Before committing to an answer, brainstorm at least three candidate principles or distinctions that might organize my cases, and give one line on how each would sort them. Then settle on the best option and defend it.

Here are my cases and verdicts:

Case 1 — Lines of code. A previous employer paid a bonus scaled to net lines of code added. Within two quarters, people were vendoring dependencies into the repo and writing sprawling duplicated code. My verdict: the metric was destroyed the moment it became a target, and paying for LOC is simply a mistake.

Case 2 — Coverage. At my current company, leadership mandated 90% line coverage. We hit it, partly by writing tests with no assertions. The coverage number is literally accurate — those lines do execute — but I consider the metric meaningless now, and I believe the codebase is less reliable than when coverage sat at 70% with honest tests.

Case 3 — MTTR. The same leadership set a target on mean time to restore service after incidents. Engineers wrote runbooks, improved rollback tooling, and built better dashboards. MTTR fell, and I genuinely believe incidents now resolve faster and better. My verdict: targeting this metric worked exactly as intended; nothing was corrupted.

Case 4 — The feed. At a prior company we optimized a recommendation feed for watch time. Early on, watch time tracked our quarterly user-satisfaction surveys closely. After two years of aggressive optimization, watch time was up 40% while its correlation with satisfaction had fallen to nearly zero, and satisfaction itself was declining. Nobody "gamed" anything; the model just did its job. My verdict: optimization pressure alone broke the proxy, with no adversary required.

Case 5 — The bug leaderboard. A team ranked engineers by bugs closed per sprint. Trivial tickets got snapped up immediately; a serious intermittent data-corruption bug sat unowned for months because it was expensive to close. My verdict: the count was still perfectly accurate as a count of closed bugs. What failed was the inference from the count to engineering contribution. I blame whoever designed the incentive, not the metric and not the engineers.

Case 6 — The release-gate benchmark. We run a performance regression suite only at release time. Its results are never tied to anyone's review or bonus, nobody tunes to it, and I trust it as a genuine signal of system health. My verdict: the same suite would stop being informative within a year if promotion depended on it.

Note where I think the tensions are: Cases 3 and 5 both involve people optimizing exactly the number they're graded on, yet I call one a success and the other a failure. Cases 2 and 3 both involve metrics that remain literally accurate, yet I call one meaningless and the other healthy. Case 4 involves no adversary at all, but I count it as Goodhart anyway — if your principle requires an agent exploiting the measure, it will rule this case out, and I want to know whether I should let it.

My own tentative principle is this: a metric is a good target if and only if improving it can never make the true goal worse. I suspect this is too strong — it might condemn Case 3, since one could improve MTTR by prematurely closing tickets — but I can't locate the repair.

Deliverables:

1. Your brainstorming pass: the candidate principles, each with its verdicts on my six cases.
2. Your final principle (or small set of principles), stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new metric at work tomorrow. For each clause, name the case or cases that motivate it.
3. An honest accounting of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and which single verdict you'd advise me to drop if forced.
4. Your principle's predictions for three cases I haven't listed: (a) metrics rotated quarterly so no one can optimize any single one for long; (b) a metric kept secret from the engineers and used only by leadership; (c) a composite score averaging a gameable metric with a robust one.
5. A verdict on my tentative principle and, if one exists, a repaired version of it.
