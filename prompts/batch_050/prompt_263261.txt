I work as a data engineer, and a large part of my job is maintaining enterprise knowledge graphs: designing schemas, choosing entity types, deciding when two records from different source systems denote the same thing, and writing the mapping layers that let legacy databases and new ones be queried together. I'd like you to use that vocabulary -- schemas, entity resolution, keys, queries, migrations, mappings -- to restate the central dispute in contemporary metaontology, and then to tell me what does not survive the translation.

Here is the case that motivates the question. My company has merged two systems. One schema contains a type CUSTOMER, individuated by household; the other contains ACCOUNT, individuated by billing relationship, and the two cross-cut: one customer can hold several accounts, several customers can share one account. When we built the merged graph we had to choose whether to keep both types with a mapping between them, collapse one into the other, or introduce a third type. My colleagues disagreed about which choice was "correct," and after an hour it became clear that nobody could say what would even count as getting it wrong, as opposed to merely inconvenient. It struck me that this is eerily similar to what philosophers call a merely verbal dispute, and possibly to the debate over quantifier variance.

So, the task. First, restate the following positions in my working vocabulary, one by one, as faithfully as you can: (1) the quantifier-variance view associated with Eli Hirsch, on which disputants can both be right because their quantifiers mean different things; (2) the heavyweight realism associated with Ted Sider, on which some candidate meanings carve nature at its joints better than others, so the dispute can be substantive after all; (3) the Carnapian distinction between internal and external questions, and the contemporary "easy ontology" it inspired; and (4) the general diagnostic notion of a merely verbal dispute and the methods proposed for detecting and dissolving one. For each position, say what translates cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no expression in the schema-engineering vocabulary at all.

Second, the residue report, which is the part I care most about. In my world, every choice is a design choice, justified by query patterns, downstream consumers, and maintenance cost; there is no such thing as the schema the world itself prefers. Does that mean my vocabulary simply lacks the expressive resources for Sider's claim that reality has a structure that one ontology can fit better than another -- or does the fact that his position has no engineering counterpart suggest that it trades on an equivocation between "the most useful representation" and "the representation that is true"? Take a stand on which diagnosis you find more plausible and why.

Third, apply the translation in the other direction. Take the classic metaontological dispute about composite objects -- whether there "really are" tables and chairs, or only simples arranged tablewise -- and restate it as a dispute about whether a knowledge graph should materialize aggregate entities or derive them at query time. Then tell me whether that restatement dissolves the philosophical dispute, sharpens it, or quietly changes the subject.

Do not include citations or a reading list; refer to positions and arguments by name and I will locate the sources myself.
