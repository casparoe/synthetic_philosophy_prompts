So I've been going in circles on this for months: is there actually a *reason* to vote, morally I mean, and if there is, what kind of reason is it? I've never studied any of this, I've just been thinking about it, and I keep running into people (including me) arguing by pointing at one case and acting like that's the whole thing. "Your one vote will never swing an election, so voting is irrational." "People died for the right to vote, so you have to." "Voting for the lesser evil is still voting for evil." Everyone has one favorite example and no standard for what a good answer would even look like.

So before I evaluate any actual position, I want the spec. What would a *theory of how you ought to vote* have to get right to count as adequate? Give me the requirements list -- the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it can't collapse, the things it has to be consistent with -- with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and say which requirements you'd call non-negotiable and which are just one camp's hobbyhorse.

Some raw material from my own thinking, so you know what cases I want the theory to handle:

- The math case: in a big election my vote essentially never changes the outcome. Any theory where the *only* point of voting is causing the winner seems to imply voting is pointless, and that feels like it proves too much.
- The safe-seat case: same as above but worse. If a theory says voting only matters in swing states, does it also say people in safe seats do nothing wrong by staying home? Is that a bullet it should bite or a failure?
- The third-party case: someone votes for a candidate who can't win because they want to send a message, or register a protest. Some people call this throwing your vote away, others say it's the most honest use of it. A theory should be able to say *something* about this other than "depends."
- The lesser evil case: both viable candidates are bad, one is worse. Is refusing to pick the less-bad one morally like staying home, like picking the worse one, or like neither?
- The ignorance case: does it matter *why* you're voting? If I vote for the good candidate because I liked his smile, did I do the same thing as someone who researched carefully? Does a theory of voting owe me anything about *how* to form my vote, or only about whether to cast one?
- The weather case: whether I vote can depend on whether it's raining, and that seems fine practically but weird morally. Like if something is a duty, can it really be that sensitive to small costs? Does a theory have to explain why the duty to vote (if there is one) seems to vanish when voting gets expensive or dangerous, or is that just human weakness and not the theory's problem?
- One distinction I keep tripping over: there's a difference between "what you should do" and "what you can be blamed for not doing," and I think half the arguments I see slide between them.

Two more things I want, beyond the list itself. First, flag any pair of requirements you think *can't be jointly satisfied* -- my hunch is that something like "the theory must make individual voting consequential enough to be worth doing" and "the theory must respect the math that one vote never decides anything" can't both hold, and if that's right then the interesting result is in the list itself, not in any theory. Second, at the end, briefly: how would the obvious contender families do against the list -- something like voting-as-duty, voting-as-expression, and voting-as-expected-impact? Keep that part short, the criteria are what I actually care about, and if I'm missing a whole contender family tell me rather than stuffing it into one of those three.
