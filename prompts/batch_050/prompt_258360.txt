I TA for a grad seminar on philosophy of AI (mixed phil + CS students, about 14 people) and we're about to do the interpretability unit. Everyone has skimmed the standard stuff on probing classifiers and feature visualisation, and discussion keeps going flat: half the room says obviously these networks have representations, look what the probes decode, the other half says the probes are just reading tea leaves. Nobody actually moves an inch.

I want a 90-minute in-class activity, ideally no laptops, that makes them feel the crux before we name it: the gap between information being decodable from a system's internal states and the system actually using that information to do what it does. My vague idea: one team secretly designs some hidden mechanism, the other team probes it under a limited query budget and reports what "features" they found, and then there's a round where they try to change the mechanism's behaviour by intervening on their discovered feature and it just... doesn't work, because the thing they decoded was never doing any causal work. I've heard you can often decode features even from a randomised network's activations, and I'd love for something like that to happen organically in the room so the probers feel had.

Give me: roles and rules, materials, a scoring scheme that doesn't need real money, how to stop the designer team from making their mechanism deliberately un-probeable (or so simple it's trivial), a debrief that maps each round onto the realism vs anti-realism question about representations, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which part of the real debate the game can't reproduce (I assume scale, and that real networks aren't adversarially designed by an opponent). And don't let them hear the word "realism" untill the debrief.
