I'm drafting a novel set in the Warring States period, and my protagonist is a wandering persuader in the mould of Gongsun Long -- the white horse chap. There's a scene where he wins a debate at court by arguing that a white horse is not a horse, and I need the argument to be genuinely slippery, not just word games, because the clever minister who loses has to be beaten fairly. So I built him a version and now I'm worried it proves far too much. It goes:

(1) "Horse" names a shape; "white" names a colour; and naming a colour is not naming a shape.
(2) So "white horse" does not name what "horse" names.
(3) If the king requisitions "a horse," a black horse or a yellow one will do; if he requisitions "a white horse," they won't.
(4) Two names that can't be swapped for each other in a demand don't name the same thing.
(5) So a white horse is not a horse.
(6) But the same pattern runs everywhere: if the king requisitions "a woman," a tall woman will do; if he requisitions "a tall woman," a short one won't. So a tall woman is not a woman.
(7) Worse, a tired horse won't do where a rested one is required, so a tired horse is not a horse -- which means nothing is what it is at any moment it happens to have some property.
(8) So no qualified description ever picks out the thing it describes, and strictly speaking language can't say what anything is.

Now, for plot reasons I actually want (5) to survive in some interesting form -- the persuader should be onto something real about names and kinds, because the minister's comeback in the next chapter only works if there was a genuine insight buried in the trick. But (7) and (8) are obviously absurd, and I can't find the step where the slide happens. Is it (4)? It looked so innocuous when I wrote it. Or is (1) already doing something sneaky by treating "names a colour" and "names a shape" as exclusive? Tell me which premise or step to cut, why it looked fine, and what it costs -- and specifically whether there's a way to keep a defensible version of "a white horse is not a horse" whilst stopping the rot before it eats all predication whatsoever. If it turns out you can only save the white horse by giving up on compound names entirely, I'd like to know that too, because then my persuader is a fraud and I have a different book on my hands.
