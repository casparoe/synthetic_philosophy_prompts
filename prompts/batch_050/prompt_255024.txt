ok here's the situation. i'm writing a paper on theories of humour and my reading list keeps serving up the same four families:

1. superiority (hobbes and co.) -- laughter as a little burst of self-satisfaction at someone else's expense, or at our own past stupidity
2. incongruity (kant, schopenhauer) -- humour as the perception of something that violates our expectations or categories
3. relief (spencer, freud) -- laughter as the discharge of pent-up nervous or psychic energy
4. benign violation (mcgraw & warren) -- humour requires something to be a violation of how things ought to be AND simultaneously appraised as benign

the problem is that benign violation already swallows most of incongruity-plus-safety, and the other three are old enough that every obvious variation on them has been tried. i need a fifth theory with genuinely different explanatory machinery -- an account of what laughter is for, or what it tracks, that isn't a tweak, special case, or hybrid of the four above. i also already know about play theories and the 'false alarm' evolutionary accounts, so don't just hand me one of those unless you rework it beyond recognition.

then, to prove the theory earns its keep, do two things:

(a) construct an actual joke (it at least has to be trying to be funny) that your theory explains well and that at least two of the four classical theories explain badly or not at all. walk me through how each of the four handles your joke, so i can see the seperation myself and not just take your word for it.

(b) give me one prediction where your theory and benign violation theory come apart -- some observable difference in what people would or wouldn't find funny -- so the two could in principle be tested against each other.

and if it turns out you can't construct a joke that seperates your theory from the classics, say so honestly and tell me what that implies about whether your theory is doing any independent work at all.
