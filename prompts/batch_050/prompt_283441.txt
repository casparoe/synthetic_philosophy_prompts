I'm a graduate student in history preparing for a methods seminar on causation and culpability in political history, and I've picked the July Crisis as my case. Having worked through the Fischer controversy and some of the later literature, I've come to accept a moderately strong version of the Fischer thesis: that the German leadership, while perhaps not executing a long-premeditated plan for war, consciously exploited the Sarajevo assassination to force a continental conflict on terms it judged favorable, knowing and accepting the substantial risk that this would bring in Britain and turn into a general European war -- and that this makes Imperial Germany primarily, though not solely, responsible for the outbreak of the First World War in a sense that goes beyond what can be said of Russia, France, or Austria-Hungary. My advisor pushed back, arguing that this verdict rests on a handful of documents (the December 1912 war council, the blank cheque, the September program) whose interpretation is genuinely contested, and that a "sleepwalkers"-style account distributes responsibility so evenly that singling out Germany becomes a historiographical choice rather than a finding.

So: what would count against my position? I don't want a summary of the debate -- I want a map of its exposure. Which specific kinds of evidence, if they turned up or were reinterpreted convincingly, would force me to abandon or substantially weaken the thesis? Please consider concrete hypotheticals: for instance, suppose an authenticated cache of Bethmann Hollweg's private papers surfaced showing he sincerely believed throughout July 1914 that Russia would not intervene for Serbia and that he actively sought to localize the conflict -- would that sink the thesis, or would a sophisticated Fischerite reply that miscalculation about others' reactions is irrelevant to the core charge of deliberately accepting the risk of general war? Distinguish between (a) evidence that would be fatal to the strong thesis but leave a weaker "primary responsibility" verdict intact, (b) evidence that would be merely awkward, and (c) criticisms that attack the framing itself -- for example, the claim that "national responsibility" is a confused category when applied to a multi-actor crisis with interlocking mobilization schedules, so that the thesis fails not on evidence but on coherence. On (c), I'd like you to engage seriously with what it could even mean to hold a nation, as opposed to particular statesmen, responsible for a war, and whether the Fischer debate was ever really about that question or about something narrower.

Finally, please flag where your own assessment is most likely to be wrong: which argument you are least sure you've weighted correctly, and what consideration or discovery would change your verdict about how exposed the Fischer thesis actually is. If you conclude that the thesis has been qualified over the decades into something hard to falsify -- always able to retreat from "planned war" to "accepted risk" to "structural pressure" -- say so plainly, and tell me whether that kind of insulation is a defect in a historiographical position or simply what happens when a thesis absorbs its critics' points.
