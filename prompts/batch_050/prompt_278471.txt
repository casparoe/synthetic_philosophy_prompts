I spent forty years as a research biochemist before retiring, and in the last few I have been reading philosophy, including the ethics of clinical research. I never ran a trial myself, though I sat for several years on our university's research ethics committee, and I find I still do not have a settled view of what makes randomization ethically permissible. The literature keeps proposing standards — equipoise in various formulations, appeal to consent, net-risk frameworks, comparisons to the risks of ordinary clinical practice — and the proponents argue past one another because, so far as I can tell, nobody states in advance what such a standard is supposed to accomplish.

Before I read any further accounts, I would like you to write the specification: the desiderata that any adequate ethical justification of randomized controlled trials must satisfy, with the consideration or case behind each one. The following strike me as candidate requirements, but treat this as a starting point rather than the list itself — add what I am missing and tell me which of my candidates are actually prejudices of one camp:

- It must explain when randomizing a patient is consistent with a physician's duty to offer that patient the best available care, since a trial asks the physician to let chance, not judgment, determine treatment.
- It must handle the fact that evidence accumulates during a trial, so that whatever uncertainty justified randomization at the outset may be gone before the trial ends; an account that freezes the analysis at the start date seems inadequate, but so does one that makes every mid-course trend grounds for stopping.
- It must say something about placebo controls when an effective standard treatment already exists, since that situation is where the demand for equipoise bites hardest.
- It must cover trials in which no individual investigator is personally uncertain — some are convinced arm A is superior — but the expert community as a whole is genuinely divided, and explain whether individual-level or community-level uncertainty is what matters, and why.
- It must explain why informed consent, however genuine, is not by itself sufficient; a patient can consent to many things a trial should still not do.
- It must be workable across contexts where its assumptions strain: oncology trials with two active arms, emergency research with deferred consent, trials in low-income settings where the relevant standard of care is disputed.

For each desideratum, state the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference, and mark it as non-negotiable or as the shibboleth of a particular school. Two things I especially want: first, an explicit statement of which subsets of these desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — for instance, whether a full-blooded duty of personal care and the social value of randomization can both be honored without remainder — since an inconsistent specification would itself be a result; second, an indication of which single requirement eliminates the most positions currently on the table. If some desiderata trade off against one another, say what the trade-offs are and what evidence or argument would move the weights. Conclude briefly with how the main contenders you know of fare against the list, but keep that short; the criteria are the deliverable, not the scorecard, and I will do my own scoring as I read.
