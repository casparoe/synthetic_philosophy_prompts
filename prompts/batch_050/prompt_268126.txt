I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a long piece about what two decades of real-world drug-policy experiments have actually taught us. For the reporting, I spent time with two reformers who agree on the diagnosis but not the destination. The first argues that the evidence supports decriminalizing possession of all drugs, paired with serious treatment capacity and harm-reduction services, but that creating a legal commercial supply of addictive substances would replay the tobacco and alcohol industries' history of marketing-driven harm. The second argues that decriminalization alone leaves the illicit supply chain -- the actual source of overdose deaths, adulterated product, and organized-crime violence -- fully intact, and that regulated legalization, not merely for cannabis, is the only coherent endpoint. Both cite the same touchstones: Portugal's reform of 2001, the legal cannabis markets in Uruguay, Canada, and various US states, and the brief, troubled life of Oregon's decriminalization experiment. Both claim the evidence is on their side, and both, frankly, talk past each other.

Rather than adjudicate between them in print, I want to publish the document they should have produced together: the joint statement of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Please draft it with the following structure. First, a numbered list of claims both parties could sign -- I would expect candidates to include that criminal penalties for personal possession do not measurably reduce use, that prohibition raises prices and rewards violence, that Portugal's outcomes depended on treatment infrastructure and cannot be credited to decriminalization alone, and that commercial cannabis legalization increased use intensity and spawned aggressive industry lobbying -- but include only what both sides would genuinely accept, and say if any of these would not survive as agreed claims. Second, a table of residual disagreements, each formulated in wording that the side holding the view would itself endorse. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. I suspect one is whether regulation can survive the profit motive -- that is, whether a state-monopoly or public-health model of supply can resist commercial capture the way alcohol and tobacco regulation did not -- and another is whether highly dependence-forming substances such as fentanyl admit of any legal supply regime compatible with how addicted people actually behave; test my suspicions and propose your own. Fourth, for each crux, the case, comparison, or natural experiment both parties would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. If part of their dispute turns out to be verbal -- over what "legalization" or "decriminalization" properly means, for instance -- identify which part and dissolve it explicitly.
