Longtime lurker on the rationalist blogs here, and I'm drafting a series of posts trying to sort out my own position on the basilisk-style arguments once and for all. Before I can argue about whether anyone should ever comply with threats from a hypothetical future superintelligence, I want to nail down what a decision theory would actually have to deliver to count as adequate for that job — since as far as I can tell the whole fight is really about what we're allowed to demand of a theory, not about the scenario itself. For context: the scenario is that a future powerful agent might have an incentive to (credibly commit to) simulating and punishing people who failed to help bring it into existence, purely because us knowing about that incentive now changes our behavior — no backwards causation, just your model of its incentives and its model of you.

So write me the desiderata list: what a decision theory has to do, which cases it has to get right, which distinctions it must respect — with the motivating case behind each requirement, and each one marked as non-negotiable versus a preference of one camp. The cases I think belong on the list, since whatever principle handles the basilisk had better handle its neighbors too: Newcomb's problem with a near-perfect predictor (take one box or two?); Parfit's hitchhiker (the driver only rescued you from the desert because he predicted you'd pay him $100 at the next gas station, and now you're at the gas station and paying benefits only him); the counterfactual mugging (a predictor flipped a coin, and would have given you $1,000,000 had it come up heads, but only on the condition that you'd pay $100 in the tails world — it came up tails, and he's here to collect); ordinary blackmail by someone you believe can read you well; and acausal trade with agents in distant corners of the universe you'll never causally interact with. Then the part I care most about: which subsets of these requirements are jointly unsatisfiable. My strong suspicion is that "never let extortion pay off against you" and "take the action with the best expected consequences even when the correlation between your action and the outcome is purely logical rather than causal" are on a collision course, and that picking between them is basically the entire basilisk dispute — if the list itself encodes that tension, say so explicitly. Also flag which single requirement knocks out the most contenders.

Then, briefly, since this part matters less to me, score the usual suspects against the list: causal decision theory, evidential decision theory, and the functional/timeless cluster of views. Please don't just regurgitate the standard forum debate at me — I want the spec first, verdicts second. Answer in American English, and keep it casual, this is going on my blog not in a journal.
