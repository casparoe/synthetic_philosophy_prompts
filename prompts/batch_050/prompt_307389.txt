So our lab is throwing a party next month because our protein-design model finally beat the benchmark people said was a decade away, and since I wrote the first janky prototype as a grad student I've been voluntold to give a five-minute speech. The audience is awkwardly split: one half is the ML engineers and comp bio folks who trained the thing, and the other half is investors, the dean, and various spouses who mostly know the model from a magazine piece. Here's the thing I'm stuck on. The usual move at these events is to credit the team — "your brilliance made this happen" — but I was complaining about this with one of the postdocs and we both felt that framing is somehow dishonest. Nobody in that room, me included, actually understands how the model does what it does. The competence emerged out of trillions of tiny adjustments by an optimization process that doesn't know what a protein is. It reminded me of Dennett's line about Darwin: before Darwin everyone assumed comprehension has to come before competence — you need a mind to make mind-like things — and natural selection flipped that, building eyes and immune systems with zero comprehension involved. And arguably Turing ran the same inversion on thinking itself: intelligence built bottom-up out of mindless symbol-shuffling.

So write the speech with that idea doing the real structural work: we're celebrating an achievement nobody fully understands, produced by a process that understands nothing, and that's the deep pattern rather than some embarrassing footnote. It should genuinely wrestle with who or what can honestly be praised — the team deserves credit, but for what exactly, building the comprehension or building the conditions under which competence could show up on its own? And it should gently head off the investors' favorite question, "ok but how does it KNOW that," without being condescending. It has to land for both halves of the room: no wincing from the engineers at oversimplifications, and the spouses should get the core idea through concrete examples rather than jargon. Warm, a little funny, five minutes.

One more thing: since the philosophical framing is the load-bearing part, add a short note after the speech saying how confident you are in the reading of the Darwin/Turing inversion you're relying on, and single out the one point in the speech where you stretched or oversimplified the idea most — basically the spot where an actual philosopher of mind in the audience would raise their hand.
