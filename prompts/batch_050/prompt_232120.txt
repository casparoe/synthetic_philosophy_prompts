I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm writing a companion piece to a long feature on emergence — why no water molecule is wet, why a traffic jam can persist while every car in it changes, why economics isn't just applied psychology. Rather than a glossary of positions, I want to give readers a "where do you actually stand?" instrument, and I'd like you to build it.

The historical anchor: in the chapter "Of the Composition of Causes" in Book III of A System of Logic, John Stuart Mill distinguishes two ways causes can combine — a mechanical mode, in which the joint effect is the sum of the separate effects (his paradigm is the composition of forces in dynamics), and a chemical mode, in which the combined effect is heterogeneous to the separate effects and can only be learned from experience with the combination itself (his paradigms are chemical reactions and, at the limit, living bodies). Open with a two-or-three-sentence paraphrase of that distinction — do not assume readers have met it — and let it supply the tree's root question: when a whole behaves in ways its parts do not, is that a fact about the world, or only about our descriptions of it?

From there, write a decision tree of at most ten branch nodes. Each node must be phrased as a question a reader can answer without knowing any labels — for instance, whether the surprising macro-behavior would be predictable in principle from a complete micro-description plus unlimited computation; whether higher-level causal talk explains or predicts anything the full micro-story misses; whether the parts would keep the properties they have inside the whole if you took them out. Wherever possible, hang the question on a case a general reader already knows: the liquidity of water, temperature, Conway's Game of Life, flocking, consciousness.

The leaves should include at least: eliminative reductionism (the macro-facts are dispensable bookkeeping); mechanistic reduction, where wholes are explained by organized parts and nothing is left over; weak or epistemic emergence, where the macro-pattern is real but only surprising-to-us; strong or ontological emergence with genuine downward causation; and Mill's own position, which deserves its own leaf rather than being folded into the others. Flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot really separate, and for any combination of answers nobody occupies, add one line diagnosing why — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
