I chair the risk commitee for a mid-sized public pension fund, and we have to choose between two catastrophe-protection plans at next month’s meeting. Plan A covers a fairly familiar set of regional disasters. The vendor’s model says there is a 3% annual chance that losses exceed the plan’s limit, and that estimate rests on decades of reasonably stable claims data plus several successful out-of-sample tests. Plan B is cheaper and covers several newer compound risks, such as a cyber outage coinciding with a climate event, but nobody can give a comparably stable probability. Its central estimate is a 2% annual exceedance risk, although defensible model choices put the figure anywhere from 0.5% to 7%.

One trustee says the Ellsberg paradox shows that preferring Plan A merely because its probability is better pinned down is a psychological bias, so Plan B should win on expected value. Another thinks uncertainty about the probability is itself a genuine reason to demand a premium, and worries that B’s attractive central estimate is basically model guesswork. Draft the committee’s decision memo recommending A, B, or approval of one subject to conditions. The memo should state a clear bottom line, explain whether ambiguity aversion is necessarily irrational, address whether the unknown probability should simply be treated as 50/50 or by the worst case in the range, and distinguish sensible robustness from an unthinking preference for familiar risks. Please include the strongest argument for the option you reject and any practical safeguards—model disclosure, scenario testing, review triggers, or limits on exposure—that would make your recommendation defensible.
