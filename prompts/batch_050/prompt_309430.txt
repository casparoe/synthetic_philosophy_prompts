I retired a couple of years ago after forty years in analytical chemistry, and lately I've been reading philosophy, which I enjoy even though it makes my head hurt. I've realised I have a homemade test for whether I really know something, carried straight over from how we judged instruments in the lab. We never trusted a balance just because it read right on the day; we trusted it if, when the quantity was different, the reading would have been different. A balance that says 5.02 g no matter what's on the pan is useless even on days it happens to be right. So my test for a belief is: if it were false, would I still believe it? If yes, then I don't actually know it.

The test does alot of good work. It says I don't know my lottery ticket will lose, since if it won I'd still believe it lost, which matches my gut. It says the eyewitness who glimpsed the intruder for two seconds in the dark doesn't know who it was, but the neighbour who chatted with him for an hour in daylight does. Fine so far.

But it breaks in two places. First, it tells me I don't know that I'm not a brain in a jar being fed experiences, because if I were, I'd still believe I wasnt. Maybe I can swallow that. But I do seem to know I'm sitting in my kitchen, and sitting in my kitchen rules out the jar, so the test apparently forces me to deny that knowing something lets you know what obviously follows from it, and that feels like giving up too much. Second, the test goes completely limp for mathematics. If 57 x 43 weren't 2451, would I still believe it was? The question doesn't even make sense, it couldn't equal anything else. Yet surely I can know it's 2451, I checked it twice.

So: does my test exist in the literature under some name, and if what I've described isn't exactly the named thing, where does mine diverge from the standard version? More importantly, are these two breakages known problems with standard fixes, or are they why the whole style of test got abandoned? If there's a fix, I want to know what it does to the lottery and eyewitness cases, where the unfixed test seemed to shine.
