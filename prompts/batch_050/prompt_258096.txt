I chair my department's professional conduct committee, and we've been handed a dispute that has split the four of us down the middle. At a lab meeting, a postdoc, Dr. Reyes, questioned a graduate student's presentation by asking, in sequence: "When you reran the analysis after excluding the contaminated samples, did the effect hold up?" and then "Have you told your co-authors which batches were affected?" The student had never disclosed any contaminated samples; there was, on the current record, no contamination. Nobody in the room objected at the time, and the meeting moved on. Three weeks later the student's advisor filed a complaint, arguing that Reyes had effectively accused the student of mishandling data in front of the whole lab. Reyes's defense is that she never asserted anything -- she asked questions, and questions can't be true or false, so she cannot have violated our code's prohibition on "false or reckless representations about a colleague's research." When I described the case to a linguist friend over dinner, she said the fight was really about whether presuppositions that go unchallenged get silently added to the conversational record, and whether Reyes was exploiting that mechanism. I found that framing clarifying, but it doesn't tell us how to rule.

The committee is stuck on three questions, and I'd like the opinion to address each in turn. First: does conveying a damaging proposition through the background assumptions of one's questions, rather than through outright assertion, count as "making a representation" under a conduct code that was surely drafted with assertions in mind? Second: does the room's silence matter? One committee member argues that once a presupposition passes unchallenged, it becomes part of what the group is treating as settled, so Reyes did put contamination on the record; another replies that the student bore the burden of objecting in the moment, and that letting a presupposition slide isn't the speaker's doing alone. Third, looking forward: what rule should we adopt so that future cases don't hinge on vibes -- for instance, should it matter whether the challenged presupposition is retracted as soon as someone flags it, or whether the speaker had independent grounds for suspecting contamination?

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate before our vote: roughly 900-1200 words, recommending a concrete disposition of the complaint (uphold, dismiss, or uphold with a remedy short of formal sanction), plus a short statement of the general policy the department should adopt. The opinion should engage seriously with the strongest version of Reyes's defense -- that holding speakers liable for unasserted presuppositions would chill ordinary scientific questioning, which runs on provisional assumptions -- rather than dismissing it as a technicality. Where the reasoning calls for it, feel free to construct small hypothetical variations on the facts (e.g., a version where someone objects mid-meeting, or where the contamination turns out to be real) to show where the proposed rule draws its lines.
