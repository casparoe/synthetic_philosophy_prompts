A friend who teaches medical ethics gave me this summary of Taurek's argument about whether numbers should count in rescue cases:

'Taurek imagines that you have a limited supply of a drug and must choose between saving five strangers on one side and one stranger on the other, where no one stands in any special relation to you. His central thought is that losses cannot be added across persons: five people each losing their life is not anyone suffering a loss five times as great as the one person's loss, so the five have no greater aggregate claim than the one. He therefore concludes that it would be wrong simply to save the five, and that the only fair procedure is to toss a coin, giving each person an equal chance of survival. On his view, numbers are morally irrelevant in any distribution of scarce goods. Since later philosophers have pointed out that Taurek cannot explain why we should save the greater number when the cost on each side is equal, his argument is generally regarded as having failed. The practical upshot is that public bodies which allocate scarce medical resources according to how many lives they expect to save are acting impermissibly.'

Something in this makes me uneasy, although I can't put my finger on it. I'd heard Taurek's position described as the weaker claim that one is not required to save the greater number, not that doing so would be wrong, and I'd also thought the coin toss was meant as an illustration of giving each person an equal claim rather than as a compulsory decision procedure. But my friend is a professional, and I don't want to rely on half-remembered second-hand accounts.

Is this a fair summary of Taurek's position? Please distinguish what is accurate, what is a common simplification or overstatement, and what a defender of Taurek would flatly reject. In particular, I'd like to know whether the concluding claim about public resource allocation follows from anything Taurek actually argued, or whether it depends on the stronger misreadings embedded earlier in the summary.
