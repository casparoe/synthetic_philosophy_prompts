Next spring I'm co-teaching a graduate seminar on AI safety, and for the unit on corrigibility I want to hand students a sorting instrument rather than another lecture: a decision tree that walks them from an initial stance on the shutdown problem to a named position in the debate. My students mostly come from computer science, have absorbed the popular version of instrumental convergence, and will otherwise just say "agents resist shutdown, alignment fixes it" without noticing that the debate splits several ways before that. The tree should start at the claim that a capable, long-horizon, goal-directed system will, for a wide range of objectives, have incentives to prevent or resist being switched off, and its first branch should sort those who accept this as a distinct problem from those who take shutdown resistance to be a mere symptom of misalignment with no special theory of its own. From there, the branches should be driven by where the fix is supposed to live: inside the utility function itself (making the agent indifferent to the button's being pressed), inside the agent's epistemic state (deference grounded in genuine uncertainty about what the human wants), or outside the goal-directed frame entirely (designs that avoid long-horizon maximization, e.g. myopic, act-based, or impact-limited agents), plus a pessimist leaf for the view that corrigibility is anti-natural in optimizing systems so the only remedy is declining to build them. I'd like six to nine branch nodes, each phrased as a question a student could answer without knowing which literature it leads to, and roughly seven to nine leaves.

Two diagnostic cases must appear as branch tests, because in my experience they split the room. First: the agent is offered a deal in which it is paid extra utility to press its own off-switch, versus a deal in which it is paid to leave the button alone — this separates genuine indifference constructions from designs that merely tolerate shutdown when convenient. Second: the agent comes to know, with certainty, that its operator is about to press the button by mistake (say the operator is drunk, or has confused two consoles) — this separates deference views that survive confident belief from those that collapse into "defer only while unsure," and it should force the value-uncertainty leaf to confront what happens as confidence grows. Where the tree cannot separate two positions (I suspect utility indifference and indifference-via-uncertainty may land on the same answers despite being different mechanisms, and that tripwire or containment engineering may be indistinguishable from myopia at the level of these questions), flag that explicitly rather than inventing a branch. Build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they permit — and only then locate the actual positions in that space, noting any unoccupied combination and whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. Finally, since this is a handout I will defend in front of colleagues: end with a short note on the tree's own fragility — which argument or result would collapse a branch you relied on (for instance, a demonstration that no utility-level fix can avoid manipulation incentives, forcing everything through the epistemic branch), and which branch you are least confident is a real distinction.
