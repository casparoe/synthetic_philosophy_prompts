Over the past couple of years, mostly through reading and some long arguments with a friend who works in archaeology, I've become a convert to what I'll call the reorganization thesis about societal collapse: that most of the canonical cases -- the end of the Late Bronze Age palace economies around the eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya abandonment of the southern lowland cities, the standard ecocide narrative about Rapa Nui -- are better described as transformations, redistributions, or changes of scale than as failures or disappearances. On this view, populations mostly moved, adapted, or persisted in less monumental forms; the "collapse" is largely an artifact of what leaves big impressive ruins and written records, namely palaces, temples, and elite literate culture, so that we mistake the death of a particular political and aesthetic order for the death of a society. My friend pushes back constantly, and I've started to worry that the thesis has quietly become unfalsifiable in my hands: whenever a case looks like a genuine catastrophe, I find myself saying that the catastrophe is still a "reorganization," just a painful one.

So here is what I'd like: a map of the reorganization thesis's exposure. What observations, cases, or lines of evidence would genuinely count against it -- not the obvious move of pointing at a depopulation event, but subtler things: what would we have to show about the fate of the non-elite population, about biological and cultural continuity, about recovery times, about whether the successor arrangements preserve anything of the prior society, for the "mere transformation" reading to fail? Are there principled criteria one could state in advance for distinguishing a collapse from a reorganization -- something like thresholds of demographic loss, of functional or institutional discontinuity, of loss of accumulated knowledge or technology -- and does the thesis survive if we apply any of the standard candidate criteria consistently across the classic cases? Conversely, is there a real risk that the thesis is protected by vagueness: that any outcome short of total extinction gets labeled transformation, so that it risks nothing at all?

Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say whether a committed defender of the reorganization thesis would themselves concede it as counting against the view or dismiss it as question-begging. I'd also like to know which single case is the most dangerous for the thesis -- the one where the "they just changed" reading is hardest to sustain -- and what the strongest version of that case looks like, steelmanned rather than caricatured. If your honest verdict is that the thesis is well insulated and that this insulation is a problem rather than a virtue, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a defeater to make me feel better.
