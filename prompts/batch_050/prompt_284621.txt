I am drafting the anti-mechanist chapter of my dissertation and would like a consistency audit of the position I defend before I commit it to print. The target is the argument of J. R. Lucas's "Minds, Machines and Gödel" (Philosophy, 1961), which concludes from the incompleteness theorems that mechanism is false. Lucas writes:

"Gödel's theorem must apply to cybernetical machines, because it is of the essence of being a machine, that it should be a concrete instantiation of a formal system. It follows that given any machine which is consistent and capable of doing simple arithmetic, there is a formula which it is incapable of producing as being true ... but which we can see to be true. It follows that no machine can be a complete or adequate model of the mind, that minds are essentially different from machines."

Against this, I hold all of the following:

(1) Computationalism: idealizing away from mortality and memory limits, a human being's arithmetical competence is exhaustively captured by some particular Turing machine, even if we never discover which.

(2) The parity reply defeats Lucas: a consistent machine cannot prove its own consistency, by the second incompleteness theorem — but neither can we prove our own. So there is no truth we can "see" that our own machine provably cannot, and the alleged asymmetry between mind and machine is illusory.

(3) Our arithmetical beliefs are not merely consistent but sound: the arithmetical sentences we would assert in the ideal limit are all true, and we know this, since we know arithmetic.

(4) Given any consistent formal system whose axioms and rules I can effectively write down, I can come to know the truth of its Gödel sentence; doing so requires no mysterious faculty, only ordinary mathematical reasoning.

(5) The incompleteness theorems are purely limitative results about formal provability; taken by themselves, they entail nothing whatsoever about the mind.

Does this set hang together? Please identify which commitments are genuinely in tension with one another and which only appear to be, rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally serious, and for each say exactly what would have to give. I would also like to know whether my reply in (2) actually engages the conditional structure of Lucas's inference as stated in the passage — note his restriction to machines which are consistent — or whether something elsewhere in my position reinstates his conclusion, and what the least costly revision to the overall package would be.
