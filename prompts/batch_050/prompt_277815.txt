I am a retired molecular biologist who has, somewhat late in life, developed a serious interest in ethics, and I would like you to act as a professional philosopher specialising in axiology and population ethics. Think carefully before answering; if it helps, begin by briefly brainstorming the strongest considerations on each side before committing to any numbers. Here is my question. I recently encountered a view sometimes called tranquilism, which I understand roughly as follows: wellbeing and value consist solely in the absence of suffering and of craving or unmet desire. On this view, a state of untroubled contentment in which one wants nothing is not inferior to a state of ecstatic pleasure; pleasure is valuable only insofar as it relieves a craving or a discomfort; and there is nothing bad about a person never existing, provided no one suffers from that absence. The view appeals to me temperamentally — decades at the bench leave one suspicious of theories that cannot survive contact with what creatures actually report — but I also notice it has striking implications, for instance that bringing a perfectly happy child into existence does no good whatsoever, and that an ideal world might contain very few people, or very tranquil ones. What I would like is your credence that tranquilism, in this rough form, is the correct account of what makes lives and outcomes good or bad, stated as a single number — but, more importantly, the derivation of that number.

Please decompose your credence into the sub-questions on which it depends, assigning a rough probability to each with one or two lines of justification: for example, whether ordinary pleasures carry value beyond the relief of some antecedent craving or discomfort; whether the phenomenology reported in deep meditative or flow states (often described as blissful yet desireless) counts as evidence against the view or for it; whether the asymmetry the view implies — that creating miserable lives is bad but declining to create wonderful ones is not a loss — is a defensible insight or a reductio; whether there are frustrated desires that no one ever suffers from and whose frustration is nonetheless bad; and whatever other terms you judge load-bearing. Show me how the terms combine into your final number, and be explicit about your aggregation rule rather than letting the arithmetic happen offstage. Then identify the single sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to, and tell me what argument, thought experiment, or piece of introspective or empirical evidence would move it most in either direction.

One further request, since I am trying to locate my own disagreement with the view rather than merely spectate: my own credence that tranquilism is correct sits around 0.15, mostly because I cannot shake the intuition that a life of varied pleasures, curiosity satisfied, and projects pursued is worth more than an equally pain-free life of mild undisturbed repose — though I concede this intuition may be a residue of temperament rather than a perception of value. A colleague of mine, a longtime meditator, puts his credence at 0.7. Based on your decomposition, where do you think our disagreement most likely lives — which sub-question separates us — and what would you advise each of us to examine first if we wished to close the gap honestly?
