A university's academic integrity panel has five members and a two-condition rule: a student is found responsible only if the panel judges both (a) that the submitted work was not the student's own, and (b) that the student intended to deceive. In a recent case, all five members voted on both conditions. Three of five endorsed (a), a different three of five endorsed (b), but only two members endorsed both. The chair then announced that the panel would vote separately on each condition and find the student responsible if each condition attracted a majority — which it did. The student was found responsible even though a majority of the panel believed him innocent.

Two members of the panel, neither of whom is me, have been arguing about this ever since, and I have been listening without taking a side. Ingrid says the outcome was correct and that the complaint conflates two different questions. The panel as a body affirmed each necessary condition; no individual member's overall view is the unit that matters, because the institution's judgment is supposed to be built up from the best-supported judgment on each distinct issue, and each issue is a separate question of fact with its own evidence. She adds that the alternative — voting only on the final verdict — would smuggle in each member's private weighting of the evidence and produce an outcome nobody could reconstruct or appeal, whereas the condition-by-condition vote leaves a transparent record.

Henrik replies that the panel has convicted a student whom most of its members believe to be innocent, and that no aggregation procedure can launder that into a just outcome. The premise-by-premise majorities are disjoint, so the verdict expresses the view of no one and everyone; if a judge reasoned that way alone — I'm confident he copied, I'm confident he intended to deceive, but these are different possible worlds and I don't actually believe both at once — we would call it incoherent, not transparent. He also argues that Ingrid's transparency point cuts against her: the record now shows that three members thought the work was the student's own or the deception unintended, and any appeal board reading that record should overturn.

Ingrid answers that Henrik is treating the panel as if it were a single mind and then faulting it for failing to be one. Panels exist precisely because individual minds are fallible in different ways; each member has different expertise — some know the subject matter and can spot plagiarism, others know the student's history and can judge intent — so it is not a bug but the design that the majority on each condition is composed of the people best placed to assess it. If anything, she says, the panel should go further and assign each condition to the members with the relevant expertise rather than letting everyone vote on everything.

Henrik says this makes things worse, not better: now the verdict depends entirely on who was assigned which question, and the panel has handed the outcome to whoever sets the agenda. Change the grouping — split the case into different conditions, or merge them into one — and you change the result with no change in anyone's evidence. A procedure whose output flips depending on how the chair happens to carve the question is not aggregating judgments; it is manufacturing them. Ingrid's final move is to concede that the choice of procedure matters but deny that this is an objection: every method of reaching a group decision embeds some substantive choice, she says, and the right response is to choose the procedure openly and defend it, not to pretend that voting on the final verdict is neutral when it merely hides the same choices inside each voter's head.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: who is right about whether the original verdict was defensible, who is right about whether individual coherence is a legitimate demand to place on a group, who is right about the expertise-based division of labor and the agenda-setting objection, and who is right about whether the conclusion-only vote is any less arbitrary. Where both are missing something, say what it is. Finally, give your overall verdict on what the panel should have done in this case, and state the single consideration you take to be decisive.
