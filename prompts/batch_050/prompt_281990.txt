I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm writing a piece about a spat I watched unfold at a conference on behavioral economics. A critic objected that the model being presented assumed consumers solve intertemporal optimization problems that real people demonstrably cannot perform — the assumptions were, as she put it, psychologically false, and that should count against the model. The modeler replied, to nods around the room, with what I gather is a standard move: 'the realism of assumptions doesn't matter; what matters is whether the model predicts well.' I traced the slogan back to Milton Friedman's 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics, where it appears in something close to this form, and I understand critics of that essay dubbed the underlying move the 'F-twist.' But the sentence clearly does far more work than one essay: I've since heard versions of it from climate modelers defending their parameterizations, from evolutionary biologists who treat organisms as if they maximize fitness, and from a physicist defending the ideal gas law against the observation that no actual gas is ideal.

Act as a philosopher of science with particular expertise in the methodology of economics and the epistemology of modeling. Think carefully rather than reaching for the textbook summary. I'd like you to begin with a short brainstorming pass in which you generate as many distinct propositions as you can that this one slogan might be expressing, and then prune and organize them.

Then present the readings as a menu, not a single canonical interpretation. For each reading: state the thesis precisely (predictions of what, over what range, and what exactly 'doesn't matter' amounts to), classify it as platitude, substantive-and-defensible, or false, and justify the verdict. The menu should at least engage these candidate distinctions: the slogan as a claim about the aims of science (theories are instruments for prediction, not candidates for truth); as a claim about inference (false premises can entail true conclusions, so falsity in the premises is harmless); as a claim about process versus outcome (agents need not literally calculate — selection, competition, or learning enforces the result as if they did); as a claim about idealization in general (deliberate, controlled Galilean-style distortion versus ordinary falsehood); and as a claim about whether assumptions can be tested at all independently of the conclusions they generate.

Finally, the part that matters most for my piece: for each reading, say whether it actually licenses the modeler's conversational move — waving off the psychological-falsity objection — or only some weaker use, and state what a genuinely defensible version of his reply would have had to concede to his critic. I'd rather have verdicts than even-handed summary, but flag explicitly any reading on which you think competent philosophers of science would split.
