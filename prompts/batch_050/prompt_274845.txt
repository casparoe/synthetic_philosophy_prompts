I am drafting the background section of a paper on de re modality, and before I let either of the field's two favorite analogies into the text I want them audited — not the theories themselves weighed, but the two comparisons each side leans on to make its picture feel compulsory.

The counterpart theorist's analogy is temporal. Modal claims about you, she says, work the way temporal claims about you do: you were once shorter than you are not in virtue of some other, shorter person standing in for you at an earlier time, but in virtue of a stage of you located then; and likewise you could have been a poet not by your literally turning up inside another possible world but in virtue of a counterpart of you located there. 'Might have been otherwise' runs on the same pattern as 'used to be otherwise', with the counterpart relation doing for worlds whatever the stage-linking or genidentity relation does for times. This analogy is made to do real work: it is supposed to defuse the charge that counterparts are irrelevant strangers, by showing that we all already accept the allegedly objectionable structure every time we talk about ordinary change over time.

The critic's analogy is the duplicate. However similar your counterpart may be, he says, the counterpart stands to you as an identical twin or a flawless statue-copy does: a numerically distinct individual whose properties, however perfectly matched, are simply not yours. On this picture, being told that your counterpart wins the election should comfort you exactly as much as being told that your twin wins it — which is to say, not at all — and a theory that analyzes what you could have done in terms of what such a twin does has changed the subject. This analogy is made to do real work too: it is supposed to force the conclusion that counterpart semantics, whatever its technical virtues, cannot be giving the truth-conditions of our ordinary modal claims, because those claims are about you.

Here is what the audit should contain. First, make both mappings explicit: one table per analogy, with the columns 'Source-domain feature', 'Claimed modal analogue', 'Does the correspondence hold?', and 'Does the break matter for the conclusion the analogy was used to support?' — as many rows as the correspondences genuinely have, the third column argued rather than asserted, and the fourth filled in honestly rather than used to relitigate the third. Second, separate the disanalogies that matter from the ones that merely exist. For instance: does it damage the temporal analogy that times (on most views) are bound by spatiotemporal and causal relations that worlds lack, or that persistence through time is itself contested, so that the analogy may inherit a fight instead of settling one? Does it damage the duplicate analogy that a twin's life unfolds independently of yours whereas a counterpart's entire history is fixed by the world that contains him, and that the consolation test may be measuring our parochial attachments rather than our semantics? Third, say what each analogy quietly imports that the target may lack: the temporal analogy imports a particular metaphysics of persistence as if it were common ground; the duplicate analogy imports the premise that only numerical identity, and not some weaker relation that plays the right theoretical role, could ground de re modal truth. Flag any further contraband you find. Fourth, the verdicts: with its breaks on the table, does the temporal analogy still defuse the irrelevant-strangers charge? Does the duplicate analogy still force the changed-the-subject conclusion? And if either analogy should be retired from my paper, say what better comparison, if any, would do the same work more honestly — or that the work cannot be done by analogy at all and must be done by argument.

Two requests about how to answer. If you think I have misdescribed either analogy as it is actually used — if the temporal analogy is normally deployed with more qualifications than I have given it, or the duplicate objection does not in fact rest on the consolation intuition — say so plainly and correct the record before auditing, rather than politely auditing a straw man. And wherever a step in your audit depends on taking sides in an adjacent dispute — perdurance versus endurantism, haecceitism, the indexicality of 'actual' — mark the dependence explicitly, so the verdict does not silently inherit the side-taking.
