So I just had a huge argument with a friend about whether the Enlightenment was basically the best thing that ever happened to humanity or basically a con -- he kept going on about how "rationality" was always just a cover for control, colonialism, bureaucracy, the whole Horkheimer/Foucault-style indictment, and I kept saying that's a massive overcorrection. At one point he pulled up this bit from Kant's essay What is Enlightenment? (1784), which you'd think would be on MY side:

"Perhaps a fall of personal despotism or of avaricious or tyrannical oppression may be accomplished by revolution, but never a true reform in ways of thinking. Farther, new prejudices will serve as well as old ones to harness the great unthinking masses."

His read: even Kant admits enlightenment talk is compatible with the masses staying harnessed, so the "progress" story was always naive. My read: Kant is making almost the opposite point, that enlightenment isn't a political event you can grade as success or failure at all. But then I realized we've had this fight like four times and never moved an inch. So what I actually want is a diagnosis, not another verdict: is this whole "Enlightenment on trial" debate a substantive disagreement about history, or are the two sides using 'enlightenment' to mean different things (a period? an attitude? a set of institutions?), or is it really a fight over which facts count as evidence -- vaccines and literacy rates vs. colonial violence and the panopticon? Tell me what kind of disagreement it is, whether the Kant passage actually supports either of us or whether we're both misusing it, and what would even count as progress here. Also please be upfront about what's genuinely settled (what Kant argued, what the historical record shows) versus what's still live and contested among people who actually work on this -- don't dress up one contested reading as the scholarly consensus.
