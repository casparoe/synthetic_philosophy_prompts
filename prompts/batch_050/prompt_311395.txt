In September 2002, a 27-year-old law student in Frankfurt kidnapped the eleven-year-old son of a banking family, intending to collect a ransom. He killed the boy in his apartment shortly after the abduction, but no one else knew this. Police arrested the kidnapper after watching him pick up the ransom money and, over the following hours, fail to release the child. With the boy presumed alive and time running out, the deputy police chief, Wolfgang Daschner, ordered a subordinate to threaten the suspect with severe pain if he did not reveal the child's whereabouts. The suspect promptly confessed and led police to the body. The German courts later convicted Daschner and the subordinate for the threat itself, imposing only suspended fines; the coerced confession was excluded from the murder trial, which rested on other evidence; and the European Court of Human Rights eventually found that the threat violated the European Convention's absolute prohibition on inhuman and degrading treatment, while holding that the murder trial had nonetheless been fair. The suspect, a convicted child murderer serving life, was even awarded a small sum in damages for the threat.

Please analyze this episode through the lens of the paradox of deontology: the puzzle of how it can be forbidden to violate one person's rights precisely in order to prevent more or graver violations of the very same kind of right. Structure the analysis as follows. First, map the case onto the puzzle with discipline: whose rights are at stake on each side, whether the threat to the suspect counts as a rights-violation of the same order as the child's right to life, and what the classic deontological responses -- that constraints constrain the agent's own conduct rather than the overall tally, that the rights-bearer here is a culpable wrongdoer rather than an innocent bystander, and that threatening is categorically different from inflicting -- would each say about Daschner's order. Second, press the two features of the episode that most resist the standard treatments: the fact that the officers reasonably believed the boy might still be alive but he was in fact already dead, so that the threat could not in fact have saved anyone (does the deontological assessment track the officers' ex ante situation or the ex post reality, and does the paradox even get a grip if the counterfactual rescue was impossible?); and the fact that what was prohibited was a threat believed by all concerned, including the suspect, rather than the infliction of pain itself. Third, turn the analysis around: what does the episode reveal about the paradox and about absolutist constraints? Consider that the legal system convicted the officers yet punished them almost symbolically, excluded the confession yet convicted the suspect anyway, and compensated the murderer for the threat against him -- and ask whether this pattern of verdicts shows the constraint doing real work, shows the system quietly reasoning consequentially while officially denying it, or shows that an absolute prohibition on degrading treatment was never really meant for cases like this one. Where the theory is genuinely silent on a feature of the case, say so rather than forcing an answer.
