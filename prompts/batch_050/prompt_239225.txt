Over the past few years I have kept a record of my own failures to follow through on plans, and a pattern has emerged that I cannot quite match to anything I have read about weakness of will. I would like to know whether it has an established name, and if so, how it is carved off from its neighbors.

The cases one reads about involve a person who does something while judging, at the very moment of doing it, that it would be better all things considered not to. That is not what my record shows. What it shows is this. In the evening I deliberate calmly and conclude that the best thing tomorrow morning is to rise at six and work for two hours before anything else; I set the alarm in complete sincerity. At six I consider the matter afresh and — without feeling overpowered, coerced, or inwardly divided — I genuinely judge that remaining in bed is the better course: rest is valuable, the work can be done later, one missed morning changes nothing. The same structure recurs with eating (the sensible lunch planned in the morning; the dessert sincerely judged acceptable in the evening) and with money (the transfer to savings resolved on payday; the purchase judged entirely reasonable a week later). In every case the judgment that licenses the lapse is made in what feels, at the time, like good faith.

Two features seem to me to matter. First, at no single moment do I act against my own judgment: at each moment, action and all-things-considered judgment are perfectly aligned. The conflict exists only across time, between the planner and the actor, as if they were two agents with opposed but each internally coherent outlooks. Second, the later judgment presents itself as reconsideration rather than surrender — from the inside it is indistinguishable from having changed one's mind for reasons — which is precisely what makes the pattern so hard to catch in oneself.

My questions, then: (1) Is there an established term for this configuration, in which the agent never once acts contrary to a currently held better judgment, because the judgment itself reverses as the tempting option draws near? (2) Does it even count as weakness of will on the standard accounts — which seem to require a clash between judgment and action at a single time — or does it belong to some adjacent category? (3) And what marks the boundary between this pattern and honest reconsideration? After all, the morning self offers reasons, and people are sometimes right to abandon their plans. I am not after a bare label; I would like to know which concept, if any, covers exactly this case, which nearby concepts it must be distinguished from, and where its edges are supposed to lie.
