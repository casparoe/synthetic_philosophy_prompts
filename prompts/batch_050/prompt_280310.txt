Two acquaintances of mine — I'll call them C and D — both work in technical biosecurity and move in roughly my social circles. Last week they had an argument, conducted partly in front of me, about a concrete situation that I think cleanly isolates a disagreement about the unilateralist's curse. I'm familiar with the standard treatment of that concept, so I'm not asking for an exposition of it; I'm asking for a verdict on the argument as it was actually made.

The situation. A preprint on computational protein design has been public for several months. C and D, along with perhaps ten other researchers in their extended network, have each independently noticed that one section of the methods, combined with tooling that is already freely available, plausibly lowers the barrier to synthesizing a particular dangerous pathogen. Nothing about the preprint is secret; it is simply that few people have put the pieces together. Each of the dozen or so people who have noticed faces, individually, the same menu of options: quietly contact the authors asking them to revise the methods section; flag the preprint to a national biosecurity body, which would create a record and likely trigger a formal process; circulate a note within the network proposing that the group deliberate before anyone escalates; or do nothing. Nobody is coordinating, and each person knows the others are deciding independently.

Move 1 (C). She argues that this is a textbook case of the unilateralist's curse and that it demands a strong personal offset. If twelve people each decide on their own noisy judgment whether escalation is warranted, escalation happens whenever even one of them lands above the threshold — the effective decision-maker is the most escalation-friendly estimate in the group, not the median. Since the median view in the network is probably that escalation is premature, the process is biased toward an outcome the group would reject if it pooled its judgment. Each person should therefore escalate only if their confidence clears a bar set well above their naive threshold, and should otherwise push for deliberation.

Move 2 (D). He replies that the argument proves too much, because it is symmetric. Inaction is not a privileged default: each of the twelve is also unilaterally deciding not to alert anyone, and whoever talks the others into waiting is unilaterally suppressing an action that pooled judgment might favor. There is no non-decision available; you cannot escape being a unilateralist by choosing the quiet option. Calling only one side of the symmetry a curse is status quo bias with a theorem attached.

Move 3 (C). She concedes the formal symmetry but denies the practical one: the curse argument is asymmetric whenever the actions are, and here they are. Escalation to a government body is close to irreversible — it creates records, triggers processes, cannot be un-rung — whereas deliberation preserves option value. Where one terminal of a decision is absorbing and the other is not, the most-optimistic-agent bias genuinely corrupts the process, and deference or delay is the correct response. Where both options are reversible the curse mostly dissolves, which is why the argument does not in fact prove too much.

Move 4 (D). He accepts the reversibility criterion but disputes both its application and the underlying arithmetic. First, delay is not reversible either: while the group deliberates, the preprint keeps circulating, the window in which a quiet note to the authors would work closes, and someone outside the network may notice the same thing and handle it worse. Preserving one option by waiting destroys another; "reversible" here just names the option whose costs are less salient. Second, the size of the curse depends on how independent the twelve estimates are, and these are not independent — everyone shares the same priors, training, and reading lists, so their errors are correlated and the most-extreme-estimate bias is far smaller than the toy model suggests. C's offset is therefore miscalibrated on two separate grounds.

Please adjudicate move by move. For each of the four moves: does it actually answer the move it responds to, is it sound on its own terms, and what unstated assumption does it lean on? Identify which move you take to be the strongest and which the weakest, and why. Then give a verdict on the practical question: should someone in C and D's position apply a personal offset toward deliberation, or is D right that this amounts to miscalibrated restraint? Finally, what — if anything — are both of them missing?
