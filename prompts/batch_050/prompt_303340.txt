I am a novelist, two decades into the craft, and I have noticed that one problem keeps returning in different costumes. Whenever I place a character under serious pressure — blackmail, addiction, a childhood of indoctrination, a marriage that has quietly rewritten what the character wants — I must decide, scene by scene, whether what the character does can be laid at the character's door: whether the reader is being invited to blame, to pity, to admire, or simply to mourn. Lately I have been deciding by instinct, and my instincts contradict themselves from book to book.

What I want is a procedure I can run on any character's decisive act, grounded in what philosophy actually knows about the varieties of freedom worth wanting — not the metaphysical question of whether determinism is true, which I am content to bracket, since my fictional worlds are whatever I say they are, but the practical question of which capacities a person must have, and retain, for their choices to be genuinely theirs. The procedure should force me to distinguish things I currently blur: acting under a threat versus acting under a compulsion versus acting on desires someone else installed; whether the character could have responded to reasons they in fact ignored; whether the motive the character acted on is one the character would endorse on reflection; and the difference, which I sense matters enormously for how a scene reads, between an act being truly the character's own and the character being answerable to others for it. I write a novel every two or three years; each contains perhaps a dozen decisions of this kind, and the final act usually turns on one.

Give me the questions in the order they must be asked, state what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and mark the points where the procedure must stop and hand the decision back to me because the remaining question is one of artistic judgment rather than of conceptual fact. Tell me which philosophical distinctions have to survive translation into a working tool and which can be safely dropped by someone with no training, and be candid about where the procedure will misfire — the cases where readers will hold a character responsible despite the procedure's verdict, or forgive where it condemns.

Then run it on three cases from my notebook: a devoted husband who betrays his business partner because a blackmailer holds evidence that would destroy his daughter; a woman raised inside an insular sect whose every adult preference, I reveal in the penultimate chapter, was deliberately engineered by its founder — a reveal that must retroactively change how the reader judges her earlier cruelties; and a queen in a world where an oracle's prophecies have never once failed, who learns she is foretold to murder her brother and spends the novel trying to refuse. I expect the procedure to give me at least one answer I will not like — most likely about the queen, whose tragedy I have been writing as if she were guilty. If so, tell me plainly, and tell me what I would have to change: the character's history, the structure of the reveal, or the ending.
