I am a retired geochemist, and in the last year or so I have been working my way through the literature on the social discount rate, prompted initially by the debate between the Stern Review and its critics (Nordhaus chief among them) over the economics of climate change. Having spent my career modeling processes with half-lives of millennia, I came in sympathetic to the long view, but I have tried to discipline my intuitions with the actual theory — Ramsey, the growth-based decomposition of the discount rate, Weitzman's work on rate uncertainty, and so on. I have now written down where I have landed, and before I inflict this on the reading group of mostly retired colleagues I organize, I would like to know whether my position is actually coherent.

My commitments are these. (1) There is no ethical justification for pure time preference: the well-being of a person matters the same regardless of when that person lives, and the only legitimate reason to weight the near future more heavily is the positive probability that humanity will not be around to occupy it — so the pure rate should be zero or very nearly so. (2) The Ramsey decomposition — discount rate equals the pure rate plus the elasticity of marginal utility times the growth rate of consumption — is the correct organizing framework for thinking about the social discount rate, not merely a piece of descriptive economics. (3) Because future generations will very likely be richer than we are, and because marginal utility diminishes, discounting ordinary public investments at something in the neighborhood of observed real market rates of return (say three to four percent) is defensible; markets, whatever their flaws, reveal the opportunity cost of capital, and it would be wasteful to fund projects returning one percent when capital elsewhere returns four. (4) Catastrophic risks several centuries out — extinction-level events, irreversible tipping points — deserve nearly the same weight per expected life affected as a catastrophe next year, and a sound policy framework should deliver that conclusion rather than burying it. (5) A social decision procedure should be time-consistent: it should not recommend plans today that the same procedure, absent new information, will reverse tomorrow.

My worry is that these five do not all fit together, but I cannot see cleanly where the strain is. In particular I suspect (1) and (3) are pulling against each other in a way I have not fully articulated to myself, and I have a vaguer worry that (4) is doing more work than (2) and (3) allow. On the other hand, I suspect the apparent conflict between (1) and (2) is only apparent, though I would like that confirmed rather than assumed. Please audit the set: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, order the genuine tensions by severity, and for each one state precisely what would have to give — which commitment must be revised or abandoned, and what that revision costs me. If there is a single revision that resolves the most strain, identify it. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in each part of the audit, and single out explicitly the one claim in your analysis you are least sure of and why.
