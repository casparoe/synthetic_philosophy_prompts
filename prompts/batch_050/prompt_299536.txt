okay so I sit on the ethics committee at our hospital and we keep getting stuck on basically the same case: a patient with moderate dementia who, fifteen years ago, wrote a very detailed advance directive refusing certain life-prolonging treatments, but who now seems... fine? content, not obviously suffering, sometimes says things that sound like wanting to go on. Meanwhile there are other cases (permanent unconsciousness, say) where nobody hesitates for a second. I want to stop re-litigating this patient by patient and figure out what an adequate theory of advance directives would even have to do before we pick one.

So write me the desiderata. What does a theory of when and why an earlier self's directive binds the later self have to deliver? Things I suspect belong on the list, but push back where I'm wrong:

- it has to handle the dementia case above without just flatly saying honor it or ignore it -- it has to say what does the justificatory work (personal identity? precedent autonomy? current interests?) and why that thing
- it has to handle the patient who is now glad to be alive but whose directive, if followed, would have let them die years earlier
- it has to handle psychiatric cases (e.g. someone with bipolar disorder who writes a Ulysses contract refusing treatment during future manic episodes) without just collapsing them into the dementia case, since the facts about continuity between the selves seem pretty different
- it has to say whether refusing treatment and requesting treatment come out the same, and if not why not
- it has to be usable by a resident at 2am, not just by philosophers with the chart and three weeks
- it can't make directives toothless (current wishes always trump) or absolute (the document always wins), since both of those are clearly wrong

For each requirement give the case or intuition behind it, and mark it non-negotiable vs. one camp's hobbyhorse -- I have a feeling half of these are basically the autonomy people vs. the wellbeing people in disguise and I'd like that exposed. And the thing I most want: if some subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, say so and prove it roughly -- e.g. if you cannot both take precedent autonomy seriously and take current wellbeing seriously without smuggling in a controversial theory of personal identity, I want that stated as a result of the list, not papered over. Which single requirement knocks out the most positions people actually hold?

Answer in American English please. No bibliography or citations needed, I know the literature exists, I want the argument.
