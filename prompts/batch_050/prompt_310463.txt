In Plato's Symposium, in Benjamin Jowett's translation, Apollodorus introduces the source of his narrative as follows:

"He had been at Agathon's feast; and I think that in those days there was no one who was a more devoted admirer of Socrates."

Please compare the appraisal and bestowal conceptions of the reasons for love, using this brief characterisation of Aristodemus as your test case. Roughly: on the appraisal view, associated above all with David Velleman, love is a response to the perceived value of the beloved — an arresting of our emotional defences in the face of a person's rational nature or excellences — whereas on the bestowal view, associated with Harry Frankfurt, love does not respond to antecedent value in its object but rather confers value and generates reasons, so that I have reason to care for my children because I love them, not the other way round. You may also wish to consider Niko Kolodny's suggestion that the reasons distinctive of love flow from valuing a relationship rather than from either the beloved's qualities or an act of bare bestowal.

In your answer, please address the following:

1. How would each view reconstruct Aristodemus' devotion to Socrates? Note that the text says nothing about what Aristodemus admired in Socrates. Does this silence embarrass the appraisal view, which seems to require specifiable valued qualities, or can the appraisal theorist allow that genuine appreciation of value may outrun the lover's ability to articulate it?

2. Suppose Aristodemus' devotion survives his discovering that Socrates lacks the excellences he took him to have. Which view best explains the intuition that such constancy can be admirable rather than merely irrational, and which view risks implying that Aristodemus ought to "trade up" to a worthier object or else stands convicted of an error?

3. Does the bestowal view, in denying that love is grounded in reasons, leave it mysterious why love is nevertheless the sort of state that can seem fitting or unfitting, deep or shallow? Conversely, does the appraisal view capture the particularity of love — that Aristodemus loves Socrates, and not simply whatever instantiates Socratic virtues, to a sufficient degree?

4. Finally, does Kolodny's relationship-based account genuinely escape the appraisal/bestowal dichotomy, or does it collapse into one of its rivals once we ask what makes a relationship itself worth valuing?

Please set out the comparison even-handedly before offering your own verdict on which view, or which combination, gives the most satisfactory account of the reasons Aristodemus might have had.
