I run a small philosophy club (8–12 people, mix of undergrads and keen amateurs, about 75 minutes) and we're doing Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (Mind, 1895). The moment I want them to feel in their bones is this one:

"If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"

Design a game or structured role-play where some players have to be tortoises: they must grant anything that gets written down on the shared sheet, but may refuse any step that isn't written, and the other players have to "force" them to Z. They should discover on their own that piling up extra premises never closes the gap, before I say modus ponens or the word regress. Give me rules, roles, a timeline, and some tiny stake so that refusing costs something rather than being free. Then a debrief that maps the stall-tactics people actually invent onto Achilles's moves (C, D, E...) and onto the quoted passage specifically: is the Tortoise just being pig-headed, or is she pointing at something real about what a rule of inference is and why it can't be another premise? Also tell me what will definately go wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of the regress a game like this can't reproduce and I'll have to supply in discussion. End with a last line giving \answer{...} containing a short phrase naming the one rule or mechanic in your design that does the most philosophical work.
