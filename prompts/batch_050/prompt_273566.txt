At the local rationality meetup I attend, a long argument broke out last week between two regulars — call them Dana and Morgan — over whether one can coherently support minimum wages while opposing rent control. I stayed out of it, and I genuinely could not decide who had the better of it, so I'd like the exchange adjudicated.

Both parties accepted the textbook framing: a minimum wage is a price floor in the labor market, rent control is a price ceiling in the housing market, and in a competitive market each produces the standard result — the floor a surplus of labor, the ceiling a shortage of housing, deadweight loss either way. The dispute was about whether anything substantive breaks the symmetry.

Dana's opening claim was that the common pairing of pro-minimum-wage and anti-rent-control views is pure coalition politics: the same supply-and-demand machinery governs both, and the asymmetry tracks only which sellers one sympathizes with (workers, not landlords) and which buyers one doesn't (employers, not tenants).

Morgan replied that market structure breaks the symmetry. Low-wage labor markets are plausibly monopsonistic — switching jobs is costly, so employers hold wage-setting power — and under monopsony a moderately binding floor can raise wages and employment at once. Rental markets, by contrast, have many competing landlords, so the competitive model applies and a ceiling simply yields shortages and under-maintenance.

Dana granted the monopsony logic but accused it of doing selective work. In tight urban markets, a landlord with a desirable unit holds local market power too, so by Morgan's own reasoning a ceiling could in principle expand what is offered — yet nobody who cites monopsony for labor seems curious about that parallel. Moreover, monopsony only licenses a floor up to the competitive wage; above that it costs jobs, and since nobody knows where the threshold lies, the argument amounts to a license for motivated calibration.

Morgan then shifted grounds: the deeper asymmetry is dynamic. Housing supply is highly elastic in the long run because new construction responds to returns, so a ceiling's costs compound for decades, whereas the supply of low-wage labor cannot be built or demolished. Dana answered that this cuts both ways: labor is also a long-run investment decision — human capital, migration, fertility — so if long-run elasticities condemn rent control, they condemn wage floors with a lag rather than exonerate them.

Morgan's final move was ethical: a floor forbids a transaction the constrained party might want, arguably as protection of the seller, while a ceiling restricts one party purely for a counterparty's benefit — protection in one case, expropriation in the other. Dana responded that this is the motivated reasoning charge in reverse: both policies void contracts between consenting adults, and relabeling one restriction as care and the other as theft is exactly the sort of coalition-driven framing Morgan opened by decrying.

Please go through the exchange point by point: who is right about the monopsony asymmetry, about Dana's local-market-power parallel, about the long-run elasticity argument and its extension to human capital, and about the alleged ethical asymmetry? Is there a principled, non-political reason to treat the two policies differently, and if so which of the ones offered survives? Finally, what relevant considerations did both of them miss?
