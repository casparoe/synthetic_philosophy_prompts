I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am currently reporting a feature on the longevity industry: the billionaire-funded laboratories working on cellular reprogramming, senolytic drugs, and other interventions whose more candid advocates describe aging itself as a disease to be cured rather than a fate to be borne. While reading background for the piece I returned to Cicero's Cato Maior de Senectute, written around 44 BC, and I was struck by how directly its central tension maps onto my story. Cicero has the elderly Cato concede that old age earns honor only insofar as it defends itself, asserts its own rights, and refuses to become subservient to anyone — a ringing brief for what we would now call healthy or active aging. Yet a few chapters later, addressing the nearness of death, Cato offers his famous similes of natural ending: a young person's death is like a fire violently doused by water, an old person's like a fire that goes out of its own accord, and fruits that are unripe must be torn from the tree while ripe ones drop by themselves. The treatise seems to me to hold both that decline is worth resisting with every faculty one has and that there is a ripeness after which resistance becomes a kind of ingratitude toward nature.

Here is what I would like from you. Suppose Cicero — or, more precisely, the Cato of this dialogue, whose voice Cicero later said he had made his own — were brought up to speed on the modern longevity project: not merely better geriatrics, but a serious research program aimed at compressing or eliminating senescence altogether, so that the ripeness he describes might never arrive. Would he regard this as the ultimate extension of his first principle, old age finally defending itself without limit, or as the violation of his second, a refusal to let the fruit fall? Where in the treatise's actual arguments does the answer turn — on his claim that each stage of life has its own proper character and season, on his insistence that what is according to nature cannot be evil, or on his distinction between fearing death and despising it? I'd like a reasoned prediction, not a verdict by quotation: tell me which passages of his reasoning do the real work, where you think he would balk, and what you would ask him, if you could, to force him to choose.
