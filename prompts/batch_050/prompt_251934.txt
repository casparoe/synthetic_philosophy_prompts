I provide respite care for my father, who is in the early stages of dementia, and I volunteer one evening a week at a hospice. I try to be honest with myself about my motives: I do these things chiefly because I find the work absorbing and the company rewarding, and I am not sure I would describe myself as acting from a sense of obligation. At a reading group last week, another member, who has read more Kant than I have, told me that on Kant's view my situation is morally worse than I like to think. He summarized the position as follows: 'For Kant, an action has genuine moral worth only when it is done from duty, that is, only when the agent acts out of respect for the moral law rather than from any desire or feeling. So the person who helps others because it gives them pleasure stands in the same position as the shopkeeper who is honest because honesty pays: both are ultimately serving their own interests. Indeed, the more enjoyment you take in a good deed, the less moral credit you deserve for it; Kant's model of virtue is the person who does good while feeling no sympathy or satisfaction whatsoever.' A friend later pointed out that there is a mocking verse from Kant's own era, to the effect that on this picture one must despise one's friends in order to help them as duty demands, which suggests the summary is at least a well-established reading.

Still, something about it strikes me as unfair to Kant, though I cannot put my finger on where. Is the summary accurate? Please separate what a careful reader of Kant would accept from what is a common simplification and what Kant would explicitly reject. In particular: is acting from inclination really the same as acting from self-interest, as the summary assumes when it pairs the sympathetic helper with the prudent shopkeeper? Does the presence of enjoyment genuinely diminish an action's worth in proportion, or is Kant's question instead whether duty was the determining motive — and can duty and enjoyment be present together in a single act? And does Kant actually hold up the joyless benefactor as the ideal of virtue, or does he describe such a person for some other purpose? Finally, I would like to know what follows for my own case. Granting the accurate parts of the summary, does it follow that my care for my father has no moral worth, or does the corrected picture say something more nuanced about people whose motives are mixed — and about the difference between an action's being right, its being praiseworthy, and its having moral worth? I would like to arrive at the next meeting with a precise account of which of my fellow reader's claims I should concede and which I should dispute.
