I work at a small foresight consultancy, and one of our ongoing jobs is auditing long-range predictions for clients -- think tanks and funds that bought into some big theory of the future a decade or two back and now want to know whether to keep acting on it. The pattern I keep hitting is this: the prediction's deadline passes, the thing didn't happen, and the theorist (or the client who's invested in the theory) says the theory wasn't wrong, just early, or that some auxiliary condition changed, or that the prediction was never really *dated* in that way. Malthus is the classic case everyone cites -- the catastrophe keeps not arriving and the theory keeps getting rescheduled rather than dropped -- but I see the same move with peak oil, with various demographic doom scenarios, and with a couple of AI timelines where the date slipped and the proponent quietly reissued it five years further out. At some point this starts to feel less like theorising and more like a shell game.

What I want is a decision procedure my team can actually run when we audit one of these, grounded in whatever philosophy of science actually has to say about falsification and the rescue of failing theories (Popper, Lakatos, that whole debate about ad hoc modifications vs progressive ones), but boiled down to something a smart generalist can apply in a day or two, not a dissertation. So: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and specifically a principled way to distinguish (a) a theory that has genuinely earned a deadline extension -- because the modification explains why the old deadline failed *and* tells you something new you didn't already know, (b) a theory being kept alive by moving goalposts, and (c) a theory that was never really falsifiable in the first place because no possible evidence would have counted against it. The procedure should also say what to do when the prediction was vague about timing from the start (do you reconstruct a deadline? whose?), and it should mark the points where the call is irreducibly a judgement call that has to go to a named senior person, not a box you tick.

Then please run the procedure on two cases: the original Malthusian argument about population outrunning food supply, treated as if it were a live theory a client still holds, and a fictional-but-typical case where an analyst predicted in 2010 that a specific resource would hit permanent shortage by 2020 and now says the shortage is "baked in" for the 2030s because the original model underestimated efficiency gains (which the theory allegedly always allowed for). I want at least one of these to come out with a verdict I probably won't enjoy -- I'm fairly sure the Malthus audit will annoy whichever side it lands on. And be honest about what the procedure can't settle: I suspect "right but early" is sometimes a coherent position and sometimes an immunising trick, and if your procedure can't cleanly seperate those, I'd rather you say where it breaks than pretend it doesn't.
