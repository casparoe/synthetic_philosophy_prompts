I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy working on population ethics, and I am drafting a chapter on whether the nonidentity problem arises with the same force in private life-planning choices as it does in public policy. Nearly every canonical case in the literature is a policy decision — depletion versus conservation, the two medical programs — and the few private-choice cases that exist rely on a single device: someone choosing when to conceive. I want a fresh test case for the chapter, and I would like you to construct it to order.

Build a thought experiment satisfying all of the following specifications:

1. Two options are available to the agent, and each option causes a different set of people to exist. The worse option must not be worse for anyone who actually exists under it, in the comparative sense: no one ends up worse off than they would have been had the other option been chosen, since under the other option those very people would not have existed at all.
2. The two options produce the same number of people. I want quality of life to do all the work, with no aggregation across differently sized populations.
3. The worse-off lives must be clearly worth living. The case must not borrow its force from the wrongfulness of creating miserable lives.
4. The agent is a private individual making a personal decision, not a state or institution choosing a policy.
5. The identity-affecting mechanism must not be the timing of conception (no "wait two months" stipulations). The difference in who exists should arise from some other feature of the agent's choice — whom the agent partners with, where the agent lives, what the agent does to their own body, or the like — and ideally from a mechanism where selecting a different person is a side effect of the choice rather than its point.
6. The worse option must involve no lying, coercion, promise-breaking, or violation of consent. If there is wrongdoing, it must be located entirely in which people get to exist and how well their lives go.
7. The scenario itself should be realistic — the sort of decision an ordinary person might actually face — and stated in a single paragraph.

After the scenario, demonstrate that it works. First, state the person-affecting principle that an act can be wrong only if it makes things worse for some person, and show precisely why that principle cannot condemn the worse option here. Second, state the impersonal principle that, when the number of people is held fixed, an outcome is worse if the people who live are worse off than the different people who would otherwise have lived, and show why it does condemn the worse option. Third, identify which single feature of your scenario is load-bearing — the one whose removal would collapse the case back into an ordinary situation where someone is harmed in the comparative sense — and run that counterfactual check explicitly. Fourth, give the strongest attack available to a defender of the person-affecting principle: would they deny that the impersonal comparison is coherent, relocate the wrongness in the agent's reasons or character, or simply bite the bullet and deny that the worse option is wrong? Assess which of these moves is most damaging to your case, and say candidly whether the case establishes that the impersonal principle is needed or merely that the intuition of wrongness survives somehow.

One further note on what counts as success: the case must generate its verdicts from the theories, not stipulate them. If the wrongness of the worse option has to be asserted rather than derived, the case fails, and I would rather you say so than paper over it.
