I lead the trust-and-safety group for a “life admin” app that learns from what users do: bookings, purchases, snoozed reminders, cancelled plans, calendar edits, and the occasional explicit instruction. Each week the system proposes about twenty preference cards for approval, such as “prefers evening meetings” or “values saving money over punctuality”, and once approved these can drive automation without asking again. The product managers reviewing them are smart but know no reinforcement learning, and we have roughly half an hour per batch, so the current process is basically a vibe check. I keep saying that inverse reinforcement learning does not license reading preferences straight off behaviour, because behaviour reflects the available menu, constraints, beliefs, habits, social pressure and the user's current policy, but nobody knows what to do with that objection in practice.

Please turn that idea into a reusable audit procedure a reviewer can actually follow before approving an inferred preference. It should reconstruct the “shadow menu” the user plausibly thought they were choosing from, identify rival explanations for the behaviour, say what evidence would discriminate between them, and classify the result as something like a stable preference, a context-specific trade-off, a constraint, a temporary tactic, or simply indeterminate. Include rules for conflicts between clicks and verbal reports, for old versus recent behaviour, and for when the system must ask the user rather than keep collecting behavioural evidence. Also mark where the procedure reaches a hard stop for autonomy reasons, even if the inference is statistically strong, and where reviewer judgement remains unavoidable rather than pretending the checklist settles everything. Please dont make this a generic AI-ethics checklist; the point is to preserve the useful core of IRL while avoiding naïve revealed-preference reasoning.

Apply the procedure to three cards: (1) a user repeatedly books the 6 a.m. flight when it is £80 cheaper, despite having told the assistant they hate early departures; (2) a user snoozes nearly every gym reminder but keeps paying for membership and reliably goes when a friend is coming; and (3) a user orders the same sugary lunch on deadline days but chooses differently at weekends. Include at least one case where the procedure refuses to certify a preference even though the behavioural data look strong, since that is the sort of answer the product team will not like. Write in British English, and at the end say how confident you are that the procedure captures the relevant IRL issues and identify the part you are least sure of.
