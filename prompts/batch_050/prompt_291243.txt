You are both a scholar of classical Chinese philosophy and an experienced designer of seminar activities. I teach a weekly undergraduate seminar on early Daoism — about 14 students, 75-minute sessions — and we are currently on the Laozi and the Zhuangzi. Design an in-class activity that makes students *feel* the paradox at the heart of wu-wei: deliberately trying to act without deliberate effort is self-undermining. I want them to run into this themselves, not hear it from me.

Requirements: the activity needs real structure — rules, roles, a scoring or success condition, and small stakes — arranged so that trying harder genuinely makes performance worse, and so that a student's conscious decision to "stop trying" is itself palpably a further act of trying. The concept should not be named until after the activity. It must work for 14 people with no special materials in about 30 minutes, leaving time for debrief. Think carefully about the mechanics first: the success condition has to make effort counterproductive, not merely difficult, and I'd like you to consider a couple of candidate designs and explain briefly why you settled on the final one.

For the debrief, map specific moments of the activity onto the philosophical material. In particular, distinguish the Laozi's wu-wei (non-striving as an ideal of action and governance) from the Zhuangzi's skill stories, such as Cook Ding, where effortlessness is the residue of years of training — my students reliably conflate these. Also address whether the activity reproduces the actual paradox or only a shallow cousin of it (choking under pressure, ironic rebound effects, and the like), and what the difference implies for how I should frame the discussion. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first two times I run this, and which feature of wu-wei no half-hour game can capture, so that I know what I have to supply myself.
