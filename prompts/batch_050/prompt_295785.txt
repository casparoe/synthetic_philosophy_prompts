A colleague in my department argues that the famous gap between is and ought -- the thesis that no conclusion about what ought to be done follows from premises that merely describe what is the case -- rests on a mistake, and that once the mistake is exposed the gap dissolves into something philosophically uninteresting. His argument runs as follows:

(1) If there were a genuine is-ought gap, there would have to be some general principle of logic that blocks the inference from purely descriptive premises to normative conclusions.

(2) The only candidate principle is the rule that a valid deductive argument cannot introduce, in its conclusion, vocabulary that does not already occur in its premises.

(3) That rule is false in full generality. From "Socrates is a man" one can validly infer "Socrates is a man or it is raining," a conclusion containing vocabulary absent from the premise; so conclusions can perfectly well outrun their premises in respect of vocabulary.

(4) Therefore there is no logical principle that stands in the way of deriving ought from is.

(5) The appearance of a gap is then explained by a contingent fact about us: we have not yet located the right purely descriptive bridging premises (perhaps facts about human flourishing, or about what ideal observers would want), just as a conjecture in mathematics can go unproven for centuries without there being any deep barrier to proving it.

(6) Hence the is-ought gap is not a thesis about the nature of normativity at all; it is merely the name we give to an unsolved search problem, and metaethics has made a mountain out of a backlog.

I find myself unable to accept (6), and I suspect the argument proves too much. If it were sound, parallel reasoning would seem to dissolve every alleged categorial boundary in philosophy: there would be no interesting barrier between the observed and the unobserved, or between the mathematical and the empirical -- each "gap" would be demoted to a temporary shortage of premises. Yet I cannot put my finger on the step where the argument slips. Which premise or inference should be rejected? Explain why that step initially looks innocuous, and what it costs to reject it -- in particular, what a repaired version of the is-ought thesis would have to look like so that it both survives the counterexample in (3) and remains a substantive claim rather than a triviality. If you think the argument is in fact sound and (6) should simply be accepted, say so, and explain what work, if any, is left for the is-ought distinction to do.
