I teach an undergraduate course on the philosophy of history -- about 20 students, one 90-minute session. Design an in-class activity that makes students feel that archives are made, not found: specifically, how power decides whose testimony gets recorded, and how silences enter the historical record without anyone lying or conspiring. The rough shape I have in mind: stage a short, slightly chaotic event in class (a simulated committee meeting, dispute, or decision), assign some students to be record-keepers working under an explicit institutional mandate and tight material constraints, and later have the rest reconstruct what happened from the surviving records alone, so that the gaps surface on their own before anyone names them. Give me concrete rules, roles, timing, and materials; the constraints on what the recorders may keep and why those constraints should mimic real institutional pressures (budget, relevance criteria, whose voices count as authoritative); a debrief mapping each stage of the activity onto the distinct moments where silences enter an archive -- what occurs, what gets written down, what gets preserved, and what gets retold; the failure modes I should expect the first time I run it; and an honest note on which feature of real archival silence the simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied in discussion.
