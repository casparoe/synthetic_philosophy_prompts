Two friends of mine — one a hydrologist who works on drought forecasting, the other a researcher in humanitarian policy — fell into an argument last week about how famines should be characterised. The hydrologist's position was roughly that famines are natural disasters whose politics are secondary; the policy researcher held that they are better understood as political crimes. Their exchange ran as follows, and I'd like it adjudicated.

They agreed on a stylised case to argue over: a country in which drought cuts the harvest by fifteen per cent — serious, but leaving aggregate food supply above subsistence needs — yet food prices triple, the poorest rural labourers can no longer afford grain, the government honours existing export contracts to service foreign debt, and mass starvation follows.

(1) The hydrologist opened by saying that the drought is the cause of the famine and the rest is detail. Remove the drought and nobody starves; remove any particular policy choice and the crisis likely takes a different shape but still occurs. Calling such an event a political crime, she said, confuses the background conditions under which a disaster unfolds with the disaster itself.

(2) The policy researcher replied that this misidentifies what a cause is. If food availability never dropped below what the population needed, then the drought is at most a trigger; the famine proper consists in the collapse of what specific groups could command — their wages, their assets, the price they face at market. A cause that operates by repricing food out of some people's reach, while others eat and grain leaves the country, is a distributional mechanism, not a weather event.

(3) The hydrologist conceded the mechanism but attacked the word "crime." A crime requires a perpetrator and at least culpable intent or recklessness attributable to someone. Bad policy, slow bureaucracy, and debt obligations honoured under pressure are failures, perhaps grave ones, but labelling them crimes evacuates the word of meaning and substitutes moral condemnation for explanation.

(4) The policy researcher answered that criminal law itself recognises recklessness and gross negligence, not just intent — a driver who kills without meaning to can still be criminally liable. A government that knows its export and pricing policies are cutting off the poor's access to food, and refuses feasible remedies, stands to the famine roughly as the reckless driver stands to the collision, not as a blameless bystander. She added that responsibility can attach to institutions and officeholders collectively, so the demand for a single villainous perpetrator is a red herring.

(5) The hydrologist responded that the "feasible remedies" clause smuggles in hindsight. Officials act on noisy data, conflicting price signals, and several emergencies at once; the counterfactual that a reasonably competent government would have prevented the famine is asserted rather than demonstrated. In at least some famines, she claimed, every available policy path involved mass death, and treating tragedy as culpability because we can imagine a cleaner alternative is a fallacy.

(6) The policy researcher closed with the observation that hindsight is not needed where contemporaneous warnings existed and were ignored, and that large famines essentially do not occur in functioning democracies with a free press, because electoral accountability and public information force early responses. The missing variable in the stylised case, she said, is not food but the political channels that would have made the poor's starvation costly to the powerful.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere between, with a brief justification. Then address the framing question the two never resolved: whether "natural disaster" and "political crime" are competing descriptions of the same event or answers to different questions (explanation of onset versus assignment of culpability), and what, if anything, both parties are missing about the stylised case they agreed on. Please write your answer in British English.
