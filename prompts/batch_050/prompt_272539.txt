I run an informal seminar at my university where biologists, astrobiologists, and a couple of philosophers meet, and our recurring fight is the definition of life. After a year, the argument has calcified. One camp keeps proposing and repairing criteria: a living thing must metabolize, maintain itself far from equilibrium, reproduce with heritable variation, and so on; every proposal gets sunk by a counterexample (mules and worker ants against reproduction, viruses against metabolism, fire against self-maintenance), and the camp responds by patching the list. A second camp has concluded the whole project is misguided: they argue that you cannot define a natural phenomenon before you have a successful theory of it, just as no armchair definition of water would have yielded H2O, so we should stop defining life and start theorizing it. A third, quieter group says both sides are wasting their time because life is just a convenient term of art, like weeds in botany, and nothing of scientific substance hangs on it.

What baffles me is that nobody disputes any facts. Everyone agrees on what viruses, prions, synthetic protocells, digital organisms, and hypothetical RNA-world replicators do; nobody's pet definition wins converts; and the theory-first camp has produced no theory of life either, which the list-makers treat as a rebuttal. My working hypothesis is that this is a disagreement about what a definition of a natural phenomenon is even for -- whether it is a discovery, a stipulation for a purpose like exoplanet biosignature detection, or just a label -- dressed up as a disagreement about the world. But I could be wrong: maybe there is a hidden empirical bet underneath, say about whether all known and unknown life shares a common physico-chemical basis, and the camps differ only in their confidence about how that bet will turn out.

Please diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, using what each camp in fact argues: verbal or substantive; about facts, about the standards a definition must meet, about which cases count as data, or about bedrock methodological intuitions neither side can defend non-circularly; whether the camps are answering different questions under one word; and whether the impasse shows the question is ill-posed or merely hard. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress here -- a distinction, a decision procedure, an empirical result, a change of question -- or argue that no progress should be expected and why. Test my working hypothesis as you go rather than assuming it.

Structure your response in four sections, in this order: "What each camp is really claiming" (about 300 words, reconstructing the strongest version of each position), "Diagnosis" (about 250 words, stating what kind of disagreement this is and evaluating my hypothesis against alternatives), "What would count as progress" (about 200 words, tied specifically to your diagnosis), and "Bottom line" (three or four sentences).
