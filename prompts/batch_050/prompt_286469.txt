I have no formal training in genetics or philosophy of science, but over the past few years I have read a fair amount of popular material on twin studies and behavioral genetics, and I have talked myself into a position that I would like stress-tested rather than confirmed.

My position is this: a heritability estimate for a trait in a given population tells us essentially nothing that matters for practice -- nothing about how malleable the trait is, nothing about whether an intervention aimed at it could work, and nothing about the causes of differences between populations. My reasons are the usual ones, as I understand them. Height is highly heritable within well-fed populations, yet average height rose dramatically with nutrition; so high heritability is compatible with large environmental effects. The variance that twin studies decompose is defined relative to the range of environments actually sampled, so the estimate is a fact about a population-and-environment pair, not about the trait itself. And because the estimate is silent about mechanisms, it cannot by itself indicate which lever, if any, would move the trait. I have also adopted a rule of thumb I invented for myself: I treat heritability claims as "policy-inert" unless the person making them can specify the actual pathway from estimate to intervention -- which, in my experience, almost never happens.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this position, since I am aware I may have overcorrected away from naive genetic determinism into an equally naive dismissiveness. In particular:

1. What observations or results would genuinely embarrass my view? For instance, would it matter if a trait's heritability turned out to be near 1 across every environment humans have ever been observed in, including environments engineered to be maximally different? Would it matter if the causal pathways were fully understood and all ran through channels we have no realistic means of altering? Does the growing ability to construct polygenic predictors that hold up within families change anything, or is that orthogonal to the variance question?

2. Where is my reasoning weakest rather than my conclusion wrong? I suspect my "policy-inert" rule does more work than my arguments license. Is there a class of cases where heritability estimates do constrain intervention policy in a way I have missed -- for example, by setting bounds on how much equalizing environments could equalize outcomes, even if they cannot tell us anything about raising everyone's absolute level?

3. Is my position falsifiable at all, or have I insulated it? When someone points to a case where heritability seems practically informative, I tend to reply that the practical work is being done by mechanistic knowledge, not the estimate itself. I notice this reply is always available to me, which makes me suspicious of it.

Please rank the defeaters you identify from decisive to merely awkward, distinguish what my position's critics would say against it from what its more careful defenders would themselves concede, and tell me whether you think the view survives. Finally, I would like you to say explicitly what would change your own assessment: which single argument or empirical finding, if you encountered it, would lead you to a different verdict about whether my position survives, and at what step your reasoning about this is most likely to break down.
