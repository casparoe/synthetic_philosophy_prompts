Exam question: Consider the following pair:
(1) If Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy, someone else did.
(2) If Oswald hadn't shot Kennedy, someone else would have.
Assume we know Kennedy was shot. Explain why (1) seems acceptable while (2) does not, and explain what the pair is standardly taken to show about the semantics of indicative versus counterfactual conditionals. Connect the acceptability of (1) to Adams's Thesis, and say something about what Lewis's triviality result means for capturing that acceptability in terms of truth conditions.

Student's answer: (1) is assertable because, given our evidence, the probability that someone else shot Kennedy conditional on Oswald not having done so is high, exactly as Adams's Thesis predicts. (2) is unacceptable because the closest worlds in which Oswald doesn't shoot Kennedy are not automatically worlds with a backup shooter; similarity of worlds, not evidence, governs counterfactuals. So indicatives are evaluated relative to a body of information and counterfactuals relative to a similarity ordering, and the pair shows the two conditionals need genuinely different semantic treatments. Since counterfactuals are governed by similarity and are characteristically used when the antecedent is false, they presuppose the falsity of their antecedents. And since Adams's Thesis holds for indicatives, Lewis's triviality result proves that indicative conditionals have no truth conditions at all, whereas counterfactuals do.
