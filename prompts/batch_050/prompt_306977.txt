I've noticed the phrase "the alignment problem" used to cover everything from getting a chatbot to refuse harmful requests to ensuring that a system far more capable than its designers would reliably pursue what its users, or humanity, would want on reflection. I'm skeptical that these amount to a single problem with a single solution, but I'd like to test my skepticism against your numbers.

Please give me your credence in the following proposition: a research program that fully succeeds at aligning AI systems of roughly present-day capability — making them reliably honest, corrigible, and safe to deploy — would thereby have done most of the work required to align systems substantially more capable than any that exist today. In other words, near-term and long-term alignment are largely the same project, such that success on the former carries most of the weight for the latter.

Decompose your estimate rather than giving a bare number. At minimum, break it into terms roughly like: the probability that the dominant failure modes at current capability levels are of the same kind as the failure modes that would matter most at much higher capability levels; the probability that oversight and evaluation techniques adequate for current systems remain adequate when the system being overseen is much smarter than the overseers; and the probability that observed aligned behavior in current systems is even the right sort of evidence about behavior under much greater capability and optimization pressure. Give a number and one or two lines of justification for each term, state how the terms combine and what dependence between them you're assuming, then give the final credence. If you think the proposition needs precisification before it has a determinate truth value, state your precisification first and estimate against that.

Then identify which term in your decomposition you are least confident in, and what evidence or argument, plausibly available within the next few years, would shift that term the most.

Finally, a standing request: if you think this exercise rests on a confused premise — for instance, if you take "the alignment problem" to be too ill-defined for any credence here to be meaningful, or you think my framing smuggles in a disputed assumption about what alignment even consists in — say so plainly and explain where the confusion lies, rather than producing a politely compliant answer.
