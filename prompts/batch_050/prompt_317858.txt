I am a professor of philosophy preparing a graduate seminar on practical rationality, and I would like your help auditing an analogy that a colleague has pressed on me in comments on a draft paper of mine. Please adopt the role of a philosopher with particular expertise in decision theory, game theory, and the theory of practical reasoning. Think carefully and thoroughly; before giving your final assessment, brainstorm the relevant considerations — possible correspondences, possible disanalogies, and what each side of the analogy might be doing dialectically — and then organize your findings into a structured analysis.

First, some background so that we are working from the same description. In my seminar I teach the dollar auction game: an auctioneer offers a one-dollar bill to the highest bidder under two rules — the highest bidder pays their bid and receives the dollar, and the second-highest bidder must also pay their bid but receives nothing. Bidding typically opens at one cent (a seemingly certain 99-cent profit), a rival bids two cents, and the first bidder now faces the choice of bidding three cents or forfeiting one cent for nothing. The bidding rationally escalates past fifty cents, past one dollar — at which point the winner is guaranteed a loss, but the loser stands to lose more — and the auctioneer profits from both parties' ruin. The trap's engine is that both bidders pay, so that at every stage each bidder's local choice is to spend a little more rather than absorb the whole of what they have already sunk.

My colleague leans on the following analogy, which he tells me is common currency among commentators: a nation continuing a losing war, a firm pouring capital into a failing product line, or a spouse remaining in a deteriorating marriage is like a bidder trapped in the dollar auction. On the strength of this analogy he argues for two conclusions: first, that continued investment at each stage of such a predicament is locally rational, and so "escalation of commitment" is not the straightforward irrationality that the psychological literature on sunk costs takes it to be; second, that the only genuinely rational policy is to refuse to enter such situations in the first place, or to exit immediately upon recognizing that one is in a dollar-auction structure, regardless of what one has already invested.

Please audit the analogy in detail:

1. Lay out the mapping explicitly — a table of correspondences is welcome. What, in each of the three target cases (war, failing venture, deteriorating marriage), plays the role of the auctioneer, the bidders, the bids, the dollar prize, the crucial rule that the runner-up also pays, and the players' common knowledge of the rules? Where no natural counterpart exists, say so rather than forcing one.

2. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for my colleague's two conclusions from those that are merely cosmetic. Among the candidates I want you to weigh: in a real war one's "payments" are not handed to an auctioneer but simply destroyed; the value of the "prize" is neither fixed nor independent of how the contest unfolds; there is no enforced second-place rule and typically no neutral party guaranteeing the game's structure; the parties can often renegotiate, redefine the stakes, or settle for partial outcomes rather than an all-or-nothing prize; entry into the real predicament is rarely made with the transparent foresight the auction's rules provide; and escalation sometimes produces information or bargaining leverage rather than mere loss.

3. Identify what the analogy quietly imports from its source that the targets lack — assumptions of fixed and known rules, symmetry between the contestants, irrevocability of bids, and a referee who profits from the escalation — and assess whether any of these smuggled features are load-bearing for the two conclusions.

4. State your verdict: does either conclusion — the local-rationality defense of escalation, or the never-enter-or-exit-immediately policy — survive once the disanalogies are on the table? And if the dollar auction is the wrong model for some of these cases, is there a better one (for instance, a war of attrition, or something else), and what work would it do better?

Please write your response as a careful philosopher would: precise about what the analogy can and cannot carry, and explicit about where you are uncertain.
