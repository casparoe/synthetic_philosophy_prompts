For our philosophy club's unit on Buddhist thought, a classmate circulated a summary of Nāgārjuna that he pieced together from a few online sources, and before we use it in discussion I want to know how much of it to trust. Here is what he wrote:

"Nāgārjuna's central teaching is that all things are empty, which means that nothing truly exists -- not the self, not physical objects, not even the Buddha's own teachings, all of which he treats as illusions. His method is the tetralemma: for any claim whatsoever, he argues that we must reject it, reject its negation, reject both together, and reject neither, so that ordinary logic simply collapses when applied to reality. And since asserting any view would commit him to treating something as real, Nāgārjuna deliberately avoids putting forward any position of his own. His practical conclusion is that suffering is empty too, so there is ultimately nothing to be done about it, and realizing this is what liberation amounts to."

I have a nagging sense that the first sentence goes too far -- that "empty" is not supposed to mean "does not exist" -- but I cannot articulate why, and to me the rest of the summary reads as internally consistent. Could you go through it and tell me what a scholar of Madhyamaka would accept, what they would wince at as a common simplification, and what is just wrong? In particular:

- Is my classmate right that emptiness amounts to the claim that nothing exists, or is he conflating two different claims?
- Is the tetralemma really a rejection of logic in general, or does it have a narrower target than his description suggests?
- Is the claim that Nāgārjuna puts forward no position of his own fair, given that he plainly wrote arguments?
- Does the practical conclusion about suffering and liberation follow from whatever survives of the accurate parts, or does it depend entirely on the misreadings?

I am not asking for a bibliography -- just a careful audit of the summary as written, so I know what to correct before our meeting.
