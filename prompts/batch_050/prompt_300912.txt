So I've been going down the rabbit hole on Goodman's new riddle of induction (the grue thing, where an emerald is grue iff examined before some future time t and green, or not examined before t and blue) and I've collected what I think are all the standard responses. Here's my list, with quick glosses:

1. Entrenchment (Goodman's own answer): "green" is a historically entrenched predicate in our inductive practice and "grue" isn't, so only projections of entrenched predicates are valid.
2. The positional-predicate reply: "grue" smuggles in a reference to a particular time, so grue-predicates are positional rather than purely qualitative, and induction only applies to qualitative ones. (And yes, I know about Goodman's symmetry comeback: if you start with grue and bleen, "green" is the one that looks positional, since green = grue and examined before t, or bleen and not examined before t. So the reply needs something to break the symmetry, and just asserting green is the basic one doesn't do it.)
3. Natural kinds / real structure of the world: green tracks a genuine natural kind and grue doesn't, and induction is only legitimate over genuine kinds.
4. Bayesianism: give grue-hypotheses lower priors (e.g. on grounds of complexity) than the green-hypothesis, so the same evidence confirms green more. No special theory of projection needed.
5. The evolutionary/reliabilist angle: induction using stable, non-time-indexed predicates has a track record of working, and creatures that used grue-ish predicates would've been selected against, so our confidence in green over grue is justified by the past reliability of the method itself. (I know this risks circularity, feel free to weigh in on that too.)

For instance, "natural kinds are just whatever's entrenched" would count as collapsing 3 into 1, so no.
