A colleague recently walked me through the following argument, and although I find its conclusion unacceptable, I cannot locate a fault in the reasoning. I would like to know exactly which step fails — or, if none does, which premise is cheapest to surrender, and what surrendering it costs.

Let A₀ be an outcome in which one person undergoes severe torture for one hour. Construct a sequence of outcomes A₀, A₁, A₂, ..., A_k as follows: each A_{i+1} is like A_i except that the number of sufferers is doubled and the intensity of each person's pain is reduced by a minute amount — as small a decrement as one likes, so long as the suffering at each stage remains a genuine, noticeable harm. With small enough decrements and enough steps, the sequence terminates at A_k, an outcome in which an astronomically large number of people each suffer nothing worse than a mild tension headache.

(1) For every i, A_{i+1} is worse than A_i. Rationale: doubling the number of people who suffer, in exchange for a reduction in each one's suffering so slight that no sufferer could reliably tell the difference, clearly makes things worse on balance. An impartial spectator choosing which world to bring about would prefer A_i to A_{i+1} at every step.

(2) "Worse than" is transitive: if A_{i+1} is worse than A_i, and A_{i+2} is worse than A_{i+1}, then A_{i+2} is worse than A_i, and likewise along the whole chain.

(3) Therefore A_k is worse than A₀: a world in which vast multitudes endure mild headaches is worse than a world in which a single person is tortured.

(4) If (3) is true, then, other things equal, a health system with limited resources should sometimes divert funds away from preventing torture-grade suffering and toward treating mild headaches en masse.

(5) But that conclusion is absurd; no acceptable moral outlook can rank a world of headaches below a world containing torture.

(6) My colleague concludes that (2) must be false — the "worse than" relation is not transitive after all, and the assumption of transitivity built into standard accounts of rational choice has to go.

I am reluctant to follow him to (6). Abandoning transitivity seems to invite money-pump cycles, in which an agent can be induced to trade in circles while paying a fee at every step, and I do not see how any workable theory of rational or moral choice survives that. Yet I am equally unwilling to accept (3) and (4). Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? And if it is valid, which premise should I give up, why does that premise look so plausible, and what exactly am I paying for each of the available ways out?
