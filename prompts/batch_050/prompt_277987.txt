I am a retired molecular biologist who has spent the past year reading philosophy of medicine, and I keep circling back to the naturalism–normativism dispute about the concept of disease: Boorse's biostatistical account and Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction analysis on one side, normativists who hold that disease attributions are irreducibly value-laden on the other. What puzzles me is the shape of the deadlock. Both sides accept the same physiological facts; both sides have sophisticated replies to the stock counterexamples (homosexuality's former classification, drapetomania, infertility, the ageing process); and as far as I can tell the literature has recycled the same moves for roughly four decades without anyone conceding ground. My tentative hypothesis is that the two camps are not really disagreeing about what disease is, but about what a definition of disease is for -- the naturalists wanting a notion that does descriptive work in biology, the normativists wanting one that constrains what medicine may legitimately treat -- and that the impasse persists because this difference in purpose is rarely stated outright. I would like you to assess whether that diagnosis is right, or whether the disagreement is genuinely substantive (about whether biological function is itself an objective, mind-independent matter), or merely verbal in a way neither side has noticed. Please say what each side actually commits to, given the diagnosis you settle on, what would count as progress -- a distinction, an agreed test case, a reframing of the question -- and be explicit about how confident you are in your diagnosis, singling out the single claim you are least sure of. Structure your answer in four sections, in this order: "The Dispute as I Have Described It" (checking my framing, about 100 words), "Diagnosis" (your main analysis, about 300 words), "What Would Count as Progress" (about 150 words), and "Confidence and Weakest Point" (about 75 words).
