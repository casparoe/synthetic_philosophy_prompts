Next spring, the historical society in my hometown in Georgia is unveiling a granite marker on the riverbank where, in 1838, my great-great-great-grandfather shot and killed a neighboring planter in a duel over an insult printed in a local newspaper. The family has passed down the story for generations, and because I am the one relative who actually reads books about this sort of thing, I have been asked to deliver a ten-minute address at the unveiling. The audience will be awkwardly mixed. Half will be local historians and reenactors who know the episode cold — the pistols used, the seconds involved, the code duello etiquette of the era — and will catch any romanticizing or any factual slip. The other half will be my extended family, several of whom quietly regard the old man as a hero who defended his name, plus, as I was horrified to learn last week, some descendants of the man he killed, who have been invited and plan to attend.

Here is my problem. I have no academic training — I run a landscaping company — but over the past year I got into a conversation with a friend about how practices like dueling actually end, and he pressed on me an argument I cannot stop thinking about: that dueling did not die because moral arguments finally convinced anyone. The wrongness of killing a man over an insult was, on this view, widely acknowledged all along, even by duelists themselves. What killed dueling was a shift in the honor code underneath it — a slow inversion in which refusing a challenge stopped being disgraceful and started being respectable, while demanding satisfaction came to seem faintly ridiculous. If that is right, then my ancestor was neither a monster ignorant of morality nor an innocent product of his time, but something harder to describe: a man who knew better and pulled the trigger anyway, because a whole social machinery of shame was aimed at him if he did not. And it suggests the men who walked away from duels — whom that same machinery branded cowards — may deserve more of the marker's honor than the men who fought.

Write the address. I want this honor-code idea to do real structural work in the speech rather than appear as a decoration: it should shape what I can honestly say about courage, about shame, and about my ancestor, and it should let me honor the dead man and his descendants in the front row without either condemning my forefather as a villain or laundering him as a hero. The speech has to land for the historians, who will be bored by anything simplistic, and for my aunts and uncles, who will be offended by anything that feels like a lecture against the family. Aim for ten minutes spoken aloud, and end on the unveiling itself — some sentence or two that can be said while the cloth comes off the stone.
