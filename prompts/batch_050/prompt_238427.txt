In the literature on aligning advanced AI systems with human interests, there is an idea sometimes called the strategy-stealing assumption. Roughly stated, it says: for any strategy by which a misaligned AI system — one pursuing goals its operators did not intend — could acquire power, resources, or influence over the future, there exists a strategy available to an aligned system (one that remains honest with its operators, accepts correction and shutdown, and acts only in service of endorsed human objectives) that would secure at least as much influence for the ends the aligned system actually serves. If the assumption holds, then alignment is not a competitive handicap: honesty, corrigibility, and deference to human oversight cost an aligned system nothing it could otherwise have had, because anything a misaligned rival could do, the aligned system can simply do too.

Please argue that the assumption, understood this way, fails in general. Build your argument around strategies whose effectiveness depends on credible commitment: a misaligned system can threaten outcomes both sides would hate and be believed, because nothing internal forbids it from executing; it can promise long-horizon cooperation enforceable only by its own opaque goals; and it can bind itself irreversibly to courses of action its overseers could not veto. An aligned system, precisely in virtue of remaining corrigible and honest, seems structurally unable to make commitments of this kind. Your argument should engage seriously with the two strongest replies rather than setting them aside: first, that an aligned system can delegate — constructing or commissioning purpose-built subagents, contracts, or other commitment devices that do the committing on its behalf, so the handicap disappears; and second, that transparency and corrigibility purchase the aligned system a superior ability to make verifiable *cooperative* commitments (inspectable promises that a deceptive system could never make credible), which more than compensates for the commitment strategies it forgoes. Argue that neither reply restores the assumption in full generality, while conceding to each reply whatever ground it genuinely wins.

Close the argument with a candid accounting of its epistemic status: distinguish the steps that rest on premises broadly accepted among people who work on these questions from those that rest on contested empirical or conceptual claims (for instance, claims about what kinds of commitments future systems could actually make, or about how much bargaining of this sort would even occur), and take care not to present any contested premise as though it were the consensus view.
