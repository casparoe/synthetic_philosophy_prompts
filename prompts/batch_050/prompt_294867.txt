In the Preface to Henry Sidgwick’s The Methods of Ethics, he writes: “I have wished to put aside temporarily the urgent need which we all feel of finding and adopting the true method of determining what we ought to do; and to consider simply what conclusions will be rationally reached if we start with certain ethical premises, and with what degree of certainty and precision.” Taking that methodological stance seriously, audit this package for Sidgwick’s dualism of practical reason: (1) rational egoism and impartial utilitarianism are each internally coherent methods; (2) when they conflict, there is no further rational principle that settles the conflict; (3) nevertheless, in every concrete case there is exactly one thing the agent ought all-things-considered to do; (4) the dualism is only epistemic, because Sidgwick has merely bracketed practice for method; and (5) intertheoretic expected choiceworthiness can resolve hard cases without assuming the two methods are commensurable. Identify the genuine tensions, order them by severity, distinguish any merely apparent ones, and state the cheapest revision and what it costs.
