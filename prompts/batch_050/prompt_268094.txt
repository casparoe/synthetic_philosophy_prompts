I'm drafting a chapter for a general-audience book on scientific reasoning, and I've leaned heavily on the detective analogy for inference to the best explanation: the scientist confronting data is like a detective confronting clues; hypotheses are suspects; accepting the theory that best explains the evidence is like identifying the culprit; background knowledge plays the role of the detective's familiarity with poisons, railway timetables, and human motives. I used the analogy to argue that IBE is not some exotic philosopher's invention but a rigorous version of reasoning we all perform, and therefore that choosing the best explanation is rational and generally truth-conducive. If we trust the great fictional detectives, I wrote, we should trust the method. A colleague who read the draft pushed back hard. She says the analogy proves too much and collapses exactly where I need it to hold: classic detective fiction is constructed so that the suspect list is closed and the clues are guaranteed to yield a unique solution, since there is an author who arranged it that way, whereas real inquiry faces hypotheses nobody has conceived yet and evidence nobody curated to point anywhere. She also claims that the detective in a well-made mystery typically reasons by eliminating possibilities until only one remains, which is a different inferential pattern from ranking live hypotheses by how well they would explain the data.

Could you audit the analogy for me? Please state the mapping explicitly in continuous prose: what corresponds to clues, to the suspect list, to the culprit, to the detective's stock of background knowledge, to the scene of the accusation. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for my intended conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. In particular, does the closed suspect list of the country-house mystery do essential work in making the detective's inference look reliable, and is there any real scientific analogue to it? Does the existence of an author who planted the clues undermine the analogy, or can that be set aside as a feature of fiction rather than of the inferential situation? Where in the mapping does the difference between eliminating the impossible and ranking the surviving options by explanatory quality show up, and which of those two is what IBE actually recommends? Relatedly, is the detective picking the likeliest suspect given prior odds, or the one whose guilt would make the most satisfying sense of the clues, and how much hangs on that distinction? Finally, once the disanalogies are on the table, does my conclusion survive that IBE inherits the detective's rational credentials, and if it does not, is there a better everyday analogy I could reach for, or does IBE only look compelling when dressed in detective clothes?
