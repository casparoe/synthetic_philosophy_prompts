I've been reading the rationalist blogs for a long time -- the Sequences, the Scott Alexander corpus, the later coordination-and-inadequacy literature -- and the Moloch framing is probably the idea from that world I invoke most often in ordinary conversation. Recently my reading group tried to convert it from an evocative diagnosis into a crisp piece of moral philosophy, and we produced an argument that seems to prove too much. I'll lay it out the way we converged on it. (1) An action is wrong only if the world is worse, on net, for its having been performed rather than not; this is just the thought that moral assessment of acts tracks their counterfactual contribution. (2) In a mature multipolar trap, any individual participant is fully replaceable: if I decline to optimize my content for engagement, refuse the performance-enhancing drug, or work a humane number of hours in a tournament profession, my slot is filled within months by someone with fewer scruples, and the aggregate dynamics -- the escalation, the degradation -- proceed exactly as before. (3) So my participation in such a trap is not wrong. (4) Premise (2) holds for every participant in every trap of this kind, so no one does anything wrong by participating in a multipolar trap. (5) If no one's participation is wrong, then no one is obligated to build, lobby for, or comply with the coordination mechanisms that would dissolve the trap, since obligations to prevent bad outcomes presuppose that someone is doing something wrong in producing them. (6) Therefore the correct moral verdict on arms races, attention-economy degradation, publish-or-perish academia, and beauty-filter escalation is silence: nobody is blameworthy, and nobody is on the hook to fix any of it.

Step (6) strikes me as absurd -- some races to the bottom are genuinely catastrophic, and a morality that can find no wrongdoing anywhere in their production and no obligation anywhere to stop them seems to have gone off the rails. But each step felt locally compelling when we walked through it, and the whole thing has the disquieting feature that it seems to follow from premises about causation and replaceability that are almost definitional of what makes these traps traps in the first place. Where does it go wrong, if it does? If forced to choose, I'd most like to keep (1) and (3), because the counterfactual picture of wrongdoing seems like load-bearing common sense and (3) honestly matches my intuition about, say, the individual grad student who starts optimizing for citation count. I'm prepared to give up (2), (4), or (5) -- but I'd like to know exactly which step fails, why it looked fine, and what the alternatives cost: whether the honest response is some Parfit-style account of collective harms where imperceptible contributions can still be wrong, a sui generis obligation to support coordination that floats free of individual wrongdoing, a rule-consequentialist repair, or an empirical denial that traps really are replaceable at the margin. Please don't just gesture at the conclusion being bad; tell me which numbered step you'd excise and what I'm paying for the surgery.
