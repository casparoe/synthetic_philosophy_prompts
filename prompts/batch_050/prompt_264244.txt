Last night, over far too much coffee, I had a long and unresolved argument with a friend about the thirteenth-century Zen master Dōgen and his doctrine of "being-time" (uji). My friend insists the doctrine amounts to nothing more than a poetic dressing-up of the familiar injunction to be present and pay attention — mindfulness in medieval costume. I suspect there is a genuine metaphysics there, stranger and more interesting than my friend allows. I want you to adjudicate.

You are a scholar of Buddhist philosophy and comparative metaphysics, equally at home with Madhyamaka, Yogācāra, and Western philosophy of time. Think carefully and slowly. Before committing to any scores, spend a short brainstorming pass noting which features of Dōgen's thinking each candidate reading captures and which it distorts; then produce the sections requested below.

Since you may not have the Shōbōgenzō at hand, here is everything you may assume about the doctrine, stated in my own paraphrase rather than quotation. Dōgen treats being and time as one: every existent thing is a time, and there is no time apart from existent things. His famous image is that firewood becomes ash, and ash does not return to firewood; yet one should not think of ash as the future of firewood or firewood as the past of ash. Each has its own "dharma-position," with its own before and after, and these positions are cut off from one another even though they also constitute one continuous passage. Time is sometimes said to "flow," but the flowing is itself a time, and beings flow within it only because each is a time. Practice, on this picture, is not a matter of escaping time but of fully actualizing the time one is.

With only that material to work from, evaluate the following four readings of uji:

Reading A (the therapeutic presentist): The doctrine is practical counsel dressed in metaphysical language; its entire content is that we suffer by living in the past and future and are liberated by inhabiting the present. Claims about being and time being identical are rhetoric, not ontology.

Reading B (the identity theorist): The doctrine is strict metaphysics: "being" and "time" are two names for a single reality, so that every thing is a time and time is nothing over and above the totality of things. Impermanence follows trivially and is not the point; the point is the identity claim itself.

Reading C (the dharma-position structuralist): The core of the doctrine is the claim that each moment is a complete, self-contained position with its own before and after, so that time is not a river carrying things along but a sequence of discrete standings, each absolute. The firewood-and-ash image is the load-bearing argument, and what the doctrine rules out is precisely the idea of a persisting subject traveling through moments.

Reading D (the soteriological perspectivist): The doctrine is neither descriptive metaphysics nor advice, but a re-description of the world from within practice: statements about being-time are performative utterances that enact, rather than report, the practitioner's mode of being. Asking whether they are literally true mistakes their genre.

Score each reading from 0 to 10 on two dimensions separately: fidelity to the paraphrased material above, and philosophical depth as a freestanding position. Then rank the four readings overall, and finally rule on my dispute with my friend: does the "mindfulness in costume" verdict survive, and if not, where exactly does it fail? Do not introduce texts, teachers, or secondary literature beyond what I have given you; if you are tempted to cite something, argue instead.

Please structure your reply in exactly these sections, in this order:
1. Scorecard — a compact table: one row per reading, one column per dimension, plus an overall rank; about 10 lines.
2. Assessment — roughly 120 words per reading, A through D, explaining each score, including what the reading gets right even if it fails overall.
3. The hardest objection — about 100 words on the strongest single objection to your top-ranked reading, taken seriously rather than dismissed.
4. Verdict on my argument with my friend — about 80 words, stating plainly which of us is closer to the truth and on what grounds.
