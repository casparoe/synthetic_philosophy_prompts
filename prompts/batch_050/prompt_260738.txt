I am drafting the threat-model section of a research agenda on scalable oversight, and I need a probability estimate with its derivation laid out rather than a bare number.

Background: Debate is a proposed method for extracting truthful answers from AI systems more capable than their human evaluators. Two systems argue opposite sides of a question before a human judge, each able to challenge the other's claims and demand that steps be broken down further; the hope is that the honest side has a structural advantage, because exposing a flaw in a false argument should be easier than concealing one. The central worry is the obfuscated-argument problem: a dishonest debater might construct arguments whose conclusions are false but whose flaws are concentrated in steps that are substantially easier to produce than to detect, so that no line of cross-examination a human judge can actually follow will locate them.

The proposition I want your credence in: by the end of 2035, the obfuscation thesis holds — in controlled evaluations where two frontier AI systems debate questions with experimenter-known ground truth before competent human judges, a debater assigned the false side (while knowing the true answer) wins at least 60% of the time against an equally resourced honest opponent. Treat the proposition as true if such a result would obtain in a well-run trial at that date, regardless of whether anyone in fact runs it.

Please decompose your credence into the following terms, giving a number and one or two sentences of justification for each:

1. Existence of obfuscation. The probability that, in the domains of interest (technical, scientific, and normative questions at the edge of human competence), there exist false-but-persuasive arguments whose flaws are much cheaper to construct than to verify — that is, a generation-verification asymmetry that grows rather than shrinks as capability increases.

2. Deliberate exploitability. Conditional on (1), the probability that a system only modestly superhuman in the relevant domain can find such arguments on demand — that constructing obfuscation is a tractable search problem rather than one requiring insight far beyond the debater's actual level.

3. Failure of mitigations. Conditional on (1) and (2), the probability that the standard remedies — recursive decomposition of contested steps into sub-debates, cross-examination of the opposing debater, adversarial training of judges, amplification through ensembles of judges — fail to restore the honest side's advantage without opening new attack surfaces (for instance, obfuscation shifted into the choice of which sub-claims to contest).

4. Robustness against the truth's structural advantage. The probability that the dishonest side's edge survives symmetry of capability — that the honest debater's genuine assets (true claims are mutually consistent under arbitrary probing; false ones must be actively maintained) do not offset the obfuscator's edge at the relevant capability levels.

Combine these into a final credence, stating the combination rule and flagging any dependencies between the terms that would make a naive product misleading. Then: (a) identify the term to which your final number is most sensitive; (b) describe one piece of empirical evidence obtainable with current or near-term systems that would move that term the most, and in which direction; (c) my own credence in the proposition is 0.30 — if yours differs by more than 0.15, diagnose where the disagreement most plausibly lives, naming the specific term and the background assumption that does the work.
