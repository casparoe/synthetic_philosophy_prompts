Ok so I run a small theology reading group (mixed crowd — a couple grad students, mostly sharp amateurs, some Muslim some not) and we're about to spend three sessions on the Islamic Euthyphro: does God command what's good, or is it good because God commands it, i.e. the whole Mu'tazilite vs. Ash'arite fight with the Maturidis hovering somewhere in the middle.

I want you to act as an expert in classical kalam who has actually taught this to non-specialists. Take your time and think it through carefully — sketch the logical space of possible answers before you build anything, then construct the instrument.

Here's the deliverable: a decision tree that sorts a member of my group into a position on the divine command question. Start from the one premise everyone in the room grants — that God is wise, just, and does nothing pointless — and each node after that should be a single question, phrased as a concrete case someone can answer WITHOUT already knowing which school it funnels them toward. No node like "do you think good and evil are knowable by reason?" phrased in the jargon; instead something like: a man grows up alone on an island and never encounters any scripture or prophet — does he wrong God by never feeling gratitude? Or: could God command pointless torture of an infant, and would it thereby become good? Or: is God obliged to do whatever is most beneficial for his creatures (the aslah question)?

Requirements:

- The leaves must include classical Mu'tazila, classical Ash'ariyya, and Maturidi at minimum. Beyond that, put in any modern position you think actually gets defended, and at least one combination of answers that nobody holds — and diagnose whether that box is empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just historically unexplored.
- One of your nodes has to dig into what "unjust" even means when applied to God — the move where injustice is defined as disposing of something you have no right over, which makes divine injustice impossible by definition since everything is God's property. I want that treated as a genuine fork (does the definition settle anything or just relocate the question?), not as a footnote.
- Somewhere the tree should force people to confront whether "God is wise" constrains what God can will, or just redescribes whatever God happens to will. I suspect this is the real crux of the whole debate and most presentations paper over it.
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate. My hunch is the Maturidis collapse into a moderated Mu'tazilism on some answer patterns — tell me if your tree confirms that or proves me wrong.
- Each leaf gets: the name, a one-line summary, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment (the thing a defender squirms at).

Keep the whole thing compact enough to fit on one printed page — maybe 5 to 7 nodes. And don't just give me the tree: after it, briefly note which node in your tree you think does the most work, i.e. where the debate really turns, and whether any of the standard positions seems to be dodging the hard fork rather than answering it.
