My epistemology professor runs this friday game where she rules on whether outright belief (not just high confidence) is rational in some case. Her verdicts so far:

1. Fair lottery, a million tickets, all you know about ticket #888,888 is its number. Believing "this ticket will lose" = NOT rational.
2. Your old barometer, right about rain 9 times out of 10 over the years, says rain tomorrow. Believing it will rain = rational.
3. A historian believes each of the 500 claims in her new book on solid archival evidence, and also believes at least one of them is false. Verdict: believing each individual claim is rational, and believing the "at least one is false" bit is rational too.
4. But that same historian should NOT flat-out believe "every single claim in my book is true," even though each conjunct is rationally believed.
5. Someone who believes of each ticket in a 10,000-ticket lottery that it will lose, while admitting one ticket has to win = irrational, the whole set is a mess.

New case for next week: a jar holds 1000 thoroughly mixed pills, 999 inert sugar, exactly 1 lethal poison. You draw one at random. Is it rational to outright believe "this pill is safe"?

Act as an expert epistemologist and think carefully here. Start by brainstorming: give me 2-3 general rules that are each consistent with verdicts 1-5 (note that a plain probability threshold rule seems dead on arrival given 1 vs 2, so go deeper). Then say what each rule predicts for the pill case, give your best guess at her actual verdict with reasoning, and name the one further case that would best distinguish the surviving rules if she answered it.
