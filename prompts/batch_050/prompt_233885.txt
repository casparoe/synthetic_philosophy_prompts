Eight months ago my father, who is seventy-three, was diagnosed with early-stage Alzheimer's disease. Questions I once treated as seminar material — what a person is, what survives change, what is lost when a mind erodes — have become uncomfortably concrete for me, and last week two close friends of mine, Daniel and Ruth, got into an extended argument about them over dinner at my house. Neither would concede, and I found myself genuinely unable to settle who was right. I would like a careful adjudication.

Daniel has studied Buddhist philosophy for years and takes the doctrine of no-self seriously. He opened by saying that my father's condition is, in a grim way, a demonstration of what the doctrine already teaches: there never was a unified, enduring self in my father — only a stream of bodily and mental processes held together by memory and habit — and so the thought that "he is disappearing" rests on a confusion. Nobody was ever there to disappear. What the disease changes, he said, is only the rate and manner of a process that was happening all along.

Ruth replied that this equivocates. Even granting there is no metaphysical substratum of selfhood, she said, nothing in that concession touches the obvious difference between an intact psychological continuity — memory, character, recognition of loved ones — and a broken one. The doctrine denies a certain theory of what a person is; it does not deny the difference between a person and a former person.

Daniel answered that Ruth was trying to grieve the loss of something she admits never existed. If the continuity she prizes was always just a convenient fiction, then consistency requires treating the disease as ordinary change, merely accelerated: my father changed from day to day before the diagnosis too, and no one called that a tragedy. He also offered what I think he regards as a consolation: if the tragedy of the disease is located anywhere, it is in us, the witnesses, who cling to the fiction — not in my father, since there is no one for whom the loss is suffered, only moments of experience, some distressed and some not.

Ruth called this last move monstrous, then refined the objection. She said Daniel's parity argument — dementia as ordinary change, only faster — proves too much: by the same reasoning, sleep and ordinary forgetting would be losses of the same kind, and he plainly does not believe that. So he must himself accept that continuities come in degrees and that the degrees matter, which was her point from the start. The difference, she suggested, is directional: ordinary life adds pages to a book; this disease removes them. And she rejected the consolation outright, arguing that the badness of the disease for my father does not require a metaphysical self — it requires only that his earlier and later moments of experience once belonged to a single ongoing project of a life, and that the project is being dismantled. She added, citing something she half-remembered, that even the Buddhist tradition Daniel invokes never flatly denied conventional persons; it spoke of a continuum, and presumably the tradition did not think a continuum being cut short was nothing.

At one point I mentioned that my father, in a lucid moment a few weeks ago, said to me, "I'm still in here." Daniel took this as a man mistaking the grammar of his language for a fact; Ruth took it as the only testimony in the room that actually counts.

Here is what I am asking for. Do not just declare a winner; go through the exchange and tell me who is right about each specific move. In particular: (1) Does no-self, consistently held, imply that no one is lost in dementia, or is Daniel conflating the denial of a substratum with the denial of everything Ruth means by a person? (2) Does Ruth's rebuttal of the parity argument succeed, or can Daniel accommodate degrees of continuity without giving up his conclusion? (3) On the badness of the disease for the patient himself, whose account is more coherent — Daniel's appeal to disconnected moments of experience, or Ruth's appeal to a dismantled life-project — and does Ruth's notion of a project smuggle back the very self she disavowed? (4) Is Ruth right about the Buddhist tradition, or is her half-remembered point about conventional persons and continuations actually compatible with Daniel's position? And finally: what, if anything, are both of them missing?
