I have been circulating the following argument in my philosophy of science seminar, and I have come to suspect that it proves too much, though I cannot locate the fault. (1) Whatever a thought experiment establishes, it establishes without new observation; that is what makes it a thought experiment rather than an experiment. (2) If a conclusion is established without new observation, then accepting it adds nothing to one's knowledge of the world that was not already contained in one's prior beliefs. (3) So thought experiments add nothing to our knowledge of the world. (4) The reasoning in (1)–(3) invokes no feature of thought experiments that mathematical proofs lack: proofs likewise proceed without new observation, by unpacking prior commitments. (5) So if (3) holds, proofs add nothing to our knowledge either — a student who works through a proof of the Pythagorean theorem learns nothing she did not already know. (6) That is absurd. (7) Yet something has to give, since Galileo's falling bodies, Stevin's chain, and Einstein's elevator are celebrated precisely for having taught us physics. (8) I conclude that one of (1), (2), and (4) must be rejected, but each looks innocent on its face.

If I had to keep one of them, I would keep (4); the parallel with proof strikes me as the argument's one irreproachable step. Tell me which premise you would in fact reject, why it appeared innocent, and what rejecting it costs. In pricing the alternatives, address at least the three standard positions in the literature: the view that thought experiments are merely disguised arguments, the view that they afford a quasi-perceptual grasp of laws of nature, and the view that they yield knowledge by exposing incoherence in our concepts or background theories. Finally, say whether your diagnosis survives a shift in target: does it cover thought experiments that establish possibility claims, such as Maxwell's demon, as well as those that purport to establish laws?
