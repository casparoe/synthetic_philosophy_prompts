I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been turning over a problem I ran into at work. We maintain a recommendation system that is retrained nightly on fresh data, so the model in production on any given day is a version no human ever directly inspected. Recently it surfaced content that caused real harm to a user, and when our team did the postmortem, every honest answer to "whose fault was this?" came out weirdly diluted: the original designers shipped a reasonable architecture, the data pipeline team followed standard practices, the on-call engineer responded correctly, and the model that caused the harm didn't exist until 3 a.m. that morning. It felt like the responsibility evaporated somewhere in the nightly retraining cycle, not because anyone was let off the hook, but because there was no one left standing in the right causal position.

Write a dialogue between two interlocutors: one who argues that cases like this reveal a genuine "responsibility gap" — that with sufficiently autonomous, continuously updating systems, there can be harms for which no one is appropriately responsible, and that this is a real moral cost we should count against deploying such systems — and one who argues that the gap is illusory, that responsibility is a social practice we assign rather than a quantity we discover, and that the felt absence of a culprit is just our existing practices lagging behind a new technical setup. Give both sides their strongest case: have the gap-skeptic press hard on what the gap-theorist thinks responsibility even is such that it can fail to exist, and have the gap-theorist explain why simply deciding to assign responsibility (to the company, the deployer, the nearest engineer) can be legitimate as policy yet still leave something morally unaddressed. They should respond to each other's actual arguments rather than restate their opening positions, and neither should be made to look naive. Ground the dispute in the nightly-retraining scenario rather than in self-driving cars or weapons systems, and feel free to let the characters introduce whatever distinctions (causal, moral, legal, forward-looking, backward-looking) they need.

Format the dialogue as exactly five numbered exchanges (each exchange being one speaking turn per character), with no introduction or conclusion outside the numbered items.
