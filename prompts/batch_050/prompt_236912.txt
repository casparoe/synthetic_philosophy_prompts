I am a PhD student in philosophy of science working on the metaphysics of laws, and I want to understand where the debate between Humeanism and anti-Humeanism actually makes a concrete difference beyond general metaphysical taste. To focus the question, I have constructed the following scenario. Imagine a deterministic world in which a single particle, exactly once in the entire history of that universe, undergoes an unusual decay: it never happens again, and it is not captured by any simple, general regularity present in the world's history of local matters of fact (what Humeans call the mosaic). On a best-systems account, it seems this event must either be forced under some ad hoc and inelegant generalization or left entirely lawless. An anti-Humean, by contrast — whether a theorist who takes laws to govern, or a dispositional essentialist who grounds laws in the natures of properties — could hold that the event is covered by a law regardless of its uniqueness, since the law's obtaining does not depend on how often it is instantiated.

Please explain the most important implications of adopting each of the three positions (Humean best-systems, governing-law realism, and dispositional essentialism) for how one should treat such one-off events. In particular, address: (1) whether the decay can be explained by a law at all on each view, and what kind of explanation is available if not; (2) which counterfactuals about the particle — for instance, that a duplicate particle in duplicate circumstances would also decay — each view entitles us to accept, and on what grounds; and (3) the epistemological consequences, that is, when (if ever) inhabitants of that world would be justified in believing in a law that is instantiated exactly once, and whether the metaphysical choice between the views makes any practical difference to how they should weigh such evidence. I am less interested in a general survey of the motivations for each position than in where, precisely, their verdicts diverge in this kind of case.

Finally, please be explicit about where your analysis is most contestable: which single consideration or argument — for example, about whether laws must play an explanatory role in order to count as laws, or whether the very idea of a single-instance law is coherent — would most plausibly overturn your verdicts, and why you nonetheless do not find it decisive.
