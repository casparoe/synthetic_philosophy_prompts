I teach an undergraduate course in practical ethics (about 24 students, mostly second-years, one 75-minute session available), and next week we're doing the unit on everyday conventions: tipping, queueing, jaywalking, which side of the escalator to stand on. My students reliably agree that murder is wrong but can't decide whether it's wrong to cut a queue when no one is obviously harmed, or whether a tourist is obliged to follow a local custom they find pointless (say, tipping 20% in the US when tipping doesn't exist in their home country). I'd like you to design an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of these questions rather than just trade intuitions about them.

Here's the shape I'm hoping for. I want the class split into two groups that separately face the same recurring coordination problem with real (small) stakes — something like a scarce resource to be allocated each round, or a payoff that depends on everyone picking compatible options — and I want each group to be left alone long enough that a convention emerges on its own, without the facilitator declaring rules. Crucially, the two groups should settle into different conventions (you can seed this subtly if needed, but the students shouldn't notice the seeding). Then comes the phase I care most about: a structured exchange where a few members of each group are sent as "visitors" into the other group and must decide whether to keep their own habit, adopt the local one, or hold out on principle, while the hosts decide how much enforcement — dirty looks, point penalties, exclusion — is warranted. I'd like one additional wrinkle: at least one of the emergent conventions should turn out to be quietly unfair to a subset of players (e.g., it systematically advantages whoever arrived first), so that in the debrief we can ask whether obligation tracks the convention even when the convention is bad, and whether visitors owe more or less deference than locals in that case.

Please give me the full package: the exact rules and materials, a payoff scheme that works without real money, a minute-by-minute timeline that fits the 75 minutes including setup, how to run the visitor exchange without it collapsing into chaos, and a debrief plan that maps specific things that happened onto the philosophical questions (is a convention a solution to a coordination problem, and does that ground obligation? does mere expectation create duties? are visitors bound by norms they never consented to?). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I'm guessing students will either negotiate explicit rules instead of letting norms emerge, or the visitors will instantly conform and kill the interesting tension — and how to handle it. Finally, be honest about which feature of the real phenomenon the activity can't reproduce; my suspicion is that no classroom game can capture the diffuse, victimless wrongness of jaywalking against a red light at an empty intersection, and I'd like a line for the debrief that names that gap rather than papering over it.
