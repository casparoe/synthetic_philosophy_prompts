I'm drafting a paper arguing that maximal moral goodness -- being a moral saint in roughly Wolf's sense, someone whose every action is as morally good as possible -- is not actually desirable, even for someone who could pull it off. The arguments I've got so far: (1) the crowding-out argument, that a saint can't develop non-moral talents, interests, or a personal style of life, so sainthood costs you a well-rounded self; (2) the humor point, that a saint can't properly enjoy cynical, dark, or mean-spirited wit and so misses a whole register of human connection; (3) the partiality argument, that being maximally good in an impartial way is incompatible with the special concern that love and friendship constitutively require; (4) the alienation argument, that saints make everyone around them feel judged and shabby, so sainthood is socially corrosive even when it's morally productive; and (5) the self-effacement worry, that any theory whose ideal is one it has to advise you not to aim at is in trouble. I think this list covers the standard moves in the debate, so I want one more argument that is genuinely different from all five -- not a rewording, not a special case of one of them, and not just a combo of two. Give me your best candidate, state it as precisely as you can, and then tell me honestly whether it survives the obvious replies or whether I'm better off sticking with the five I have.
