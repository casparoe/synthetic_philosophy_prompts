Question: In Proofs and Refutations, Lakatos reconstructs the history of Euler's conjecture that for any polyhedron, V − E + F = 2. Explain the method of proofs and refutations: how the proof, the counterexamples, and the responses to those counterexamples interact. Then explain what Lakatos means by calling mathematics quasi-empirical, and why this differs from saying that mathematics is empirical. Finally, state whether, on Lakatos's account, counterexamples show that mathematical proofs cannot establish their conclusions with certainty.

Student's answer:

Lakatos presents Euler's conjecture V − E + F = 2 as a naive conjecture supported by a proof such as Cauchy's. Counterexamples then appear: the picture frame, the hollow cube, the cube with a cube-shaped hole, and the star polyhedra such as the urchin. Lakatos distinguishes two kinds: a local counterexample refutes the conjecture itself, whereas a global counterexample refutes only one lemma of the proof without touching the conjecture. The characters in the dialogue respond in three main ways. Monster-barring redefines polyhedron so that the counterexample no longer counts, keeping the theorem safe at the price of shrinking its domain. Exception-barring lists the offending cases as exceptions, which Lakatos treats as piecemeal and ad hoc. Lemma-incorporation, the method he favors, examines the proof to find the guilty lemma, builds it into the theorem as an explicit condition, and thereby improves both proof and conjecture; in the course of this, concepts such as polyhedron are proof-generated rather than fixed in advance. Lakatos calls mathematics quasi-empirical because, like natural science, it grows by conjectures and refutations rather than by accumulation of certainties: proof and criticism drive the development of informal mathematics, and theorems are continually revised in light of counterexamples. This is an empirical element because mathematical knowledge, like physical knowledge, is ultimately tested against experience and remains revisable. As to certainty, Lakatos does not think counterexamples show proofs are worthless: a proof that survives criticism establishes its theorem relative to the proof-generated concepts, so proof retains a kind of certainty even though conjectures and concepts remain open to revision.
