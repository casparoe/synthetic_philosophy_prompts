I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in applied ethics, and I'm putting together this year's final exam. My interest in the topic is not purely academic: two months ago a friend visited me from abroad, flatly refused to tip at restaurants on the grounds that tipping is a foolish custom where she comes from, and then, on our way home, waited obediently at a red light on an empty street at midnight because that is what one does where she comes from. I have not been able to settle, to my own satisfaction, whether her two choices were consistent, and I want an exam question that forces students to do better than I have.

Please write one essay question that students will have 75 minutes to answer, together with a model answer of roughly 600 words and a marking scheme.

The question should present students with three cases: (1) a visitor from a country without a tipping custom who leaves no tip at an American restaurant, knowing that the server is paid a tipped wage and relies on tips; (2) a visitor from a city where crossing against the light is universal who waits at an empty intersection in a city where doing so is both legally required and socially enforced -- or, if you prefer the mirror image, one who crosses mid-block where that is condemned; and (3) a newcomer to a queue-governed institution (a deli counter, a bus stop) who walks to the front and simply asks to be served, on the grounds that queuing is only a convention and conventions bind no one.

The question must not be answerable by reciting a generic account of conventions (Lewis-style coordination, "conventions create expectations, frustrated expectations cause harm, therefore follow conventions"). It should require students to determine whether any single principle yields plausible verdicts across all three cases, and -- if they conclude that none does -- to identify which differences among the cases are doing the moral work: whether the convention solves a coordination problem versus redistributes burdens; whether noncompliance harms identifiable victims or no one at all; whether the convention is self-enforcing or survives only through sanction; and whether visitors, given their shorter exposure, possible ignorance, and inability to renegotiate local practices, stand in the same position as locals or a different one. The question should also ask students to take a stand on whether a convention's being arbitrary, inefficient, or even foolish weakens the obligation to comply with it while one is subject to it.

For the marking scheme, use a 25-point total built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on the specific way weaker students typically fumble each point (for instance, conflating the badness of a convention with permission to defect from it, or treating "everyone expects it" as sufficient for obligation without addressing whether the expectation is reasonable to impose on the noncompliant party). Finally, include a short paragraph explaining what distinguishes an A answer from a B answer, framed in terms of the specific moves each must contain rather than general qualities like clarity or thoroughness.
