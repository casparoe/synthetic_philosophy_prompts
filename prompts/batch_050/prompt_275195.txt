I run a small reading group on the philosophy of mathematics, and we keep circling back to heuristic reasoning: the kind of argument where you model a deterministic arithmetic structure as if it were random and read off a prediction. I've noticed my own trust in these arguments isn't uniform, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, is doing the work. Here are my verdicts. The argument that the probability of two random integers being coprime is the product over primes p of (1 − 1/p²), treating divisibility by distinct primes as independent: completely solid, even though nothing here is literally random. The naive independence argument for twin primes (each odd number is prime with probability about 1/ln n, so twins occur with probability about 1/(ln n)²) needs a correction factor for the shared obstruction modulo small primes, and once that factor is inserted I trust it just as much as the coprime argument. In the birthday problem, treating real birthdays as independent and uniform strikes me as fine even though births are seasonal, because the errors are small relative to the effect. But here's my trouble case: the random-walk heuristic for the sum of the Möbius function suggested that this sum stays bounded by the square root of x forever, and that turned out to be false. Yet I don't feel the heuristic was a bad argument, or that we should now distrust the coprime and twin prime arguments, which use the same machinery. Meanwhile I do feel that anyone who used the same machinery to conclude that primes are split exactly evenly between the residue classes 1 and 3 mod 4 at every scale would have been led astray, since there is a persistent, structured bias toward 3. So: sometimes the independence presumption fails and I blame the conclusion, and sometimes it fails and I blame the application, and I can't articulate the difference.

What I'd like from you: state the most precise principle you can that reproduces as many of my verdicts as possible — when modeling deterministic arithmetic events as independent random ones is legitimate, when it needs local correction factors, and when it is liable to fail outright and in what signature way (drift, bias, rare large deviation). Be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot save and has to overturn, and say what the principle predicts for two cases I haven't listed: the heuristic estimate for the number of primes up to x, and a heuristic about coincidences in a crowd, say the chance that two strangers at a party share a birthday month.
