I'm TAing an undergrad aesthetics seminar and we're doing the paradox of fiction next week -- you know, why do we seem to feel real fear, pity, sadness etc. for characters we fully know don't exist, when emotions supposedly require believing their object exists and matters. I've taught this a couple times now and the syllabus basically cycles through the same four solutions:

(1) the irrationality response -- we genuinely do feel the emotions, and they're just irrational or arational given what we believe, full stop, nothing to explain away; (2) the make-believe/pretense response -- we don't really feel fear of the slime, we fictionally feel it, what we actually experience is a kind of quasi-emotion generated by participating in a game of pretense, so the apparent contradiction dissolves; (3) the thought theory -- emotions don't need existential beliefs at all, merely entertaining or vividly imagining a content is enough to trigger genuine emotion, so there's no paradox once you drop the belief requirement; (4) the "we sort of do believe" response -- in the grip of a good story we temporarily, partially lose track of the fact that it's fiction, and the emotion rides on that fleeting quasi-belief.

Here's my problem: I want to end the unit with something the students haven't seen, so I'm trying to come up with a fifth solution that is genuinely not on this list. Not a rewording of one of them, not a special case of one of them applied to some genre, and not just a hybrid of two of them. It should handle the original puzzle (real-seeming emotion, known fictionality) at least as well as the others, and ideally say something about cases the standard four handle awkwardly, like emotions toward abstract fiction, or why we can feel things for characters we actively dislike the portrayal of.

Structure your answer like this: first a section called "The fifth solution" (about 150-200 words) stating the view and how it dissolves or resolves the paradox; then "Why it's not one of the four" (about 100 words) where you check it against each listed solution, briefly; then "Weakest point" (2-3 sentences) on the objection most likely to sink it. If you genuinely can't produce a solution that survives your own weakest-point check, say so in that section and explain what makes the space of possible solutions feel closed.
