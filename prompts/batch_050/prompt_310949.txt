Having spent my career as an experimental physicist, I came to philosophy of mathematics only in retirement, largely through a reading group at my local university. What prompted my interest was noticing how frequently Gödel's incompleteness theorems are invoked outside their proper home: I have encountered them in arguments that the human mind cannot be a machine, that physics can never be completed, that there are truths accessible only through religious or aesthetic insight, and even that bureaucracies are doomed to inconsistency. After working through the theorems themselves (at the level a determined amateur can manage), I have arrived at a position, and I would like to know what would count against it. My position is this: the incompleteness theorems are results about sufficiently strong formal systems capable of representing arithmetic, and nothing whatsoever follows from them about minds, physics, theology, or human institutions except in combination with substantial independent philosophical premises — premises which, in every case I have examined, do all the real argumentative work, with Gödel's theorems serving as decoration. The popular invocations are at best loose metaphors and at worst category errors.

I am aware of the strongest candidate counterexample, namely the Lucas–Penrose style of argument, which purports to derive a conclusion about minds from the theorems plus apparently modest assumptions about idealized human arithmetical reasoning. My inclination is to say this confirms rather than refutes my position, since the contested premises (that human mathematical thought is sound and can be idealized as a consistent formal system) carry the entire weight. But I worry that this response risks rendering my position unfalsifiable: if every argument from Gödel to an extra-mathematical conclusion can be dismissed on the grounds that its auxiliary premises "do the real work," then I have insulated the position rather than defended it, since virtually no interesting argument proceeds from a single theorem alone. So I would like a map of my position's exposure. What, specifically, would count against it? Please distinguish defeaters that would be decisive (for instance, a domain outside mathematics where an incompleteness-style diagonalization yields a conclusion without contentious bridging premises, if such a thing exists) from developments that would merely be awkward (for instance, cases where the metaphorical use has nonetheless proved scientifically fruitful). I would also like to know what defenders of my restrictionist view have themselves conceded as genuine costs, not merely what its critics allege, and whether the analogous phenomenon in computability theory — where undecidability results do seem to constrain certain idealized physical or economic models — is a genuine counterexample or can be assimilated to mathematics proper.

For context, what I would personally find most persuasive is a case where the incompleteness theorems, rather than some premise about minds or physics smuggled in alongside them, demonstrably exclude a possibility that would otherwise be open. What I would find least persuasive is a restatement of the Lucas–Penrose dialectic, which I have already read. Please organize the response as an ordered list of candidate defeaters, from most to least decisive, followed by your assessment of whether my position survives scrutiny in its present form or requires qualification. End with a final line of the form \answer{survives | survives with qualification | fails}, indicating which verdict your analysis supports.
