Christopher Nolan's Memento is usually discussed as a puzzle about plot, but I'd like a reading of it through the philosophy of mind, specifically the color phi phenomenon and Dennett's distinction between Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions of experience. For reference: in the color phi effect, two differently colored dots are flashed in sequence at different positions, and subjects report seeing a single dot that moves and changes color midway along its path -- apparently experiencing the color change before the second dot has even been presented. Dennett uses this to ask whether the brain revises an already-conscious experience in memory (Orwellian revision, after the rewriting of history in 1984) or instead delays and fabricates the experience before it ever enters consciousness (Stalinesque revision, after the staged show trials), and he argues that once the timescales get short enough, the distinction between the two dissolves.

Read Memento against this framework. Leonard cannot form new memories, so his experienced present is continually being re-anchored by tattoos, notes, and photographs left by his past self -- and the film's reversed chronology forces the viewer into a version of the same predicament, interpreting each scene in light of what is revealed later, which is chronologically earlier. Some questions I'd like the reading to engage: Does the film treat Leonard's condition as Orwellian (he experiences, then the record is rewritten) or Stalinesque (the record is rigged in advance so that his experience arrives pre-falsified, a show trial he stages for himself)? Does the climax, where we learn Leonard deliberately planted false clues to steer his future self toward killing Teddy, collapse the distinction in something like Dennett's way -- and does it show Leonard exploiting the collapse on purpose? And what does the film's structure do to the viewer that argument alone can't: is watching Memento itself a kind of color phi experiment, in which our experience of each scene is retroactively colored by information that arrives after it? Finally, where does the analogy strain? Be honest about what the film dramatizes well and what it gets wrong or simply ignores about the timing of experience -- I'm writing an essay on this and would rather have the friction points than a tidy fit.
