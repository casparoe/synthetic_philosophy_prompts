I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for our awards-season package I'm building an interactive feature: a decision tree that sorts readers into positions on what, if anything, filmmakers and novelists owe the real people they dramatize -- the dead especially, with the living as a comparison case. Readers are notoriously unreliable at naming their own views on this, so I want the tree to route them through concrete reactions rather than letting them pick a flattering label.

Please design the tree from the logical space first, not from a roster of named positions. Start by identifying the questions on which views genuinely diverge -- candidates include whether the dead can be wronged at all, whether factual accuracy matters in itself or only as a safeguard against reputational harm, whether obligations dilute with the passage of time, whether the consent or objection of descendants and estates carries weight, and whether the genre or aims of the work (satire, tragedy, polemic) change the duty -- and only then locate actual defensible positions at the leaves.

Constraints:

- Six to eight diagnostic nodes. Each must be posed as a concrete scenario a reader can react to without knowing any theory: for instance, a prestige drama that invents an affair, a series that attributes a real crime to a real person dead for a century, a novel that compresses three actual colleagues into one unsympathetic composite, a comedy that mocks a recently deceased public figure's final illness.
- Label every branch with the claim that splits it, phrased as a proposition a reader simply accepts or rejects.
- The leaves must include, at minimum: a strong fidelity view (accuracy is owed to the subject regardless of consequences); a harm-based view (duties run only to the living, so the dead are fair game but their grieving families are not); a dignity view (the dead can be wronged by unfair portrayal even where no factual claim is false); a license view (once a work announces itself as fiction, it owes nothing); and a contextual or stakeholder view (duties vary with time elapsed, degree of fame, and family involvement). If your tree's questions cannot keep two of these apart, flag the collapse honestly rather than papering over it.
- Mark any unoccupied combinations of answers and diagnose each in a sentence: is the position nobody holds incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored?
- Close each leaf with one sentence naming the portrayal that would most embarrass that position -- the case on which its defender has to bite the bullet.

Two further requests. First, keep every node worded neutrally enough that no answer reads as obviously the respectable one; a quiz whose questions preach is useless to me. Second, deliver the tree as a clean indented outline I can hand directly to our graphics desk, with node text short enough to fit in a flowchart box.
