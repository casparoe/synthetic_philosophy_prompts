I am preparing a fifty-minute session for philosophically capable undergraduates who know basic set notation but have not studied structuralism in the philosophy of mathematics. The aim is for them to understand why the question whether 2 is an element of 4 creates an identity problem for mathematical structuralism, rather than merely to learn that different set-theoretic constructions of the natural numbers exist. Please design a teaching plan, not the lecture itself.

Build the plan around two simple constructions: one in which each natural number is the set of all its predecessors, so that 2 ∈ 4, and one in which zero is the empty set and each successor is the singleton of its predecessor, so that 2 ∉ 4. Both constructions induce isomorphic successor structures and validate the same elementary arithmetic. Specify the order in which the session should introduce concrete number construction, isomorphism, arithmetic structure, positions within a structure, and cross-construction identity, explaining what each stage must establish before the next becomes intelligible. In particular, indicate how to prevent students from taking the two constructions to concern different subjects before they have seen why both are supposed to present the same natural-number structure.

Explain what the carrying example must make visible and what it should temporarily suppress. The plan should address, in the order they are likely to arise, at least these misconceptions: that the discrepancy is just a disagreement over notation; that choosing one construction by convention resolves the philosophical problem; that structuralism must deny numbers have any properties whatsoever; that a structural fact is simply any fact shared by two representations; and that isomorphism preserves membership facts as well as arithmetic relations. For each misconception, state where in the sequence it should be anticipated and what intervention would correct it without prematurely introducing the whole taxonomy of eliminative, in re, and ante rem structuralisms.

Include one brief check for understanding involving a third, structurally adequate but extensionally different construction, designed to reveal whether students can distinguish an implementation-specific fact from a fact about the structure itself. Conclude by identifying what you would deliberately leave out of this introductory session—for example, technical issues about categoricity, plurality of structures, or the metaphysics of positions—and how you would tell students that these omissions do not make the central identity question disappear.
