I am a novelist planning a political fantasy in which a city’s economy runs on sworn undertakings: some enforced by law, some by reputation, some by an uncanny rite that detects insincerity. I do not want the book to turn into a lecture, but I do want the moral geometry to be exact when characters disagree about whether a vow still binds after betrayal, coercion, changed circumstances, or the death of the person to whom it was made. Please produce, in British English, a classification instrument rather than an essay: a decision tree or taxonomy for the question why promises bind, aimed at philosophically literate general readers, with no more than eight diagnostic branch-questions and no more than twelve leaves.

Build the tree from the logical space first, then place the familiar positions in it: Humean social practice, Kantian fidelity as a requirement of rational agency, consequentialist reliance and expectation, Scanlon-style duties created by shaping others’ expectations, contractualist or mutual-recognition accounts, virtue or integrity accounts, natural-duty accounts of fair play or beneficence, sceptical or deflationary views on which promising is only conventional threat-and-assurance, and any mixed view you think genuinely occupied. Each branch should be phrased as a question answerable without jargon, for example whether a promise binds only because a practice exists, whether the promisee’s uptake matters as well as the promisor’s intention, whether an unjust or coerced undertaking has any moral weight, whether breach leaves a residue after compensation, and whether institutions can inherit or extinguish personal obligations. Use concrete cases at the nodes: a promise extracted by a blackmailer, a promise to a dying person whose heir releases it, a promise that becomes ruinous through nobody’s fault, a promise made in a fictional role, a promise between states or companies, and a promise kept only because detection is certain.

Every leaf should carry the position’s name, one sentence on its core source of bindingness, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment for a novelist to dramatise. Note any unoccupied combinations and say briefly whether they look incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply underexplored; flag pairs of leaves your questions cannot cleanly separate rather than pretending the tree is sharper than it is. End with a short scoring key by which a reader’s pattern of answers locates a position, and add two lines on how I might give each major position a dramatically fair voice without endorsing it.
