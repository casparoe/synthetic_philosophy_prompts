Since retiring from a career in experimental biology, I have been reading moral philosophy, and I keep returning to Warren Quinn's self-torturer puzzle — partly because it seems to me a problem my own field half-solved long ago, and partly because I cannot get my own reactions to it to line up. Let me set out my verdicts on a series of cases and ask what principle, if any, lies behind them.

The device, in my version: a dial with settings 0 to 1,000. Each click upward raises a painful stimulus by an increment below the threshold of perceptibility and credits me with $25, payable when I stop; I may stop whenever I like.

My verdicts. (1) From any setting n, clicking once more is rational: I cannot feel the difference, and twenty-five dollars is twenty-five dollars. (2) Yet someone who clicks all the way to 1,000 has gone badly wrong; no accumulated sum justifies agony one plainly feels. So my tentative principle is that rationality assesses patterns of choices, not only choices taken one at a time: a sequence of individually rational steps can compose an irrational whole.

Now the harder cases. (3) Same device, but a trustee advances the dial one notch each night while I sleep, and I may terminate the arrangement any morning. My verdict: the rational move is to fix a stopping rule in advance rather than re-decide each morning — which seems to confirm the pattern principle. (4) Offered pairwise choices between adjacent settings-with-payments, I genuinely prefer n+1 to n at every n, while preferring setting 3 to setting 900 outright. My verdict: these preferences are not confusion on my part; the intransitivity is real, not apparent. (5) But if someone proposes to pump me — repeatedly trading me around the cycle n, n+1, back to n at a dollar a turn — I would refuse, and I take refusing to be rational. So I deny that my intransitive preferences license pumping me. (6) Finally, a case from psychophysics: three color samples, each adjacent pair indiscriminable, the outer pair plainly different. Indiscriminability simply is not transitive, and that is a fact about perception, not a defect of judgment. I suspect "not preferred to" may behave the same way.

My trouble is that these strain against one another. The money pump is supposed to be the standard argument that intransitive preferences are irrational; my refusal in (5) denies the inference, while my pattern principle in (2) seemingly accepts something like it. And if local rationality does not guarantee global rationality, I need a principled account of which wholes are assessable — why the dial sequence is one, but my pairwise preferences apparently are not.

What I would like: a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible — distinguishing, if need be, between evaluating preferences, individual choices, sequences, and stopping rules, and saying which verdict motivates each clause; an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and so must overturn; and its verdict on two cases I have not decided: a committee that approves a hundred individually trivial budget increments and ends with a total no member endorses, and a person whose tastes drift imperceptibly over decades until she endorses a life she would once have rejected. Please do not offer a reading list; I want the principle and an accounting of its costs.
