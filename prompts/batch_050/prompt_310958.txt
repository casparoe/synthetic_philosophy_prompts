I'm a high school student taking a social science elective, and for my final project I chose to read about cultural psychology, specifically the distinction between "tight" and "loose" cultures -- the idea that some societies enforce strong social norms and punish deviance, while others tolerate a wider range of behavior, and that a society's position on this spectrum may be shaped by its history of threats like invasions, famines, and disease. I got stuck on a question my teacher couldn't fully answer, so I'm asking it here. Please write your answer in American English.

Analyze the early COVID-19 pandemic response (roughly 2020) as a test case for the tight-loose framework. Take as given these widely reported facts: New Zealand closed its borders, imposed one of the strictest lockdowns among democracies, framed the effort in collective terms, and achieved very low death rates early on; Sweden deliberately avoided mandatory lockdowns, kept schools open for younger children, relied on voluntary guidance, and stressed individual responsibility, while experiencing substantially higher per-capita deaths than its Nordic neighbors; and the United States saw sharp internal division, with some states imposing strict mandates and others resisting even basic measures. Here's what puzzles me: on the theory, countries with histories of low external threat should be loose and therefore bad at collective compliance, yet New Zealand is geographically isolated and had faced few recent threats -- so shouldn't it have responded loosely, like Sweden? And Sweden's response seems loose on the surface, but it rested on enormous voluntary compliance with guidance and trust in authorities, which sounds more like a tight-society trait. Does the pandemic show that tightness and looseness are stable national traits that predicted responses, or that societies can tighten on demand when a threat arrives -- and if the latter, does the theory actually explain anything, or just redescribe what happened? Please map the framework carefully onto the episode first (which parts of the theory genuinely predict what), then identify where the mapping breaks down, and conclude with a verdict: is COVID a confirmation of the tight-loose account, an anomaly it needs to patch, or evidence that the theory's real explanatory work is done by something else, like institutional trust?
